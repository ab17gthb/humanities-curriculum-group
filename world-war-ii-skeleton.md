# The World War II Unit: Assignment Skeleton

_Seventh built unit for the Knoweth curriculum and planner site, sitting in course order after the Depression and New Deal and before the Cold War. Modeled on the Progressive Era pilot, the Gilded Age, Imperialism and World War I, the Twenties, and the Depression and New Deal units. Every lesson has the same working plate so teachers can pick pieces without losing the line of the unit._

## How to read this

- **HAVE**: an audited source or image already exists in the TPSP bank
- **BUILD**: create for this unit
- **LINK**: use the TPSP bank rather than rebuilding it

## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- How did the Second World War transform the United States, and how far did the country live up to the democracy it claimed to defend?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's core plan is 18 instructional days. The unit runs from the neutrality legislation of 1935 to the surrender of Japan in September 1945 and its immediate reckoning. It opens with the road to war, because the Depression and New Deal unit closes by naming the boundary plainly: war spending, not the New Deal, finished the Depression, and the mobilization itself belongs here. The four TPSP sources the Depression picker left untagged, the "Quarantine" speech of 1937, the "Arsenal of Democracy" fireside chat, the Four Freedoms address, and Executive Order 8802, are taught in this unit's Lessons 1 and 6, where they belong. The unit closes with the war's end and its immediate reckoning: the Yalta and Potsdam conferences, the atomic decision, the costs, and the transformed country that came home. It does not open the Cold War. Containment, NATO, and the Marshall Plan as policy belong to the Cold War unit; the conferences and the bomb are this unit's closing material, and the last lesson names that boundary plainly.

**The plate in this build**

1. **Reading**: 850 to 900 words with five questions and a teacher key
2. **Guided notes**: keyed to the reading and lesson, with the vocabulary set verbatim and a teacher key
3. **Activity**: stations, jigsaw, map work, simulation, debate, or structured chart work in the locked print design
4. **Notebook page**: a single organizer students keep
5. **Quick check**: a short formative tied to the blueprint
6. **Writing task**: a paragraph or short essay with a scaffold and a teacher key or rubric

TPSP source analyses and photo sets are linked where the audited bank already carries them. They are not rebuilt merely to fill a folder.

**Six state-neutral era topics**

1. The road to war and American entry
2. Mobilization and the home front
3. The global war: Europe and the Pacific
4. Democracy tested at home
5. Atrocity, the Holocaust, and war crimes
6. Victory, cost, and transformation

## Lesson 1: The Road to War: Neutrality and Its Critics

**Lesson question:** Between 1935 and 1941, how did the United States move from legislated neutrality toward war without declaring it?

**Reading scope:** The memory that governed the decade first: the First World War's costs, the Nye Committee's charge that bankers and arms makers had pulled the country in, and the determination in Congress that it would not happen again. The Neutrality Acts of 1935 to 1937, barring arms sales and loans to nations at war. The wars Americans watched: Japan in China from 1937, Italy in Ethiopia, the Spanish Civil War, and Germany's march through Austria and Czechoslovakia to Poland. Roosevelt's critics of his own policy inside his own speeches: the "Quarantine" address of October 1937 and its cold public reception, the fall of France in 1940, the destroyers for bases deal, the first peacetime draft, and cash-and-carry giving way to Lend-Lease in March 1941. The interventionist case stated in its own words: the Four Freedoms of January 1941 and the "Arsenal of Democracy" fireside chat. The opposition stated fairly: the America First Committee and the isolationist argument that defense at home was the only safe course. The Atlantic Charter of August 1941 and the undeclared naval war in the Atlantic, the Greer, the Kearny, and the Reuben James, closing on a country arming an ally and shooting at sea while its laws still said neutral.

**Activity concept:** The road in ten steps. Students work through ten dated stations from 1935 to 1941, a Neutrality Act provision, the "Quarantine" passage, a Nye Committee charge, a fall of France account, the Lend-Lease section, the Four Freedoms passage, an America First statement, the Atlantic Charter, and the Reuben James record, and for each step judge whether it moved the country toward war, held it back, or did both at once, then draw the road themselves and mark the step they judge decisive.

**Vocabulary:** isolationism (carried verbatim from the Twenties unit), Neutrality Acts, fireside chat (carried verbatim from the Depression and New Deal unit), cash-and-carry, Lend-Lease, Four Freedoms, Atlantic Charter

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP "Quarantine" speech, "Arsenal of Democracy" fireside chat, Four Freedoms address, Lend-Lease Act, and Atlantic Charter, all audited records. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 2: Pearl Harbor and the Arsenal of Democracy

**Lesson question:** What did the attack on Pearl Harbor settle, and what did it leave for Americans to decide?

**Reading scope:** December 7, 1941 in sequence: the Japanese carrier strike on the Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor, the ships and aircraft lost and the more than 2,400 Americans killed, the attacks the same day on American and British positions across the Pacific, and the Philippines invasion that followed. Roosevelt's address to Congress on December 8, asking for a declaration of war against Japan, and the votes, one dissent in the House. Germany and Italy declaring war on the United States on December 11, settling the question of a one-ocean war by the enemy's own choice. What the attack settled: the neutrality debate ended in a day, and the country entered united in a way it had not entered in 1917. What it left open: how to fight a two-ocean war against three enemies at once. The answer built in the war's first conferences: the Grand Alliance with Britain and the Soviet Union, the Germany first strategy, and the Soviet demand for a second front in France. The cost of the war's first six months stated plainly: Bataan and the fall of the Philippines, the Doolittle Raid as an answer in morale rather than damage, and an arsenal that in December 1941 existed mostly as a promise and a set of factory orders.

**Activity concept:** Settled and unsettled. Students work from the Pearl Harbor address, the December 11 German declaration record, the Germany first agreement, a Bataan account, and a 1942 production order table, and sort the war's opening facts into what December 1941 decided and what it left to be decided, then judge which of the open questions carried the most risk.

**Vocabulary:** Pearl Harbor, Axis powers, Allies, Grand Alliance, Germany first, second front, theater

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Pearl Harbor Address, audited record. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 3: Mobilizing the Home Front

**Lesson question:** How did the United States turn a peacetime economy into the arsenal the war required, and who did the work?

**Reading scope:** The scale of the conversion first: an army of under 200,000 in 1939 becoming a force of more than 12 million in uniform by 1945, fed by volunteers and by the Selective Training and Service Act of 1940, the first peacetime draft. The production miracle stated in figures: factories converted from cars to tanks and planes, shipyards building Liberty ships in weeks, aircraft production rising past 300,000 planes across the war, and unemployment, the Depression's defining figure, falling to near one percent as the labor shortage replaced the job shortage. The machinery that ran it: the War Production Board allocating steel, rubber, and machine tools; the Office of Price Administration holding prices and running rationing of gasoline, rubber, sugar, meat, and shoes; war bonds and the new mass income tax paying for a war that cost more than ten times the First World War. The workers the economy had left out in 1941: six million women entering paid work, drawn by posters of Rosie the Riveter into shipyards and aircraft plants; Black, Mexican American, and Native American workers migrating to war industry cities in a second Great Migration that redrew the map of American population; and the strains that came with it, crowded housing, working mothers, and the ration book in every kitchen. The reading closes by holding two facts together: the mobilization ended the Depression, and it did so by government direction of the economy on a scale the New Deal never attempted.

**Activity concept:** The conversion desk. Student groups receive one shortage file each, rubber, shipyard labor, aircraft output, kitchen sugar, and housing in a boomtown, and work the documents to decide what the government did about each shortage, which agency acted, who gained work, and who paid the cost, then assemble the class conversion chart.

**Vocabulary:** mobilization, Selective Training and Service Act, War Production Board, war bonds, rationing, Office of Price Administration, Rosie the Riveter

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP "We Can Do It!" poster record and federal war photographs and posters. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 4: The War in Europe and North Africa

**Lesson question:** How did the Allies carry the war from North Africa to the surrender of Germany, and what did each stage cost?

**Reading scope:** The war the Allies inherited: Germany holding Europe from France to the gates of Moscow, and the Eastern Front after June 1941 carrying the largest land fighting of the war, with Stalingrad in 1942 to 1943 as its turning point. The American entry into the ground war: Operation Torch in North Africa in November 1942, the first joint campaign, then Sicily and Italy in 1943, a hard mountain war that tied down German divisions without ending the argument over the second front. The air war: the strategic bombing campaign from England, daylight precision doctrine tested at Schweinfurt, the combined bomber offensive against German oil and transport, and the civilian cost under the bombs stated as record, including Hamburg and Dresden. D-Day, June 6, 1944, in sequence: the largest seaborne invasion ever mounted, the five beaches, airborne drops, the artificial harbors, and the breakout from Normandy in July. The drive across France, the failure at Arnhem, the Battle of the Bulge in December 1944 as Germany's last offensive in the west, the Rhine crossings, the meeting with Soviet forces at the Elbe in April 1945, Hitler's suicide, and the unconditional surrender signed at Reims, marked as VE Day on May 8, 1945. The reading closes with the accounting: the Soviet Union bore the largest share of the land war and the largest losses, and any honest map of the European war shows it.

**Activity concept:** Five maps, one war. Students work through five dated theater maps with a short record for each, North Africa 1942, Italy 1943, Normandy 1944, the Bulge 1944, and Germany 1945, and for each map state the Allied aim, the result, and the cost, then judge which stage they would defend as the turning point of the European war.

**Vocabulary:** amphibious landing, Eastern Front, Stalingrad, strategic bombing, D-Day, Battle of the Bulge, VE Day

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP "Into the Jaws of Death" photograph, Eisenhower's Order of the Day, and the "In Case of Failure" message, audited records. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 5: The War in the Pacific

**Lesson question:** How did the Allies cross the Pacific and close on Japan, and what did each step toward the home islands cost?

**Reading scope:** The Pacific the Japanese attack had won: an empire running from Manchuria to the Solomons by the spring of 1942, and the American problem stated as distance, thousands of miles of ocean with Japanese garrisons on the islands between. The turn at sea: the Coral Sea in May 1942 and Midway in June 1942, where American code breaking and carrier aircraft sank four Japanese carriers and ended Japan's ability to take the offensive. Guadalcanal from August 1942, the first Allied offensive, six months of fighting on land, at sea, and in the air for one island and its airfield. The strategy the rest of the war followed: island hopping, seizing selected islands for airfields and bypassing strong garrisons to wither, run by two commands, MacArthur through New Guinea toward the Philippines and Nimitz across the central Pacific. The submarine campaign against Japanese merchant shipping, strangling an island empire that imported its oil, steel, and food. The Marianas in 1944, putting Japan itself in range of the B-29, and the firebombing campaign that followed, Tokyo on March 9 to 10, 1945 as its deadliest night. The last steps: Iwo Jima in February and March 1945, eight square miles taken at a cost of about 6,800 American dead; Okinawa from April to June 1945, the largest battle of the Pacific war, kamikaze attacks in waves, and civilian deaths on a scale that made planners read the battle as a preview of invading Japan itself. The reading closes with the arithmetic the summer of 1945 faced: blockade and bombardment were starving Japan, and its government still had not surrendered.

**Activity concept:** The stepping stone ledger. Students work a Pacific theater map with five battle records, Midway, Guadalcanal, the Marianas, Iwo Jima, and Okinawa, and keep a ledger for each: what the island or battle bought the advance, what it cost in American and Japanese dead, and what it taught about the next step, then judge from the ledger what the invasion of Japan itself was expected to cost and who was doing the expecting.

**Vocabulary:** Battle of Midway, island hopping, kamikaze, firebombing, naval blockade, unconditional surrender, Guadalcanal

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. Federal Navy and Marine Corps photographs, National Archives. **LINK** / **BUILD** from public-domain records.

## Lesson 6: A War for Democracy? Exclusion, Internment, and Service

**Lesson question:** How did a war fought in democracy's name test democracy at home, in who could work, who could serve, and who was imprisoned?

**Reading scope:** The contradiction stated at the start: a nation fighting racist empires abroad while running a segregated army and a discriminating war economy at home. The fight for war work first: A. Philip Randolph's threatened march on Washington in 1941 and Executive Order 8802, barring discrimination in defense industries, with the Fair Employment Practices Committee to hear complaints, its real enforcement limits stated. The Double V campaign of the Black press, victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home. Service in a segregated military: more than a million Black Americans served, most assigned to service and labor units under white officers, with the Tuskegee Airmen and the 761st Tank Battalion as the combat record that answered the policy; the Navy and Marines opening slowly; Benjamin O. Davis Sr. as the first Black general. Women in uniform: more than 350,000 served across the services, some 150,000 of them in the Women's Army Corps over the course of the war, alongside the WAVES, the SPARs, and more than 59,000 Army nurses. Mexican American service in large numbers, Native American code talkers, and more than half a million Jewish Americans in uniform. Then the exclusion that defines the lesson: Executive Order 9066 of February 19, 1942, the removal of about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry from the West Coast, two thirds of them American citizens, the camps behind barbed wire in the interior, the property sold at a loss or lost outright, and Korematsu v. United States in 1944 upholding the exclusion as military necessity over a dissent that named it racism. The 442nd Regimental Combat Team and the Military Intelligence Service answering from inside the contradiction. The reading closes with the question the lesson title asks, left open on purpose: the war widened the claims Americans could make on their own democracy, and the claims and the camps stand in the same years.

**Activity concept:** The contradiction file. Students work through five documents, Executive Order 8802, a Double V editorial passage, a Tuskegee record, Executive Order 9066, and a Korematsu passage, and for each one record who gained a claim on American democracy, who was denied one, and what reason the government or the document itself gave, then rank the five by the size of the gap between the war's stated aims and the document's facts.

**Vocabulary:** Executive Order 8802, Fair Employment Practices Committee, Double V campaign, Executive Order 9066, internment, Korematsu v. United States, 442nd Regimental Combat Team

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Executive Order 8802 and Executive Order 9066, Korematsu majority opinion, Munson Report, and the Mochida evacuation and "American Gothic" photographs, all audited records. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 7: The Holocaust and the War's Crimes

**Lesson question:** What did Nazi Germany do to the Jews of Europe and to others it marked for persecution and death, and what did the world know, do, and answer?

**Reading scope:** Taught with documentary sobriety, from the record. The persecution in sequence: antisemitism as Nazi state doctrine, the Nuremberg Laws of 1935 stripping Jews of citizenship, Kristallnacht in November 1938, and the refugees the world mostly refused, the Evian Conference and the ship St. Louis turned away in 1939. The machinery after the invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941: the Einsatzgruppen shooting squads that murdered more than a million people in open air killings, Babi Yar among them; the ghettos of occupied Poland, Warsaw largest, where Jews were confined under starvation conditions; and the decision carried out from 1942, the Final Solution, deportations by rail to killing centers built for murder, Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, and the others, where most arrivals were killed in gas chambers within hours. The count stated as the record supports it: six million Jews murdered, about two thirds of Europe's Jewish population, alongside millions of others, Roma, people with disabilities murdered in the T4 program before the camps, Polish and Soviet civilians, and more than three million Soviet prisoners of war. Knowledge and response handled as questions of evidence: what Allied governments knew and when, the reports that reached them from 1942, the War Refugee Board of 1944 and what it saved, and the debate over bombing the rail lines and camps, stated as a debate historians still argue from documents. The war's other crimes in the same record: the Bataan Death March of 1942, Japanese treatment of prisoners and occupied peoples, and German treatment of Soviet prisoners. Liberation in 1945: what advancing armies found, Eisenhower's order that Ohrdruf be documented and witnessed, and the answer the Allies chose in law, the Nuremberg trials and Justice Jackson's opening statement, that the reckoning would be made from evidence in a courtroom.

**Activity concept:** The record and the response. Students work five documents in sequence, a Nuremberg Laws provision, a St. Louis passenger record, the Ohrdruf cable, a War Refugee Board summary, and the Jackson opening passage, and build the lesson's timeline from them: persecution, refusal, murder, documentation, and judgment, marking at each step what was known and by whom, and what was done and what was not.

**Vocabulary:** antisemitism, Nuremberg Laws, Holocaust, ghetto, Final Solution, death camp, war crimes

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Eisenhower Ohrdruf cable and Jackson Nuremberg opening statement, audited records; United States Holocaust Memorial Museum and National Archives photographs chosen for evidentiary value and captioned exactly. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 8: Victory and the Atomic Reckoning

**Lesson question:** How did the war end, what did the atomic bomb decide and leave undecided, and what country came home from the war?

**Reading scope:** The conferences that planned the peace while the war ran: Yalta in February 1945, the United Nations promised, Germany's division sketched, Soviet entry against Japan pledged; Potsdam in July 1945, Truman in Roosevelt's place after April 12, the occupation argued over, and the Potsdam Declaration of July 26 demanding Japan's unconditional surrender and warning of prompt and utter destruction. The atomic decision taught from the documents: the Manhattan Project, a secret city building a weapon for more than two years at a cost of about two billion dollars; the Franck Report and the scientists who argued for a demonstration; the interim committee's recommendation; the target list and the arguments made at the time, that the bomb would end the war without an invasion whose cost Okinawa had previewed. Trinity on July 16, Hiroshima on August 6, the Soviet declaration of war and invasion of Manchuria on August 8 and 9, Nagasaki on August 9, and the Japanese government's divided vote broken by the emperor. The surrender signed on the Missouri on September 2, 1945. The reckoning in figures: about 405,000 American dead; worldwide deaths estimated between 60 and 85 million, the Soviet Union's share the largest at about 27 million; the displaced across Europe and Asia. The country that came home: 12 million in uniform demobilized into the GI Bill's tuition and loans, an economy that had doubled its output, women pushed back toward the home as veterans took the jobs, Black veterans returning to the South they had left, and a United States standing as one of two great powers with its cities untouched. The reading closes by framing the argument the next unit takes up: the alliance that won the war was already divided over Europe's future at Potsdam, and the weapon unveiled in August 1945 made the division dangerous in a new way.

**Activity concept:** The decision file. Students receive the July and August 1945 file in order, the Potsdam Declaration, a Franck Report passage, an invasion planning summary, the Truman Hiroshima statement, and the surrender instrument, and write the decision memorandum they would have sent on August 1, 1945, then compare it against the record of what was decided and defend the difference or the agreement.

**Vocabulary:** Yalta Conference, Potsdam Conference, Potsdam Declaration, Manhattan Project, atomic bomb, demobilization, GI Bill

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Potsdam Declaration, Franck Report, Truman Hiroshima statement, and Japanese Instrument of Surrender, all audited records. **LINK** / **HAVE**. TPSP premium DBQ number 12, "Was the U.S. Right to Drop the Atomic Bomb?" is the natural writing extension here. **LINK** for the planner picker, not rebuilt.

## Changes from the working map

- The unit opens in 1935 rather than in 1939 or 1941, because the neutrality debate is the argument the whole war answers, and because four audited TPSP sources the Depression unit's picker left untagged, the "Quarantine" speech, the "Arsenal of Democracy," the Four Freedoms, and Executive Order 8802, are taught here where they belong, in Lessons 1 and 6.
- Internment and the wider service record share one lesson rather than splitting, because the lesson's question is the contradiction itself, and the 442nd's record reads differently when the camps that held its families are in the same hour.
- The Holocaust has its own lesson rather than a section of the European war lesson, because its chronology runs from 1933 to 1945 and its questions, knowledge, refusal, and reckoning, are not campaign questions. It is taught from documents and photographs of evidentiary value, without sensationalism.
- The unit closes with the atomic decision and the surrender, and with the conferences that planned the peace. It does not open containment, NATO, or the Marshall Plan. Those are the Cold War unit's opening material, and the last lesson names the division visible at Potsdam without teaching the policy that answered it.
