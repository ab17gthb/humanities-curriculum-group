Guided Notes: Montgomery and Little Rock

Lesson question: How did a bus boycott and nine schoolchildren teach the movement its methods, and force the federal government to act?

Unit question: How did the civil rights movement change American law and American life, and what did it leave unfinished?

Key Terms

boycott: a refusal to buy goods or use services as a form of protest, meant to pressure an institution by withdrawing the money and cooperation it depends on

Montgomery bus boycott: the protest of 1955 and 1956 in which Black residents of Montgomery, Alabama refused to ride the city's buses for 381 days, ending when the Supreme Court ordered the buses desegregated

nonviolent direct action: protest that confronts an unjust law or practice openly and without violence, accepting arrest and punishment in order to expose the injustice and force negotiation

Southern Christian Leadership Conference: the organization founded in 1957 by Martin Luther King Jr. and other Black ministers to coordinate nonviolent protest campaigns across the South

Emmett Till: the fourteen-year-old Chicago boy murdered in Mississippi in August 1955 after being accused of offending a white woman, whose open-casket funeral and published photographs showed the nation what southern racial violence looked like

Little Rock Nine: the nine Black students who desegregated Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas in 1957 under the protection of federal troops after the state's governor tried to bar their entry

Executive Order 10730: President Eisenhower's order of September 24, 1957 federalizing the Arkansas National Guard and directing federal troops to enforce school desegregation at Little Rock

The Tactics at a Glance. Montgomery: Black residents withdrew their fares for 381 days through the Montgomery Improvement Association, its car pool of 23 dispatch centers, and weekly mass meetings, enduring indictments and a bombing, and the Supreme Court's ruling in Browder v. Gayle desegregated the buses. It proved that economic pressure sustained by an organization of the movement's own could defeat a segregation practice without waiting for the city's consent. The Till record: Mamie Till-Mobley held an open-casket funeral and let Jet publish David Jackson's photographs after an all-white jury acquitted her son's killers. It proved that a published record could make the nation look at violence it had excused, and it taught the movement the power of witness. Little Rock: Governor Faubus barred nine students with his National Guard, and President Eisenhower answered with Executive Order 10730, the federalized Guard, and the 101st Airborne. It proved that a court order defied by a governor could bring federal troops behind it, and Faubus's Lost Year closings showed the resistance's answer when the troops succeeded.

Exit ticket 1. A boycott is a refusal to buy goods or use services as a form of protest, meant to pressure an institution by withdrawing the money and cooperation it depends on. The Montgomery Improvement Association ran the Montgomery boycott, and its president was Martin Luther King Jr.

Exit ticket 2. Executive Order 10730 placed the Arkansas National Guard under federal command, taking it out of Governor Faubus's control, and authorized and directed the Secretary of Defense to use federal troops, including the 101st Airborne Division, to enforce the federal court's desegregation orders at Little Rock.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer pairing one method fact with one federal fact. The strongest answers run from the boycott's economic pressure and organization in Montgomery, or the witness of the published Till photographs, to Eisenhower's Executive Order 10730 and the 101st Airborne escorting the Little Rock Nine on September 25, 1957.
