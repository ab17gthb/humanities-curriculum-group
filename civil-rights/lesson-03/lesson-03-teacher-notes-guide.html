Montgomery and Little Rock

The Civil Rights Movement · Lesson 3 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: The Civil Rights Movement, Lesson 3 of 8. Teach it directly after Lesson 2, which ended with Brown declared, Brown II delayed, massive resistance organized, and Cooper v. Aaron settled on paper while barely one Black child in a hundred in the Deep South attended school with white children by 1964. This lesson turns from the courts to direct action: the warning letter of May 1954, the arrest of December 1, 1955, the 381-day Montgomery bus boycott, the murder of Emmett Till in August 1955, and Little Rock in September 1957, where a governor barred a schoolhouse door and the president answered with an address, Executive Order 10730, and the 101st Airborne. Lesson 4 follows the methods learned here into the sit-ins, the Freedom Rides, and the Albany Movement.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “Montgomery and Little Rock: The Movement Finds Its Tactics” and the guided notes, which follow the deck in sections A to G: a warning before the arrest, December 1, 1955: the arrest, the boycott holds: 381 days, the summer's wound: Emmett Till, Little Rock: a governor bars the door, the president acts, and the answer, and the method. The activity is “The Tactics Ledger: Montgomery and Little Rock.” The notebook page is “Notebook Page: Montgomery and Little Rock.” The quick check is “Quick Check: Soldiers at the Schoolhouse Door.” The writing task is “Writing Task: The Methods and the Federal Answer.”

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Students need Lessons 1 and 2: Jim Crow and the institutions Black Americans built inside it, the NAACP and its court strategy, Brown v. Board of Education and Brown II, massive resistance, and Cooper v. Aaron arising from Little Rock. No prior knowledge of Jo Ann Robinson, the Women's Political Council, the Montgomery Improvement Association, Browder v. Gayle, Emmett Till, Orval Faubus, or Executive Order 10730 is assumed; each is taught in sequence.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (analyze connections among events: a prepared boycott network, an arrest, a sustained campaign, a published record of violence, a governor's defiance, and a federal answer, in sequence), D2.His.9 (construct arguments using historical evidence: the writing task joining the movement's methods to the compulsion that forced federal action), D2.His.16 (assess options for individual and collective action: boycott, organization, witness, litigation, and federal enforcement weighed against their limits); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from warning letter to boycott to Little Rock to method), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task argued from the evidence bank).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain how a bus boycott and nine schoolchildren taught the movement its methods and forced the federal government to act: Jo Ann Robinson warned Montgomery's mayor in May 1954 that three-fourths of the bus riders were Black, Rosa Parks was arrested on December 1, 1955 and the Women's Political Council printed leaflets through the night, the Montgomery Improvement Association sustained a boycott for 381 days with a car pool of 23 dispatch centers and weekly mass meetings until Browder v. Gayle ended bus segregation, the published photographs of Emmett Till taught the power of witness, Governor Orval Faubus used the Arkansas National Guard to bar nine students from Central High School, and President Eisenhower answered on September 24, 1957 with an address and Executive Order 10730, federalizing the Guard and sending the 101st Airborne, while Faubus replied by closing Little Rock's high schools for the Lost Year.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes: opening, question, and map (slides 1 to 3) 5 minutes; a warning before the arrest (slides 4 to 5) 5 minutes; the arrest (slides 6 to 7) 6 minutes; the boycott holds (slides 8 to 10) 8 minutes; Emmett Till (slides 11 to 12) 5 minutes; Little Rock (slides 13 to 14) 6 minutes; the president acts (slides 15 to 17) 7 minutes; the answer and the method (slides 18 to 20) 5 minutes; vocabulary, synthesis, and the turn to work (slides 21 to 24) 3 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes.

  • In Montgomery, Alabama, a boycott taught the movement how to sustain pressure for more than a year.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: THE QUESTION FOR TODAY · How did a bus boycott and nine schoolchildren teach the movement its methods, and force the federal government to act? · Unit question: How did the civil rights movement change American law and American life, and what did it leave unfinished?

  • [Read the lesson question aloud and separate its two halves, because the exit ticket and the writing task grade them separately.]

Slide 4 · Section A: A Warning Before the Arrest (divider)

On the slide: SECTION A · A Warning Before the Arrest · May 21, 1954: a professor writes to the mayor, lists three requests, and names the riders' power.

  • [Teach this section as the correction to the easiest version of the Montgomery story, in which a tired seamstress acted alone and a movement appeared from nowhere.]
  • Jo Ann Robinson was an English professor at Alabama State College and president of the Women's Political Council, an organization of Black professional women that had been pressing the city on bus treatment for years.
  • Her letter of May 21, 1954 followed a hearing the council had won that March.
  • Its three requests were specific: seating from back toward front and front toward back, an end to paying at the front and boarding at the rear, and stops at every corner in Black neighborhoods.
  • Around those requests sits the fact the whole boycott would rest on.
  • Three-fourths of the riders were Black.
  • The letter is a negotiation document carrying a threat, and students should read it that way.

Slide 5 · Three-fourths of the riders

On the slide: THREE-FOURTHS OF THE RIDERS · ▪ Robinson's letter went to Mayor Gayle on May 21, 1954. ▪ The letter made three requests on seating, boarding, and bus stops. ▪ Three-fourths of the riders were Black. ▪ The buses could not operate without their fares. ▪ More than twenty-five organizations were talking boycott.

  • [Carry the definition exactly as the guided notes print it.]
  • boycott: a refusal to buy goods or use services as a form of protest, meant to pressure an institution by withdrawing the money and cooperation it depends on
  • Then work the letter's arithmetic aloud, because it is the boycott's whole theory in one sentence.
  • A bus company in Montgomery sold rides, and three of every four rides were sold to Black passengers who were insulted on board as a condition of the purchase.
  • Withdraw those fares for long enough and the company, not the riders, faces the crisis.
  • Robinson's letter states that power politely, as a plea, and dates it: May 1954, the same month as the Brown decision, a year and a half before the arrest that made Montgomery famous.
  • When students later call the boycott spontaneous, send them back to this letter.
  • The network, the grievances, and the tactic were all on paper before Rosa Parks was arrested.

Slide 6 · Section B: December 1, 1955: The Arrest (divider)

On the slide: SECTION B · December 1, 1955: The Arrest · Rosa Parks refuses the order, is fingerprinted and charged, and the leaflets go out that night. · Photograph: Rosa Parks being fingerprinted by Deputy Sheriff D. H. Lackey on February 22, 1956.

  • Picture: Rosa Parks being fingerprinted by Lieutenant D. H. Lackey. State its date exactly: February 22, 1956, during the mass indictments of boycott leaders, not the day of her arrest. The photograph is by Gene Herrick for the Associated Press, held through the Smithsonian National Portrait Gallery record.
  • [Introduce the arrest through the photograph, with its date stated exactly.]
  • The arrest itself, on December 1, 1955, produced a paper record the bank carries: a fingerprint card signed Rosa L. Parks, dated 12-1-55, and a police report charging her with refusing to obey an order of the bus driver under the city code.
  • Parks was secretary of the Montgomery branch of the NAACP and a trained activist.
  • Her own written reflection, carried in the bank, says she had been pushed around all her life and could not take it anymore.
  • Teach the arrest as the act of a prepared person inside a prepared network, because that is what the records show.

Slide 7 · The charge, and the leaflets

On the slide: THE CHARGE, AND THE LEAFLETS · ▪ Parks refused the driver's order on December 1, 1955. ▪ She was charged with refusing to obey the bus driver. ▪ She was secretary of the local NAACP branch. ▪ The council printed leaflets through the night. ▪ A one-day boycott was called for Monday, December 5. · Photograph: Rosa Parks being fingerprinted by Deputy Sheriff D. H. Lackey on February 22, 1956.

  • Picture: Rosa Parks being fingerprinted by Deputy Sheriff D. H. Lackey on February 22, 1956, during the Montgomery bus boycott indictments. Caption it with the February 1956 date, not the December 1955 arrest date.
  • [Walk the night of December 1 in sequence, because the speed of it is the evidence of preparation.]
  • Parks was arrested in the early evening, booked, fingerprinted, and released on bond.
  • Jo Ann Robinson and the Women's Political Council then worked through the night at Alabama State College mimeographing tens of thousands of leaflets, and students and couriers carried them across Black Montgomery the next day.
  • The leaflet called for one day off the buses, Monday, December 5, the day of Parks's trial.
  • Note what the council did not do: it did not call an open-ended campaign it could not yet sustain.
  • It proposed a single day, a test of whether the ridership Robinson had counted in 1954 would in fact stay home.
  • The answer to that test fills the next section, and it changed the one day into 381.

Slide 8 · Section C: The Boycott Holds: 381 Days (divider)

On the slide: SECTION C · The Boycott Holds: 381 Days · An association to run it, a car pool to replace the buses, and mass meetings to hold the line.

  • [Frame the boycott as an organizational achievement before it is a moral one, because the organization is what students can transfer to later campaigns.]
  • Within days of December 5, Montgomery's Black residents had formed the Montgomery Improvement Association, an organization of their own, and elected as its president a twenty-six-year-old pastor new to the city, Martin Luther King Jr.
  • The association had to solve a transport problem for some 42,000 people, absorb the arrests of its leaders, survive the bombing of its president's house, and keep a weekly rhythm of decision and encouragement running for more than a year.
  • Bayard Rustin, the veteran organizer who visited in February 1956 and whose diary is Station 2 of the activity, recorded the system at work.
  • His diary is the section's best evidence, and the next slide reads it closely.

Slide 9 · The system that replaced the buses

On the slide: THE SYSTEM THAT REPLACED THE BUSES · ▪ The Montgomery Improvement Association ran the boycott. ▪ 42,000 riders stayed off the buses. ▪ Police harassed and arrested taxi drivers. ▪ A car pool ran with 23 dispatch centers. ▪ Weekly mass meetings met in Black churches.

  • [Carry both definitions exactly.]
  • Montgomery bus boycott: the protest of 1955 and 1956 in which Black residents of Montgomery, Alabama refused to ride the city's buses for 381 days, ending when the Supreme Court ordered the buses desegregated
  • nonviolent direct action: protest that confronts an unjust law or practice openly and without violence, accepting arrest and punishment in order to expose the injustice and force negotiation
  • Then read Rustin's diary as an operations report.
  • When police harassed, intimidated, and arrested the Black taxi drivers who had carried boycotters at bus fare, the association built the car pool: 23 dispatch centers where riders gathered for free transportation, hundreds of private cars, and church stations across the city.
  • Rustin met men who had walked 7 miles and 14 miles every day since December 5.
  • That is the texture to teach: a tactic is a leaflet for one day, but a campaign is dispatch centers, mass meetings, bail funds, and walkers, held together for 381 days until a court ended the practice the boycott had already defeated in fact.

Slide 10 · 381 days: the boycott's full length

On the slide: 381 days · of boycott in Montgomery, December 1955 to December 1956 · Ended when the Supreme Court ruled in Browder v. Gayle that bus segregation was unconstitutional

  • 381 days
  • [Stop on this number and make the class price it.]
  • Three hundred eighty-one days is not a demonstration; it is a second transport system run by volunteers through a winter and a summer, under indictments of its leaders, with King's house bombed in January 1956 while the boycott held.
  • The boycott's ending deserves the same precision.
  • The boycott did not persuade Montgomery to relent.
  • A federal case, Browder v. Gayle, brought by Black plaintiffs through the courts, was affirmed by the Supreme Court in November 1956, and when the written order reached Montgomery in December the association voted to end the boycott and ride the desegregated buses.
  • Teach the pairing honestly: protest created the pressure and the record, and litigation delivered the legal result.
  • The movement carried both tools out of Montgomery, and the rest of this unit is partly the story of learning when each one worked.

Slide 11 · Section D: The Summer's Wound: Emmett Till (divider)

On the slide: SECTION D · The Summer's Wound: Emmett Till · August 1955: a fourteen-year-old murdered, his killers acquitted, and a mother who made the nation look.

  • [Set the handling rule for this section before the content, and state it the way the student materials state it.]
  • The Jet photographs of Emmett Till are copyrighted magazine images.
  • They are not reproduced in this lesson, in the documents, or in this deck, following the bank record, which describes them without reproducing them.
  • What is taught is the record: what happened in Mississippi in August 1955, what a jury did, what Mamie Till-Mobley decided, and what the publication of David Jackson's photographs did to the country.
  • The chronology matters.
  • Till was killed three months before the Montgomery boycott began, and for many of the young people who would staff the movement's next decade, the published photographs were a formative fact.
  • Handle the section at documentary weight: exact dates, exact sequence, no embellishment, and let the record carry the effect.
  • Note: No Till photograph is used anywhere in this lesson, by the unit rule recorded in the image sources.

Slide 12 · Described, not shown: the Till record

On the slide: DESCRIBED, NOT SHOWN · ▪ Emmett Till, fourteen, was visiting Mississippi from Chicago. ▪ He was murdered in August 1955 after an accusation. ▪ An all-white jury acquitted in about an hour. ▪ The men confessed to a magazine for money. ▪ His mother published the photographs in Jet.

  • [Carry the definition exactly.]
  • Emmett Till: the fourteen-year-old Chicago boy murdered in Mississippi in August 1955 after being accused of offending a white woman, whose open-casket funeral and published photographs showed the nation what southern racial violence looked like
  • Then teach the bank record's description as the document it is, in the record's own sequence.
  • In September 1955 Jet magazine published photographs by David Jackson showing the open casket of Emmett Till.
  • His mother insisted the world see what had been done to her son.
  • The bank record's verdict is measured and students should quote it rather than inflate it: the images shocked the nation and helped galvanize the civil rights movement.
  • Connect the record forward.
  • The boycott that began that December drew on a generation that had seen those pages, and the tactic at work, witness, the deliberate making of a public record that cannot be unseen, stands beside boycott in the movement's ledger for the rest of the unit.

Slide 13 · Section E: Little Rock: A Governor Bars the Door (divider)

On the slide: SECTION E · Little Rock: A Governor Bars the Door · September 1957: the Arkansas National Guard posted at Central High to keep nine students out. · Photograph: soldiers of the 101st Airborne Division escorting students to Central High School.

  • Picture: Soldiers of the 101st Airborne Division escorting African-American students to Central High School in Little Rock in September 1957, a United States Army photograph courtesy of the National Archives. The soldiers in this photograph are escorting the students into Central High under federal orders.
  • [Introduce Little Rock through the photograph's reversal, which students will meet again in the next section.]
  • Three weeks earlier, soldiers of a different command, the Arkansas National Guard answering to Governor Orval Faubus, stood at the same school to keep the same students out.
  • The nine students, remembered as the Little Rock Nine, had been selected to desegregate Central High under a federal court order following Brown.
  • Faubus claimed he acted to prevent violence.
  • On September 4 the students were turned away, and Elizabeth Eckford, fifteen, who had not received word of the group's meeting plan, walked toward the school alone through a hostile crowd.
  • The photograph of her walk, taken by Will Counts, is copyrighted and is not used in this lesson; the Army photograph on this slide is the federal record of the answer.

Slide 14 · Turned away at the door

On the slide: TURNED AWAY AT THE DOOR · ▪ A federal court ordered nine students admitted. ▪ Faubus posted the Arkansas National Guard. ▪ Elizabeth Eckford walked alone on September 4. ▪ The Nine entered on September 23 and were withdrawn within hours. ▪ A governor's soldiers stopped a federal court order. · Photograph: soldiers of the 101st Airborne Division escorting students to Central High School.

  • Picture: The United States Army escort photograph, showing soldiers of the 101st Airborne escorting students to Central High. Caption it as the federal escort of September 1957, not the September 4 turning away described in the bullets.
  • [Carry the definition exactly.]
  • Little Rock Nine: the nine Black students who desegregated Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas in 1957 under the protection of federal troops after the state's governor tried to bar their entry
  • Then slow down on the legal shape of the confrontation, because it explains everything Eisenhower does next.
  • This was not a protest against a local custom.
  • A federal district court had ordered the students admitted under a desegregation plan the school board itself had drawn.
  • The governor interposed his state's military force between the court's order and its execution.
  • On September 23 the students entered through a side door while a crowd gathered outside, and police withdrew them within hours, judging that their safety could not be guaranteed.
  • At that point every ordinary agency had failed in public: the court had ordered, the governor had obstructed, and the city could not protect nine teenagers.
  • The question the reading ends the section with is the right one: what would the federal government do?

Slide 15 · Section F: The President Acts (divider)

On the slide: SECTION F · The President Acts · September 24, 1957: an address to the nation, an executive order, and the 101st Airborne.

  • [Introduce Eisenhower with the fact students find hardest to hold: he had never publicly endorsed Brown, and his address does not endorse it.]
  • His public case was narrower and, for his purpose, stronger.
  • Disorderly mobs had prevented the carrying out of proper orders from a federal court, local authorities had not eliminated the opposition, and the responsibility of the president to uphold the federal courts was, in his word, inescapable.
  • Teach the address and the order as one instrument in two parts.
  • The address, delivered on radio and television on September 24, explained the duty to the nation.
  • The order, signed the same day, performed the legal act: it took the Arkansas Guard out of the governor's hands and put federal troops at the Secretary of Defense's disposal.
  • Words and mechanism together are the section's subject.

Slide 16 · The order behind the troops

On the slide: THE ORDER BEHIND THE TROOPS · ▪ Eisenhower addressed the nation on September 24, 1957. ▪ “Mob rule cannot override the decisions of our courts”. ▪ Executive Order 10730 was signed the same day. ▪ The Arkansas Guard was placed under federal command. ▪ The 101st Airborne escorted the Nine on September 25. · Photograph: President Dwight D. Eisenhower, official portrait, July 1956.

  • Picture: President Dwight D. Eisenhower, official portrait, July 1956, a White House photograph held through the Eisenhower Library. Caption it with its true date, July 1956.
  • [Carry the definition exactly.]
  • Executive Order 10730: President Eisenhower's order of September 24, 1957 federalizing the Arkansas National Guard and directing federal troops to enforce school desegregation at Little Rock
  • Then read the order's mechanism from the bank text, because its plainness is the point.
  • Section 1 authorizes the Secretary of Defense to call the Arkansas National Guard into federal service for an indefinite period and until relieved by appropriate orders.
  • Section 3 authorizes him to use such of the armed forces of the United States as he may deem necessary.
  • The same Guard that had barred the door now answered to Washington, and paratroopers of the 101st Airborne Division, a federal force with no local chain of command, escorted the nine students into Central High on September 25.
  • The students finished the school year under military protection.
  • A court order, defied, had acquired an army.

Slide 17 · 101st: the Airborne at Little Rock

On the slide: 101st · Airborne Division sent to Little Rock, September 1957 · Federal paratroopers escorted nine students to class after a governor defied a federal court

  • 101st
  • [Use this slide to mark the scale of the precedent, with the facts kept exact.]
  • The 101st Airborne was an elite combat division, and its soldiers were sent to an American high school to walk nine teenagers past a crowd to their classes.
  • The Archives account of the order records that Eisenhower sent 1,000 paratroopers to Little Rock, and the order itself placed the Arkansas National Guard under federal command for an indefinite period.
  • Tell students what this was in constitutional terms rather than in spectacle.
  • For the first time since Reconstruction, a president had used federal troops to enforce the civil rights of Black citizens against a state government.
  • The precedent, not the headcount, is the figure to remember: defiance of a federal court by a governor would be answered with federal force.

Slide 18 · Section G: The Answer, and the Method (divider)

On the slide: SECTION G · The Answer, and the Method · Faubus calls Arkansas occupied territory, closes the schools, and the movement counts its lessons.

  • [Close the lesson by giving the resistance its own voice before summarizing the method, because students judge Faubus better from his words than from a summary of them.]
  • His statement of September 26, 1957, carried in the bank from the Eyes on the Prize transcript, opens with the claim that Arkansas was now an occupied territory and appeals to God, liberty, and decency against the federal action.
  • It commanded no soldiers after September 24, and its power was political rather than military: it kept resistance respectable for the voters who returned Faubus to office, and it framed the following year, in which he closed Little Rock's high schools entirely rather than operate them desegregated.
  • The Lost Year is the honest ending to the Little Rock story.
  • The troops won the schoolhouse door.
  • They did not win the school system, and the difference between those two results is a fact the rest of the unit keeps meeting.

Slide 19 · What the two cities taught

On the slide: WHAT THE TWO CITIES TAUGHT · ▪ Faubus called Arkansas an occupied territory. ▪ Little Rock's high schools closed for the Lost Year. ▪ A boycott withdraws the money an institution needs. ▪ Witness publishes the record the nation must face. ▪ When a court order is defied, federal power follows.

  • [Carry the definition exactly.]
  • Southern Christian Leadership Conference: the organization founded in 1957 by Martin Luther King Jr. and other Black ministers to coordinate nonviolent protest campaigns across the South
  • Its founding in 1957, weeks after the boycott ended, is the institutional proof that Montgomery's method was meant to travel: King and other Black ministers built a regional organization to coordinate campaigns like the one the Montgomery Improvement Association had improvised locally.
  • Then assemble the lesson's ledger with the class.
  • Montgomery contributed the boycott and the sustaining organization.
  • The Till record contributed witness.
  • Little Rock contributed the federal lever and its limit: a court order could summon troops, and a governor could still close the schools.
  • The campaigns of the next lessons, the sit-ins, the Freedom Rides, and Birmingham, were designed by people who had studied exactly this ledger.

Slide 23 · Answering the question

On the slide: ANSWERING THE QUESTION · ▪ The methods were boycott, organization, and witness. ▪ The occasions were an arrest, a murder, and a barred door. ▪ The compulsion was defiance that left the president no exit. ▪ The limit was clear: troops opened a school, and a governor closed it.

  • [Run the synthesis as the writing task rehearsal, and build it from the class's own answers before summarizing.]
  • Strong answers hold the lesson's two halves together and refuse to drop either.
  • The first half is capacity: by the end of 1957 the movement possessed a tested boycott method, an organization built to sustain it, and a hard education in what published evidence could do to national opinion.
  • The second half is compulsion: at Little Rock the movement's own tools could not open the school, because the opposing force was a state's military under a governor's orders.
  • What forced the federal government to act was the defiance itself, a court order publicly stopped by soldiers, which no president could let stand without surrendering the authority of every federal court.
  • Test the synthesis against its rival before closing: the federal government was not persuaded in 1957.
  • It was cornered, and the difference explains the decade that followed.
