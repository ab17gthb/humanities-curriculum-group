Montgomery and Little Rock: The Movement Finds Its Tactics

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. A Warning Before the Arrest

In Montgomery, Alabama, Black passengers on city buses paid their fare at the front, stepped off, and entered again through the rear door. They sat in the back, gave up seats when the white section filled, and depended on drivers who could order them to move. On May 21, 1954, Jo Ann Robinson, a professor at Alabama State College and president of the Women's Political Council, wrote to Mayor W. A. Gayle. Her letter listed three requests, on seating, boarding, and bus stops in Black neighborhoods, and it carried a warning. Three-fourths of the riders were Black, she wrote, and if Black riders did not use the buses, the buses could not operate. More than twenty-five local organizations were already talking of a citywide boycott.

B. December 1, 1955: The Arrest

On December 1, 1955, Rosa Parks refused the driver's order to give up her seat to a white passenger. The police arrested her, fingerprinted her, and charged her with refusing to obey an order of the bus driver under the city code. Parks was no stranger to the work. She was secretary of the local branch of the NAACP, and she later wrote of the arrest in plain words: "I had been pushed around all my life and felt at this moment that I couldn't take it anymore." That night the council mimeographed tens of thousands of leaflets calling for a one-day boycott on Monday, December 5.

C. The Boycott Holds: 381 Days

The one-day boycott became a movement. Black residents formed the Montgomery Improvement Association to run it and chose a twenty-six-year-old pastor, Martin Luther King Jr., as its president. Some 42,000 Black residents stayed off the buses, and when police harassed the taxi drivers who carried them, the association built a car pool with 23 dispatch centers. Weekly mass meetings in Black churches held the effort together, and King, as reported by a listener at a March 1956 meeting, told the crowd: "Freedom doesn't come on a silver platter. With every great movement toward freedom there will inevitably be trials. Somebody will have to have the courage to sacrifice." The city indicted the boycott leaders, and King's house was bombed. The boycotters walked on. In November 1956 the Supreme Court ruled in Browder v. Gayle that bus segregation was unconstitutional, and on December 21 the boycott ended with the buses desegregated.

D. The Summer's Wound: Emmett Till

Behind the boycott stood a killing from the summer before. Emmett Till, fourteen, was visiting Mississippi from Chicago in August 1955 when he was accused of offending a white woman. He was murdered. An all-white jury acquitted the two men charged after about an hour, and the men then confessed to a magazine for money, safe from a second trial. His mother, Mamie Till-Mobley, held an open-casket funeral in Chicago and chose to let Jet magazine publish photographs of her son's body, taken by photographer David Jackson. Tens of thousands filed past the casket, and the published photographs showed the nation what the jury had excused. The photographs are copyrighted magazine images. They are described in this lesson rather than shown.

E. Little Rock: A Governor Bars the Door

In September 1957 the test moved to a schoolhouse door. A federal court had ordered nine Black students admitted to Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas. Governor Orval Faubus posted the Arkansas National Guard at the school and barred the students from entering. On September 4, Elizabeth Eckford, fifteen, walked toward the school alone through a shouting crowd while soldiers turned her away. The nine students entered the building on September 23 and were withdrawn within hours for their safety as a crowd gathered outside. A governor had used his state's soldiers to stop a federal court order, and the question of what the federal government would do passed to the president.

F. The President Acts

President Dwight D. Eisenhower had never publicly endorsed the Brown decision, and he acted on a narrower ground: a governor's defiance of a federal court could not stand. On September 24, 1957, he addressed the nation on radio and television. Disorderly mobs, he said, had prevented the carrying out of proper orders from a federal court, and he closed the argument in one sentence: "Mob rule cannot be allowed to override the decisions of our courts." The same day he signed Executive Order 10730. The order placed the Arkansas National Guard under federal command, reversing the force that had barred the students, and authorized federal troops to enforce the court's orders. Soldiers of the 101st Airborne Division arrived, and on September 25 they escorted the nine students into Central High. The students finished the school year there under military protection.

G. The Answer, and the Method

Governor Faubus answered on television on September 26. "We are now an occupied territory," he told Arkansas, as the bank record quotes him. When the school year ended, he closed Little Rock's high schools entirely for the next year, a shutdown remembered as the Lost Year, rather than operate them desegregated. The two confrontations had taught the movement its working method. A boycott could withdraw the money an institution depended on. An organization of one's own, such as the Montgomery Improvement Association and, from 1957, the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, could sustain a campaign for more than a year. A published record could make the nation look at what it had excused. And a court order, when a governor defied it, could bring federal authority, up to soldiers, behind the movement's claim. Those lessons were carried into every campaign that followed.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the three requests in Robinson's letter, and state the warning it carried. The Women's Political Council asked for a seating arrangement in which Black riders sat from back toward front and white riders from front toward back, an end to the practice of making Black riders pay at the front and enter at the rear, and bus stops at every corner in Black residential sections as in white ones. The warning was that more than twenty-five local organizations were talking of a citywide boycott of the buses. The fact that made it credible was Robinson's own figure: three-fourths of the riders were Black, and without their fares the buses could not operate.

2. Describe the arrest of December 1, 1955. Rosa Parks refused the driver's order to give up her seat to a white passenger. Police arrested and fingerprinted her and charged her with refusing to obey an order of the bus driver under Chapter 6, Section 11 of the Montgomery city code, as the arrest record carried in the bank states the charge. Parks was secretary of the local NAACP branch. That night Jo Ann Robinson and the Women's Political Council mimeographed tens of thousands of leaflets calling for a one-day boycott of the buses on Monday, December 5.

3. Explain how the boycott held for 381 days. The Montgomery Improvement Association ran the boycott under its president, Martin Luther King Jr. The systems that carried the boycotters were the car pool, organized with 23 dispatch centers after police harassed Black taxi drivers, and the walkers themselves, with weekly mass meetings in Black churches sustaining morale and decisions. Some 42,000 Black residents stayed off the buses. The costs the city imposed included indictments of the boycott leaders and pressure on drivers; King's house was also bombed, by an attacker the reading leaves unnamed. The boycott ended after the Supreme Court ruled in Browder v. Gayle in November 1956 that bus segregation was unconstitutional; the boycott closed on December 21 with the buses desegregated.

4. Tell the Till record in order. Emmett Till, fourteen, visiting Mississippi from Chicago in August 1955, was accused of offending a white woman and was murdered. An all-white jury acquitted the two men charged after about an hour of deliberation, and the men later confessed to a magazine for money, protected from a second trial. Mamie Till-Mobley held an open-casket funeral in Chicago and chose to let Jet magazine publish David Jackson's photographs of her son's body. Tens of thousands filed past the casket. The photographs are described rather than shown in this lesson because they are copyrighted magazine images, and the bank record carries them as a description without reproducing them.

5. Answer the lesson question. Full credit answers name a method from Montgomery, a method from the Till record or Little Rock, and the federal result. Montgomery taught economic pressure sustained by an organization of the movement's own: 42,000 riders withdrawn, a car pool with 23 dispatch centers, mass meetings, and endurance through indictments and a bombing until Browder v. Gayle desegregated the buses after 381 days. The Till record taught witness: a mother's decision to publish photographs made the nation look at a killing a jury had excused. Little Rock taught the limit and the lever: a governor could bar a schoolhouse door with his own Guard, and only federal power could open it, which Eisenhower supplied through Executive Order 10730, the federalized Guard, and the 101st Airborne. Any three accurate facts, one from Montgomery and one from Little Rock, earn full credit.
