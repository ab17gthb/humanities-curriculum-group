Part 3: Sources (15 points)

Answer each question in complete sentences, citing the sources by letter.

Source A

U.S. Supreme Court, Brown v. Board of Education, May 17, 1954. From U.S. Supreme Court, 'Brown v. Board of Education,' 1954 (Justia transcription of U.S. Reports, 347 U.S. 483). Cuts are marked with ellipses in brackets.

“To separate them from others of similar age and qualifications solely because of their race generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community that may affect their hearts and minds in a way unlikely ever to be undone. [...] We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of “separate but equal” has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal. Therefore, we hold that the plaintiffs and others similarly situated for whom the actions have been brought are, by reason of the segregation complained of, deprived of the equal protection of the laws guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment.”

A1. (2 points) According to the Court, what does separating children from others of similar age and qualifications solely because of their race generate, and what may that feeling affect?

A2. (3 points) State the Court's conclusion about the doctrine of separate but equal in the field of public education, name the amendment the plaintiffs were held to be deprived of protection under, and explain why the conclusion held even where the physical facilities of the separate schools might be equal.

Source B

U.S. Congress, Civil Rights Act of 1964, signed July 2, 1964. From U.S. Congress, 'Civil Rights Act of 1964,' 1964 (Statutes at Large, verified via GovInfo). Cuts are marked with ellipses in brackets.

Title II, Section 201. “(a) All persons shall be entitled to the full and equal enjoyment of the goods, services, facilities, privileges, advantages, and accommodations of any place of public accommodation, as defined in this section, without discrimination or segregation on the ground of race, color, religion, or national origin.” [...] Title VI, Section 601. “No person in the United States shall, on the ground of race, color, or national origin, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.”

B1. (2 points) Under Title II, what are all persons entitled to, and on what four grounds may they not be discriminated against or segregated in a place of public accommodation?

B2. (3 points) State the Title VI rule for programs receiving federal financial assistance, and explain what power that rule gave the federal government over a segregated program, naming the consequence the act allowed.

Source C

U.S. Information Agency (photographer Rowland Scherman), March on Washington photograph, August 28, 1963. From U.S. Information Agency (photographer Rowland Scherman), 'March on Washington: Edith Lee-Payne,' August 28, 1963 (National Archives; image described, banner caption transcribed). The bank record describes the photograph rather than reproducing it, and this test presents the photograph from that description. The photograph itself is not reproduced here. The United States Information Agency sent photographers to document the March on Washington for audiences abroad.

The bank record describes the image in these words: “A young Black girl, Edith Lee-Payne of Detroit, holds a banner reading “MARCH FOR JOBS AND FREEDOM / AUGUST 28” at the March on Washington, August 28, 1963. She had just turned twelve that day and attended the march with her mother. She had no idea she had been photographed until years later, when a cousin spotted the image in a calendar of African American history.”

C1. (2 points) Who appears in the photograph, what does her banner read, and what two facts does the description give about her age and her knowledge of being photographed?

C2. (3 points) Explain how this photograph served the movement's public case on August 28, 1963, and explain why the identity of the agency that sent the photographer, and the audience it worked for, must shape how a historian reads the image as evidence.

Part 3: Sources

A1. The Court states that separating children from others of similar age and qualifications solely because of their race generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community. That feeling, the Court says, may affect their hearts and minds in a way unlikely ever to be undone. One point for the feeling named in the source's words, one point for the effect the source states. (Blueprint 3)

A2. The Court concludes that in the field of public education the doctrine of separate but equal has no place, because separate educational facilities are inherently unequal. The plaintiffs were held to be deprived of the equal protection of the laws guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment. The conclusion held even where tangible factors such as buildings and curricula had been equalized, because the harm the opinion identifies is the effect of segregation itself on the children, not a comparison of facilities. Full credit requires the conclusion, the amendment, and that reasoning. (Blueprint 3)

B1. Title II entitles all persons to the full and equal enjoyment of the goods, services, facilities, privileges, advantages, and accommodations of any place of public accommodation. The four grounds named are race, color, religion, and national origin. One point for the entitlement and one point for all four grounds. (Blueprint 11)

B2. Title VI provides that no person in the United States shall, on the ground of race, color, or national origin, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving federal financial assistance. The rule gave the federal government a funding lever over segregated programs. As the act is defined in the unit's vocabulary, the government could cut off funds to segregated programs, so a school, hospital, or agency that took federal money risked losing it by discriminating. Full credit requires the rule stated from the source and the funding consequence named. (Blueprint 11)

C1. The photograph shows Edith Lee-Payne of Detroit, a young Black girl holding a banner reading MARCH FOR JOBS AND FREEDOM / AUGUST 28 at the March on Washington on August 28, 1963. The description states that she had just turned twelve that day and that she had no idea she had been photographed until years later, when a cousin spotted the image in a calendar of African American history. One point for the subject and the banner, one point for both facts. (Blueprint 10)

C2. The photograph put a single child's face on a demonstration of about 250,000 people who came to the Lincoln Memorial to demand jobs and freedom and to press Congress to pass the civil rights bill, which is the movement's public case stated in one frame, orderly, young, and carrying its demands in plain words. A historian must also weigh its making. Rowland Scherman was sent by the United States Information Agency, the government's overseas storytelling service, to document the march for audiences abroad, so the image was framed by a federal agency with an interest in showing a peaceful and hopeful America during the Cold War. That purpose does not make the photograph false. It means the image is evidence both of the march and of what the government wanted the world to see of it. Full credit requires the march's demands, the agency and its audience, and that double reading. (Blueprint 10)
