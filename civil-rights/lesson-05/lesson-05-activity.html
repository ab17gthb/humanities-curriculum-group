The Pressure File: Birmingham and Washington, 1963

Directions: Work through the five stations in order. Each station holds one record from the spring and summer of 1963, quoted from the edition named in its Source line, with cuts marked by ellipses, or labeled as a summary where the record is summarized. As you work, keep the chain of pressure in Part 2: for each event, enter what it added, what it cost, and who it moved. At the end, judge whether Birmingham or the march did more to move the federal government, and answer the strongest claim for the other side.

One note before you begin. Two of this lesson's texts, Martin Luther King Jr.'s Letter from Birmingham Jail and his I Have a Dream address, are under copyright. This unit quotes each of them at no more than 90 words in total. Station 1 holds the only words quoted from the Letter anywhere in this unit, sixteen words in two short passages, counted exactly. Everything else of King's in this file is paraphrase, and the stations say so.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: The Letter from Birmingham Jail, April 1963

Source: Martin Luther King Jr., Letter from Birmingham Jail, April 1963, as carried in the audited source record (published letter text; longer excerpt supplied from a classroom edition, with the edition's headnote and footnotes not reproduced). King wrote the letter in a Birmingham jail cell, answering eight white Alabama clergymen who had called the demonstrations unwise and untimely. The two passages below are quoted exactly. Together they run to sixteen words, the whole of this unit's quotation from the letter. The rest of King's argument at this station is paraphrase, written for this lesson and labeled as such.

"I am in Birmingham because injustice is here."

"Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere."

Paraphrase of the rest of the argument, written for this lesson. King argues that no American can be treated as an outsider inside the United States, because communities are tied to one another and what touches one touches all. He argues that Black Americans had waited more than three centuries for constitutional rights, and that people who had never felt segregation found it easy to counsel patience. He argues that a law can be unjust, when it degrades human personality or when it is imposed on a people denied the vote, and that an unjust law binds no one's conscience. And he argues that the deepest obstacle was not the open segregationist but the white moderate, who preferred order to justice and kept setting the timetable for another people's freedom.

The first quoted sentence answers the charge that King was an outsider in Birmingham. State in your own words what reason it gives for his being there, and what it assumes about where a protester belongs.

The second quoted sentence is the letter's widest claim. Restate it as a rule about cities: if the sentence is true, what follows for a reader in a city far from Birmingham?

This station quotes sixteen words and paraphrases the rest. What does a reader lose by meeting most of this letter in paraphrase, and what in the two quoted sentences can a paraphrase not carry in the same way?

Station 2: Kennedy's Address, June 11, 1963

Source: John F. Kennedy, "Report to the American People on Civil Rights," June 11, 1963 (Teaching American History; John F. Kennedy Presidential Library), as carried in the audited source record. Kennedy spoke on television and radio on the evening of June 11, hours after federal officers faced down Governor Wallace at the University of Alabama. Cuts are marked by ellipses. Presidential addresses are public documents.

"We are confronted primarily with a moral issue. It is as old as the scriptures and is as clear as the American Constitution. The heart of the question is whether all Americans are to be afforded equal rights and equal opportunities, whether we are going to treat our fellow Americans as we want to be treated. If an American, because his skin is dark, cannot eat lunch in a restaurant open to the public, if he cannot send his children to the best public school available, if he cannot vote for the public officials who represent him, if, in short, he cannot enjoy the full and free life which all of us want, then who among us would be content to have the color of his skin changed and stand in his place? Who among us would then be content with the counsels of patience and delay? [...]

One hundred years of delay have passed since President Lincoln freed the slaves, yet their heirs, their grandsons, are not fully free. They are not yet freed from the bonds of injustice. They are not yet freed from social and economic oppression. And this Nation, for all its hopes and all its boasts, will not be fully free until all its citizens are free. [...] Now the time has come for this Nation to fulfill its promise. [...]

Next week I shall ask the Congress of the United States to act, to make a commitment it has not fully made in this century to the proposition that race has no place in American life or law."

Kennedy calls civil rights a moral issue before he calls it anything else. What does he gain by that order, for an audience of white Americans watching at home?

The long middle passage asks the viewer to change places with a Black American. Name the three denials it lists, lunch counter, school, and vote, and state what kind of argument the change of places makes that a statistic could not.

The last passage is a promise about Congress. State exactly what Kennedy says he will ask, and hold it for Part 2: a promise is not a law. What still had to happen after June 11 for the promise to be kept?

Station 3: The Children's Crusade, May 1963 (Summary Record)

Source: Summary record written for this lesson from contemporaneous reporting of the Birmingham campaign of May 1963 and the public record of the movement, including the accounts held by the Birmingham Public Library and the National Park Service. This station is a summary, not a quotation. It is labeled as a summary on its face, and no sentence in it is presented as a participant's exact words. The famous photographs of these days were press photographs and are under copyright. They are described here and not reproduced.

By the first days of May 1963, the Birmingham campaign had stalled. Adult demonstrators had been arrested in the hundreds since April, bail money was running short, and the downtown boycott had not yet brought the merchants to settle. James Bevel, a young organizer from the Nashville student movement, proposed a different body of marchers: the city's Black schoolchildren, trained in nonviolence in workshops at the 16th Street Baptist Church and other movement churches.

On Thursday, May 2, students left their classrooms across the city and gathered at the church. They came out of its doors in trained groups, class by class, singing, and marched toward the downtown business district. Police arrested them in waves. By the end of the first day, more than nine hundred students had been arrested and jailed, some held in cells and some in a stockade at the fairgrounds. School buses were used to carry them away.

On Friday, May 3, more students marched, and the city's jails were full. Commissioner Eugene Connor ordered fire hoses turned on the demonstrators. The water came at a pressure that could strip bark from trees, knock a child down, and roll the child along the pavement. Police dogs were set on demonstrators and on bystanders near Kelly Ingram Park. Television crews and newspaper photographers recorded the day, and the film and pictures ran across the country that night and around the world within days.

The demonstrations continued into the next week, joined again by adults. On May 10, one week after the hoses, Birmingham's senior business leaders and the movement announced a settlement. The agreement provided for the desegregation of downtown lunch counters, restrooms, fitting rooms, and drinking fountains within ninety days, the hiring of Black clerks in downtown stores, the release of jailed demonstrators on low bail, and a standing channel between Black and white leaders. Dr. King's brother's home and the movement's headquarters motel were bombed the following night. The settlement held.

State the problem the Children's Crusade was designed to solve, in the summary's own terms: what had stalled, and what resource had run short?

The summary says the film and pictures ran across the country that night. Why did national sight of May 3 do work that the marches of April, equally nonviolent, had not done?

Read the settlement terms closely. Name two things the May 10 agreement won in Birmingham itself, and one large thing it did not win anywhere.

Station 4: The March on Washington: Program and Demands, August 28, 1963

Source: Bayard Rustin and Cleveland Robinson, "Final Plans for the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom," 1963 (Library of Congress), as carried in the audited source record, for the passage quoted below. The ten demands are then summarized for this lesson from the march's printed program, a public document of the march organization. The summary is labeled as a summary. Photograph: Rowland Scherman, United States Information Agency, National Archives, via Wikimedia Commons.

From the organizers' final plans, under the heading Why We March: "We march to redress old grievances and to help resolve an American crisis. That crisis is born of the twin evils of racism and economic deprivation. They rob all people, Negro and white, of dignity, self-respect, and freedom. They impose a special burden on the Negro, who is denied the right to vote, economically exploited, refused access to public accommodations, subjected to inferior education, and relegated to substandard ghetto housing. [...] Thus the rate of Negro unemployment is nearly three times that of whites."

The ten demands, summarized for this lesson from the printed program. The march demanded a comprehensive civil rights act from Congress in that session. It demanded the desegregation of all public schools in the fall of 1963, and federal withholding of funds from programs that discriminated. It demanded a federal fair employment law barring discrimination in government, unions, and employers, and a national minimum wage of two dollars an hour. It demanded the enforcement of the Fourteenth Amendment's provision reducing a state's representation when the vote is denied, protection of demonstrators from police brutality, and the arrest of officials who break the law in office. It demanded, in short, jobs and freedom together, and Bayard Rustin read the full list aloud to the crowd at the Lincoln Memorial, which answered it with approval.

The organizers name twin evils. Name both, and state from the passage how the second evil shows itself in the unemployment figure the plans cite.

The march's name joins two words. From the summarized demands, sort three demands under jobs and three under freedom, and write one sentence on why the organizers refused to separate them.

The demands are addressed to the federal government, nearly every one of them. What does that choice of addressee show about where the movement believed power to settle these questions now sat, after Birmingham?

Station 5: The Bombing of the 16th Street Baptist Church, September 15, 1963 (Summary Record)

Source: Summary record written for this lesson from the public record of the bombing, including the Federal Bureau of Investigation's account of its investigation and the National Park Service's history of the church. This station is a summary, not a quotation, and it is labeled as a summary on its face. No photograph of the damage is reproduced in this file. The facts are stated plainly.

The 16th Street Baptist Church stood in downtown Birmingham, a few blocks from city hall. Through the spring of 1963 it had served the movement as a meeting place and as the gathering point from which the schoolchildren had marched in May. On Sunday, September 15, 1963, the church was preparing for its annual Youth Day, when young people would take part in the adult service.

At about 10:22 that morning, a bomb exploded beneath the church's side steps. The dynamite had been planted during the night by members of the Ku Klux Klan. The blast tore through the building's basement. In a basement restroom, five girls in their Sunday dresses had been preparing for the service. Four of them were killed: Addie Mae Collins, age fourteen; Cynthia Wesley, age fourteen; Carole Robertson, age fourteen; and Carol Denise McNair, age eleven. The fifth girl, Sarah Collins, survived and lost the sight of one eye. More than twenty other people in the church were injured.

A joint funeral for three of the girls was held the following week and drew thousands of mourners. Dr. King preached the eulogy. No Birmingham city official attended. In Washington, the civil rights bill President Kennedy had proposed in June was still before Congress, held in committee and opposed by the southern bloc in the Senate. It had not passed when the year ended.

The investigation ran for decades. Robert Chambliss, a Klansman, was convicted of murder in 1977. Two more of the bombers, Thomas Blanton and Bobby Frank Cherry, were convicted in 2001 and 2002. A fourth suspect had died before he could be tried.

State the facts of the bombing as the summary gives them: the date, the time, the place in the church's year, and the names and ages of the four girls.

The church was not a random target. From the summary, state the two roles the church had played in 1963 before September, and what attacking it on Youth Day attacked.

The summary ends with two kinds of delay, the bill's and the trials'. State both, with their lengths, and write one sentence on what the pair of them shows about the difference between a verdict and a law.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: The Letter from Birmingham Jail

The reason for being there. The sentence gives presence as a response to injustice: King is in Birmingham because injustice is there. It assumes that a protester belongs wherever the wrong is, and that residence in a city is not the test of a citizen's standing to act inside his own country. (Blueprint 9)

The rule about cities. Restated: injustice in any one place threatens justice in every place. For a distant reader it follows that Birmingham's segregation is not a local matter that ends at the city line, and that silence at a distance is a position with consequences at home. (Blueprint 9)

Paraphrase and quotation. Paraphrase loses voice, cadence, biblical and legal layering, and the pressure of a long argument built sentence by sentence; it also loses the reader's ability to check the summarizer. The two quoted sentences carry in a few words what paraphrase spreads out: a reason for presence stated as fact, and a claim about the country's connectedness stated as law. Students should also name the reason for the limit itself: the letter is under copyright, and the unit quotes it at sixteen words in total, within its 90 word cap, counted and disclosed here and in the lesson's source notes. (Blueprint 9)

Station 2: Kennedy's Address, June 11

Why moral first. Calling the question moral before calling it legal or legislative moves it out of the column of sectional politics and into the conscience of the viewer at home. A legal argument can be answered by a lawyer. Kennedy's order asks the audience to judge their own conduct first, which is the audience the bill would need. (Blueprint 9)

The three denials and the change of places. A restaurant open to the public, the best public school available, and the vote for the officials who represent him. The change of places makes the argument by substitution rather than by measurement: it does not tell the viewer how widespread the denials are, it asks whether the viewer would accept one of them, and a statistic cannot ask that question of a conscience. (Blueprint 9)

The promise and what remained. Kennedy says that next week he will ask Congress to act on the proposition that race has no place in American life or law. What remained was the whole legislative road: a bill drafted and introduced, committee action, a House vote, and a Senate in which the southern bloc could filibuster, which is why the bill was still unpassed at the year's end. The address moved the presidency. It did not move a single vote by itself. (Blueprint 9)

Station 3: The Children's Crusade (Summary Record)

The problem. The adult campaign had stalled after hundreds of arrests since April, and bail money had run short, so the boycott could not be sustained at the pressure the plan required. The Crusade supplied marchers the jails could not exhaust and families the whole city would watch. (Blueprint 9)

Why sight did the work. April's marches were reported. May 3 was seen: children knocked down by water and set on by dogs, in film, in homes far from Alabama, on the same night. The summary's point is about audience and evidence together. National opinion and the Kennedy administration were moved by an event the country watched rather than read about, and Connor supplied the pictures himself. Students should note that this station describes the photographs rather than reproducing them, because they are copyrighted press photographs. (Blueprint 9)

The settlement, won and not won. Won in Birmingham: desegregation of downtown lunch counters, restrooms, fitting rooms, and drinking fountains within ninety days, and the hiring of Black clerks in downtown stores, alongside release of demonstrators and a standing channel between the communities. Not won: anything beyond the city's downtown, and nothing in federal law. The settlement was a local agreement with merchants, which is why the campaign's next address was Washington. (Blueprint 9)

Station 4: The March on Washington

Twin evils. Racism and economic deprivation. The second shows itself in the unemployment figure the plans cite: the rate of Negro unemployment is nearly three times that of whites, which the passage ties to denial of training and to being the first victims of automation. (Blueprint 10)

Jobs and freedom. Under jobs: the fair employment law, the two dollar minimum wage, and the training and employment grievances in the Why We March passage. Under freedom: the civil rights act, school desegregation, protection from police brutality, and enforcement of the Fourteenth Amendment on the vote. The organizers refused to separate them because the same citizen was denied both, and a lunch counter seat without a living wage, or a wage without the vote, left half the grievance standing. Accept any accurate sorting with that reasoning. (Blueprint 10)

The addressee. Nearly every demand asks the federal government to act: Congress to legislate, the executive to withhold funds and enforce the Constitution. After Birmingham the movement had won what a city could give. The demands show it had concluded that the remaining questions, employment, the vote, the schools, could only be settled where national law is made. (Blueprint 10)

Station 5: The Bombing (Summary Record)

The facts. Sunday, September 15, 1963, at about 10:22 in the morning, at the 16th Street Baptist Church in Birmingham, as the church prepared for Youth Day. Addie Mae Collins, fourteen; Cynthia Wesley, fourteen; Carole Robertson, fourteen; Carol Denise McNair, eleven. Sarah Collins survived with the loss of an eye, and more than twenty others were injured. Grade the names exactly. They are the record's center. (Blueprint 10)

The target. The church had been the movement's meeting place and the gathering point from which the children marched in May. Attacking it on Youth Day attacked the congregation at worship and its children in particular, in the building where the spring campaign had assembled. (Blueprint 10)

Two delays. The bill: proposed in June 1963 and still unpassed at the year's end, a delay measured in months that stretched into the next Congress. The trials: the first conviction came in 1977, fourteen years later, and the last in 2002, thirty nine years later. A strong sentence observes that a law can be demanded by a living movement in a season, while a verdict waits on evidence, witnesses, and a state willing to prosecute, and that Birmingham's families waited for both. (Blueprint 10)

Part 2: The Chain and the Verdict (Grading Guide)

Chain, in brief. The Letter, April: added the movement's fullest public argument, that delay and moderation were themselves positions; cost King his liberty for the days of its writing and answered no ordinance; moved the national clergy and reading public, and no vote in Washington directly. The children, May: added televised crisis and the numbers the jails could not hold; cost more than nine hundred arrests on the first day, injuries from hoses and dogs, and the risk the movement debated before it sent them; moved Birmingham's merchants to settle within the week and the Kennedy administration to finish its bill. The address, June 11: added the presidency itself, a moral framing and a promised bill; cost Kennedy the southern wing of his party's support; moved the question into Congress, where it then stalled. The march, August 28: added a national constituency made visible, a quarter of a million people and the ten demands read into the record; cost the organizers a year of work and the discipline of reviewed speeches; moved the country's sense of the bill's support, without moving the Senate's arithmetic that summer. The bombing, September 15: added nothing the movement chose; cost Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley, Carole Robertson, and Carol Denise McNair their lives; moved national sympathy again, and still not the bill, which ended the year unpassed. (Blueprint 9 and 10)

Full credit verdicts argue from the chain rather than from admiration. For Birmingham: Kennedy's own sequence is the evidence, no bill before the hoses, a bill promised six weeks after them, in an address whose timing follows the Birmingham settlement and the University of Alabama confrontation; the march then defended a bill Birmingham had already forced into being. The counterclaim to answer is that a bill introduced is not a constituency, and that without August 28 the bill's support could not be shown to Congress or the country. For the march: Birmingham produced a local settlement and a presidential promise, while the march converted both into a national demand with named terms, the ten demands, and a visible quarter million behind them, which is the form in which Congress could count support. The counterclaim to answer is the June date: Kennedy had moved before the march, so the march at most confirmed a motion Birmingham began. Either verdict earns full credit when the counterclaim is answered from the stations, and no verdict earns full credit that treats the bombing as a step the movement took. It was a crime committed against it, and the chain records its cost in lives, not in leverage.
