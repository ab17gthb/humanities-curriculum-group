Birmingham and the March on Washington

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The City Chosen on Purpose

Birmingham, Alabama, was chosen on purpose. It stood among the most segregated cities in the United States, and its commissioner of public safety, Eugene Connor, known as Bull, had built his power on open defiance of the federal courts. The Southern Christian Leadership Conference joined the Reverend Fred Shuttlesworth and his local Alabama movement and aimed a spring campaign downtown. This was the Birmingham campaign, the protest campaign of 1963 in Birmingham, Alabama, led by the Southern Christian Leadership Conference and the local movement, whose televised repression pushed President Kennedy to propose a national civil rights bill. The plan, Project C, for confrontation, ran sit-ins, marches on city hall, and a boycott of downtown stores at Easter.

B. The Letter from the Cell

On Good Friday, April 12, Martin Luther King Jr. was arrested and held alone in a cell. There he read a statement by eight white Alabama clergymen who called the demonstrations unwise and untimely. His answer became the Letter from Birmingham Jail, Martin Luther King Jr.'s letter of April 1963, written from a Birmingham jail cell, defending nonviolent direct action against white clergymen who had called his protests unwise and untimely. Its argument is paraphrased here. He was no outsider, because wrong in one city endangered fairness in every city. Delay was itself a decision after three centuries of waiting. A law that degraded human personality, or was imposed on a people denied the vote, was unjust. The deepest obstacle was the white moderate, who preferred order to justice and set the timetable for another people's freedom.

C. The Children March

By early May the adult campaign had stalled. Bail money was short, arrests had slowed, and the boycott had not moved the merchants who could settle. James Bevel proposed sending the schoolchildren. On May 2, thousands of Black students left their classrooms, gathered at the 16th Street Baptist Church, and marched downtown. Connor arrested more than nine hundred that day. On May 3, with the jails full, he answered with high pressure fire hoses and police dogs. This was the Children's Crusade, the marches of May 1963 in Birmingham in which thousands of Black schoolchildren left their classes to demonstrate and were met with fire hoses, police dogs, and jail, producing the images that turned national opinion. Within a week, Birmingham's business leaders settled. Lunch counters and fitting rooms would desegregate, and Black clerks would be hired.

D. The President Moved

President Kennedy had watched Birmingham with his bill still unwritten. The hoses and the dogs changed his calculation. On the evening of June 11, 1963, hours after federal officers faced down Governor George Wallace at the University of Alabama, he addressed the nation. This was Kennedy's civil rights address, President Kennedy's televised address of June 11, 1963, after the Birmingham crisis, calling civil rights a moral issue and announcing that he would send Congress a comprehensive civil rights bill. That same night, in Jackson, Mississippi, an assassin shot Medgar Evers, the NAACP field secretary for his state, in the driveway of his home.

E. A March Older Than the Summer

The march was older than the summer. A. Philip Randolph had threatened a march on Washington for jobs in 1941 and won a fair employment order by the threat alone. In 1963 Randolph returned as the march's elder leader, and Bayard Rustin ran its logistics. Its printed program carried ten demands, among them a comprehensive civil rights law, desegregation of the public schools within the year, a federal law against discrimination in employment, and a two dollar minimum wage. The Kennedy administration first tried to talk the leaders out of marching, then worked to support and shape it. The organizers promised a disciplined day, with trained marshals and reviewed speeches.

F. August 28 at the Lincoln Memorial

On August 28, the ground along the Reflecting Pool filled. This was the March on Washington, the demonstration of August 28, 1963 that brought about 250,000 people to the Lincoln Memorial to demand jobs and freedom and to press Congress to pass the civil rights bill. John Lewis spoke after cutting his sharpest lines. King spoke last. His address was I Have a Dream, Martin Luther King Jr.'s address closing the March on Washington on August 28, 1963, casting the movement's demands as a claim on the promises of the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution. Paraphrased here except for one passage, the address presented the founding documents as a promise not yet honored for citizens of color. Then King described the country beyond the bill, in the thirty five words this lesson quotes: “I have a dream that my four little children will one day live in a nation where they will not be judged by the color of their skin but by the content of their character.”

G. The Answer from Birmingham

Eighteen days later, on Sunday, September 15, Birmingham answered the summer. A bomb planted by Klansmen beneath the church steps exploded at 10:22 in the morning. This was the 16th Street Baptist Church bombing, the bombing of a Birmingham church by Ku Klux Klan members on September 15, 1963, which killed four Black girls, Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley, Carole Robertson, and Carol Denise McNair, as they prepared for Sunday services. Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley, and Carole Robertson were fourteen, and Carol Denise McNair was eleven. More than twenty other people were injured. In Washington, the civil rights bill remained stalled in Congress, unpassed when the year ended. Birmingham had moved a president and filled the Mall. It had not yet bought a law.

Questions

1. Why did the movement choose Birmingham in the spring of 1963, and what was the method of Project C? Answer with the city's conditions and the campaign's schedule of actions.

2. The reading paraphrases the argument of King's Letter from Birmingham Jail in four steps. State all four steps in your own words, and name the audience King was answering.

3. Explain why the movement turned to schoolchildren in May 1963, what Connor did on May 2 and May 3, and what the Birmingham settlement of the following week contained.

4. What changed in President Kennedy's position on June 11, 1963, and what two events of that same day framed his address? Include what happened in Jackson, Mississippi that night.

5. State what the March on Washington demanded, explain how King's closing address framed those demands as a claim on the nation's founding promises, and state where the civil rights bill stood after the church bombing of September 15. Answer in a short paragraph.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Why Birmingham, and the method of Project C. Birmingham was chosen because its segregation was total and publicly defended, Commissioner Connor could be counted on to answer protest with visible force, and Shuttlesworth's local movement had already built the organization a campaign needs. Project C ran nonviolent direct action on a schedule: sit-ins, marches on city hall, a downtown boycott at Easter, and arrests accepted in numbers meant to fill the jails and force the city to bargain. (Blueprint 9)

2. The four steps of the Letter, paraphrased. King was answering eight white Alabama clergymen who had called the demonstrations unwise and untimely. The steps, as the reading paraphrases them: he was no outsider, because injustice in one place endangered justice everywhere and no American was a stranger in his own country; delay was itself a decision after more than three centuries of waiting for promised rights; a law that degraded human personality, or that was imposed on a people denied the vote, was unjust and bound no one's conscience; and the white moderate who preferred order to justice and set the timetable for another people's freedom was a deeper obstacle than the open segregationist. Students must paraphrase, not reproduce King's sentences. This key quotes none of the letter. (Blueprint 9)

3. Why children, what Connor did, and the settlement. The adult campaign had stalled by early May, with bail money short and arrests slowed, so James Bevel proposed marching the students and the movement agreed after debate. On May 2 more than nine hundred students were arrested. On May 3 Connor used high pressure fire hoses and police dogs against the next waves, before television cameras. Within a week Birmingham's senior business leaders settled: desegregation of downtown lunch counters and fitting rooms, hiring of Black clerks, and release of jailed demonstrators. (Blueprint 9)

4. Kennedy on June 11, and the day's frame. Kennedy moved from court action and quiet negotiation to public sponsorship of legislation. In a televised address that evening he called civil rights a moral issue and announced a comprehensive civil rights bill. The day's frame: the federal confrontation with Governor Wallace at the University of Alabama that afternoon, and the assassination of Medgar Evers in Jackson that night. Full credit requires all three elements. (Blueprint 9)

5. The march's demands, King's frame, and the bill after September 15. The march's printed program carried ten demands, including a comprehensive civil rights law, school desegregation within the year, protection of demonstrators from police violence, a federal fair employment law, and a two dollar minimum wage. King framed the demands as a claim on the promises of the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution, a debt the nation had signed and not honored. The one passage the reading quotes runs to thirty five words. After the bombing of September 15, which killed Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley, Carole Robertson, and Carol Denise McNair, the bill remained stalled in Congress, opposed by the southern bloc and unpassed at the year's end. (Blueprint 10)
