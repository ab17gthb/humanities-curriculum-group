Writing Task: Which Did More, Birmingham or the March?

The prompt. In the spring and summer of 1963, the movement applied pressure in two places: in Birmingham, where a local campaign met fire hoses and jail, and in Washington, where a quarter of a million people carried ten demands to the Lincoln Memorial. Answer this question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Which did more to move the federal government, Birmingham or the March on Washington? Argue from the documents in the Pressure File. A strong answer states what each place added, follows what the president did and when he did it, and reckons with September 15, when the bill the pressure had produced still sat unpassed. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each one supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson documents. Write the history in your own sentences. Where the bank paraphrases King's words, keep the paraphrase. His exact sentences are quoted only where the lesson quotes them, within the unit's limit, and your essay does not need them.

Evidence Bank

1. By early May 1963 the adult campaign in Birmingham had stalled, with bail money short. On May 2 more than nine hundred schoolchildren were arrested. On May 3 Commissioner Connor answered the next waves with fire hoses and police dogs before television cameras. Within a week, Birmingham's business leaders settled on desegregated lunch counters and fitting rooms and the hiring of Black clerks.

2. From his jail cell in April, King answered eight white clergymen in a letter whose argument this lesson paraphrases: he was no outsider in Birmingham, delay was itself a decision after three centuries of waiting, an unjust law bound no conscience, and the white moderate who preferred order to justice was the deepest obstacle.

3. On June 11, 1963, Kennedy addressed the nation, called civil rights a moral issue as old as the scriptures and as clear as the Constitution, and announced that he would ask Congress to act on a comprehensive civil rights bill. No such bill had been announced before Birmingham.

4. The march's printed program carried ten demands, among them a comprehensive civil rights law, school desegregation within the year, a federal fair employment law, and a two dollar minimum wage. Rustin read the demands aloud at the Lincoln Memorial, and about 250,000 people stood behind them.

5. King closed the march by presenting the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution as a promise made to every American and not yet honored for citizens of color. The one passage this lesson quotes from the address, thirty five words, describes children judged by the content of their character rather than the color of their skin.

6. On September 15, Klansmen bombed the 16th Street Baptist Church in Birmingham, killing Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley, Carole Robertson, and Carol Denise McNair. The civil rights bill remained stalled in Congress and was unpassed when the year ended.

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your claim, build from Birmingham and the march in the order the year produced them, answer the other side in one or two sentences, and end by stating what the federal government had and had not done by December 1963.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the question directly, one place or the other, and rests on what the federal government actually did, and when, rather than on which event the student finds more moving. (Blueprint 9 and 10)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank items 1 and 3 establish the Birmingham case: the televised crisis of May 2 and 3, the local settlement within a week, and a presidential bill announced on June 11 that had not been announced before Birmingham. (Blueprint 9) Bank items 4 and 5 establish the march case: ten named demands read into the record and a national constituency of about 250,000 standing behind them, with the movement's claim framed as a debt on the founding documents. Item 2 supplies the argument that traveled with the pressure, and item 6 supplies the fact both sides must absorb, that by December the bill was still unpassed and four girls were dead. A full credit essay uses items from both groups and item 6.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive distinction is between producing a bill and sustaining it: Birmingham can be credited with the bill's existence, the march with its public backing, and neither with its passage in 1963. An essay that never separates introduction from passage cannot judge the question.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest argument on the other side. For Birmingham: that the march showed Congress a national constituency no city campaign could show, answered by asking what the marchers were backing, a bill that already existed because of Birmingham. For the march: that Kennedy had moved on June 11, before the march, answered by asking what a promise without a visible constituency was worth in a Senate that let the bill sit.

Model opening for teacher reference. Birmingham did more, because it changed what the president did. Before May 1963 there was no civil rights bill. After the children of Birmingham were arrested by the hundreds and met with hoses and dogs on national television, and after the city's own merchants settled within the week, President Kennedy went on television on June 11, called civil rights a moral issue, and announced the bill. The march in August then gave that bill the largest public backing of the year, and an essay for the other side can build honestly on the ten demands and the quarter of a million people behind them. A full model continues in this pattern, weighs both, answers the counterclaim, and closes on December 1963, when the bill the pressure produced remained unpassed and the movement was counting the four girls of September 15 among its costs.

Common weak answers. Retelling the year month by month describes the pressure without judging between its two places. Treating Kennedy's June 11 promise as a law passed repeats the error Station 2 of the activity is built to catch, and an essay that does it has usually skipped item 6. Crediting the march with the bill's introduction reverses the calendar: the address came first, in June, and the strongest march essays concede the date and argue from constituency instead.
