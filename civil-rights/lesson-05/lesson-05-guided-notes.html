Guided Notes: Birmingham and the March on Washington

Lesson question: How did Birmingham force the nation's hand, and what did the March on Washington ask the nation to become?

Unit question: How did the civil rights movement change American law and American life, and what did it leave unfinished?

Key Terms

Birmingham campaign: the protest campaign of 1963 in Birmingham, Alabama, led by the Southern Christian Leadership Conference and the local movement, whose televised repression pushed President Kennedy to propose a national civil rights bill

Letter from Birmingham Jail: Martin Luther King Jr.'s letter of April 1963, written from a Birmingham jail cell, defending nonviolent direct action against white clergymen who had called his protests unwise and untimely

Children's Crusade: the marches of May 1963 in Birmingham in which thousands of Black schoolchildren left their classes to demonstrate and were met with fire hoses, police dogs, and jail, producing the images that turned national opinion

Kennedy's civil rights address: President Kennedy's televised address of June 11, 1963, after the Birmingham crisis, calling civil rights a moral issue and announcing that he would send Congress a comprehensive civil rights bill

March on Washington: the demonstration of August 28, 1963 that brought about 250,000 people to the Lincoln Memorial to demand jobs and freedom and to press Congress to pass the civil rights bill

I Have a Dream: Martin Luther King Jr.'s address closing the March on Washington on August 28, 1963, casting the movement's demands as a claim on the promises of the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution

16th Street Baptist Church bombing: the bombing of a Birmingham church by Ku Klux Klan members on September 15, 1963, which killed four Black girls, Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley, Carole Robertson, and Carol Denise McNair, as they prepared for Sunday services

Summary table. Birmingham: in the spring of 1963 the SCLC and Fred Shuttlesworth's local movement aimed Project C at downtown Birmingham, and King's Letter from Birmingham Jail of April 1963 answered the white clergymen who had called the protests unwise and untimely, in paraphrase arguing that he was no outsider and that the white moderate was the deepest obstacle. The children and the settlement: after the adult campaign stalled, James Bevel's Children's Crusade of May 2 and 3 met mass arrests, fire hoses, and police dogs on television, and Birmingham's business leaders settled within the week on desegregated counters and fitting rooms and Black clerks. The president and the march: Kennedy's address of June 11, 1963 announced a comprehensive civil rights bill, Medgar Evers was assassinated that night, and the March on Washington of August 28 brought about 250,000 people to the Lincoln Memorial behind ten demands, closing with King's I Have a Dream address. September 15: the bombing of the 16th Street Baptist Church killed Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley, Carole Robertson, and Carol Denise McNair, while the civil rights bill remained stalled in Congress at the year's end.

Exit ticket 1. Birmingham was chosen because its segregation was total and publicly defended by Commissioner Connor, whose visible force against protest could be counted on, and because Shuttlesworth's local movement had already built the organization a campaign needs.

Exit ticket 2. Any two of the ten demands, including a comprehensive civil rights law, desegregation of the public schools within the year, protection of demonstrators from police violence, a federal law against discrimination in employment, and a two dollar minimum wage. King said the nation had not honored the promises of the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that pairs the Birmingham pressure with the march's claim and uses two dates. The strongest answers run April and May 1963 in Birmingham, the Letter, the Children's Crusade, and the settlement, through Kennedy's June 11 address to the march of August 28 and its demands, and note that the bill was still stalled after September 15.
