Writing Task: A Right Declared, a Decade Taken

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: How did the NAACP turn a Kansas school case into the end of legal segregation, and how did the South answer? A strong answer does two things at once. It explains the legal campaign, through Houston's strategy, the graduate school cases, and the unanimous holding of 1954, and it explains the answer, through Brown II's timetable, the organized resistance, and Cooper v. Aaron, ending with a judgment on the delay. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. Charles Hamilton Houston planned to attack segregation where its inequality was easiest to prove. In Sweatt v. Painter and McLaurin v. Oklahoma, both in 1950 and both argued by Thurgood Marshall, the Supreme Court ruled for Black graduate and professional students against separate and unequal provision.

2. Five cases from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware, with a companion case from the District of Columbia, were joined under Brown v. Board of Education and reargued in 1953 on whether segregation itself, whatever the facilities, did constitutional harm.

3. On May 17, 1954, a unanimous Court held that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal and that segregation in public schools denies Black children the equal protection of the laws guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment.

4. Brown II, on May 31, 1955, required a prompt and reasonable start, placed the burden of proving delay on the school boards, set no date, and ordered admission with all deliberate speed.

5. The Southern Manifesto of 1956, signed by 101 southern members of Congress, denounced Brown as a clear abuse of judicial power and pledged all lawful means to bring about a reversal. In Prince Edward County, Virginia, the public schools stayed closed from 1959 to 1964, and about 1,700 Black children had no formal schooling in the county.

6. Cooper v. Aaron, in 1958, held that the Court's interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment in Brown is the supreme law of the land under Article VI, binding on state officials, and that Brown rights can be nullified neither openly nor indirectly. Ten years after Brown, in the autumn of 1964, barely one Black child in a hundred in the Deep South attended school with white children.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the lesson question, how the NAACP turned a school case into the end of legal segregation and how the South answered. (Blueprint 3 and Blueprint 4)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank items 1 through 3 support the campaign and the holding: Houston's planned strategy, Sweatt and McLaurin in 1950, the five joined cases and reargument, and the unanimous holding of May 17, 1954. (Blueprint 3) Bank items 4 through 6 support the answer: Brown II's open timetable, the Manifesto and the Prince Edward closings, and Cooper's settlement of the law beside the 1964 figures. (Blueprint 4) A full credit essay draws on both groups.
  • Dates and figures used as evidence, not decoration. The years 1950, 1954, 1955, 1956, 1958, and 1964, the 101 signers, the 1,700 children, and the one in a hundred each carry a step in the argument, and strong essays say what the step is.
  • A closing judgment on the delay that follows from the evidence. Either verdict earns full credit when argued: the price of unanimity, because the restraint that held nine justices together in 1954 also kept enforcement out of the holding and produced Brown II; or a separate failure, because the burden and good faith language in Brown II gave district courts tools that went largely unused for a decade while a resistance organized in the open.

Model opening for teacher reference. The NAACP did not stumble into Brown. Houston planned the campaign in the 1930s as an attack on segregation where inequality was easiest to prove, Marshall carried it through the graduate schools in Sweatt and McLaurin in 1950, and five joined cases forced the question whether separation itself, in equal buildings, still denied equal protection. On May 17, 1954 a unanimous Court answered that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal. The South's answer began in the decision's own timetable. Brown II set no date and ordered admission with all deliberate speed, and into that open space stepped massive resistance: a manifesto signed by 101 members of Congress, placement laws and tuition grants, and a Virginia county that closed its public schools for five years. Cooper v. Aaron settled the law in 1958, and the schools were still, in 1964, almost entirely separate. A full model continues in this pattern and closes by judging whether that decade was the price of the unanimous holding or a failure that followed it.

Common weak answers. The holding only answer (the Court ruled in 1954 and segregation ended) cannot account for the closed schools of 1959 or the figures of 1964, both of which are in the bank. The disobedience only answer treats the decade as simple defiance of a clear order, which Brown II's own text contradicts: the order contained no date. A chronology with no judgment lists the case and the resistance without ever weighing whether the delay belonged to the decision or to what was done with it, which is the weighing the question asks for.
