Guided Notes: Brown and the Massive Resistance

Lesson question: How did the NAACP turn a Kansas school case into the end of legal segregation, and how did the South answer?

Unit question: How did the civil rights movement change American law and American life, and what did it leave unfinished?

Key Terms

Plessy v. Ferguson: the 1896 Supreme Court decision that upheld segregation under the doctrine of separate but equal [carried from the Gilded Age unit]

separate but equal: the doctrine set by the Supreme Court in Plessy v. Ferguson in 1896, holding that segregation was constitutional as long as the facilities provided for each race were equal

Brown v. Board of Education: the Supreme Court decision of May 17, 1954 that declared segregation in public schools unconstitutional, ruling that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal

Brown II: the Supreme Court's 1955 decision ordering school desegregation to proceed with all deliberate speed, a phrase southern officials used to justify years of delay

massive resistance: the campaign by southern state governments after Brown to prevent school desegregation, using new laws, defiance of federal courts, and in some places the closing of public schools

Southern Manifesto: the declaration of 1956 signed by 101 southern members of Congress denouncing Brown v. Board of Education as an abuse of judicial power and pledging to resist it

Cooper v. Aaron: the Supreme Court decision of 1958, arising from Little Rock, holding that state officials are bound by the Court's interpretation of the Constitution and cannot nullify its rulings

Summary table. The strategy: Charles Hamilton Houston planned an attack where inequality was easiest to prove, won Sweatt and McLaurin in the graduate schools in 1950 under Thurgood Marshall, and carried the campaign to the elementary schools. The decision: five cases joined under the Kansas case were reargued on whether segregation itself harmed children, and on May 17, 1954 a unanimous Court held that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal under the Fourteenth Amendment. The delay: Brown II in 1955 set no date, required a prompt and reasonable start, and ordered admission with all deliberate speed. The resistance: massive resistance laws, pupil placement, tuition grants, the Southern Manifesto signed by 101 members of Congress in 1956, and in Prince Edward County five years of closed public schools. The reply and the gap: Cooper v. Aaron in 1958 held state officials bound by the Court's reading of the Constitution, yet in the autumn of 1964 barely one Black child in a hundred in the Deep South attended school with white children.

Exit ticket 1. On May 17, 1954 the Supreme Court held that segregation in public schools is unconstitutional because separate educational facilities are inherently unequal, denying Black children the equal protection of the laws under the Fourteenth Amendment. The doctrine displaced was separate but equal, set by Plessy v. Ferguson in 1896.

Exit ticket 2. The phrase is all deliberate speed, from Brown II (1955). Tools used in the time it allowed include massive resistance closing laws, pupil placement laws, tuition grants to private schools, and outright school closings, fullest in Prince Edward County, Virginia, where the public schools stayed closed from 1959 to 1964.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that names the legal victory and the organized answer, with two supporting facts. The strongest answers pair the strategy and the unanimous 1954 holding with Brown II's open timetable, the Southern Manifesto, the Prince Edward closings, and the Cooper holding that nullification, open or indirect, was closed off.
