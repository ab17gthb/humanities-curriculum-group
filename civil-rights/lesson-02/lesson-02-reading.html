Brown and the Massive Resistance

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Strategy: Attack Where Inequality Showed

The campaign against school segregation was planned, not improvised. Charles Hamilton Houston, the NAACP lawyer who built its legal program in the 1930s, argued for attacking segregation where its inequality was easiest to prove.

In Sweatt v. Painter in 1950, the Court held that a hastily created Black law school in Texas could not equal the state university's. That year, in McLaurin v. Oklahoma, it ordered that a Black doctoral student admitted to a white university be treated like every other student. Thurgood Marshall, Houston's student and successor, argued both cases. The next target was the elementary schools.

B. Five Cases Become One

Five cases reached the Supreme Court together, from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware, with a companion case from the District of Columbia, joined under the Kansas case. In Topeka, Oliver Brown and twelve other parents sued after their children were assigned to Black schools farther from home than the white schools nearby. His daughter Linda rode a bus across town although a white school stood blocks away.

Argued in December 1952, the cases were set for reargument in 1953 on a sharper question: whether segregation itself, whatever the buildings, did constitutional harm. Marshall and the NAACP lawyers answered that it did. The school boards answered that Plessy v. Ferguson, the 1896 Supreme Court decision that upheld segregation under the doctrine of separate but equal, had settled the question two generations earlier.

C. May 17, 1954: Separate Is Inherently Unequal

Chief Justice Earl Warren worked to bring all nine justices to one opinion, and on May 17, 1954 the Court spoke with a single voice. Where a state provided public education, the opinion held, the opportunity had to be made available to all on equal terms.

To separate children from others of similar age and qualifications solely because of their race, the Court held, generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community in a way unlikely ever to be undone, and that harm did not depend on equal buildings or books. The conclusion came in one sentence: separate educational facilities are inherently unequal. School segregation therefore denied Black children the equal protection of the laws under the Fourteenth Amendment, and in public education the separate but equal doctrine had no place.

D. Brown II: All Deliberate Speed

The decision announced a principle and set no date. The Court heard a further round of argument on relief alone. On May 31, 1955, in Brown II, the Court gave the work to the federal district courts, ordered school boards to make a prompt and reasonable start, and put the burden on the boards to prove that further delay was necessary and sought in good faith.

The order closed by directing admission on a racially nondiscriminatory basis with all deliberate speed, a phrase with no deadline in it. Southern officials read it as permission to move slowly. The delay was built into the victory, and the resistance organized inside it.

E. Massive Resistance Organizes

The organized answer took its name from Virginia. Senator Harry F. Byrd, whose organization ran the state, called for massive resistance, and Virginia adopted laws to close any school ordered to integrate and to cut state funds from districts that did. Other states passed versions of the same program. Pupil placement laws and tuition grants toward private fees did much of the quieter work.

In March 1956 the Southern Manifesto, signed by 101 southern members of Congress, denounced the decision as a clear abuse of judicial power, commended states resisting forced integration by any lawful means, and pledged its signers to use all lawful means to bring about a reversal.

F. Closing the Schools: Prince Edward County

The fullest working out came in Prince Edward County, Virginia, one of the districts joined in Brown. When a federal court ordered the county to admit students without regard to race for the term beginning in September 1959, the board of supervisors refused to levy school taxes or appropriate money. The county's public schools did not open that fall.

They stayed closed for five years. White children attended Prince Edward Academy, a private school opened by a local foundation and supported at first by state tuition grants and county tax credits. About 1,700 Black children had no formal schooling in the county. Some left to live with relatives elsewhere, and many got no schooling at all. The public schools reopened only in 1964, after the Supreme Court, in Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County, ordered them opened.

G. Cooper v. Aaron, and the Gap

The Court answered the claim that its rulings could be treated as advice in a case from Little Rock. Arkansas officials argued that a state's governor and legislature were not bound by the Court's reading of the Constitution in Brown. In Cooper v. Aaron, in September 1958, a unanimous Court rejected the claim in an opinion signed by all nine justices. Article VI makes the Constitution the supreme law of the land, and since Marbury v. Madison the federal judiciary has held the duty of saying what the law is. The reading of the Fourteenth Amendment given in Brown therefore bound state officials, and Black children's rights could be nullified neither openly nor indirectly through evasive schemes.

The law was settled. The schools, in most of the South, were not. Ten years after Brown, in the autumn of 1964, barely one Black child in a hundred across the states of the Deep South attended school with white children.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Describe Houston's strategy and the two 1950 cases. Charles Hamilton Houston planned to attack segregation where its inequality was easiest to prove, beginning with graduate and professional schools. In Sweatt v. Painter (1950) the Court held that a hastily created Black law school in Texas could not equal the state university's law school. In McLaurin v. Oklahoma (1950) it ordered that a Black doctoral student admitted to a white university be treated like every other student. Thurgood Marshall argued both. The cases established the method and the precedent the NAACP then carried to the elementary schools.

2. What question did reargument pose, and how was it answered? Whether segregation itself, whatever the condition of the buildings, did constitutional harm to the children assigned by race. Marshall and the NAACP lawyers answered that it did, presenting evidence on what state enforced separation taught Black children about their place. The school boards answered that it did not, and that Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) had settled the question two generations earlier.

3. State the holding of May 17, 1954. A unanimous Court, in an opinion by Chief Justice Earl Warren, held that separating children solely because of their race generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community in a way unlikely ever to be undone, a harm that did not depend on equal buildings or books. Its conclusion was that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal. School segregation therefore denied Black children the equal protection of the laws guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment, and the separate but equal doctrine had no place in public education.

4. How did Brown II build delay into desegregation? Brown II (May 31, 1955) announced no date. It gave enforcement to the federal district courts district by district, required only a prompt and reasonable start, allowed further time where boards could show necessity and good faith, and closed by ordering admission with all deliberate speed, a phrase with no deadline in it. Southern officials read the phrase as permission to move slowly, and the resistance organized in the space the timetable left open.

5. How did the NAACP turn a Kansas school case into the end of legal segregation, and how did the South answer? Full credit answers hold both halves together. The NAACP built a planned campaign from Houston's graduate school strategy through Sweatt and McLaurin, joined five cases under the Topeka case, won reargument on the question whether segregation itself harmed children, and on May 17, 1954 won a unanimous ruling that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal under the Fourteenth Amendment. The South answered first with time, Brown II's all deliberate speed, and then with organization: massive resistance laws, pupil placement, tuition grants, the Southern Manifesto of 1956 signed by 101 members of Congress, and in Prince Edward County the closing of the public schools for five years. Cooper v. Aaron (1958) settled that state officials were bound by the ruling and could not nullify it, yet in the autumn of 1964 barely one Black child in a hundred in the Deep South attended school with white children. Answers that end at the 1954 holding, with no account of the answer it drew, earn partial credit.
