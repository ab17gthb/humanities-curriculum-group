Brown and the Massive Resistance

The Civil Rights Movement · Lesson 2 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: The Civil Rights Movement, Lesson 2 of 8. Teach it directly after Lesson 1, which built the groundwork of Jim Crow, the institutions Black Americans built inside it, and the NAACP. This lesson follows the NAACP legal campaign planned in the 1930s through the graduate school victories of 1950 and the five cases joined under Brown to the unanimous ruling of May 17, 1954, then follows the ruling into Brown II in 1955, massive resistance and the Southern Manifesto, the Prince Edward County school closings of 1959 to 1964, Cooper v. Aaron in 1958, and the enrollment gap of 1964. Lesson 3 turns from the courts to direct action, in Montgomery and at Little Rock.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “Brown and the Massive Resistance” and the guided notes, which follow the deck in sections A to G: the strategy, five cases become one, May 17, 1954, Brown II, massive resistance organizes, closing the schools, and Cooper v. Aaron and the gap. The activity is “The Case File: Brown and the Resistance.” The notebook page is “Notebook Page: Brown and the Massive Resistance.” The quick check is “Quick Check: The Decree.” The writing task is “Writing Task: A Right Declared, a Decade Taken.”

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Students need Lesson 1 and the earlier units: Plessy v. Ferguson and separate but equal, Jim Crow law and disfranchisement, the NAACP founded in 1909 and its court strategy, and Smith v. Allwright and Shelley v. Kraemer as proof that federal courts could be made to move. No prior knowledge of Sweatt v. Painter, McLaurin v. Oklahoma, the five Brown cases, Brown II, the Southern Manifesto, Prince Edward County, or Cooper v. Aaron is assumed; each is taught in sequence.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (analyze connections among events: a legal strategy planned in the 1930s, the graduate school precedents of 1950, the Brown holding of 1954, the enforcement order of 1955, organized resistance, and the settlement in Cooper, in sequence), D2.His.9 (construct arguments using historical evidence: the writing task judging whether the delay after Brown was the price of unanimity or a separate failure), D2.His.16 (assess options for individual and collective action: litigation planned across decades against resistance carried on through state law and school closings); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from strategy to decision to resistance to reply), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task argued from the evidence bank).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain how the NAACP turned a Kansas school case into the end of legal segregation, and how the South answered: Charles Hamilton Houston planned a campaign that attacked segregation first where inequality was easiest to prove, Thurgood Marshall won the graduate school cases of 1950, five cases were joined under Brown, a unanimous Court held on May 17, 1954 that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal, Brown II in 1955 ordered admission with all deliberate speed and set no date, southern governments answered with massive resistance, the Southern Manifesto signed by 101 members of Congress, pupil placement laws, tuition grants, and school closings that left Prince Edward County without public schools for five years, Cooper v. Aaron in 1958 held state officials bound by Brown, and by 1964 barely one Black child in a hundred in the Deep South attended school with white children.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes: opening, question, and map (slides 1 to 3) 5 minutes; the strategy (slides 4 to 6) 7 minutes; five cases become one (slides 7 to 8) 5 minutes; May 17, 1954 (slides 9 to 11) 7 minutes; Brown II (slides 12 to 13) 5 minutes; massive resistance organizes (slides 14 to 16) 7 minutes; closing the schools (slides 17 to 19) 6 minutes; Cooper and the gap (slides 20 to 22) 5 minutes; glance, vocabulary, synthesis, and the turn to work (slides 23 to 27) 3 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes.

Slide 4 · Section A: The Strategy (divider)

On the slide: SECTION A · The Strategy · A campaign planned in the 1930s, aimed first where inequality was easiest to prove.

  • [Set up the strategy as a piece of planning.]
  • Students often imagine the Brown case as a single lawsuit that rose on its own.
  • It was the last step of a campaign built over twenty years.
  • Charles Hamilton Houston, dean of Howard Law School and then the NAACP's chief lawyer, trained a generation of Black lawyers and designed the sequence this lesson follows.
  • The sequence began where the inequality was most visible and most expensive for states to defend, won precedents there, and moved step by step toward the schools attended by the youngest children.
  • Houston died in 1950, the year his graduate school cases reached the Supreme Court, so the students he trained, led by Thurgood Marshall, carried the campaign he had drawn.
  • Keep the word strategy in front of the class.
  • Every later section is this plan working, and the test will ask students to describe the plan before they describe the case.

Slide 5 · Attack where inequality showed

On the slide: A · THE STRATEGY · Attack where inequality showed · ▪ Houston built the NAACP legal program in the 1930s. ▪ States were ordered to make separate schools truly equal. ▪ The campaign began with graduate and professional schools. ▪ Precedent came first, and elementary schools came last.

  • [Teach the logic of the sequence in Houston's own terms.]
  • Southern states spent far more on white schools than on Black schools at every level, and the gap was widest and hardest to defend in graduate education, where several states provided Black students no law school or medical school at all.
  • A court order requiring truly equal separate provision would force a state to build a second full university system or admit the Black applicant to the existing one.
  • Either outcome cost the state money or broke segregation at its smallest and least visible point, before judges sympathetic to the argument because they were themselves products of professional schools.
  • Houston also understood the audience.
  • Graduate cases involved adult plaintiffs, small numbers, and institutions far from the emotional center of the school question.
  • The elementary schools, where millions of children attended under law, came last, after the precedents existed.
  • Note: No portrait of Houston is used in this lesson. The portrait carried on Wikimedia Commons rests on a rights claim that was not relied on for this build, as the lesson's image sources record, so his strategy is taught in text.

Slide 6 · 1950: the graduate school victories

On the slide: A · THE STRATEGY · 1950: the graduate school victories · ▪ Sweatt v. Painter held that a makeshift law school cannot equal the university. ▪ McLaurin v. Oklahoma held that admission means equal treatment inside. ▪ Both cases were argued by Thurgood Marshall. ▪ The method was proven, and the precedent was set. · Photograph: Thurgood Marshall in his judicial robes in 1976.

  • Picture: Thurgood Marshall in his judicial robes in 1976, from the official portraits of the Supreme Court held by the Library of Congress. Caption it with its true date, 1976; in 1950 Marshall was the NAACP's chief counsel, Houston's former student, arguing the cases his teacher had designed.
  • In Sweatt v. Painter, Texas had created a separate law school for Heman Sweatt in haste rather than admit him to the University of Texas.
  • The Supreme Court held that the new school could not equal the established one in faculty, standing, or the qualities that make a law school strong.
  • In McLaurin v. Oklahoma, George McLaurin had been admitted to a doctoral program but was seated apart in the classroom, the library, and the cafeteria, and the Court ordered that he be treated like every other student.
  • Together the cases taught that equality could not be supplied by a building thrown up for the purpose, and that admission with internal segregation was not equality either.
  • The elementary schools were next.

Slide 7 · Section B: Five Cases Become One (divider)

On the slide: SECTION B · Five Cases Become One · Five districts, one name, and a question the Court insisted on asking twice.

  • [Teach the assembly of the case as a teaching point in itself.]
  • The NAACP did not bring one family's complaint and hope for a national ruling.
  • It brought cases from four states, Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware, joined with a companion case from the District of Columbia decided the same day, so that the Court would confront school segregation as a national system rather than a local dispute.
  • The Kansas case gave the group its name because it was listed first.
  • Oliver Brown was a Topeka parent, a welder and assistant pastor, whose daughter Linda was assigned to a Black school across town although a white school stood blocks from the family home.
  • Note for students that the Virginia case in the group, Davis, began with a student strike at Moton High School in Prince Edward County in 1951.
  • That county returns in Section F, at the far end of the resistance.
  • Note: The well known 1953 photographs of the Brown family and of Monroe Elementary classrooms are press photographs held in a commercial collection and are not public domain, so they are not used, as the lesson's image sources record.

Slide 8 · Reargument: does separation itself harm?

On the slide: B · FIVE CASES BECOME ONE · Reargument: does separation itself harm? · ▪ The cases were argued in December 1952 and reargued in December 1953. ▪ The Court asked whether segregation itself does constitutional harm. ▪ The NAACP answered yes, with evidence on children. ▪ The boards answered that Plessy settled the question in 1896.

  • The first argument, in December 1952, left the Court divided, and the justices took an unusual step: they set the cases for reargument and posed written questions.
  • The sharpest question asked whether segregation itself, even where buildings and budgets were equal, deprived Black children of equal educational opportunity.
  • That question is the hinge of the whole case, and students should be able to state it in their own words.
  • Marshall's team answered yes, presenting testimony and research on what state enforced separation taught Black children about their standing in the community.
  • The school boards answered that Plessy v. Ferguson, decided in 1896, had upheld segregation for two generations and that the Fourteenth Amendment had never been understood to forbid separate schools.
  • Between the two arguments Chief Justice Fred Vinson died, and Earl Warren, the former governor of California, took the center chair.
  • Section C takes up what Warren did with a divided Court.
  • Ask: What question did the Court pose at reargument?
  • Answer: whether segregation itself, even where buildings and budgets were equal, deprived Black children of equal educational opportunity.

Slide 9 · Section C: May 17, 1954 (divider)

On the slide: SECTION C · May 17, 1954 · The Warren Court, in its official photograph of October 1953.

  • Picture: The official portrait of the Court as it stood in October 1953, held by the United States Government, the same Court that decided Brown the following May. Caption it as the Court of October 1953.
  • Earl Warren understood that a divided ruling on school segregation would hand the South a reason to treat the decision as one faction's opinion.
  • He worked through the winter and spring of 1954 to bring all nine justices to a single, short, unanimous opinion, written in language a newspaper reader could follow.
  • He assigned the opinion to himself and traded drafts until the last holdouts signed.
  • Teach the unanimity as a deliberate achievement, not a natural outcome.
  • At the first conference after argument, the Court had been split, with several justices prepared to uphold Plessy.
  • What unanimity bought, and what it may have cost in the enforcement decision that followed, is the judgment students will make in the activity and the writing task.

Slide 10 · Separate is inherently unequal

On the slide: C · MAY 17, 1954 · Separate is inherently unequal · ▪ Warren read a unanimous opinion. ▪ Separation generates a feeling of inferiority. ▪ The harm does not depend on equal buildings. ▪ Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal. ▪ The Fourteenth Amendment carries the holding.

  • [Read the holding slowly, in the opinion's own sequence, because the activity puts this text in front of students verbatim.]
  • The Court first established that public education had become a central function of government and that, where a state provided it, the opportunity had to be available to all on equal terms.
  • It then answered the reargument question: to separate children from others of similar age and qualifications solely because of their race generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community that may affect their hearts and minds in a way unlikely ever to be undone.
  • Because the harm lay in the separation itself, equalizing buildings or budgets could not cure it.
  • The conclusion followed in one sentence: separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.
  • Segregation in public schools therefore denied Black children the equal protection of the laws guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment, and the separate but equal doctrine had no place in public education.

Slide 11 · 9 to 0: a unanimous Court

On the slide: C · MAY 17, 1954 · 9 to 0 · a unanimous Court on May 17, 1954 · One opinion, no dissents, no concurrences · Every justice signed the result · Plessy displaced in public education after 58 years

  • 9 to 0
  • [Give students the weight of the unanimous vote.]
  • School segregation had stood under Plessy's protection for fifty eight years, and the Court that ended it spoke in a single opinion with no dissenting or concurring opinions to qualify it.
  • Warren's opinion was deliberately short, about eleven pages, nontechnical, and addressed to the country as much as to lawyers.
  • Southern newspapers printed it in full the next day, and southern officials understood at once both its clarity on the principle and its silence on enforcement, because the opinion decided constitutionality only and restored the cases for a further argument on relief.
  • That silence is the bridge to Section D.
  • Students sometimes read unanimity as strength in every direction.
  • Teach it precisely: unanimity made the holding hard to dismiss as partisan, and it was purchased in part by postponing every hard question about timetables, districts, and remedies to a second case the following year.

Slide 12 · Section D: Brown II (divider)

On the slide: SECTION D · Brown II: All Deliberate Speed · A second decision, a year later, that set the work in motion and set no date for finishing it.

  • [Have students understand why Brown II exists before they judge its language.]
  • The 1954 opinion decided that segregation was unconstitutional and nothing else.
  • The cases came from districts with different school systems, transportation maps, and local laws, and the Court heard a full second round of argument, in the spring of 1955, on relief alone.
  • The NAACP asked for desegregation by the following autumn.
  • Several southern states asked for gradual, open ended compliance supervised locally.
  • What the Court produced on May 31, 1955 tried to hold both the principle and the machinery: local district courts would supervise, school boards bore the burden of proof, and the work was to begin promptly.
  • Its closing phrase, however, is the part history kept, and Section D's single content slide reads the decision in full so students can weigh its strict sentences against its famous last words.

Slide 13 · A prompt start, and no deadline

On the slide: D · BROWN II · A prompt start, and no deadline · ▪ Brown II was decided on May 31, 1955. ▪ District courts took charge, district by district. ▪ Boards had to make a prompt and reasonable start. ▪ The burden of proving delay sat on the boards. ▪ Admission was ordered with all deliberate speed. · Photograph: the judgment and decree issued in Brown on May 31, 1955.

  • Picture: The judgment and decree issued in Brown on May 31, 1955, the implementation decree held by the National Archives. Caption it as the Brown judgment of May 31, 1955.
  • [Teach the decision as two texts in one.]
  • The strict text requires defendants to make a prompt and reasonable start toward full compliance.
  • It permits additional time only where the defendants themselves prove it necessary in the public interest and consistent with good faith compliance at the earliest practicable date, and it instructs the district courts to retain jurisdiction.
  • The loose text is the closing order, directing that the plaintiffs be admitted to public schools on a racially nondiscriminatory basis with all deliberate speed.
  • Deliberate in ordinary use can mean slow and careful, and the phrase contains no date, no percentage, and no penalty.
  • Southern officials quoted the closing phrase and ignored the burden language, and district courts across the South, staffed by local judges, largely let them.
  • The delay that followed was therefore not only disobedience.
  • Part of it was written into the order itself.

Slide 14 · Section E: Massive Resistance Organizes (divider)

On the slide: SECTION E · Massive Resistance Organizes · Virginia gives the answer its name, and Congress gives it 101 signatures.

  • [Meet the resistance as a program rather than as a mood.]
  • The resistance that followed Brown was organized, legalistic, and proud of both qualities.
  • Its architects were not street crowds.
  • They were governors, legislators, a United States senator who ran a state political machine, and members of Congress.
  • Their method was to use the forms of law, statutes, placement boards, funding rules, and constitutional declarations, to make the Brown holding as difficult and slow to exercise as possible, while professing fidelity to the Constitution as they read it.
  • Senator Harry Byrd of Virginia supplied the program's name in 1956: massive resistance.
  • Within two years of Brown II, southern states had passed hundreds of measures in its spirit.
  • This section covers the program's tools and its congressional statement, the Southern Manifesto, which students will read verbatim in the activity alongside the two court decisions it answered.
  • Note: No photograph with a verified public domain basis was used for the Southern Manifesto or for Senator Byrd in this build, as the lesson's image sources record, so both are taught in text.

Slide 15 · The tools of delay

On the slide: E · MASSIVE RESISTANCE ORGANIZES · The tools of delay · ▪ Closing laws shut any school ordered to integrate. ▪ Pupil placement assigned each child individually, with race unnamed. ▪ Tuition grants paid public money toward private fees. ▪ Interposition declared the ruling void in the state.

  • [Walk the four tools in order and name what each one does to a Black family's practical position.]
  • Closing laws, adopted first in Virginia in 1956, required the governor to shut any public school ordered to desegregate and cut its state funds, converting a court order against one district into a threat against every district.
  • Pupil placement laws created boards that assigned each child individually under criteria such as home environment, aptitude, and the possibility of friction, written to produce segregated results without using the word race, and nearly impossible to challenge one child at a time.
  • Tuition grants paid public money toward fees at private schools, which sprang up across the South as segregation academies.
  • Interposition resolutions declared Brown void within the state, a constitutional theory with no support in the courts that nonetheless gave local officials a document to cite.
  • The shared design is delay through procedure.
  • Every tool forced the NAACP back into court, district by district and child by child.

Slide 16 · 101 signers of the Southern Manifesto

On the slide: E · MASSIVE RESISTANCE ORGANIZES · 101 · signers of the Southern Manifesto, 1956 · Nineteen senators and eighty two representatives · Brown named a clear abuse of judicial power · Resistance pledged by all lawful means

  • 101
  • The Southern Manifesto, formally the Declaration of Constitutional Principles, was read into the Congressional Record in March 1956 and signed by 101 southern members of Congress: nineteen of the South's twenty two senators and eighty two of its representatives.
  • Its text, which students read in the activity, calls the school decisions a clear abuse of judicial power, charges the Court with encroaching on rights reserved to the states and the people, commends the states that had declared their intention to resist forced integration by any lawful means, and pledges the signers to use all lawful means to bring about a reversal of the decision.
  • Teach the word lawful as the document's hinge.
  • It let members of Congress lead a resistance while professing constitutional fidelity, and it set the program's public limit at the same time.
  • The two southern senators who refused to sign, Lyndon Johnson of Texas and Estes Kefauver of Tennessee, are worth naming: the document's near unanimity was a choice, not a condition of southern politics.
  • Ask: How many members of Congress signed the Southern Manifesto, and what did they pledge?
  • Answer: 101 signers, nineteen senators and eighty two representatives, pledged to use all lawful means to bring about a reversal of the Brown decision.

Slide 17 · Section F: Closing the Schools (divider)

On the slide: SECTION F · Closing the Schools · In Prince Edward County, resistance reached its limit: no public schools at all.

  • [Have students feel the connection between this section and the lesson's beginning before the facts.]
  • The county's Davis case, one of the five joined in Brown, began in April 1951 when Barbara Johns, a sixteen year old student at Moton High School, led a strike against the school's tarpaper shack classrooms and the NAACP agreed to sue, on condition that the suit attack segregation itself rather than ask for a better separate school.
  • Eight years later the same county became the fullest test of what massive resistance would do when every delaying tool had been used and a court order still stood.
  • Rather than admit Black and white children to the same schools, the county ended public education altogether.
  • This section states what that meant, in years and in children, and how it finally ended in a second Supreme Court decision in 1964.
  • Note: No public domain photograph of the closed Prince Edward County schools or of the Prince Edward Academy was verified for this build, as the lesson's image sources record, so the closing is taught in text and figures and the activity's fifth station is a text summary on its face.

Slide 18 · Prince Edward County, 1959 to 1964

On the slide: F · CLOSING THE SCHOOLS · Prince Edward County, 1959 to 1964 · ▪ The board of supervisors refused to levy school taxes. ▪ Public schools did not open in the fall of 1959. ▪ A private academy served white children. ▪ About 1,700 Black children had no school in the county. ▪ Griffin ordered the schools reopened in 1964.

  • [State the mechanism precisely, because it shows the resistance working through ordinary government powers.]
  • A federal court ordered the county to admit students without regard to race for the term beginning in September 1959.
  • The county school board lacked the power to raise its own money.
  • The board of supervisors, which levied taxes, refused to appropriate any funds for public schools, and without money the schools could not open.
  • A private foundation opened Prince Edward Academy for white children that fall, supported at first by state tuition grants and county tax credits.
  • No school was provided for Black children in the county.
  • Some families sent children to live with relatives in other counties or states, churches and volunteers ran improvised classes, and many children received no formal education for years.
  • Classes in county buildings began in 1963, and in Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County in 1964 the Supreme Court ordered the supervisors to levy taxes and reopen public schools on the same terms as other Virginia counties.

Slide 19 · 5 years without public schools

On the slide: F · CLOSING THE SCHOOLS · 5 years · Prince Edward County without public schools · Fall 1959 to fall 1964 · One Virginia county · About 1,700 Black children shut out of schooling

  • 5 years
  • [Hold this figure up against the language of law that surrounds the lesson.]
  • Five school years is the whole of a child's passage from first grade to sixth, or from seventh grade to graduation.
  • The children who lost those years in Prince Edward County were not parties to a new lawsuit by choice.
  • Their parents' case, Davis, had been won in 1954, when many of them were small children, and the county answered the victory by abolishing the institution the case was about.
  • About 1,700 Black children were shut out of formal schooling in the county during the closing, a figure carried in the Griffin record and the standard accounts, while the white academy enrolled the county's white children with public support.
  • When teachers discuss what a court order can and cannot do, this is the concrete case: the order of 1959 was clear, the Constitution had been settled in the county's own case, and the schools stayed closed for five years anyway, until a new order reached the county's tax levy itself.

Slide 20 · Section G: Cooper v. Aaron, and the Gap (divider)

On the slide: SECTION G · Cooper v. Aaron, and the Gap · The Court closes every legal exit, and the lesson closes on what the law had not moved.

  • [Join a legal settlement to a statistical verdict.]
  • Cooper v. Aaron grew out of Little Rock, which the next lesson studies in full.
  • Arkansas's governor and legislature claimed that state officials were not bound by the Supreme Court's interpretation of the Constitution in Brown, and that the decision was not the law for Arkansas until Arkansas accepted it.
  • The claim was the constitutional theory underneath all of massive resistance, stated in its purest form by a governor.
  • The Court's answer in 1958 closed the theory completely, in an opinion every justice signed individually.
  • Section G then sets that complete legal victory beside the enrollment figures of 1964, ten years after Brown.
  • The distance between the two is the lesson's last idea and the unit's recurring question in miniature: what a court can settle, and what settling the law leaves still to be done by other means.

Slide 21 · No official may nullify Brown

On the slide: G · COOPER V. AARON, AND THE GAP · No official may nullify Brown · ▪ Little Rock asked whether a state was bound by Brown. ▪ Article VI makes the Constitution the supreme law of the land. ▪ Marbury, in 1803, held that the courts say what the law is. ▪ Brown is binding on every state official, by oath. ▪ Brown could be nullified neither openly nor indirectly.

  • [Teach Cooper as a chain of three links, the way the activity asks students to trace it.]
  • The first link is Article VI: the Constitution is the supreme Law of the Land, and state officers swear an oath to support it.
  • The second link is Marbury v. Madison of 1803, in which Chief Justice Marshall declared that it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is, a principle the Cooper opinion calls a permanent and indispensable feature of the constitutional system.
  • The third link is the conclusion: the interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment given in Brown is therefore itself the supreme law of the land, binding on Arkansas officials notwithstanding any state law or constitution.
  • The opinion, signed individually by all nine justices, closed the case with a sentence students should copy: the rights declared in Brown can be nullified neither openly and directly by state officers, nor indirectly through evasive schemes for segregation, whether attempted ingeniously or ingenuously.

Slide 22 · 1 in 100 by 1964

On the slide: G · COOPER V. AARON, AND THE GAP · 1 in 100 · Black children in the Deep South in school with white children, 1964 · Ten years after Brown · The law settled since 1958 · The schools, in most of the South, still separate in fact

  • 1 in 100
  • [End the content of the lesson on this figure and let it stand without softening.]
  • In the autumn of 1964, ten years after a unanimous Court held separate schools inherently unequal, and six years after Cooper closed every legal theory of resistance, barely one Black child in a hundred across the states of the Deep South attended school with white children.
  • The figure is the lesson's verdict on the difference between declaring a right and delivering it.
  • The courts had supplied the declaration, twice, in the strongest available language.
  • What the declaration lacked was an enforcement instrument equal to the resistance: no deadline in 1955, district judges drawn from the communities resisting, and families forced to sue school by school and child by child.
  • The movement's answer to that gap, mass protest that created pressure no court timetable had produced, begins with the next lesson, in Montgomery and at Little Rock itself.

  • Plessy v. Ferguson: the 1896 Supreme Court decision that upheld segregation under the doctrine of separate but equal
  • separate but equal: the doctrine set by the Supreme Court in Plessy v. Ferguson in 1896, holding that segregation was constitutional as long as the facilities provided for each race were equal
  • Brown v. Board of Education: the Supreme Court decision of May 17, 1954 that declared segregation in public schools unconstitutional, ruling that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal
  • Brown II: the Supreme Court's 1955 decision ordering school desegregation to proceed with all deliberate speed, a phrase southern officials used to justify years of delay
  • Teach the four as two pairs.
  • Plessy and its doctrine supplied the legal shelter for school segregation from 1896 to 1954.
  • Brown removed the shelter in public education, and Brown II then determined, in practice, how fast the removal would reach actual classrooms.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for the answer

On the slide: THE VOCABULARY · Words for the answer · massive resistance · the southern campaign to prevent desegregation · Southern Manifesto · the 1956 declaration signed by 101 members of Congress · Cooper v. Aaron · the 1958 ruling: state officials are bound by Brown

  • Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly.
  • massive resistance: the campaign by southern state governments after Brown to prevent school desegregation, using new laws, defiance of federal courts, and in some places the closing of public schools
  • Southern Manifesto: the declaration of 1956 signed by 101 southern members of Congress denouncing Brown v. Board of Education as an abuse of judicial power and pledging to resist it
  • Cooper v. Aaron: the Supreme Court decision of 1958, arising from Little Rock, holding that state officials are bound by the Court's interpretation of the Constitution and cannot nullify its rulings
  • Keep the sequence in view for review: the resistance organized first under Byrd's name and the Manifesto's signatures, and Cooper answered it last, in 1958, by holding that the Court's reading of the Constitution binds state officials and cannot be nullified.

Slide 26 · A right declared, a decade taken

On the slide: THE LESSON IN ONE VIEW · A right declared, a decade taken · Declared · Separate schools held inherently unequal, May 17, 1954 · Delayed · No date set in 1955; all deliberate speed instead · Resisted · Manifesto, placement laws, grants, and closed schools · Answered · Cooper in 1958: nullification closed, openly and indirectly

  • [Close the argument before the work begins, and make students say the four rows aloud in order, because the sequence is the lesson.]
  • Declared: on May 17, 1954 a unanimous Court held separate educational facilities inherently unequal, ending the constitutional standing of legal segregation in the public schools after a campaign the NAACP had planned for two decades.
  • Delayed: Brown II in 1955 required a prompt and reasonable start and then ordered admission with all deliberate speed, a phrase with no deadline in it.
  • Resisted: into that open timetable stepped massive resistance, the Southern Manifesto signed by 101 members of Congress, pupil placement laws, tuition grants, and whole districts, Prince Edward County first among them, closing their public schools.
  • Answered: Cooper v. Aaron in 1958 held state officials bound by the Court's reading of the Constitution and closed nullification in both its open and indirect forms, while the schools themselves remained almost entirely separate in 1964.
