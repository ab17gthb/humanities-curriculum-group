The Laws Change: 1964 and 1965

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Fight in the Senate

President Kennedy sent Congress a civil rights bill in June 1963. It was still stalled when he was assassinated that November. President Johnson, who had run the Senate as majority leader, told Congress that no memorial could honor Kennedy better than passing the bill whole. The House passed it in February 1964, and the fight moved to the Senate.

The southern weapon was the filibuster, the Senate practice of extending debate to block a bill from coming to a vote, broken on the civil rights bill of 1964 when the Senate voted for cloture after more than two months of debate. It ran for sixty working days. Republican votes supplied the margin. On June 10 the Senate voted 71 to 29 for cloture, the first time in the century it had closed debate on a civil rights bill. The Senate passed the bill on June 19, and Johnson signed it on July 2.

B. The Civil Rights Act: What It Reached

The act was the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the federal law that banned discrimination in public accommodations and employment and allowed the government to cut off funds to segregated programs, the movement's first great legislative victory. Title II barred discrimination in hotels, restaurants, and theaters. Title VI let federal agencies cut off money to programs that discriminated. Title VII, the section of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 that banned discrimination in employment on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, or national origin, covered hiring and firing.

The reach had edges. Genuinely private clubs stood outside Title II, and housing stood outside the act altogether. The new employment commission could investigate complaints and try to settle them, but at first it could not order an employer to stop. The voting provisions were weak, leaving the government to sue county by county over registration abuses.

C. Freedom Summer

Freedom Summer, the campaign of 1964 that brought about a thousand mostly northern student volunteers to Mississippi to register Black voters and run Freedom Schools, joined those volunteers to local organizers. On June 21, three workers, James Chaney, a Black Mississippian, and Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner, white New Yorkers, disappeared after investigating a burned church near Philadelphia, Mississippi. Their bodies were found on August 4, buried in an earthen dam. All three had been murdered.

D. Atlantic City: The Freedom Democrats

The Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, the integrated political party organized in Mississippi in 1964 that challenged the seating of the state's all-white regular delegation at the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City, had been chosen in its own elections open to every resident. Its delegates argued that the regulars, chosen with Black Mississippians shut out, could not speak for the state. Fannie Lou Hamer, fired and driven from her home for trying to register, testified before the credentials committee on national television.

The convention offered two at-large seats while the regulars kept the rest. The Freedom Democrats refused, holding that a delegation elected with Black voters shut out had no claim to the seats. They went home without the victory, and their argument went home with the country.

E. Selma: The Vote Denied

The 1964 act had left registration with local officials, and Selma showed what they did. In Sheriff Jim Clark's Dallas County, Black residents were about half the population and almost none were registered. Registrars opened on few days, asked questions no white applicant faced, and failed applicants for any error. Organizers marched to the courthouse and filled the jails.

On February 18, a state trooper shot Jimmie Lee Jackson at a night march in nearby Marion, as Jackson stepped in front of his mother. Jackson died on February 26. The answer was a march from Selma to Montgomery, the state capital, to lay the vote question before the governor and the nation.

F. Bloody Sunday and the President Answers

On March 7, about six hundred marchers crossed the Edmund Pettus Bridge. Bloody Sunday, the attack of March 7, 1965 by Alabama state troopers and a sheriff's posse on voting rights marchers at the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, televised to the nation that evening, began when troopers ordered the marchers back, gave them a moment, and charged. Troopers beat the marchers with clubs and fired tear gas. John Lewis, the SNCC chairman, suffered a fractured skull.

Eight days later President Johnson addressed Congress and asked for a voting bill. He told Congress, "Their cause must be our cause too," and closed, "And we shall overcome." A federal court cleared the march, federal troops guarded the route, and about twenty-five thousand people reached Montgomery on March 25.

G. The Voting Rights Act and the Arithmetic

Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act of 1965, the federal law that suspended literacy tests, sent federal examiners to register voters in covered states, and required those states to clear any change in voting rules with the federal government first, on August 6, 1965. Where a test or device had stood on November 1, 1964 and fewer than half of voting-age residents had been registered or had voted, the tests were suspended at once.

The arithmetic followed quickly. About a quarter of a million new Black voters were registered by the end of 1965. Mississippi, where under seven percent of Black voting-age residents had been registered in 1964, reached about sixty percent within three years. The two acts stood side by side, one won inside the Senate, the other on a bridge. What law could not reach, in housing and in equality as a result, Johnson had named at Howard University.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State how the Civil Rights Act got through the Senate. Southern senators held the bill with a filibuster that ran for sixty working days. Ending it required cloture, a two-thirds vote to close debate. Republican votes supplied the margin, and on June 10, 1964 the Senate voted 71 to 29 for cloture, the first time in the century it had closed debate on a civil rights bill. The Senate passed the bill on June 19, the House approved it again, and President Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 on July 2. Full credit requires the filibuster, the sixty working days, the cloture vote, and the signing date.

2. Name three things the Civil Rights Act of 1964 did, and state two limits on its reach. Title II barred discrimination in hotels, restaurants, theaters, and other places of public accommodation serving the public. Title VI allowed the government to cut off federal funds to programs that discriminated. Title VII banned discrimination in employment on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, or national origin, covering hiring, firing, pay, and union membership, and the act created a commission to receive job complaints. Limits, any two: genuinely private clubs stood outside Title II; housing stood outside the act altogether; the employment commission at first could investigate and seek settlements but could not order an employer to stop; the voting provisions were weak and left the government to sue county by county.

3. Explain what the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party asked for, what it was offered, and why it refused. The Freedom Democrats, an integrated party built through the registration work of Freedom Summer and chosen in elections open to every resident, asked the Atlantic City convention to seat their delegation in place of the state's all-white regular delegation, arguing that a delegation chosen with Black Mississippians shut out could not speak for the state. Fannie Lou Hamer carried the argument to the credentials committee on national television. The convention offered two at-large seats chosen by the national party, with the regulars keeping the rest. The Freedom Democrats refused because the offer left in place the claim they had come to challenge, that a delegation elected with Black voters shut out could represent Mississippi.

4. Explain what registration looked like in Dallas County, and name the two events that carried the question to Congress. Black residents made up about half of Dallas County's population and almost none were registered. Registrars opened on few days, asked Black applicants questions no white applicant faced, and failed them for any error. The two events: the killing of Jimmie Lee Jackson, shot by a state trooper at a night march in Marion on February 18, 1965 and dead on February 26; and Bloody Sunday, March 7, 1965, when troopers and a sheriff's posse attacked the marchers on the Edmund Pettus Bridge on national television, fracturing John Lewis's skull. Full credit requires the county picture and both events with dates.

5. Use the registration figures and the tools of the Voting Rights Act to explain why the 1965 act reached voters the 1964 act could not, and name one thing both acts left unreached. The 1964 act left registration with local officials and gave the government case-by-case lawsuits, which Selma showed could not keep pace with registrars who failed applicants for any error. The 1965 act changed the machinery in covered places: it suspended the tests and devices at once where a test had stood on November 1, 1964 and fewer than half of voting-age residents had been registered or had voted, it sent federal examiners who could register voters directly, and it required covered states to clear any new voting rule with Washington before the rule could take effect. The figures measure the change: about a quarter of a million new Black voters registered by the end of 1965, and Mississippi moving from under seven percent Black registration in 1964 to about sixty percent within three years. Unreached, accept housing, wealth, or equality as a result rather than a right, the question Johnson named at Howard University. Answers that give the figures without the machinery, or the machinery without the figures, earn partial credit.
