The Laws Change: 1964 and 1965

The Civil Rights Movement · Lesson 6 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: The Civil Rights Movement, Lesson 6 of 8. Teach it directly after Lesson 5, which ended with the civil rights bill announced by President Kennedy on June 11, 1963 and still stalled in Congress at the end of that year, and with four girls killed in the bombing of the 16th Street Baptist Church on September 15, 1963. This lesson follows the bill into law: the Senate filibuster and the cloture vote of June 10, 1964, President Johnson signing the Civil Rights Act on July 2, 1964, Freedom Summer in Mississippi that same summer, the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party challenge at Atlantic City in August 1964, Selma and Bloody Sunday on March 7, 1965, President Johnson's address to Congress on March 15, 1965, and the Voting Rights Act signed on August 6, 1965. Lesson 7 follows with the movement turning north and inward, to de facto segregation, Watts, Black Power, and the limits the two great acts did not reach.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading "The Laws Change: 1964 and 1965" and the guided notes, which follow the deck in sections A to G: the fight in the Senate, the Civil Rights Act and what it reached, Freedom Summer, Atlantic City and the Freedom Democrats, Selma and the vote denied, Bloody Sunday and the president's answer, and the Voting Rights Act and its arithmetic. The activity is "The Statute File." The notebook page is "Notebook Page: The Laws Change: 1964 and 1965." The quick check is "Quick Check: The Marchers on Highway 80." The writing task is "Writing Task: What Could the Two Great Acts Actually Reach?"

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Students need Lessons 1 to 5: Jim Crow, disfranchisement, and segregation, Brown and massive resistance, the Montgomery bus boycott and nonviolent direct action, the sit-ins, SNCC and CORE, the Freedom Rides, the Birmingham campaign, the Letter from Birmingham Jail in paraphrase, the Children's Crusade, Kennedy's civil rights address of June 11, 1963, and the March on Washington. No prior knowledge of the filibuster, cloture, the titles of the 1964 act, Fannie Lou Hamer, Sheriff Jim Clark, or Jimmie Lee Jackson is assumed; each is taught in sequence. Note on photographs: the famous Bloody Sunday photographs are copyrighted press photographs and are not used in this lesson; Bloody Sunday is taught from the written record, and the Selma images used here show the completed march later in March 1965 and are captioned for what they show.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (analyze connections among events: a Senate filibuster, two statutes, a Mississippi summer, a convention challenge, and an attack on a bridge, in sequence), D2.His.9 (construct arguments using historical evidence: the writing task weighing what the two great acts could actually reach), D2.His.16 (assess options for individual and collective action: registration work, a party challenge, and a march to a state capital weighed as routes to federal law); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from the Senate fight to the registration arithmetic), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task argued from the Statute File records).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to trace how the movement turned protest into law in 1964 and 1965 and state what the two great acts could and could not reach: southern senators filibustered the civil rights bill for sixty working days before cloture carried 71 to 29 on June 10, 1964, and Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act on July 2; Title II opened public accommodations, Title VI allowed federal funds to be cut off to segregated programs, and Title VII banned employment discrimination, while housing, genuinely private clubs, and voter registration stood outside or weakly covered; Freedom Summer brought about a thousand volunteers to Mississippi, where James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner were murdered; the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party refused two at-large seats at Atlantic City after Fannie Lou Hamer testified on national television; in Dallas County, Alabama, Black residents were about half the population and almost none could register; troopers attacked about six hundred marchers at the Edmund Pettus Bridge on March 7, 1965, and the film ran on national television; Johnson addressed Congress on March 15 in the movement's own words; and the Voting Rights Act suspended tests in covered places, empowered federal examiners, and required preclearance, producing about 250,000 new Black voters by the end of 1965 and moving Mississippi from under 7 percent Black registration in 1964 to about 60 percent within three years.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes: opening, question, and map (slides 1 to 3) 5 minutes; the fight in the Senate (slides 4 to 6) 6 minutes; the Civil Rights Act and its edges (slides 7 to 9) 7 minutes; Freedom Summer (slides 10 to 12) 6 minutes; Atlantic City (slides 13 to 14) 5 minutes; Selma (slides 15 to 16) 5 minutes; Bloody Sunday and the answer (slides 17 to 19) 7 minutes; the Voting Rights Act and the arithmetic (slides 20 to 22) 6 minutes; glance, vocabulary, synthesis, and the turn to work (slides 23 to 27) 3 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes.

Slide 4 · Section A: The Fight in the Senate (divider)

On the slide: SECTION A · The Fight in the Senate · A bill sent in 1963, stalled at a president's death, and passed whole by his successor.

  • [Set the starting point precisely before the Senate fight begins.]
  • President Kennedy sent Congress a civil rights bill in June 1963, after Birmingham, and the bill was still stalled when he was assassinated that November.
  • His successor was the rarest kind of president for a Senate fight: Lyndon Johnson had run the Senate as majority leader, knew its rules, and knew its members.
  • Johnson told Congress that no memorial could honor Kennedy better than passing the bill whole, and he refused to trade away its strength for an easier vote.
  • The House of Representatives passed the bill in February 1964.
  • Everything then depended on the Senate, where a determined minority had killed civil rights bills for decades with a single weapon, extended debate.
  • Section A of the guided notes holds the sequence, so have students keep the dates separate: the bill, the assassination, the House vote, and then the long spring in the Senate.

Slide 5 · Sixty working days

On the slide: A · THE FIGHT IN THE SENATE · Sixty working days · ▪ President Kennedy's bill stalled, then President Johnson staked his presidency on it. ▪ The House passed the bill in February 1964. ▪ Southern senators filibustered for sixty working days. ▪ Cloture carried 71 to 29 on June 10. ▪ President Johnson signed the act on July 2, 1964.

  • [Teach the filibuster as a mechanism before teaching it as an episode.]
  • A minority that cannot win a vote can still prevent one, by holding the floor and refusing to let debate close.
  • The only answer the Senate's rules supply is cloture, a two-thirds vote to end debate, and on civil rights bills that vote had never been won in this century.
  • Republican votes supplied the margin in 1964, which students should note carefully, because the coalition that passed the act crossed party lines.
  • On June 10 the Senate voted 71 to 29 for cloture.
  • The bill passed the Senate on June 19, and Johnson signed it on July 2.
  • Have the three figures written in section A of the notes in order: sixty working days, 71 to 29, July 2.

Slide 6 · 60 working days of filibuster

On the slide: A · THE FIGHT IN THE SENATE · 60 · working days of filibuster against the civil rights bill · Ended on June 10, 1964, when the Senate voted 71 to 29 for cloture

  • 60
  • [Let the number stand before explaining it.]
  • Sixty working days means the Senate spent more than two months of its working life on a single bill, while the bill's opponents spoke in relays to keep debate alive.
  • The length is itself the evidence of what the act faced: not majority opposition it could outvote, but a procedural wall that required two thirds of the Senate to move.
  • When the cloture vote came, it carried 71 to 29, and the nine votes above the required two thirds came from the cross-party coalition Johnson and the bill's managers had assembled.
  • Ask: What did the filibuster's defeat change beyond this bill?
  • Answer: a weapon used against civil rights bills for decades had been beaten once, in public, and could be beaten again, which the voting bill's managers remembered the next year.

Slide 7 · Section B: The Civil Rights Act (divider)

On the slide: SECTION B · The Civil Rights Act: What It Reached · Johnson signs the act, East Room, July 2, 1964. · Photograph: President Johnson signing the Civil Rights Act on July 2, 1964.

  • Picture: A portrait crop of Cecil Stoughton's White House photograph of the signing on July 2, 1964, held by the Johnson Library. It is a work of the United States federal government and is public domain.
  • Johnson signed with dozens of pens, handing them to the legislators and movement leaders who had carried the bill, and Martin Luther King Jr. stood behind him.
  • Section B now slows down over what the signature actually enacted, because students often treat the act as a general statement against discrimination.
  • It was a set of specific legal tools, title by title, each with a defined field and a defined limit.
  • The guided notes divide the section the same way: the three titles that did the work, and then the edges of the reach.
  • Have students list the titles with their fields in section B before the limits are introduced, so the limits land as qualifications of real powers rather than as a general disappointment.

Slide 8 · Three titles, three fields

On the slide: B · THE CIVIL RIGHTS ACT · Three titles, three fields · ▪ Title II opened public accommodations. ▪ Hotels, restaurants, theaters, and lunch counters had to serve all customers. ▪ Title VI allowed federal funds to be cut off to segregated programs. ▪ Title VII banned employment discrimination. ▪ A commission was created to receive job complaints.

  • [Work the titles one at a time, with the field of each stated plainly.]
  • Title II barred discrimination in places of public accommodation, the hotels, restaurants, theaters, and lunch counters whose refusal had been the daily texture of segregation for travelers and for local people alike.
  • Title VI put the federal budget behind the Constitution: programs receiving federal money could lose it for discriminating, which gave the government a lever that did not require a lawsuit for every school or hospital.
  • Title VII carried the ban into employment, covering hiring and firing, and the act created a commission to receive job complaints.
  • Students should be able to match each title to its field without notes by the end of this slide, because the quick check and the unit test both ask the match in exactly this form.
  • Ask: Which title reached employment, and which reached programs receiving federal money?
  • Answer: Title VII reached employment, and Title VI reached programs receiving federal money.

Slide 9 · The edges of the reach

On the slide: B · THE CIVIL RIGHTS ACT · The edges of the reach · ▪ Genuinely private clubs stood outside Title II. ▪ Housing stood outside the act altogether. ▪ The jobs commission could investigate, not order. ▪ Voting provisions left registration to lawsuits. ▪ County by county enforcement was too slow to work.

  • [State the limits with the same precision as the powers, because this section sets up everything that follows.]
  • A genuinely private club stood outside Title II, a line the act's opponents had demanded and its supporters accepted.
  • Housing, where segregation did its deepest economic work, stood outside the act altogether and waited until 1968.
  • The new employment commission could investigate a complaint and try to settle it, but it could not order an employer to stop, a weakness Congress repaired only years later.
  • Above all for this lesson, the voting provisions were weak: the government could sue a county over registration abuses, one county at a time, while registrars kept failing applicants.
  • Hold that limit in view.
  • Mississippi and Selma are about to demonstrate it.

Slide 10 · Section C: Freedom Summer (divider)

On the slide: SECTION C · Freedom Summer · A thousand volunteers join Mississippi's registration work, in the summer the act was signed.

  • [Turn from the signing in Washington to the test of the law in Mississippi.]
  • The act was signed on July 2, 1964, and in Mississippi that same summer the movement tested what a law on paper could do against a registrar in a courthouse.
  • Freedom Summer brought about a thousand mostly northern student volunteers into the state to register Black voters and run Freedom Schools, joining local organizers who had been doing the work, at far greater risk and with far less attention, for years.
  • Students should hear the structure clearly: the volunteers were new, the work and its dangers were not.
  • Registration meant preparing residents to face tests designed to fail them, in counties where trying to register could cost a job, a home, or a life.
  • Section C is taught plainly and without heightening.
  • Three of the campaign's workers were murdered in its first days, and the lesson states that fact the way the reading states it.

Slide 11 · Registration at its cost

On the slide: C · FREEDOM SUMMER · Registration at its cost · ▪ Volunteers canvassed and taught in Freedom Schools. ▪ Residents prepared to face the registrar. ▪ On June 21 Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner disappeared. ▪ On August 4 their bodies were found. ▪ All three had been murdered.

  • [Name the three workers and say what happened in plain words.]
  • James Chaney, a Black Mississippian, and Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner, white New Yorkers, disappeared on June 21 after investigating a burned church near Philadelphia, Mississippi.
  • Their bodies were found on August 4, buried in an earthen dam.
  • All three had been murdered.
  • The search for them, carried on by federal agents and sailors for six weeks, found the graves of other victims as well, a fact students may raise and should hear answered honestly.
  • Then return to the work itself, because the campaign did not stop: registration canvasses and Freedom Schools continued through the summer.
  • The point is the contrast the summer exposed.
  • A federal statute now banned discrimination in public accommodations, and in Mississippi the older work of the movement, the voter by voter work, went on at its established price.

Slide 12 · 1,000 volunteers

On the slide: C · FREEDOM SUMMER · 1,000 · mostly northern student volunteers joined Mississippi registration work in 1964 · Three of the campaign's workers were murdered in its first weeks

  • 1,000
  • [Let the two facts on this slide stand together, because the campaign's meaning is in the pairing.]
  • About a thousand volunteers, most of them students, most of them northern, many of them white, came to Mississippi for one summer to do work that local Black organizers had been doing for years at the same risk.
  • The country watched that summer with an attention it had not given the years before, and students should be asked why: the volunteers carried the nation's own children into the danger, and the murders of Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner in June made the price undeniable on the national news.
  • The volunteers also built something that outlasted the summer, an organized Mississippi constituency with its own political party, which is where the lesson goes next, to Atlantic City.
  • Ask: Why did the summer of 1964 draw national attention that earlier registration work had not?
  • Answer: about a thousand mostly northern student volunteers joined the work, and three campaign workers were murdered in its first weeks.

Slide 13 · Section D: Atlantic City (divider)

On the slide: SECTION D · Atlantic City: The Freedom Democrats · Fannie Lou Hamer at the convention, August 1964. · Photograph: Fannie Lou Hamer at the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City in August 1964.

  • Picture: Fannie Lou Hamer at the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City in August 1964, photographed by Warren K. Leffler for U.S. News and World Report and held by the Library of Congress. The photograph is public domain, from a Library of Congress collection with no known restrictions on publication.
  • Hamer had been a sharecropper in Sunflower County, fired and driven from the plantation where she had lived for years after she tried to register to vote.
  • By August 1964 she was the best known voice of the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, the integrated party the registration work had built.
  • Section D follows the challenge that party carried to the convention: it asked the Democratic Party to seat its delegates in place of Mississippi's all-white regular delegation.
  • The question underneath the credentials fight was the lesson's question in party form: who speaks for a state whose elections shut out half its people?

Slide 14 · Two seats, refused

On the slide: D · ATLANTIC CITY · Two seats, refused · ▪ The Freedom Democrats held their own open elections. ▪ Fannie Lou Hamer testified before the credentials committee. ▪ Her testimony ran on national television. ▪ The convention offered two at-large seats. ▪ The Freedom Democrats refused the offer.

  • [Walk the sequence carefully, in order.]
  • The Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party had been chosen in its own elections, open to every resident, while the regular Mississippi delegation was chosen in a process that shut Black Mississippians out.
  • Hamer testified before the credentials committee and described, on national television, what registration cost in Mississippi, including the beating she had survived in a jail the year before.
  • The convention, pressed by a president who wanted no public fight, offered a compromise: two at-large seats, chosen by the national party, with the regulars keeping the rest.
  • The Freedom Democrats refused.
  • Their ground was the principle they had come with: a delegation elected with Black voters shut out had no claim to the seats at all.
  • They went home without the victory, and their argument went home with the country.

Slide 15 · Section E: Selma (divider)

On the slide: SECTION E · Selma: The Vote Denied · A county where Black residents were half the population, and almost none could vote.

  • [Turn from what the movement built to what the 1964 act had failed to move.]
  • The act's voting provisions left registration in the hands of local officials and gave the federal government lawsuits, county by county.
  • Selma, in Dallas County, Alabama, is the case the lesson studies, and its figures should be stated before its story.
  • Black residents made up about half the county's population.
  • Almost none of them were registered to vote.
  • That was not an accident of apathy, and students should be able to describe the machinery that produced it: registrars who opened on few days, asked Black applicants questions no white applicant faced, and failed them for any error.
  • Sheriff Jim Clark enforced the local order around the courthouse.
  • SNCC organizers, and then the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, built a campaign there through the winter of 1965, marching to the courthouse and filling the jails.

Slide 16 · The registrar's desk

On the slide: E · SELMA · The registrar's desk · ▪ Registrars opened on few days. ▪ Black applicants faced questions no white applicant faced. ▪ Any error failed the application. ▪ Courthouse marches continued through the winter of 1965. ▪ On February 18 Jimmie Lee Jackson was shot in Marion.

  • [End the section with the killing that turned a local campaign into a national question, stated plainly.]
  • On February 18, during a night march in nearby Marion, a state trooper shot Jimmie Lee Jackson, a twenty six year old demonstrator who had stepped in front of his mother.
  • Jackson died on February 26.
  • The Selma organizers answered with a plan: a march from Selma to Montgomery, the state capital, to lay the question of the vote before the governor and the nation.
  • Note what the campaign had already established before any march began.
  • The county's rolls were the product of a system, not of a few rude officials, and a system of that kind had survived the 1964 act's voting title intact.
  • That is why the demand that winter was not for another lawsuit.
  • It was for a new federal law with machinery of its own.

Slide 17 · Section F: Bloody Sunday and the President Answers (divider)

On the slide: SECTION F · Bloody Sunday and the President Answers · Marchers rest on the road to Montgomery, March 1965. · Photograph: marchers resting during the completed Selma to Montgomery march in March 1965.

  • Picture: Marchers resting on a wall during the completed Selma to Montgomery march later in March 1965, photographed by Peter Pettus and held by the Library of Congress. The photograph is public domain. One placard reads that they march together for the dignity and brotherhood of all men.
  • Tell students plainly why this lesson shows the completed march and not the attack of March 7: the famous Bloody Sunday photographs are copyrighted press photographs, and this unit does not use them.
  • The attack is taught from the record in words, which are exact enough.
  • On March 7, about six hundred marchers crossed the Edmund Pettus Bridge out of Selma.
  • Troopers ordered them back, gave them a moment, and charged.
  • Section F covers the attack, its broadcast, and the president's answer eight days later, in that order, because the order is the argument.

Slide 18 · March 7, 1965

On the slide: F · BLOODY SUNDAY · March 7, 1965 · ▪ About six hundred marchers crossed the bridge. ▪ Troopers ordered them back, then charged. ▪ Clubs and tear gas drove the march back into Selma. ▪ John Lewis suffered a fractured skull. ▪ The film ran on national television that evening.

  • [State the attack plainly and completely, the way the reading states it.]
  • The marchers were unarmed and marching two by two.
  • The troopers and Sheriff Clark's posse met them at the foot of the bridge, ordered them to disperse, and charged within about a minute, beating marchers with clubs, firing tear gas, and driving the column back across the bridge into Selma.
  • John Lewis, the SNCC chairman marching in the front line, suffered a fractured skull.
  • Then give the second fact its full weight: the attack was filmed, and the film ran on national television that evening, interrupting a major network broadcast, so that the country saw, in its own homes, what a registration system defended itself with.
  • The movement had argued for years that the registrar's desk rested on violence.
  • On March 7 the violence was televised.

Slide 19 · We shall overcome

On the slide: F · BLOODY SUNDAY AND THE ANSWER · We shall overcome · ▪ On March 15 President Johnson addressed Congress. ▪ Johnson said their cause must be our cause too. ▪ He closed with the movement's own words. ▪ A federal court cleared the march to Montgomery. ▪ On March 25 about 25,000 people reached the capital.

  • [Follow the attack with the presidential answer, eight days later.]
  • Eight days after Bloody Sunday, President Johnson addressed a joint session of Congress and asked for a voting bill, and he framed the cause in the movement's own language: their cause must be our cause too, and we shall overcome.
  • Students should register what that borrowing meant.
  • A president of the United States, a southerner, took the movement's anthem as the closing of a legislative address, because Selma had made the question a national one and he chose to answer it at full strength rather than bargain it down.
  • The practical sequence followed at once: a federal court cleared the Selma to Montgomery march, federal troops guarded the route, and about twenty five thousand people reached the capital on March 25.
  • The bill then moved through Congress in the spring and summer, with the Selma film behind it.

Slide 20 · Section G: The Voting Rights Act and the Arithmetic (divider)

On the slide: SECTION G · The Voting Rights Act and the Arithmetic · Johnson signs the act at the Capitol, August 6, 1965. · Photograph: President Johnson signing the Voting Rights Act at the Capitol on August 6, 1965.

  • Picture: A portrait crop of Yoichi Okamoto's photograph of President Johnson signing the Voting Rights Act at the Capitol on August 6, 1965, held by the Johnson Library, with Martin Luther King Jr. and other leaders looking on. The photograph is public domain.
  • Section G studies the act as machinery, because machinery is what separates it from everything the movement had won before.
  • The 1964 act had stated rights and left their enforcement to lawsuits.
  • The 1965 act, in the places where disfranchisement was systematic, removed the registrar's discretion in advance: tests suspended by the statute itself, federal examiners empowered to register voters directly, and a duty on covered states to clear any change in voting rules with Washington before the change could take effect.
  • Have students hold those three tools separate in section G of the notes.
  • The arithmetic that closes the lesson is unintelligible without them.

Slide 21 · Machinery, not only rights

On the slide: G · THE VOTING RIGHTS ACT · Machinery, not only rights · ▪ Section 2 barred any rule that denied the vote by race. ▪ The coverage formula used a test in 1964 and under half registered or voting. ▪ Tests and devices were suspended at once in covered places. ▪ Federal examiners registered voters directly. ▪ Voting changes had to be cleared with Washington first.

  • [Work the machinery in the act's own order.]
  • Section 2 states the standing ban, nationwide: no voting qualification, standard, practice, or procedure may be imposed to deny or abridge the right to vote on account of race or color.
  • The coverage formula then identifies where stronger medicine applies, places that had kept a test or device on November 1, 1964 and where fewer than half of voting age residents had been registered or had voted.
  • In those places the tests, literacy tests above all, were suspended by the act itself, without a lawsuit for each county.
  • Federal examiners could register voters over the local registrar's refusal.
  • And preclearance reversed the old sequence: instead of a discriminatory rule operating until a court struck it down years later, a covered state had to clear its change with Washington first.
  • Ask: How did preclearance reverse the old sequence?
  • Answer: a covered state had to clear a voting change with Washington before the change could take effect, instead of the change operating until a court struck it down.

Slide 22 · 250,000 new voters

On the slide: G · THE VOTING RIGHTS ACT · 250,000 · new Black voters registered by the end of 1965 · Mississippi moved from under 7 percent Black registration in 1964 to about 60 percent within three years

  • 250,000
  • [Read the two figures as a pair, the way the guided notes require.]
  • About a quarter of a million new Black voters were registered by the end of 1965, within five months of the signing.
  • Mississippi, the state of Freedom Summer and the Atlantic City challenge, where under seven percent of Black voting age residents had been registered in 1964, reached about sixty percent within three years.
  • Ask students to explain the speed with the machinery from the previous slide rather than with goodwill: the tests were already suspended, the examiners were already empowered, and a registrar who invented a new barrier found that the new rule itself needed clearance from Washington.
  • The figures are the lesson's verdict on the difference between a right announced and a right administered.
  • Keep both figures in section G, because the quick check asks for the Mississippi pair by name.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for the two statutes

On the slide: THE VOCABULARY · Words for the two statutes · Civil Rights Act of 1964 · the act that opened public life and employment · filibuster · extended debate used to block a vote · Title VII · the act's ban on employment discrimination · Freedom Summer · Mississippi registration work, summer 1964

  • These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.
  • Civil Rights Act of 1964: the federal law that banned discrimination in public accommodations and employment and allowed the government to cut off funds to segregated programs, the movement's first great legislative victory
  • filibuster: the Senate practice of extending debate to block a bill from coming to a vote, broken on the civil rights bill of 1964 when the Senate voted for cloture after more than two months of debate
  • Title VII: the section of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 that banned discrimination in employment on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, or national origin
  • Freedom Summer: the campaign of 1964 that brought about a thousand mostly northern student volunteers to Mississippi to register Black voters and run Freedom Schools

  • Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly.
  • Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party: the integrated political party organized in Mississippi in 1964 that challenged the seating of the state's all-white regular delegation at the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City
  • Bloody Sunday: the attack of March 7, 1965 by Alabama state troopers and a sheriff's posse on voting rights marchers at the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, televised to the nation that evening
  • Voting Rights Act of 1965: the federal law that suspended literacy tests, sent federal examiners to register voters in covered states, and required those states to clear any change in voting rules with the federal government first
  • Note the order of this set.
  • The first term names the constituency the registration work built, the second names the attack that moved the presidency, and the third names the machinery the movement's decade of work finally obtained.

Slide 26 · Judging the reach of the law

On the slide: SYNTHESIS · Judging the reach of the law · ▪ Pressure outside and mastery inside made the laws. ▪ The reach covered public life, the job, and the ballot. ▪ The limits included housing, wealth, and equality as a result. ▪ Judging the laws requires a weighing, not a narration.

  • [Close the teaching here, before the work slide, and model the judgment the writing task will ask for.]
  • The movement turned protest into law by working both sides of the Capitol wall at once, and the proof is in the pair of years: a filibuster broken in June 1964 and a bridge in March 1965 each produced a statute within months.
  • Against that achievement, state the limits with equal clarity, because Johnson stated them himself at Howard University in June 1965, before the second act was even signed: freedom is not enough, and equality as a right is not yet equality as a result.
  • The strongest student writing will hold both halves in one argument, measuring the quarter of a million new voters against the housing and wealth the acts never touched, and will say which measurement should govern the verdict and why.
