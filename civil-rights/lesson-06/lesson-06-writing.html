Writing Task: What Could the Two Great Acts Actually Reach?

The prompt. Answer the question in a developed essay of about 300 words: How did the movement turn protest into law, and what could the two great acts actually reach? Argue the sequence before you judge the reach. A strong answer sets out the making of the laws first, the filibuster broken in the Senate and the pressure built in Mississippi and at Selma, then the reach of each act, public accommodations, employment, federal funds, and the registrar's desk, and then the limits, the places and subjects the acts left outside, closing with President Johnson's Howard University standard. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, drawn from both halves of the bank, and explain how each piece supports your judgment.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the Statute File. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The Civil Rights Act passed the House in February 1964 and was held in the Senate by a filibuster that ran for sixty working days. On June 10, 1964 the Senate voted 71 to 29 for cloture, the first time in the century it had closed debate on a civil rights bill, and Johnson signed the act on July 2.

2. Title II of the act gave all persons the right to the full and equal enjoyment of any place of public accommodation, without discrimination or segregation on the ground of race, color, religion, or national origin. The same title left a private club or other establishment not in fact open to the public outside its reach. (Audited TPSP record civil-rights-act-1964.)

3. Title VII made it an unlawful employment practice for an employer to refuse to hire or to discharge any individual, or otherwise to discriminate, because of race, color, religion, sex, or national origin, and bound labor organizations the same way. (Audited TPSP record civil-rights-act-title-vii-1964.)

4. Freedom Summer brought about a thousand mostly northern student volunteers to Mississippi in 1964 to register Black voters and run Freedom Schools. Three workers, James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner, were murdered that June. In August the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party carried the registration argument to Atlantic City, where Fannie Lou Hamer testified on national television and the party refused an offer of two at-large seats.

5. In Dallas County, Alabama, Black residents were about half the population and almost none were registered, because registrars failed applicants for any error. On March 7, 1965, state troopers and a sheriff's posse attacked voting rights marchers on the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, and the attack was televised to the nation that evening. Eight days later President Johnson asked Congress for a voting bill, telling Congress, "Their cause must be our cause too," and closing, "And we shall overcome." (Audited TPSP record lbj-we-shall-overcome-1965.)

6. The Voting Rights Act of August 6, 1965 banned any voting qualification used to deny or abridge the vote on account of race or color, suspended tests and devices in covered states and counties, sent federal examiners to register voters, and required covered states to clear any change in voting rules with the federal government first. (Audited TPSP record voting-rights-act-1965.)

7. The registration figures moved at once: about a quarter of a million new Black voters by the end of 1965, and Mississippi from under seven percent Black registration in 1964 to about sixty percent within three years. At Howard University on June 4, 1965, Johnson had set the standard the figures could not meet by themselves: "We seek not just freedom but opportunity. We seek not just legal equity but human ability, not just equality as a right and a theory, but equality as a fact, and equality as a result." (Audited TPSP record lbj-howard-university-address-1965.)

Plan Before You Write

My judgment (one sentence that answers both halves of the question directly):

My evidence picks (bank numbers, at least one from items 1 to 4 and at least one from items 5 to 7) and what each one proves:

The strongest evidence against my judgment, and how I will answer it:

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your judgment, present the making of the two laws, present the reach and the limits of each, answer the strongest evidence against your judgment in one or two sentences, and end by restating what the record shows.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment that answers both halves of the prompt directly, how protest became law and what the acts could reach, rather than narrating one half. (Blueprint 11 and Blueprint 12)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, with at least one from each half. Bank items 1 through 4 carry the making of the 1964 act and its reach and limits: the filibuster and the cloture vote, Title II with its private club limit, Title VII, and the Mississippi pressure built the same summer, including the murders and the Atlantic City refusal. (Blueprint 11) Bank items 5 through 7 carry Selma and the 1965 act: the Dallas County rolls, Bloody Sunday and the presidential address, the act's machinery, the registration figures, and the Howard standard. (Blueprint 12)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the judgment. The strongest essays name the difference between a right stated and a machinery built: the 1964 act stated rights in public life and employment and left registration to lawsuits, while the 1965 act suspended the tests, sent examiners, and required clearance before a rule could change.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest evidence on the other side, answered from the record rather than dismissed. An essay stressing the acts' reach must answer the private club limit, the missing housing title, and the Howard standard. An essay stressing the limits must answer the quarter of a million new voters and Mississippi's move toward sixty percent.

Model opening for teacher reference. The movement turned protest into law by working both sides of the Capitol wall at once, registering voters in Mississippi and marching in Selma while its allies counted votes in the Senate, and the two acts reached as far as law with machinery behind it can reach. A full model continues in this pattern, carries the filibuster and Atlantic City beside Title II and Title VII, carries Selma beside the suspension of the tests and the examiners, answers the strongest opposing evidence, and closes on what the record shows.

Common weak answers. Laws only (the filibuster, the titles, the machinery) describes how the acts read without the protest that produced them. Protest only (Freedom Summer, Atlantic City, Selma) makes the same error in reverse. A judgment that treats the 1965 figures as proof that nothing was left unreached, or that treats the Howard standard as proof that nothing was reached, earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a reach measured against both the figures and the standard.
