Guided Notes: The Laws Change: 1964 and 1965

Lesson question: How did the movement turn protest into law, and what could the two great acts actually reach?

Unit question: How did the civil rights movement change American law and American life, and what did it leave unfinished?

Key Terms

Civil Rights Act of 1964: the federal law that banned discrimination in public accommodations and employment and allowed the government to cut off funds to segregated programs, the movement's first great legislative victory

filibuster: the Senate practice of extending debate to block a bill from coming to a vote, broken on the civil rights bill of 1964 when the Senate voted for cloture after more than two months of debate

Title VII: the section of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 that banned discrimination in employment on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, or national origin

Freedom Summer: the campaign of 1964 that brought about a thousand mostly northern student volunteers to Mississippi to register Black voters and run Freedom Schools

Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party: the integrated political party organized in Mississippi in 1964 that challenged the seating of the state's all-white regular delegation at the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City

Bloody Sunday: the attack of March 7, 1965 by Alabama state troopers and a sheriff's posse on voting rights marchers at the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, televised to the nation that evening

Voting Rights Act of 1965: the federal law that suspended literacy tests, sent federal examiners to register voters in covered states, and required those states to clear any change in voting rules with the federal government first

Summary table. The Senate fight: Kennedy's stalled bill passed the House in February 1964, southern senators held it with a sixty-working-day filibuster, and the Senate voted 71 to 29 for cloture on June 10, closing debate on a civil rights bill for the first time in the century; Johnson signed the act on July 2. The 1964 act: Title II opened public accommodations, Title VI put federal money behind the ban, and Title VII banned employment discrimination, while private clubs and housing stood outside the act and its voting provisions left registration with local officials. Freedom Summer and Atlantic City: about a thousand volunteers joined Mississippi registration work, Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner were murdered, and the Freedom Democrats carried the registration argument to Atlantic City, where Hamer testified on television and the party refused two at-large seats. Selma: in Dallas County, Black residents were about half the population and almost none were registered; Jimmie Lee Jackson was killed in February 1965, and Bloody Sunday on March 7 put the attack on the Edmund Pettus Bridge on national television. The 1965 act: signed August 6, it suspended tests where they had stood in 1964 with under half registered or voting, sent federal examiners, and required covered states to clear voting changes first; about a quarter of a million new Black voters registered by the end of 1965.

Exit ticket 1. The filibuster ran for sixty working days and ended when the Senate voted for cloture on June 10, 1964, by 71 to 29, with Republican votes supplying the margin. It was a first because the Senate had never before in the century voted to close debate on a civil rights bill.

Exit ticket 2. About a quarter of a million new Black voters were registered by the end of 1965. Mississippi moved from under seven percent Black registration in 1964 to about sixty percent within three years.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that pairs a reach fact with a limit fact. The strongest answers run from protest outside the Capitol and mastery inside it, the filibuster broken and the bridge at Selma, to the two acts opening public accommodations, employment, and the registrar's desk, and close on what stood outside the acts, housing and equality as a result, the question Johnson named at Howard University.
