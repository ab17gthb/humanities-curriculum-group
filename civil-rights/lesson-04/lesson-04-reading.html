Sit-Ins and Freedom Rides: The Students Take the Lead

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Greensboro: Four Students at a Lunch Counter

On February 1, 1960, four freshmen at North Carolina Agricultural and Technical College bought small items, kept their receipts, and sat down at the lunch counter reserved for white customers. Joseph McNeil, Ezell Blair Jr., Franklin McCain, and David Richmond asked to be served, were refused, and stayed in their seats until the store closed. They had decided, after months of dormitory talk, to close that distance themselves.

They returned with more students each day, and within a week hundreds were sitting in across Greensboro. This was the Greensboro sit-in, and it sparked sit-ins in cities across the South. Woolworth served its first Black customers at that counter on July 25, 1960.

B. A Tactic Any Student Could Copy

The tactic ran because it was simple. A sit-in, a protest in which demonstrators seat themselves at a segregated facility and refuse to leave until they are served on equal terms or arrested, required no budget, no headquarters, and no one's permission. Students sat in at lunch counters, libraries, theaters, and pools, By the end of 1960 they had sat in in more than a hundred southern cities.

C. Nashville: Training, Jails, and a Mayor's Answer

Its students, from Fisk and three other Black colleges and medical schools, had trained since the fall of 1959 in workshops run by James Lawson. Lawson drilled them in how to meet hatred without returning it. Beaten and arrested on February 27, they filled the jails rather than posting bail.

On April 19 a bomb wrecked the home of their lawyer, Z. Alexander Looby, and thousands marched to the courthouse. There Diane Nash, a Fisk student of twenty-one, asked Mayor Ben West whether he believed it was wrong to discriminate against a person by the color of his skin. West answered that it was wrong, and agreed the counters should open. On May 10, 1960, Nashville became the first major southern city to desegregate its lunch counters.

D. The Students Organize: SNCC

Ella Baker, the acting executive director of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, called the sit-in leaders to Shaw University in Raleigh in April 1960 and urged them to build their own organization, not a youth wing of an older one. The result was the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, the organization founded by student activists in April 1960, known as SNCC, which organized sit-ins, Freedom Rides, and voter registration drives in the deepest parts of the South. Baker's rule stayed with it: local people lead their own fights.

E. The Freedom Rides: Testing Boynton

In December 1960 the Supreme Court decided Boynton v. Virginia. Bruce Boynton, a Black law student, had been convicted of trespass for sitting in the white section of a Richmond bus terminal restaurant. The Court reversed the conviction, holding that when a bus carrier makes terminal and restaurant facilities available to its interstate passengers ..., the terminal and restaurant ... must perform these services without discriminations prohibited by the Act. The Congress of Racial Equality, the civil rights organization founded in 1942, known as CORE, which pioneered nonviolent direct action and organized the Freedom Rides of 1961, set out to test the decision.

On May 4, 1961, thirteen riders, seven Black and six white, left Washington on Greyhound and Trailways buses. These were the Freedom Rides, the bus journeys of 1961 in which interracial groups of activists rode through the South to test court orders desegregating interstate bus travel and bus terminals. At each stop the riders used the waiting rooms and lunch counters the law had opened.

F. Fire at Anniston, Prison at Parchman

On May 14, outside Anniston, Alabama, a mob attacked the Greyhound bus, threw a firebomb through a window, and held the doors shut while the bus burned. The riders escaped and were beaten as they came out. The Trailways riders were beaten the same day in Birmingham, where police had been arranged to be absent. A week later a mob beat riders at the Montgomery bus station, and the Kennedy administration, which had asked for a cooling-off period, sent federal marshals.

Nashville students organized by Diane Nash replaced the injured and rode on to Jackson, Mississippi, where riders were arrested for using the white waiting room and sent to Parchman prison when they refused to pay their fines. Through the summer, more than four hundred riders rode into the South. On May 29, Attorney General Robert Kennedy asked the Interstate Commerce Commission to issue enforcing regulations.

G. The Federal Answer and the Albany Limit

On September 22, 1961, the Commission acted. Its regulations, effective November 1, barred carriers from seating passengers by race and required every ticket to carry a printed notice that seating in interstate commerce is without regard to race, color, creed, or national origin.

Albany, Georgia, taught the other half of the lesson, where SNCC workers arrived in the fall of 1961 and the Albany Movement filled the jails with marchers that December. Police Chief Laurie Pritchett had studied the movement's method. His officers arrested more than a thousand demonstrators without beating them before cameras, and he borrowed jail space from surrounding counties, so no outrage forced Washington to act. King came, was jailed, and was released against his will when someone paid his fine. He left in August 1962 without an agreement.

The arithmetic was plain. Protest that created a crisis the federal government could not ignore had opened lunch counters and bus terminals. Protest that a disciplined opponent absorbed quietly had not. De jure segregation stood weaker in 1962 than in February 1960, and it had been weakened chiefly by people under twenty-five.
