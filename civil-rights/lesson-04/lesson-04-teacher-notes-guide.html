Sit-Ins and Freedom Rides

The Civil Rights Movement · Lesson 4 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: The Civil Rights Movement, Lesson 4 of 8. Teach it directly after Lesson 3, which taught the boycott, organization, and witness methods of Montgomery and the federal answer at Little Rock. This lesson follows students a decade younger than the movement's leaders as they change its pace, tactics, and demands: the Greensboro sit-in of February 1, 1960, sit-ins in more than a hundred cities, Nashville's trained victory, the founding of SNCC in April 1960, the Freedom Rides testing Boynton v. Virginia, the violence at Anniston and imprisonment at Parchman, the Interstate Commerce Commission regulations of September 22, 1961, and the Albany campaign, where Police Chief Laurie Pritchett absorbed protest without a crisis. Lesson 5 follows the arithmetic learned here into Birmingham, where King chose the city, the opponent, and the ground in advance.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “Sit-Ins and Freedom Rides: The Students Take the Lead” and the guided notes, which follow the deck in sections A to G: Greensboro, a tactic any student could copy, Nashville, the students organize, the Freedom Rides, fire at Anniston and prison at Parchman, and the federal answer and the Albany limit. The activity is “The Crisis File: Sit-Ins and Freedom Rides.” The notebook page is “Notebook Page: Sit-Ins and Freedom Rides.” The quick check is “Quick Check: The Counter and the Bus.” The writing task is “Writing Task: The Students' Arithmetic.”

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Students need Lessons 1 to 3: Jim Crow, the NAACP and the SCLC, Brown, the Montgomery bus boycott and nonviolent direct action, and the Little Rock federal enforcement precedent. No prior knowledge of the Greensboro four, Diane Nash, James Lawson, Ella Baker, SNCC, CORE, Boynton v. Virginia, Parchman, the ICC regulations, or Laurie Pritchett is assumed; each is taught in sequence.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (analyze connections among events: a dormitory decision, a copyable tactic, trained local campaigns, a new student organization, a legal test, mob violence, federal regulation, and a disciplined opponent, in sequence), D2.His.9 (construct arguments using historical evidence: the writing task arguing the rule the victories and Albany together prove), D2.His.16 (assess options for individual and collective action: sit-in, jail filling, interstate test rides, and federal petition weighed against the Albany limit); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from Greensboro to the ICC to Albany), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task argued from the Crisis File records).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain how students changed the movement's pace, tactics, and demands: four North Carolina A&T freshmen sat at a Woolworth counter on February 1, 1960 and returned daily until the counter served Black customers on July 25; the tactic spread to more than a hundred cities; Nashville students trained by James Lawson filled the jails and won a mayor's public answer and open counters on May 10, 1960; Ella Baker helped students found SNCC at Shaw University in April 1960; thirteen Freedom Riders left Washington on May 4, 1961 to test Boynton, met a firebombed bus at Anniston and beatings in Birmingham and Montgomery, were replaced by Nashville students, filled Parchman after Jackson arrests, and grew to more than four hundred riders until the ICC desegregated interstate terminals effective November 1, 1961; and in Albany, Chief Laurie Pritchett's polite mass arrests and borrowed jail space denied the movement a crisis, so King left in August 1962 without an agreement.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes: opening, question, and map (slides 1 to 3) 5 minutes; Greensboro (slides 4 to 6) 6 minutes; the spread (slides 7 to 8) 4 minutes; Nashville (slides 9 to 11) 8 minutes; SNCC (slides 12 to 13) 4 minutes; the Freedom Rides begin (slides 14 to 16) 6 minutes; the ordeal (slides 17 to 20) 8 minutes; the federal answer and Albany (slides 21 to 23) 6 minutes; glance, synthesis, vocabulary, and the turn to work (slides 24 to 28) 3 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes.

  • The segregated system in this photograph was twenty years old when this lesson begins, and it still governed where a traveler could wait, eat, or use a restroom across the South.
  • Between February 1960 and the fall of 1961, students barely out of high school attacked that system at lunch counters and in bus terminals.
  • They forced the federal government to enforce rights it had already declared.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: THE QUESTION FOR TODAY · How did students a decade younger than the movement's leaders change its pace, its tactics, and its demands? · Unit question: How did the civil rights movement change American law and American life, and what did it leave unfinished?

  • [Read the question slowly and take its three verbs one at a time.]
  • Pace: before February 1960 the movement moved at the speed of court cases and national organizations; after it, any student in any town could open a front in a week.
  • Tactics: the students replaced the lawsuit and the one day boycott with the sustained occupation of segregated space, the filled jail, and the interstate bus ridden straight into the hardest counties.
  • Demands: they stopped asking for gradual improvement and demanded service now, on equal terms, at the counter in front of them.
  • Keep the ages in front of the class all hour.
  • The Greensboro four were freshmen.
  • Diane Nash was twenty one.
  • Martin Luther King Jr. was thirty one in 1960, a full decade older, and in this lesson the older leaders repeatedly follow where students have already gone, most plainly when King comes to Albany after SNCC has been there for months.
  • Ask: What three things does the lesson question say the students changed?
  • Answer: its pace, its tactics, and its demands.

Slide 4 · Section A: Greensboro (divider)

On the slide: SECTION A · Greensboro: Four Students at a Lunch Counter · February 1, 1960. Four freshmen, one store, and a refusal that would not move.

  • [Set the scene with care, because students often imagine the sit-in as a spontaneous gesture by students who had not thought it through.]
  • The four freshmen at North Carolina Agricultural and Technical College had talked for months in their dormitory rooms.
  • Joseph McNeil had been refused service at a bus terminal lunch counter while traveling back from military reserve duty, and the four had read and argued about the Constitution and about the movement's slow pace.
  • They chose Woolworth because a national chain was vulnerable in a way a local store was not: its northern stores served Black customers, and its name was known everywhere.
  • They also bought small items first and kept their receipts, proof that the store was glad to take their money everywhere except at the counter.
  • The protest was planned down to that detail, and students should register the planning, because the lesson's argument about students changing the movement begins with how deliberate they were.
  • Note: No public domain photograph of the February 1960 Greensboro sit-in itself was verified, so none is used; see the lesson's image sources.

Slide 5 · They stayed until the store closed

On the slide: A · GREENSBORO · They stayed until the store closed · ▪ Four freshmen sat at the Woolworth counter on February 1, 1960. ▪ Refused service, they studied and stayed until closing. ▪ They returned each day with more students. ▪ Within a week, hundreds were sitting in across Greensboro. ▪ On July 25, 1960, the counter served Black customers.

  • [Walk the first day slowly.]
  • The four bought school supplies and other small items, kept their receipts, and then sat at the lunch counter and asked for coffee and food.
  • The waitress refused, an older Black employee told them they were hurting their own people, and the manager watched.
  • The four stayed until the store closed, reading and studying, and they told a reporter they would return.
  • Notice what did not happen on the first day: no arrest, no violence, no federal official, no national leader.
  • The power of the event was its repeatability.
  • Each day the crowd of students grew, the newspapers photographed rows of well dressed students at the counter, and the store faced a choice it could not escape: serve them, close the counter, or have them removed by force on camera.
  • Woolworth held out for nearly six months and then, on July 25, 1960, quietly served its first Black customers at that counter, four store employees in their work clothes among them in some accounts.
  • The students had won with a tactic that cost almost nothing.

Slide 6 · 4 freshmen

On the slide: A · GREENSBORO · 4 · freshmen began the Greensboro sit-in · February 1, 1960 · Joseph McNeil, Ezell Blair Jr., Franklin McCain, and David Richmond, all students at North Carolina A&T

  • 4
  • [Let the smallness of the number do the teaching.]
  • Four students, none of them officers of any national organization, none older than eighteen or nineteen, opened the campaign that reshaped the movement.
  • Set the four beside the institutions of the earlier lessons: the NAACP, founded in 1909, with its lawyers and its decades of litigation; the churches and colleges of Lesson 1; the Supreme Court itself, whose Brown decision was six years old.
  • Those institutions had built the ground, and students should not be taught to dismiss them.
  • But the decision to sit down on February 1, 1960 was taken in a dormitory, not a boardroom, and it was taken by freshmen who had concluded that the existing pace was too slow.
  • Ask: What two facts should the class hold together for the rest of the lesson?
  • Answer: the students stood on ground the older movement had built, and they moved faster than the older movement had planned to move.
  • That tension runs through SNCC's founding in section D and through Albany in section G.

Slide 7 · Section B: A tactic any student could copy (divider)

On the slide: SECTION B · A Tactic Any Student Could Copy · No budget, no headquarters, no permission. By the end of 1960, sit-ins in more than a hundred cities.

  • [Explain the spread through the tactic's design.]
  • A lunch counter sit-in needed nothing that could be confiscated, funded, or enjoined in advance.
  • It needed students, a segregated counter, and the discipline to stay seated.
  • The targets stood in every southern town, and the chain stores that ran many of them answered to national offices in New York, which northern supporters could picket in sympathy.
  • Television and the wire services did the rest: a photograph of students in coats and ties being shouted at over a counter carried the story further in a day than a pamphlet could in a year.
  • Students should also see the limit built into the design.
  • A sit-in could open a lunch counter.
  • It could not by itself register a voter, desegregate a school system, or employ anyone, and the movement's later turns toward voter registration and federal legislation begin in that limit.
  • Ask the class to name what the tactic required of the people who used it, because Nashville answers that question next.

Slide 8 · More than a hundred cities by the end of 1960

On the slide: B · THE TACTIC SPREADS · More than a hundred cities by the end of 1960 · ▪ Students sat in at counters, libraries, theaters, and pools. ▪ They dressed in their best clothes and carried their books. ▪ Insults and blows were absorbed, never returned. ▪ Northern supporters picketed the same chain stores. ▪ Local campaigns were run by local students, city by city.

  • [Teach the spread as a series of local decisions rather than a wave that carried people along.]
  • In each city, students at a Black college or high school chose their target, trained as well as they could, and sat in, knowing the local record of arrests and violence.
  • The discipline was the message.
  • Students wore their best clothes, brought books and homework, and refused to answer insults or blows, so that the only disorder in the photograph was the disorder directed at them.
  • The economic pressure was real but modest at any single store; the political pressure was the larger weapon, because each sit-in forced a city to declare, in public, whether it would defend a lunch counter's customs with its police.
  • By the end of 1960 students had sat in in more than a hundred southern cities.
  • Some counters opened quickly, some only after months, and some not at all, which is why the movement needed the organization and the federal strategy built later in this lesson.
  • The tactic spread because it was copyable; it won where preparation and pressure met, which Nashville shows in full.

Slide 9 · Section C: Nashville (divider)

On the slide: SECTION C · Nashville: Training, Jails, and a Mayor's Answer · Diane Nash and the Reverend Kelly Miller Smith, Nashville, April 9, 1960. · Photograph: Diane Nash and the Reverend Kelly Miller Smith in Nashville on April 9, 1960.

  • Picture: Diane Nash and the Reverend Kelly Miller Smith in Nashville on April 9, 1960, reviewing a statement that answered the city's biracial committee, photographed by Gerald Holly for The Tennessean and held to be public domain on its Wikimedia Commons file page because the copyright was not renewed.
  • [Frame section C as the lesson's study in preparation.]
  • Nashville's students came from Fisk University, Tennessee State, Meharry Medical College, and American Baptist College, and they had been training since the fall of 1959, months before Greensboro, in workshops run by the Reverend James Lawson.
  • When their moment came, they were the best prepared sit-in students in the South, and their campaign produced the movement's first major city victory.
  • Nash, a Fisk student of twenty one, became its public voice, and her exchange with the mayor on the courthouse steps is the section's center.
  • Students should watch for what training purchased: the ability to keep discipline after arrests, after a bombing, and at the moment of negotiation itself.

Slide 10 · Trained before they sat down

On the slide: C · NASHVILLE · Trained before they sat down · ▪ James Lawson's workshops ran from the fall of 1959. ▪ Students practiced absorbing insults and blows. ▪ The rule was to meet hatred without returning it. ▪ On February 27, 1960, they were beaten, arrested, and jailed. ▪ They filled the jails rather than post bail.

  • [Describe the workshops concretely, because students romanticize nonviolence as temperament when Nashville treated it as a skill.]
  • In Lawson's sessions, students sat on stools while their classmates shouted insults, blew smoke, shoved them, and pulled them to the floor, and the students practiced protecting their heads, keeping their hands down, and helping each other back onto the stools.
  • Lawson, who had studied nonviolence in India, taught the theology and the strategy together: the demonstrator's task was to expose the violence inside segregation by refusing to add any of his own.
  • The test came on February 27, 1960, when Nashville sit-in students were attacked at the counters and then arrested, the attackers left alone.
  • The students refused bail and filled the jails, a choice that raised the city's cost of holding them and kept the campaign's initiative with the students.
  • Expulsion threats from the colleges followed and were beaten back.
  • By April the students, not the city, were setting the terms of the confrontation.

Slide 11 · A mayor's answer on the courthouse steps

On the slide: C · NASHVILLE · A mayor's answer on the courthouse steps · ▪ On April 19, 1960, the Looby home was bombed. ▪ Thousands marched in silence to the courthouse. ▪ Diane Nash questioned Mayor Ben West directly. ▪ West answered that discrimination by skin color was wrong. ▪ On May 10, 1960, Nashville opened its lunch counters.

  • [Narrate April 19 in order.]
  • In the early morning a bomb wrecked the home of Z. Alexander Looby, the students' lawyer and a city councilman.
  • No one was killed, and by midday thousands of students and residents marched to the courthouse in silence, a discipline the whole city could read.
  • On the steps, Diane Nash confronted Mayor Ben West.
  • She asked whether he believed it was wrong to discriminate against a person by the color of his skin.
  • West answered that it was wrong, and when she pressed him on the counters themselves, he agreed they should be opened to everyone.
  • The exchange took minutes, and it worked because the march behind her made evasion more costly than concession.
  • Three weeks of negotiation followed, and on May 10, 1960, Nashville became the first major southern city to desegregate its lunch counters.
  • Mark the shape of the victory: a local crisis, aimed at a local power, answered by that power.
  • No federal official appears in this story, which becomes an important contrast when the riders need Washington in section F.

Slide 12 · Section D: The students organize (divider)

On the slide: SECTION D · The Students Organize: SNCC · Shaw University, April 1960. Ella Baker tells the students to build their own organization.

  • [Keep this section short in telling and large in weight.]
  • By April 1960 the sit-ins had produced something the movement had not planned: a generation of tested local leaders, most of them under twenty two, who owed their authority to arrests they had served rather than to offices they held.
  • The question was what structure that generation would take.
  • The Southern Christian Leadership Conference, King's organization, called the conference at Shaw University in Raleigh that Easter weekend, and a youth wing of the SCLC was the expected outcome.
  • Ella Baker, the SCLC's acting executive director and the conference's organizer, argued against it.
  • Baker had spent decades organizing for the NAACP, and she believed movements were built by developing local leaders, not by gathering followers around a national voice.
  • She urged the students to found an organization of their own.
  • They did, and the habits Baker taught it mark every campaign in the rest of this unit, including the voter registration work of Lesson 6.

Slide 13 · An organization of their own

On the slide: D · SNCC · An organization of their own · ▪ They met at Shaw University in Raleigh in April 1960. ▪ Ella Baker urged independence from the older groups. ▪ The Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee was founded. ▪ Baker's rule was that local people lead their own fights. ▪ SNCC went to the deepest parts of the South, and stayed.

  • [Give the founding facts and then the operating habits, because the test asks about both.]
  • The conference met at Shaw University in Raleigh, North Carolina, in April 1960, drawing student leaders from the sit-in cities.
  • The organization they founded took the name Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, known everywhere as SNCC.
  • Its structure was deliberately loose: field secretaries went into a county, found the people already respected there, and built the campaign around them, rather than importing a program.
  • Baker's rule, that local people lead their own fights, was both a democratic conviction and a survival strategy, since a local leader could not be transferred away or fired by a distant board.
  • SNCC workers lived on subsistence pay, stayed in the hardest counties after the cameras and the national staff left, and accepted risks the older organizations often judged too high.
  • In this lesson SNCC appears first as a child of the sit-ins, then as the reinforcements of the Freedom Rides, and finally as the organizer who reached Albany first.
  • Track those appearances; they are the organization's biography in one lesson.

Slide 14 · Section E: The Freedom Rides (divider)

On the slide: SECTION E · The Freedom Rides: Testing Boynton · James Farmer of CORE, photographed by Jackson, Mississippi police after his arrest, May 24, 1961. · Photograph: the Jackson, Mississippi police mug shot of James Farmer, May 24, 1961.

  • Picture: The Jackson, Mississippi police mug shot of James Farmer, taken on May 24, 1961, after his arrest as a Freedom Rider, and marked public domain on its Wikimedia Commons file page.
  • [Set up the Freedom Rides as a legal test before it becomes a physical ordeal.]
  • Farmer was the national director of the Congress of Racial Equality, and at forty one he was a generation older than the students of this lesson, a founder of CORE in 1942 who had pioneered the nonviolent direct action the students now practiced.
  • In December 1960 the Supreme Court had decided Boynton v. Virginia, reversing the trespass conviction of a Black law student who had refused to leave the white section of a bus terminal restaurant.
  • The right existed in the United States Reports.
  • What did not exist was southern obedience to it, and CORE designed the rides to find out whether a declared right would be honored when ordinary travelers claimed it in person.

Slide 15 · A right declared, and unenforced

On the slide: E · BOYNTON · A right declared, and unenforced · ▪ Boynton v. Virginia was decided in December 1960. ▪ A law student had been convicted of trespass in a terminal restaurant. ▪ The Court reversed the conviction. ▪ Terminal services had to be given without discrimination. ▪ Five months later, the terminals were still segregated.

  • [State the holding in the Court's own structure, because the activity file quotes it and the quick check tests it.]
  • Bruce Boynton, a Black law student traveling by bus, sat in the white section of the restaurant in the Richmond Trailways terminal, refused to move to the section for Black customers, and was convicted of trespass under Virginia law.
  • The Supreme Court reversed.
  • When a bus carrier makes terminal and restaurant facilities available to its interstate passengers as a regular part of their transportation, the Court held, the terminal and restaurant must perform those services without the discriminations prohibited by the Interstate Commerce Act.
  • Note the ground: a federal statute, not the Fourteenth Amendment, which let the Court decide for Boynton without ruling on segregation generally.
  • Then make the enforcement gap vivid.
  • The decision came in December 1960, and through the winter and spring the terminals kept their white and colored signs.
  • No agency inspected them.
  • A right that nobody enforces is a claim, and the Freedom Rides were designed to convert the claim into a test the federal government would have to grade.

Slide 16 · Thirteen riders leave Washington

On the slide: E · THE RIDES BEGIN · Thirteen riders leave Washington · ▪ On May 4, 1961, they rode Greyhound and Trailways buses. ▪ Seven riders were Black and six were white. ▪ CORE organized the rides, and Farmer led the first group. ▪ At each stop, they used the white waiting room and lunch counter. ▪ Their destination was New Orleans, by way of the deep South.

  • [Walk the plan as its organizers understood it.]
  • Thirteen riders, seven Black and six white, divided between a Greyhound bus and a Trailways bus, left Washington on May 4, 1961.
  • Black riders would sit in the front of the buses and use the white waiting rooms, restrooms, and lunch counters at each stop; white riders would use the facilities marked for Black travelers.
  • The destination was New Orleans, reached on May 17, the seventh anniversary of Brown.
  • The route ran through Virginia and the Carolinas into Georgia, Alabama, and Mississippi, deliberately entering the states where Boynton was most openly defied.
  • Farmer and his colleagues expected arrests and probable violence, and the riders had trained in nonviolent discipline and made out wills before leaving.
  • The first week passed with scattered arrests and little national notice.
  • As long as the South answered with routine arrests, the test produced no crisis.
  • The crisis came when Alabama answered with a mob, and section F takes up that answer in plain terms.

Slide 17 · Section F: Fire at Anniston, prison at Parchman (divider)

On the slide: SECTION F · Fire at Anniston, Prison at Parchman · Cordell Reagon, photographed by Jackson, Mississippi police after his arrest, June 2, 1961. · Photograph: the Jackson, Mississippi police mug shot of Cordell Reagon, June 2, 1961.

  • Picture: The Jackson, Mississippi police mug shot of Cordell Reagon, a Nashville student and SNCC worker, taken on June 2, 1961, and marked public domain on its Wikimedia Commons file page as a work of the Jackson police.
  • [Teach this section in plain terms: exact places, exact dates, no embellishment, because the documented facts need none.]
  • Reagon's face belongs to section G as much as to this one, since he carried the organizing into Albany that fall.
  • Alabama's mobs tried to end the rides by terror.
  • Mississippi's officials tried to end them by processing the riders through arrest, conviction, and imprisonment.
  • The Kennedy administration tried to end them by negotiation, offering protection in exchange for a pause.
  • The riders, reinforced by Nashville students when the original group could not continue, refused every ending offered to them.
  • That refusal converted a two week test into a summer long crisis the federal government finally had to settle.
  • Note: No public domain image of the burning bus at Anniston was verified, so the violence is carried in plain text; see the lesson's image sources.

Slide 18 · May 14, 1961: Anniston and Birmingham

On the slide: F · THE ORDEAL · May 14, 1961: Anniston and Birmingham · ▪ A mob attacked the Greyhound bus outside Anniston. ▪ A firebomb was thrown, and the doors were held shut. ▪ Riders escaped the burning bus and were beaten. ▪ In Birmingham, riders were beaten at the terminal. ▪ Police had been arranged to be absent.

  • [Teach May 14 in plain sequence, and keep the language exact.]
  • Near Anniston, Alabama, a mob surrounded the Greyhound bus, slashed its tires, and followed it out of town.
  • When the bus stopped, a firebomb was thrown through a window and the doors were held shut while the bus burned; the riders escaped through the windows and the rear door and were beaten as they came out.
  • Several were hospitalized.
  • The Trailways bus reached Birmingham, where riders were beaten at the terminal by a mob that Police Commissioner Eugene Connor had arranged to face no police for its first minutes.
  • The Federal Bureau of Investigation had been told of the Birmingham plan in advance, a fact students will meet again in later study.
  • Pause on the design of the test: the riders had claimed facilities the Supreme Court had opened, in daylight, without weapons, and the answer was attempted murder by fire.
  • Whatever the administration in Washington preferred, the photographs of the burning bus made the Freedom Rides a national question within the day, which is precisely the mechanism the riders had counted on.

Slide 19 · The rides continue, into Parchman

On the slide: F · THE ORDEAL · The rides continue, into Parchman · ▪ On May 20, riders were beaten at the Montgomery bus station. ▪ Federal marshals were sent to Montgomery. ▪ Nashville students replaced the injured riders. ▪ Jackson arrested riders who used the white waiting room. ▪ Refusing fines, riders were sent to Parchman prison.

  • [Follow the crisis to Mississippi, and mark who kept it alive.]
  • After the Montgomery beating of May 20, in which a Justice Department official was knocked unconscious, the administration sent federal marshals and pressed the riders to accept a cooling off period.
  • CORE's injured group could not continue, and the rides should have ended there.
  • Instead, Nashville students organized by Diane Nash insisted on continuing, writing that they would not stop until the job was done, and rode from Montgomery to Jackson.
  • Mississippi's answer was orderly: arrest in the white waiting room, a breach of peace conviction, and a choice of fine or jail.
  • The riders chose jail, were stripped of bedding and books at Parchman, the state penitentiary, and sang freedom songs through the cellblock bars.
  • Through the summer more than four hundred riders, most of them students, rode into the South and filled its jails.
  • The administration's problem compounded weekly: every new busload renewed the question of whether federal law ran in interstate terminals.
  • On May 29, Attorney General Robert Kennedy petitioned the Interstate Commerce Commission for the regulations that end this section of the story.

Slide 20 · 400+ Freedom Riders

On the slide: F · THE ORDEAL · 400+ · Freedom Riders rode into the South in 1961 · May to November 1961 · Most of them students · Arrested in Jackson and sent to Parchman when they refused their fines

  • 400+
  • [Use the number to correct a common picture of the rides as a single bus and a single week.]
  • The first group numbered thirteen.
  • By the end of the summer, more than four hundred riders had ridden into the South, the great majority of them students, Black and white, from colleges across the country, and most were arrested in Jackson, Mississippi, for using the white side of the terminal.
  • The supply of riders was the strategy.
  • Alabama's violence was supposed to make the price of riding higher than students would pay; instead, each attack recruited the next busload, and the jails and then Parchman prison filled with young people whose families, colleges, and home newspapers followed their cases.
  • That arithmetic is what the Kennedy administration faced when it calculated the cost of inaction.
  • A crisis that renews itself every week cannot be waited out, and a movement that can replace every arrested rider has changed its own bargaining power.
  • Connect the number back to the lesson question: this is the pace the students set, a pace set by their willingness to be replaced and to return.

Slide 21 · Section G: The federal answer and the Albany limit (divider)

On the slide: SECTION G · The Federal Answer and the Albany Limit · Ruth Moskowitz, photographed by Jackson, Mississippi police after her arrest, July 21, 1961. · Photograph: the Jackson, Mississippi police mug shot of Ruth Moskowitz, July 21, 1961.

  • Picture: The Jackson, Mississippi police mug shot of Ruth Moskowitz, a Freedom Rider arrested in the summer of 1961, marked public domain on its Wikimedia Commons file page because the copyright was not renewed.
  • [Tell students the pairing of this section's two endings is the lesson.]
  • In the first ending, the crisis the riders created reached the Interstate Commerce Commission, and the federal government issued the regulations that desegregated interstate terminals across the South, the clearest federal enforcement victory the student movement had yet won.
  • In the second ending, in Albany, Georgia, the movement's methods met Police Chief Laurie Pritchett, who had studied those methods as carefully as any organizer, and the campaign closed without the agreement it had come for.
  • The guided notes summary table calls this row the limit, and the writing task will require students to argue from both endings at once.
  • Teach the ICC order first, in its own words, and Albany second, in its chief's own method.

Slide 22 · September 22, 1961: the Commission acts

On the slide: G · THE FEDERAL ANSWER · September 22, 1961: the Commission acts · ▪ Robert Kennedy petitioned the ICC on May 29, 1961. ▪ Regulations were adopted on September 22 and took effect on November 1. ▪ No carrier could seat passengers by race. ▪ Every ticket printed the seating notice. ▪ Segregation signs came down in the terminals.

  • [Quote the order's operative language, because students meet it at Station 4 of the activity file and should hear it twice.]
  • No motor common carrier of passengers, the regulations provide, shall operate a vehicle in interstate commerce on which the seating of passengers is based upon race, color, creed, or national origin.
  • Every ticket had to print the notice that seating aboard vehicles operated in interstate or foreign commerce is without regard to race, color, creed, or national origin, and terminals had to post the regulations and operate without segregation.
  • Compare the machinery with Boynton's.
  • The Court had declared a right that an individual passenger could sue over, one terminal at a time.
  • The Commission's rule bound an entire industry at once, with an agency behind it.
  • That difference, a right declared against a rule enforced, is the distinction this lesson most wants students to carry into Lesson 5 and Lesson 6, where the movement turns from protest to national legislation.
  • The signs students saw in the title photograph began coming down that fall.

Slide 23 · Albany: the crisis that never came

On the slide: G · THE LIMIT · Albany: the crisis that never came · ▪ SNCC organizers reached Albany in the fall of 1961. ▪ Mass marches filled the jails in December. ▪ Pritchett ordered polite arrests, with no beatings. ▪ Jail space was borrowed from surrounding counties. ▪ In August 1962, King left without an agreement.

  • [Carry the definition exactly as the guided notes print it.]
  • Albany Movement: the campaign of 1961 and 1962 in Albany, Georgia, where a police chief's polite mass arrests denied the movement a televised crisis and taught it the limits of protest that moved no federal power
  • [Then teach Albany as a contest of methods, not as a failure of courage.]
  • The record forbids the easy reading: more than a thousand Albany demonstrators were arrested, and King himself went to jail there twice.
  • Police Chief Laurie Pritchett had read the movement's accounts of itself and understood its mechanism exactly.
  • Protest worked by creating a crisis, televised violence or overflowing jails, that forced federal intervention or negotiation.
  • Pritchett denied it both halves.
  • His officers made mass arrests politely, in front of cameras, without the brutality that had shamed Birmingham and Montgomery.
  • He borrowed jail space from every surrounding county, so Albany's cells never overflowed and the pressure never peaked.
  • When King chose jail over a fine in July 1962, an unknown person paid the fine and the chief released him, denying the movement even its martyr.
  • King left Albany on August 10, 1962, without a desegregation agreement.
  • The general desegregation of Albany's facilities came only later and piecemeal.
  • The students drew the arithmetic plainly, and Birmingham, in the next lesson, was planned with Albany open on the desk.

Slide 25 · Synthesis: what the students changed

On the slide: SYNTHESIS · What the students changed · ▪ A front could open anywhere, in a week. ▪ The tactics were to occupy the space, fill the jail, and ride the bus. ▪ The demands were service now, on equal terms, in person. ▪ Crisis moved federal power, and quiet did not. ▪ A disciplined opponent could absorb protest.

  • [Draw the lesson together around the question's three verbs, because the exit ticket and the writing task both return to them.]

Slide 26 · Vocabulary: words for the counters and the organizations

On the slide: THE VOCABULARY · Words for the counters and the organizations · sit-in · seating yourself at a segregated facility and refusing to leave · Greensboro sit-in · the February 1, 1960 Woolworth protest that sparked the wave · SNCC · the student organization founded in April 1960 · CORE · the 1942 organization that organized the Freedom Rides

  • These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.
  • sit-in: a protest in which demonstrators seat themselves at a segregated facility and refuse to leave until they are served on equal terms or arrested
  • Greensboro sit-in: the protest of February 1, 1960 in which four Black college students sat at a segregated Woolworth lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina and refused to leave, sparking sit-ins in cities across the South
  • Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee: the organization founded by student activists in April 1960, known as SNCC, which organized sit-ins, Freedom Rides, and voter registration drives in the deepest parts of the South
  • Congress of Racial Equality: the civil rights organization founded in 1942, known as CORE, which pioneered nonviolent direct action and organized the Freedom Rides of 1961
  • Test the distinction students most often blur: CORE is the older organization, founded in 1942, and SNCC is the students' own, founded in April 1960.

  • Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly.
