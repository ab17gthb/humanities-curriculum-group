Writing Task: The Students' Arithmetic

The prompt. How did students a decade younger than the movement's leaders change its pace, its tactics, and its demands? Answer in a developed essay of about 300 words, argued from the records in The Crisis File. A strong answer does more than list what the students did. It states the rule their victories and their failure together prove about when protest moves the federal government, it uses Albany as evidence and not as an afterthought, and it answers the strongest claim on the other side: that the students succeeded only where courts and presidents had already cleared the way.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. Greensboro: on February 1, 1960, four freshmen sat at a Woolworth lunch counter, were refused, and stayed; they returned with more students each day, students sat in in more than a hundred southern cities by the end of 1960, and the Greensboro counter was desegregated on July 25, 1960.

2. Nashville: students trained from the fall of 1959 in James Lawson's workshops were beaten and arrested on February 27, 1960, filled the jails rather than posting bail, and, after Diane Nash questioned Mayor Ben West on April 19, won desegregated counters on May 10, 1960.

3. SNCC: at Shaw University in April 1960, Ella Baker urged the students to build their own organization rather than a youth wing of an older one, and the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee was founded under the rule that local people lead their own fights.

4. Boynton and the rides: in December 1960 the Supreme Court held that terminal and restaurant services for interstate passengers must be performed without discriminations prohibited by the Interstate Commerce Act. CORE's Freedom Rides tested the holding from May 4, 1961; a bus was firebombed at Anniston, riders were beaten in Birmingham and Montgomery and jailed at Parchman, and more than four hundred riders came.

5. The federal answer: Attorney General Robert Kennedy petitioned the Interstate Commerce Commission on May 29, 1961, and its regulations of September 22, 1961, effective November 1, barred seating by race and required the ticket notice that seating in interstate commerce is without regard to race, color, creed, or national origin.

6. Albany: in 1961 and 1962, Police Chief Laurie Pritchett arrested more than a thousand demonstrators politely, without violence before cameras, and borrowed jail space from surrounding counties. No federal enforcement followed, and King left Albany on August 10, 1962, without a desegregation agreement.

7. The northern student's-eye view: Jesús Colón, "Little Things are Big," 1961, an audited record in the Primary Source Project bank, recalls a New York subway in the 1950s: "...a Puerto Rican hours past midnight, a valise, two white children and a white lady with a baby on her arm [badly] needing somebody to help her, at least until she descended the long concrete stairs." Colón walked past rather than risk being read as a threat. The passage is quoted with a cut at its opening, marked by ellipses.

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your claim, develop it with your evidence, answer the other side in full, and end by restating what the students changed and what Albany showed they could not change by protest alone.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that states a rule about when protest moves federal power, not a claim that the students were brave or important in general. (Blueprint 7, 8)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, including at least one sit-in item (bank 1, 2, or 3) and the ICC regulations with their dates (bank 5). Yields, dates, and names must be right: February 1, 1960; May 10, 1960; September 22 and November 1, 1961. (Blueprint 7, 8)
  • Albany used as evidence in the argument, with Pritchett's two-part method stated (polite arrests without televised violence, borrowed jail space), not mentioned in passing. (Blueprint 8)
  • The cleared-the-way claim answered from the record. The strongest form of the answer notes that Boynton sat unenforced from December 1960 until the riders forced the issue in May 1961, and that in Albany a federal right existed on paper too, yet no enforcement came, because no crisis did.

Model opening for teacher reference. The students changed the movement by discovering its arithmetic. A court could declare a right, as Boynton did in December 1960, and the right could sit unenforced for five months while terminals stayed segregated. What the students added was the crisis that made enforcement unavoidable: a counter occupied in Greensboro in February 1960, jails filled in Nashville that spring, and buses ridden into Anniston and Jackson in 1961, until the Attorney General petitioned the Interstate Commerce Commission and the regulations of September 22, 1961 followed. A full model continues in this pattern, carries Albany as the proof of the rule's limit, and closes on the rule itself rather than on praise.

Common weak answers. A courage claim (the students were brave, so they won) never explains why the same courage failed in Albany. A courts-did-it claim (Boynton and the ICC show the system working) ignores the five months Boynton sat unenforced and the petition the rides forced. An essay that treats Albany as a minor setback rather than the rule's negative proof cannot earn full credit under this prompt, because the prompt requires the limit to be argued, not noted.
