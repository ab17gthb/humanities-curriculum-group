Guided Notes: Sit-Ins and Freedom Rides

Lesson question: How did students a decade younger than the movement's leaders change its pace, its tactics, and its demands?

Unit question: How did the civil rights movement change American law and American life, and what did it leave unfinished?

Key Terms

sit-in: a protest in which demonstrators seat themselves at a segregated facility and refuse to leave until they are served on equal terms or arrested

Greensboro sit-in: the protest of February 1, 1960 in which four Black college students sat at a segregated Woolworth lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina and refused to leave, sparking sit-ins in cities across the South

Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee: the organization founded by student activists in April 1960, known as SNCC, which organized sit-ins, Freedom Rides, and voter registration drives in the deepest parts of the South

Congress of Racial Equality: the civil rights organization founded in 1942, known as CORE, which pioneered nonviolent direct action and organized the Freedom Rides of 1961

Freedom Rides: the bus journeys of 1961 in which interracial groups of activists rode through the South to test court orders desegregating interstate bus travel and bus terminals

Albany Movement: the campaign of 1961 and 1962 in Albany, Georgia, where a police chief's polite mass arrests denied the movement a televised crisis and taught it the limits of protest that moved no federal power

de jure segregation: segregation imposed and enforced by law, as under the southern statutes the courts struck down one by one

Summary table. The counters: four Greensboro freshmen opened the tactic on February 1, 1960, students sat in in more than a hundred cities by the end of 1960, and trained Nashville students won the first major city desegregation on May 10, 1960, at the cost of beatings, arrests, and a bombed home. The organization: at Shaw University in April 1960 Ella Baker pushed the students to found SNCC as their own organization under the rule that local people lead their own fights. The buses: CORE's Freedom Rides tested Boynton from May 4, 1961, met firebombing and beatings in Alabama and Parchman prison in Mississippi, and forced the Interstate Commerce Commission regulations of September 22, 1961, effective November 1. The limit: in Albany, Chief Laurie Pritchett's polite mass arrests and borrowed jail space denied the movement a crisis, and it left in August 1962 without an agreement.

Exit ticket 1. A sit-in is a protest in which demonstrators seat themselves at a segregated facility and refuse to leave until they are served on equal terms or arrested.

Exit ticket 2. The Interstate Commerce Commission issued its regulations on September 22, 1961, effective November 1, 1961. The Freedom Rides of 1961 forced them, after Attorney General Robert Kennedy asked the Commission for enforcing regulations on May 29, 1961.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that uses at least two facts and addresses pace, tactics, or demands, for example the copyable sit-in of February 1960, Nashville's trained discipline and jail filling, SNCC's founding, or the Albany lesson that protest absorbed quietly moved no federal power.
