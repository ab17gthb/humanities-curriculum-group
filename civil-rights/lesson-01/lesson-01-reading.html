Foundations: The Movement Before the Movement

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The System in Place

Start with the system the movement would have to break. Across the South, Jim Crow governed daily life by law and by custom. Statutes and ordinances required separate schools, railroad cars, waiting rooms, theaters, restaurants, and drinking fountains, and custom enforced the separation where no statute reached. In Plessy v. Ferguson in 1896 the Supreme Court upheld the arrangement under the doctrine called separate but equal. The facilities provided were separate. They were plainly unequal, in school funding most of all.

Segregation did quiet work as well. It taught every child in the region a daily lesson about rank, and it marked Black citizens as a class apart in the ordinary business of life, buying a ticket, eating a meal, entering a courthouse. The system rested on law, on habit, and on violence behind both, and it presented itself as the natural order of things rather than as choices that men had made and could unmake.

B. Shut Out of the Ballot Box

The ballot was guarded first. Southern states disfranchised Black voters by the poll tax, a fee for voting that poor citizens could not always pay, and by the literacy test, administered by white registrars who could fail a Black applicant on any reading they chose. The white primary closed the one election that counted in the one party South, since the Democratic nominee faced no serious opposition in November.

Violence stood behind the paperwork. A Black citizen who pressed a claim to register risked a job, a loan, a home, or his life, and lynching remained unpunished in almost every case. In 1940 only about three in every hundred Black adults in the South were registered to vote, and in the deepest states the figure stood lower. A people shut out of the ballot box could not vote the system down. It would have to be broken another way.

C. The Institutions That Held

The system could not close everything. The Black church was an institution Black Americans owned outright, with buildings, budgets, and a weekly audience no white official controlled. Black colleges trained the teachers, lawyers, and doctors the community needed. The Black press, led by papers such as the Chicago Defender and the Pittsburgh Courier, carried news the white papers ignored and spoke to a national audience.

Above these stood the NAACP, founded in 1909 after a race riot in Springfield, Illinois. The association lobbied Congress, published its magazine The Crisis, and built a legal program that carried segregation into the courts case by case, funded by dues from branches across the country. By the 1940s it had a legal staff learning its trade. The famous decade ahead would be led by people these institutions trained, funded, and taught to organize.

D. Victories in Court Before 1950

The courts gave the groundwork its first victories. In Smith v. Allwright in 1944 the Supreme Court struck down the white primary. Texas had argued that a party was a private club free to choose its members. The Court answered that a primary run under state law was state action, and that the Fifteenth Amendment forbade the exclusion. Thurgood Marshall of the NAACP legal staff argued the case.

In Shelley v. Kraemer in 1948 the Court took up restrictive covenants, private agreements by which homeowners promised never to sell to a Black buyer. The Court held that state courts could not enforce such agreements, because enforcement was state action denying Black purchasers the equal protection of the laws under the Fourteenth Amendment. Neither decision ended segregation. Each removed one legal prop from under it.

E. The War and the Moving North

War changed the ground under all of it. About a million Black Americans served in the Second World War, in a military that kept them in segregated units and mostly in service jobs. The Black press answered with the Double V campaign, demanding victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home. In 1941 A. Philip Randolph threatened a march on Washington unless defense work was opened, and President Roosevelt answered with Executive Order 8802, barring discrimination in defense industries.

The war also moved people. A second wave of the Great Migration carried Black southerners to the war industries of the North and West, to Chicago, Detroit, and the California shipyard cities. There their votes counted. Northern politicians who needed Black ballots in close national elections began to court them, and a constituency the South had silenced became one that presidents had to answer.

F. A Committee, a Report, and a Split Party

In 1946 President Truman created a Committee on Civil Rights. Its report, To Secure These Rights, appeared in October 1947. It named lynching, disfranchisement, and segregation as federal business and called for laws against lynching and the poll tax and for an end to segregation in American life. In February 1948 Truman sent Congress a program built on the report. Southern delegates answered that summer by walking out of the Democratic convention and running their own ticket, the Dixiecrats.

G. The Order of July 26, 1948

Randolph appears twice in this groundwork, and the second appearance explains the first. In 1948 he told Congress he would lead resistance to a segregated draft. On July 26, 1948, Truman signed Executive Order 9981, declaring equality of treatment and opportunity in the armed forces without regard to race. The order was carried out slowly, then under the pressure of the Korean War, until the military became the most integrated institution in American life. Organized mass pressure, the movement's central tactic, was already older than its famous decade.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Explain Jim Crow as law and as custom, with one place for each. As law, Jim Crow was the set of statutes and ordinances that required separation, in schools, railroad cars, waiting rooms, theaters, restaurants, and public facilities generally. As custom, it was the separation enforced by habit and expectation where no statute reached, backed by the same threat of violence. Credit any reading example for each half, such as separate schools for law and the daily marking of rank in ordinary transactions for custom. The distinction matters because striking down the laws, the work of the next lesson, would still leave the custom standing.

2. Name the three devices of disfranchisement, and what stood behind them. The poll tax, a fee for voting that poor citizens could not always pay; the literacy test, administered by white registrars with discretion to fail Black applicants; and the white primary, which closed the only election that decided officeholders in the one party South. Behind all three stood violence and economic retaliation: a citizen who pressed a claim to register risked a job, a loan, a home, or his life, and lynching went unpunished in almost every case. The 1940 figure, about three registered voters in every hundred Black southern adults, measures the devices' combined effect.

3. Pick two institutions and state what each gave the later movement. The Black church gave owned ground: buildings, budgets, meeting space, and a weekly audience no white official controlled, plus ministers who could speak from independent pulpits. Black colleges gave trained people: the teachers, lawyers, and doctors the community produced for itself. The Black press gave a national voice and a shared agenda, carrying news white papers ignored. The NAACP gave continuity and craft: a dues funded national organization that lobbied, published The Crisis, and ran a legal program across decades. Any two, with the specific contribution named, earn full credit.

4. State the Court's answer in Smith and in Shelley. In Smith v. Allwright (1944) the Court held that a primary conducted under state law is state action, so Texas could not escape the Fifteenth Amendment by calling the Democratic Party a private club, and the white primary fell. Thurgood Marshall argued the case for the NAACP. In Shelley v. Kraemer (1948) the Court held that judicial enforcement of a restrictive covenant is state action, so state courts could not enforce private agreements barring sales to Black buyers without denying the equal protection of the laws under the Fourteenth Amendment. In both cases the word private failed once the state's machinery was doing the excluding.

5. What had Black Americans already built, and already won? Full credit answers hold both halves of the question together. Built: a church, a college system, and a press that no white authority owned, and a national association, founded in 1909, with branches, dues, a magazine, and a legal staff. Won: the white primary struck down in 1944, restrictive covenants made unenforceable in 1948, a fair employment order in 1941, a presidential committee's report in 1947 naming lynching, disfranchisement, and segregation as federal business, and an executive order in 1948 desegregating the armed forces, plus a northern electorate whose votes presidents now had to court. A paragraph that lists only organizations or only victories earns partial credit, because the lesson's claim is that the famous decade inherited both.
