## Gaps recorded honestly
- No public domain photograph of the Pittsburgh Courier's Double V campaign pages was verified on a Commons file page for this lesson, and the Courier's 1942 pages are a newspaper record of the period. Station 3 of the activity therefore teaches the campaign as a labeled summary from the public record and reproduces no Courier page or emblem.
- No portrait of A. Philip Randolph from 1941 or 1948 with a verified public domain basis was located. The deck uses the verified 1963 Library of Congress portrait listed above and captions it with its true date.
- The deck crop chicago-port.jpg was prepared from the Chicago Easter photograph but is not used in the final deck build. The photograph appears in the activity and is recorded here with the document copies.

## Text sources quoted in this lesson, with editions
- President's Committee on Civil Rights, To Secure These Rights, 1947 (Internet Archive scan of the 1947 printing), as carried in the audited TPSP record, id to-secure-these-rights-1947, in civil-rights.json. Quoted in the activity, the writing evidence bank, and the deck notes. Cuts are marked with ellipses in the documents.
- Harry S. Truman, Executive Order 9981, 1948 (Harry S. Truman Library), as carried in the audited TPSP record, id executive-order-9981-1948, in cold-war.json. Quoted in the activity, the quick check, the writing evidence bank, and the deck notes. Cuts are marked with ellipses in the documents.
- Smith v. Allwright, 321 U.S. 649 (1944), opinion as published by the Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School. Quoted in the activity and the writing evidence bank. Cuts are marked with ellipses.
- Shelley v. Kraemer, 334 U.S. 1 (1948), opinion as published by the Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School. Quoted in the activity and the writing evidence bank. Cuts are marked with ellipses.
- The Double V station and the Great Migration station of the activity are labeled teaching summaries on their faces, built from the public record: the Pittsburgh Courier campaign of 1942 for the first, and United States Census Bureau population counts for Chicago (about 44,000 in 1910, about 234,000 in 1940, about 492,000 in 1950) for the second.
- No text by Martin Luther King Jr. or Malcolm X is quoted in this lesson. The unit copyright caps for those works are untouched by Lesson 1.
