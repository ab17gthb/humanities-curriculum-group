Writing Task: Built, Won, or Given?

The prompt. Answer the closing question of the lesson in a developed essay of about 300 words: Were the federal steps of 1947 and 1948, the report and the order, won by the groundwork Black Americans had built, or given by a president acting on his own? A strong answer does more than state a side. It works through the groundwork in order, from the institutions to the court victories to the pressure of 1941 and 1948, and it weighs what presidents did against what made them do it. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading, stations, and documents. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording, except where a bank item quotes a document.

Evidence Bank

1. The NAACP, founded in 1909, spent decades building a legal program on members' dues. Its lawyer Thurgood Marshall argued Smith v. Allwright, and in 1944 the Supreme Court ended the white primary, holding that a primary run under state law is state action under the Fifteenth Amendment. (Smith v. Allwright, 321 U.S. 649, opinion as published by the Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School.)

2. In Shelley v. Kraemer in 1948 the Court held that state courts could not enforce restrictive covenants: in granting judicial enforcement of the restrictive agreements, the States have denied petitioners the equal protection of the laws, and the action of the state courts cannot stand. (Shelley v. Kraemer, 334 U.S. 1, opinion as published by the Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School.)

3. In 1941 A. Philip Randolph threatened a march on Washington unless defense jobs were opened. President Roosevelt answered with Executive Order 8802, barring discrimination in defense industries, and the march was called off. The sequence is threat first, order second, inside a single season.

4. To Secure These Rights, the 1947 report of Truman's own Committee on Civil Rights, concluded that in going to school, working, participating in the political process, serving in the armed forces, and living in specific communities and neighborhoods, distinctions of race, color, and creed have no place. It named lynching, disfranchisement, and segregation as federal business. (President's Committee on Civil Rights, 1947, Internet Archive scan of the 1947 printing, as carried in the audited TPSP record.)

5. In the spring of 1948 Randolph told Congress he would lead resistance to a segregated draft. On July 26, 1948 Truman signed Executive Order 9981, declaring equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed services without regard to race, color, religion or national origin, to be put into effect as rapidly as possible, without impairing efficiency or morale. (Harry S. Truman Library, as carried in the audited TPSP record.)

6. The second Great Migration had changed the electorate presidents faced. Chicago's Black population rose from about 234,000 in 1940 to about 492,000 in 1950, and the newcomers voted in states whose electoral votes decided national elections. Truman's civil rights program split his party in 1948, and he carried the election that fall with northern Black votes part of his margin. (United States Census Bureau counts, as summarized in the activity.)

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your claim, develop it with your three pieces of evidence in the order of the groundwork, answer the other side in one or two sentences, and end by restating what the years 1947 and 1948 would have looked like without the forty years behind them.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the question directly and holds the federal steps up against the groundwork that preceded them. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank items 1 and 2 support the built reading: the court victories of 1944 and 1948 were produced by an organization founded in 1909 and funded by dues, not requested by any president. (Blueprint 1) Bank items 3 and 5 support the pressure reading: in both 1941 and 1948 a Randolph threat preceded an executive order within a season. (Blueprint 2) Bank item 4 supports the given reading at its strongest, since the report was a president's own committee speaking in a president's name. (Blueprint 2) Bank item 6 supports the constituency reading: the migration had made inaction expensive for the man who signed. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2) A full credit essay draws on at least two of these groups.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive link is sequence: institutions built across decades, victories won in court, and threats delivered by the same organizer in 1941 and 1948 all stand before the signatures of 1947 and 1948, and students should argue about what that order of events proves.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest argument on the other side, such as the point that only a president could sign an order and that Truman paid a party splitting price for signing, or the point that pressure without a president willing to yield had failed for decades before, answered from the record rather than dismissed.

Model opening for teacher reference. The steps of 1947 and 1948 were won, and the winning can be dated. The report and the order were signed by a president, but everything that made them signable was built by other hands: a legal program four decades old that had already ended the white primary and covenant enforcement, a press that had taught a million servicemen to demand two victories at once, a migration that had put Black ballots where presidents needed them, and an organizer who had extracted one order with a threatened march in 1941 and returned in 1948 to threaten resistance to the draft itself. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the Shelley and census evidence in groundwork order, answering the presidential conviction argument from the record, and closing on the forty years behind the two signatures.

Common weak answers. Biography only (Truman was a fair minded man, or Truman only wanted votes) asserts a motive without working the groundwork. Documents only (the order desegregated the military) skips the question of what produced the order, and pressure only (Randolph forced everything) skips the institutions that made his threats credible. A claim with no other side stated earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a weighing, not a verdict alone.
