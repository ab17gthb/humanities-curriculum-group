Writing Task: What Freedom Required Next

The prompt. Answer this question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Why did the movement's methods stop working as it moved north, and which of the answers its own people gave in 1966 and after was best supported by the evidence of the decade? Take one answer as your verdict: Black political power built by Black people themselves, an economic campaign aimed at the federal government, or a renewed claim on the American promise through its institutions. A strong essay first states the northern problem accurately, in the reading's terms, before it judges any answer to it. It also answers the strongest fact against the answer it chooses. If you choose Black political power, you must deal with the gains the older coalition had already won. If you choose the economic campaign, you must deal with the coalition's refusal to fund it. If you choose the claim on the promise, you must deal with Chicago, where the promise was made in an agreement and not enforced. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Linked extension. This task prepares you for TPSP premium DBQ number 22, "Evaluate the extent to which Black Americans in the 1960s shared one vision of freedom," which is assigned separately and is not reproduced here.

Evidence Bank

1. The Voting Rights Act was signed on August 6, 1965. Five days later Watts rose in six days of violence that left thirty four people dead. Residents named police treatment, poor housing, low pay, and weak schools, grievances no voting statute reached.

2. Northern segregation was de facto, built by restrictive covenants, redlining by lenders, white flight, and school lines drawn around sorted neighborhoods. No single statute stood at its center for a court to strike down.

3. In Chicago in 1966, open housing marchers were met at Marquette Park by mobs throwing bottles and bricks, and a brick struck King. The Summit Agreement promised steps toward open housing, and the city did not enforce it.

4. At Greenwood in June 1966, after his twenty seventh arrest, Stokely Carmichael announced that the movement had been saying freedom for six years and had received nothing, and called for Black Power. SNCC and CORE moved toward Black leadership.

5. Malcolm X, assassinated in 1965, had argued that Black people should control the politics and the economy of their own communities and should not wait for a hostile system to admit them. The Black Panther Party, founded in Oakland in October 1966, joined police patrols and a program of jobs, housing, and an end to police brutality to free breakfast programs and health clinics.

6. King's Poor People's Campaign planned to bring an interracial army of the poor to Washington to demand jobs, income, and housing. It drew little support from the coalition that had passed the acts of 1964 and 1965. King was assassinated in Memphis on April 4, 1968 while supporting a sanitation strike whose signs asserted the workers' standing as men.

7. The Kerner Commission, a federal body appointed by the president, reported in March 1968 that the nation was moving toward two societies, one black, one white, separate and unequal, and that white institutions had created and maintained the ghetto.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • An accurate statement of the northern problem before any verdict. The methods had worked by forcing officials to enforce court orders against segregation statutes. In the North the separation was de facto, built from housing, lending, and custom, as bank item 2 states, and Watts, bank item 1, is the dated proof that the new laws did not reach it. (Blueprint 13)
  • A verdict carried by documents. The Black political power verdict rests chiefly on bank items 4 and 5, Carmichael at Greenwood, Malcolm's nationalist argument, and the Panthers' program and services. (Blueprint 14) The economic campaign verdict rests chiefly on bank items 1 and 6, grievances that were economic at Watts and a strike in Memphis, answered by a campaign aimed at jobs, income, and housing. (Blueprint 14) The claim on the promise verdict rests chiefly on bank item 7, a federal commission assigning responsibility to white institutions in the nation's own name, supported by the acts the coalition had passed in 1964 and 1965. (Blueprint 13)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately and in date order. Bank item 3, Chicago and the unenforced agreement, serves every verdict and damages every verdict differently, which is why the prompt assigns it as a required counterclaim for one side and expects it in strong answers for the others.
  • A direct answer to the strongest fact against the verdict chosen. A Black power essay that never mentions the Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act has not met the record. An economic essay that never mentions the coalition's refusal has argued a wish rather than a campaign. An essay for the promise that never mentions the unenforced Summit Agreement has skipped the hardest document in the file.

Model opening for teacher reference. The methods stopped working because their target disappeared. In the South the movement had sued statutes and forced governors and sheriffs to defy federal courts in front of cameras, and each defiance had moved Washington. In Chicago there was no statute to sue and no governor to defy, only covenants, lenders, and neighbors, and the agreement the city signed in 1966 it simply did not enforce. On that record the best supported answer was the economic one, because the grievances at Watts and the claim in Memphis were already economic, and King's campaign was the one proposal aimed at the level of government that could act on jobs, income, and housing across the whole country at once. A full model in this line concedes the coalition's refusal directly and argues that a correct diagnosis refused is a different thing from a wrong diagnosis, while noting that the campaign's fate after April 1968 belongs to that refusal and to the assassination, not to the evidence for the need.

Common weak answers. A verdict that the movement simply became violent earns partial credit at most, because the documents show organizations arguing programs, not a single turn. An essay that treats Black Power as a slogan without Greenwood, the arrests, or the organizational turn behind it has not used bank item 4. An essay that praises or condemns the Panthers without both halves of bank item 5, the patrols and the breakfast programs, has read half a document.
