The Movement Turns North and Inward

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Five Days After the Vote: Watts

The Voting Rights Act was signed on August 6, 1965. Five days later the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles rose in six days of violence that left thirty four people dead and more than a thousand injured. Watts was not a protest the movement had organized, and no leader directed it. Its force came from residents who had watched laws pass in Washington and could measure the distance between those victories and their own streets, where the grievances named afterward were police treatment, poor housing, low pay, and weak schools.

B. The Northern Problem

The northern problem had a name: de facto segregation, segregation in fact rather than in law, produced by housing patterns, economics, and custom. In the South, segregation rested on statutes that courts could strike down one by one. In northern cities, no single statute ordered a Black family to live on one side of a line. The line was built from parts that each looked private. Restrictive covenants barred sales to Black buyers in whole neighborhoods. Lenders treated Black neighborhoods as bad risks and steered mortgage money to white suburbs, a practice called redlining. White families who could move did so, and school boards drew attendance lines around neighborhoods that housing had already sorted by race.

C. Chicago: The Movement Marches North

In 1966 Martin Luther King Jr. took the movement north in the Chicago Freedom Movement, his campaign against housing and school segregation in that city. He rented a tenement flat on the West Side and led open housing marches into white neighborhoods. At Marquette Park in August 1966 the marchers were met by mobs carrying swastikas and throwing bottles and bricks, and a brick struck King himself. He said afterward, in paraphrase, that he had not seen mobs as hostile even in Mississippi or Alabama. The campaign ended in a Summit Agreement promising steps toward open housing. The city did not enforce it, and the housing lines stayed close to where they had stood.

D. The Argument Inside: Black Power

In June 1966 James Meredith, who had desegregated the University of Mississippi, set out alone on a March Against Fear and was shot and wounded on the second day. The movement organizations continued the march together, and on it the argument broke open. At Greenwood, Stokely Carmichael, the new chairman of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, was arrested for the twenty seventh time and came from jail to a night rally calling for Black Power. His organization and the Congress of Racial Equality moved toward Black leadership and away from reliance on the interracial liberal coalition. King refused the slogan, fearing it would be heard as a call to violence. He also understood its source. A decade of disciplined nonviolence had been answered with bombs, beatings, and acquittals.

E. Malcolm X and the Panthers

Behind that call stood Malcolm X, assassinated in February 1965 after breaking with the Nation of Islam. His father, an organizer for Marcus Garvey, had held the Garvey belief that "freedom, independence and self-respect could never be achieved by the Negro in America," and Malcolm carried that nationalist case in his Autobiography and in his 1964 speech The Ballot or the Bullet, arguing in paraphrase that Black people should control the politics and the economy of their own communities. In October 1966, in Oakland, California, Huey Newton and Bobby Seale founded the Black Panther Party. Its members patrolled police stops carrying law books and rifles where state law allowed, and its Ten Point Program demanded freedom, full employment, decent housing, and an end to police brutality. The same party ran free breakfast programs and health clinics, and the Federal Bureau of Investigation worked through surveillance and raids to destroy it.

F. The Turn to Economics

King's own answer to the northern problem was economic. Through the winter of 1967 and 1968 he planned the Poor People's Campaign, a campaign to bring an interracial army of the poor to Washington to demand federal action on poverty, with jobs, income, and housing stated in an economic bill of rights. He argued that legal equality at lunch counters and voting booths had cost the nation little, and that equality in wages and housing would cost far more, which was why it had not come. The plan drew little support from the coalition that had passed the acts of 1964 and 1965. King went ahead anyway.

G. Memphis and April 4, 1968

In February 1968 Black sanitation workers in Memphis struck after two workers were killed by a malfunctioning truck, and the city refused to recognize their union. The strikers marched carrying signs that read I Am a Man, a claim that the dispute concerned dignity before it concerned wages. King came to support them. A march he led in late March broke into violence, and he returned in April determined to lead a peaceful march. On April 3 he spoke at Mason Temple and, in paraphrase, told the strikers he was unafraid and glad to be alive in a time of struggle, whatever came next. On April 4, 1968 he was shot on the balcony of the Lorraine Motel and died that evening. Uprisings followed in more than a hundred cities. The federal commission appointed to study the urban disorders had already delivered its verdict, taken up in the next lesson: a nation moving toward two societies.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State what happened in Watts, and name two grievances its residents named afterward. Five days after the Voting Rights Act was signed on August 6, 1965, the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles rose in six days of violence that left thirty four people dead and more than a thousand injured. No movement organization planned or directed it. The grievances named afterward were police treatment, poor housing, low pay, and weak schools. Any two earn credit.

2. Define de facto segregation, and name three practices that built the northern line. De facto segregation is segregation in fact rather than in law, produced by housing patterns, economics, and custom. The practices named in the reading are restrictive covenants barring sales to Black buyers, redlining by lenders who treated Black neighborhoods as bad risks and steered mortgage money to white suburbs, white flight by families who could move, and school attendance lines drawn around neighborhoods already sorted by race. Any three earn credit.

3. What did the Chicago Freedom Movement demand, what met its marches, and what happened to the Summit Agreement? The campaign of 1966 demanded an end to housing and school segregation in Chicago, pursued through a tenement residence on the West Side and open housing marches into white neighborhoods. At Marquette Park in August 1966 the marchers were met by mobs carrying swastikas and throwing bottles and bricks, and a brick struck King. The campaign ended in a Summit Agreement promising steps toward open housing, which the city did not enforce.

4. Where and when did Carmichael raise the call of Black Power, and how did King answer it? Carmichael raised the call at a night rally in Greenwood, Mississippi, in June 1966, during the Meredith March, after his twenty seventh arrest. His organization, SNCC, and CORE moved toward Black leadership and away from reliance on the interracial liberal coalition. King refused the slogan because he feared it would be heard as a call to violence and would cost the movement its allies. He also understood its source, a decade of disciplined nonviolence answered with bombs, beatings, and acquittals. Both parts of the answer are required for full credit.

5. Connect the Memphis strike to the Poor People's Campaign, and state what each demanded. The Memphis sanitation strike of February 1968 began after two workers were killed by a malfunctioning truck and the city refused to recognize the workers' union. Its signs read I Am a Man, and its demands joined union recognition and safer, fairer treatment to a claim of dignity. King came to Memphis while planning the Poor People's Campaign, his project to bring an interracial army of the poor to Washington to demand federal action on poverty, including jobs, income, and housing in an economic bill of rights. Strong answers state the link as the reading states it. Memphis was a local labor dispute whose claim was dignity at work, and the campaign was King's attempt to carry that economic claim to the federal government itself. He was assassinated in Memphis on April 4, 1968, before the campaign reached Washington.
