The Argument File

Directions: Work through the five stations in order. Each station carries a vision of freedom argued inside the movement or stated about it: integration, nationalism, economic transformation, or self-defense. At each station, answer both questions. At the end, sort the five records by the vision each one carries and judge which vision the evidence of the decade supported best.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: The Ballot or the Bullet, April 12, 1964

Source: Malcolm X, "The Ballot or the Bullet," delivered in Detroit, April 12, 1964, printed from the audited TPSP bank record. Edition named in the bank record: Bill of Rights Institute transcription. The speech is under copyright. The two passages below total 82 quoted words, the whole of this unit's quotation from the speech, and cuts inside the passages are marked.

"I'm not a Democrat. I'm not a Republican, and I don't [...] consider myself an American. [...] I'm not an American. I'm one of the 22 million black people who are the victims of Americanism."

"Just as it took nationalism to remove colonialism from Asia and Africa, it'll take black nationalism today to remove colonialism from the backs and the minds of twenty-two million Afro-Americans here in this country. And 1964 looks like it might be the year of the ballot or the bullet."

Malcolm refuses three labels in the first passage. Name them, and state the ground on which he refuses the third one.

In the second passage, what removed colonialism from Asia and Africa, in his account, and what does he say will do the same work in this country? Name the choice he sets before the country in the last sentence.

Station 2: Black Power at Greenwood, June 16, 1966

Source: Stokely Carmichael, remarks at a night rally in Greenwood, Mississippi, June 16, 1966, during the Meredith March, as carried in the public record at BlackPast.org, "Stokely Carmichael's Black Power Speech (1966)." Carmichael had been arrested that day for the twenty seventh time, for setting up tents for the marchers.

"This is the twenty-seventh time I have been arrested. I ain't going to jail no more. We been saying freedom for six years and we ain't got nothin'. What we got to start saying now is Black Power!"

What does Carmichael say the movement has been saying for six years, and what does he say it has received? Answer from the text.

The new phrase replaces the old word rather than adding to it. Explain what changes when the demand stops being a condition, freedom, and becomes a thing to be built and held, power.

Station 3: The Ten Point Program, 1966 and 1967

Source: The Black Panther Party for Self-Defense, Ten Point Program, "What We Want Now!", written by Huey Newton and Bobby Seale in October 1966 and published in The Black Panther, May 15, 1967. Four of the ten points are printed below in their short form, word for word. Points 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, and 10 are not printed, and the omissions are marked.

1. We want freedom. We want power to determine the destiny of our Black Community.

2. We want full employment for our people.

[...]

4. We want decent housing, fit for shelter of human beings.

[...]

7. We want an immediate end to police brutality and murder of Black people.

[...]

Points 2 and 4 are demands a government could meet with money and law. Point 1 asks for something prior to both. State what point 1 asks for, in the program's own words.

Point 7 names the grievance that Watts residents also named first. State it, and explain why a party that patrolled police stops would place it in a list that also demands jobs and housing.

Station 4: The Memphis Posters, 1968

Source: Civil Rights Posters, 1968, audited TPSP bank record. Edition named in the bank record: Gilder Lehrman Institute, poster slogans transcribed. The record carries two posters from the Memphis sanitation workers' strike. The slogans are transcribed below; the posters themselves are not reproduced here.

Poster 1, carried by striking sanitation workers on their marches in February and March 1968: I AM A MAN. The word AM is underlined on the poster.

Poster 2, carried after April 4, 1968 by marchers led by Coretta Scott King: HONOR KING: END RACISM!

The first slogan is three words long and makes no request. State what it asserts, and explain why the strikers, whose dispute concerned a union, pay, and safety, chose an assertion rather than a request.

The second poster joins a name to a demand. State the demand, and explain what the pairing claims about how King's memory should be used.

Station 5: The Kerner Commission, March 1, 1968

Source: Report of the National Advisory Commission on Civil Disorders, Introduction, March 1, 1968, printed from the audited TPSP bank record. Edition named in the bank record: Teaching American History. The commission was appointed by President Johnson after the urban uprisings of the summer of 1967. Cuts are marked.

"This is our basic conclusion: Our nation is moving toward two societies, one black, one white, separate and unequal."

[...]

"What white Americans have never fully understood but what the Negro can never forget, is that white society is deeply implicated in the ghetto. White institutions created it, white institutions maintain it, and white society condones it."

State the commission's basic conclusion in its own sentence, and name the direction it says the nation is moving.

The second passage assigns responsibility in three verbs. Name the three verbs, and state who holds the responsibility in each case.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: The Ballot or the Bullet

Malcolm refuses three labels. Name them, and state the ground for refusing the third. He refuses Democrat, Republican, and American. The ground he gives for the third refusal is that he counts himself among the 22 million Black people he describes as victims of the American system, so the national label claims a membership his experience denies. Credit answers that keep his claim in his own terms, a claim about victimhood and exclusion, rather than converting it into a general statement about patriotism.

What removed colonialism from Asia and Africa, and what does he say will do the same work here? In his account, nationalism removed colonialism from Asia and Africa, and Black nationalism will remove it from the backs and minds of twenty two million Afro Americans in this country. The choice he sets before the country in the last sentence is the one in his title, between the ballot and the bullet, between a political settlement Black voters can win and violence if that settlement is refused.

Station 2: Black Power at Greenwood

What has the movement been saying, and what has it received? In the text, the movement has been saying freedom for six years and has received nothing, in Carmichael's words at his twenty seventh arrest. The count of arrests is part of the evidence. The speaker presents himself as a man who has submitted to the system's punishment repeatedly and is announcing that he will submit no more.

What changes when the demand becomes power? Freedom, as the old word was used, names a condition that an authority can grant, delay, or partly deliver, and the asking of it leaves the authority in place. Power names a capacity the community builds and holds itself, in its own organizations, votes, and institutions, and it cannot be granted because it is not the granter's to give. Strong answers connect this to the organizational turn that followed, SNCC and CORE moving toward Black leadership rather than asking the coalition to carry the demand.

Station 3: The Ten Point Program

State what point 1 asks for. Point 1 asks for freedom defined as power to determine the destiny of the Black community. The program therefore does not begin with a benefit. It begins with control, and the demands for employment and housing that follow are presented as things a self determining community is owed, not as favors to be requested.

State the grievance in point 7, and explain its place in the list. Point 7 demands an immediate end to police brutality and the murder of Black people. A party founded to patrol police stops placed it in the same list as jobs and housing because its account joined the grievances. The community it described was policed violently, employed last, and housed worst by the same arrangement, so the program answers the arrangement whole rather than one grievance at a time. Credit answers that also note the Watts parallel, where residents named police treatment first among their grievances.

Station 4: The Memphis Posters

State what the first slogan asserts, and why an assertion rather than a request. I AM A MAN asserts the manhood and full human standing of the strikers, with AM underlined as the contested word. A request for a union contract could be negotiated and partly granted by the city. The assertion underneath it could not, because it named the standing from which the workers negotiated at all: men whose dignity the city's treatment of them, in pay, in safety, and in the deaths that began the strike, had denied. The slogan reaches back to an abolitionist formula and claims the same standing against a municipal employer in 1968.

State the demand in the second poster, and explain the pairing. The demand is to end racism. Pairing it with the instruction to honor King claims that his memory is honored by continuing the work against racism rather than by ceremony alone. The poster was carried four days after his assassination by marchers led by his widow, and it converts mourning into a program in five words.

Station 5: The Kerner Commission

State the basic conclusion. The commission's basic conclusion is that the nation is moving toward two societies, one black, one white, separate and unequal. The direction is movement apart, and the report states that the movement can be reversed, so the conclusion is a warning about a direction rather than a claim that the division is complete or permanent.

Name the three verbs, and state who holds the responsibility. White institutions created the ghetto, white institutions maintain it, and white society condones it. The responsibility is assigned to institutions twice and to society once, not to the residents of the ghetto and not to outside agitators, which is the explanation the president who appointed the commission had expected and did not receive.

Part 2: Sort by Vision (Grading Guide)

Station 1, nationalism, with self-defense standing behind the choice the title names. Station 2, nationalism, in its organizational form: Black leadership building Black political power. Station 3, economic transformation joined to nationalism, with self-defense present in the party's founding purpose and point 7's subject; either nationalism or economic transformation earns credit as the main entry when the second is noted. Station 4, integration in the older movement's form, a claim of full standing inside the American promise, argued by a labor strike; strong answers note that its method, a union strike for recognition, is also economic. Station 5, integration as diagnosis, a federal commission arguing that the nation's institutions built the separation and must unbuild it; it carries no program of its own here beyond the verdict. Closing verdicts earn full credit when they argue from the sorting and answer a counterclaim from the file. The nationalism case rests on Stations 1 and 2 and the record of the coalition's limits in Chicago. The economic case rests on Station 3 and on Watts and Memphis, where the grievances were jobs, housing, and dignity at work. The integration case rests on Stations 4 and 5 and on the fact that the decade's enforceable gains, the acts of 1964, 1965, and 1968, were won in its language. The rubric credits the evidence and the counterclaim answered, not the vision chosen.
