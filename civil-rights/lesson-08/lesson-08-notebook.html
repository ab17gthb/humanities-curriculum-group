Teacher Answer Key

Question of the reckoningWhat the documents showWhat was decided
Would the last great statute carry enforcement strong enough to work?The Fair Housing Act, Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, signed April 11, 1968, one week after King's assassination, made it unlawful to refuse to sell or rent, to refuse to negotiate, or to discriminate in the terms of a sale or rental because of race, color, religion, or national origin.The right was announced without machinery to guarantee it. The Department of Housing and Urban Development could investigate and persuade but could not order a violator to stop, and the person denied housing otherwise had to bring a private lawsuit at personal expense.
What did the nation's own commission say was still true in 1968?The Kerner Commission, appointed by President Johnson to explain the uprisings of 1967, reported on March 1, 1968 that the nation was moving toward two societies, one black, one white, separate and unequal. White institutions created the ghetto, white institutions maintain it, and white society condones it.Johnson received the report coldly and its recommendations for large federal programs in jobs, housing, and education were shelved. The report sold hundreds of thousands of copies in paperback, so the diagnosis circulated while the prescription was set aside.
How far would the courts carry school desegregation?In Swann in 1971 a unanimous Court upheld busing as one tool of desegregation and ruled that plans could not be limited to the walk-in school. In Milliken in 1974 the Court held five to four that without an interdistrict violation and interdistrict effect, there is no constitutional wrong calling for an interdistrict remedy.Desegregation ran to the school district line and stopped there. Charlotte's single district could be integrated whole; Detroit's remedy could not reach its suburbs, and schools could resegregate by address inside the same metropolitan area.
What did the backlash build, and what did it win?George Wallace carried five southern states in 1968, winning nearly ten million votes against federal enforcement and the pace of change. Richard Nixon answered with the southern strategy, courting white voters with promises of law and order and slower enforcement. Black officeholding grew in the same years, from a few hundred officials in 1965 past five thousand within the decade after 1970.The backlash won the presidency in November 1968 and set the ground of the next decade's argument. The harvest and the reaction grew together; neither canceled the other, and the courts became the ground on which the contest continued.
Could the country use race to remedy what race had built?Johnson posed the question at Howard University in 1965, arguing for equality as a fact and equality as a result. The revised Philadelphia Plan of 1969 set goals for minority hiring by federal contractors. In Bakke in 1978 the Court called racial classifications inherently suspect, struck down the Davis medical school's set aside of sixteen seats, and still allowed race as a plus in an applicant's file.The settlement of 1978: a quota by race was unconstitutional, and race as one factor among others survived. The remedial argument was narrowed to the diversity argument, and the question Howard had posed remained open when the decade closed.

The lesson in one paragraph. Strong answers sort the decade rather than celebrate or mourn it. Example: The movement won the law. By April 11, 1968 the three great statutes banned discrimination in public life, in employment, at the ballot box, and in housing, and the vote those laws protected raised Black officeholders from a few hundred in 1965 past five thousand within the decade after 1970. The victories were answered at once: the Kerner program was shelved in 1968, Wallace carried five states that year, the southern strategy took the presidency, and the courts that upheld busing in Swann in 1971 stopped Detroit's remedy at the district line in Milliken in 1974. What stayed undone was what law alone could not move: housing enforced by persuasion, schools resegregating by address, and the equality as a result that Bakke narrowed in 1978 without settling.
