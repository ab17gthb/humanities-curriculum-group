Guided Notes: Victory, Backlash, and the Unfinished Agenda

Lesson question: What did the movement win, what answered it, and what was still undone when its famous decade ended?

Unit question: How did the civil rights movement change American law and American life, and what did it leave unfinished?

Key Terms

Kerner Commission: the federal commission appointed by President Johnson whose 1968 report concluded that the nation was moving toward two societies, one black, one white, separate and unequal, and charged white racism with building the conditions of the urban uprisings

Fair Housing Act: the Civil Rights Act of 1968, passed in the week after Martin Luther King Jr.'s assassination, banning discrimination in the sale and rental of most housing

white backlash: the political reaction among white voters against the pace and reach of civil rights change, visible in the Wallace campaigns of the 1960s and the law and order politics that followed

southern strategy: the Republican electoral strategy of the Nixon years that sought white southern voters by opposing federal civil rights enforcement, busing, and the pace of change the movement had set

busing: the court-ordered transportation of students across neighborhood lines to desegregate schools, upheld by the Supreme Court in Swann in 1971 and resisted in cities across the country

affirmative action: government policies that take race into account in hiring, contracting, and college admissions in order to remedy the effects of past discrimination, ordered for federal contractors in the revised Philadelphia Plan of 1969

Bakke decision: the Supreme Court decision of 1978 in Regents of the University of California v. Bakke, which struck down a set-aside of seats by race in admissions and allowed race to be used as one factor among others

G. Johnson's argument at Howard: speaking at Howard University in 1965, Johnson argued that freedom alone was not enough, that a person hobbled by chains for years and then set at a starting line had not been treated fairly, and that the nation should seek equality as a fact and equality as a result, not equality as a right and a theory only. What Bakke settled and allowed: in 1978 the Supreme Court struck down the University of California medical school's set aside of sixteen seats by race and ordered Allan Bakke admitted, while allowing race to remain one factor among others in admissions decisions.

The Reckoning at a Glance. Won: the Fair Housing Act signed April 11, 1968; Black elected officials rising from a few hundred in 1965 to near fifteen hundred by 1970 and past five thousand within the decade after; Black mayors in Cleveland and Gary in 1967 and Atlanta and Detroit in 1973; the Congressional Black Caucus founded in 1971. Answered: the Kerner recommendations shelved by the president who commissioned them; Wallace carrying five states in 1968; the southern strategy winning the presidency that November. Decided in court: Swann in 1971 upholding busing within a district, and Milliken in 1974 stopping Detroit's remedy at the district line, with Bakke in 1978 closing the decade on remedy. Undone: housing enforcement that could persuade but not order, segregated schools returning inside metropolitan lines, and wealth, housing, and schools left substantially where law alone could not move them.

Exit ticket 1. The Fair Housing Act is the Civil Rights Act of 1968, passed in the week after King's assassination, banning discrimination in the sale and rental of most housing; its limit was enforcement, since the housing department could investigate and persuade but not order, leaving private suits at personal expense as the other route. The Kerner Commission is the federal commission appointed by President Johnson whose 1968 report concluded the nation was moving toward two societies, one black, one white, separate and unequal, and charged white racism with building the conditions of the urban uprisings; its recommendations for large federal programs were shelved.

Exit ticket 2. In Swann (1971) a unanimous Court upheld busing to dismantle segregation across a whole school district, ruling that plans could not be limited to the walk-in school. In Milliken (1974) the Court held five to four that Detroit's remedy could not include suburban districts with no proven violation. The line that decided the difference was the school district line.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that keeps the three parts separate and dates them. Won: the three great statutes completed by the Fair Housing Act in 1968 and the thousands of Black officeholders the voting law produced. Answered: the Wallace campaign, the southern strategy, and the Milliken line of 1974. Still undone in 1978: housing and school segregation in fact, the unbuilt Kerner program, and the wealth gap the Howard argument had named in 1965, with Bakke allowing race as one factor while striking the quota down.
