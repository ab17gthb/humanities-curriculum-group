Quick Check: The Signing, April 11, 1968

Directions: Study the photograph and the background note, then answer the questions.

Background. The bill on the president's desk had been debated in Congress for two years as an open housing measure and had gone nowhere. Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated in Memphis on April 4, 1968. Uprisings followed in more than a hundred cities. Congress completed the bill within the week, and Johnson signed it on April 11. Its central title, the Fair Housing Act, banned discrimination in the sale and rental of most housing. The photograph was made by the president's official photographer, Yoichi Okamoto, and is held by the Johnson Library.

1. The photograph shows a routine signing ceremony. Name two facts outside the frame, from the background note, that explain why this bill was signed on this date and not in either of the two years before it.

2. The act's central title banned housing discrimination across the country. State the two limits on its enforcement, and explain why a historian would call the signing both a victory and an unfinished piece of business.

3. Put these in order and give each date: the Bakke decision, the Fair Housing Act is signed, the Kerner Commission reports, Milliken v. Bradley, Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg.

4. A student writes that April 1968 finished the movement's work in law. Using the record from this lesson, name two dated events after the signing that the claim leaves out, and say what each one did to the settlement the photograph records.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Why was the bill signed on April 11, 1968? Any two, accurately stated. The bill had been debated for two years without passage, so the date requires an explanation beyond the bill's merits. King was assassinated in Memphis on April 4, 1968. Uprisings followed in more than a hundred cities in the week after. Congress completed the bill within that week, under the pressure of the assassination and the disorders. The signing date is evidence about how the last statute passed: not on the bill's second year of argument, but in a week of national crisis. (Blueprint 15)

2. The two enforcement limits, and the double judgment. The Department of Housing and Urban Development could investigate a complaint and persuade, but it could not order a violator to stop. A person denied housing otherwise had to file a private lawsuit at personal expense. The signing is a victory because the third great statute completed the legal ban on racial discrimination, reaching housing, the ground on which schools and wealth were sorted. It is unfinished business because the right was announced without machinery to guarantee it, which is the gap the Kerner Commission's shelved program and the later school cases kept exposing. (Blueprint 15)

3. Put the five events in order, with dates. The Kerner Commission reports, March 1, 1968. The Fair Housing Act is signed, April 11, 1968. Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg, 1971. Milliken v. Bradley, 1974. The Bakke decision, 1978. (Blueprint 15)

4. Name two dated events after the signing that the claim leaves out. Any two, with their work stated. Wallace carrying five states in the presidential election of 1968 and Nixon winning on the southern strategy that November, which organized the backlash into national power. Milliken v. Bradley in 1974, which stopped Detroit's desegregation remedy at the city line and let metropolitan schools resegregate by address. The Bakke decision of 1978, which struck down a set aside of seats by race while keeping race as one factor, narrowing the remedial settlement the movement's laws had implied. The student's claim fails because the signing closed the statute book, not the argument: every later event above redefined what the statutes of the 1960s would be allowed to do. (Blueprint 15)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Question 1 is the one to read closely: a student who explains the date by the bill's importance has missed the lesson's mechanism, in which the movement's last statute passed in a week of crisis after two years of failure. Question 4 separates students who can carry a settlement forward through its answers from students who treat a signing photograph as an ending.
