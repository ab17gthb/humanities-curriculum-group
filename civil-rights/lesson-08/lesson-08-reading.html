Victory, Backlash, and the Unfinished Agenda

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Last Statute

Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated in Memphis on April 4, 1968. In the week that followed, uprisings spread through more than a hundred cities, and Washington itself filled with smoke and soldiers. Congress, which had debated a fair housing bill for two years without passing it, now moved within days. On April 11, President Lyndon Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act of 1968, whose central title, the Fair Housing Act, banned discrimination in the sale and rental of most housing. It was the third great statute of the movement, after the acts of 1964 and 1965. It was also the weakest in its machinery. A person denied housing could complain to the Department of Housing and Urban Development, which could investigate and persuade but not order anyone to stop, or sue privately at personal expense. The law announced a right that its enforcement could not guarantee.

B. The Diagnosis

Five weeks before the signing, a commission Johnson himself had appointed delivered the era's hardest official verdict. The National Advisory Commission on Civil Disorders, chaired by Governor Otto Kerner of Illinois, had been charged with explaining the uprisings of 1967. Its report of March 1, 1968 concluded that the nation was moving toward two societies, one black, one white, separate and unequal, and that white racism had built and maintained the ghetto whose conditions produced the disorders. The commission recommended large federal programs in jobs, housing, and education. Johnson, who had expected a different answer, received the report coldly, and its recommendations were shelved. The report sold hundreds of thousands of copies in paperback. The country read the diagnosis and declined the prescription.

C. The Harvest in Office

Measured in officeholders, the harvest was large and fast. In 1965 a few hundred Black Americans held elected office in the whole country. By 1970 the number stood near fifteen hundred, and it passed five thousand within the decade after. Carl Stokes won the mayor's office in Cleveland in 1967 and Richard Hatcher won in Gary, Indiana the same year. In 1973 Maynard Jackson took Atlanta and Coleman Young took Detroit, cities whose Black voters the new laws had made decisive. In 1971 Black members of the House organized the Congressional Black Caucus. The opened professions and the federal service enlarged a Black middle class whose incomes and home ownership the movement's laws had made possible for the first time.

D. The Backlash Organized

The victories organized their opposition. George Wallace, the Alabama governor who had stood in a schoolhouse door in 1963, ran for president in 1968 and carried five southern states, winning nearly ten million votes on resentment against federal enforcement and the pace of change. Richard Nixon, the Republican nominee, answered with a southern strategy that courted white voters who believed the movement had gone too far, promising law and order and slower enforcement without defending segregation by name, and won the presidency that November. The phrase white backlash named the pattern: each advance of the movement produced a counter mobilization among white voters, and national politicians learned to price it.

E. The Courts Become the Ground

With Congress and the presidency contested, the courts became the ground on which school desegregation was decided. In Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg in 1971, a unanimous Supreme Court upheld busing as a tool for dismantling a segregated school system, ruling that desegregation plans could not be limited to the walk-in school. Three years later, in Milliken v. Bradley, the Court stopped a desegregation plan for Detroit at the city line. Detroit's schools were majority Black and its suburbs largely white, and the Court held, five to four, that suburban districts with no proven violation could not be swept into the remedy. Inside the metropolitan line the law had drawn, schools could resegregate by address.

F. The Movement Widens

The movement also taught people beyond itself. Its methods, its language of rights, and its legal tools were taken up by Mexican American farmworkers, Native American activists, Asian American students, the women's movement, and gay Americans after 1969. The bridge stands in this unit's records at Delano, California, where Cesar Chavez led a grape strike from 1965 and fasted for twenty five days in 1968 to hold his movement to nonviolence. On March 10, 1968, Senator Robert F. Kennedy came to Delano as Chavez broke the fast and told the farmworkers they were locked with him in the struggle for justice. Chavez said openly that his movement had learned from King. The borrowing ran in both directions. The farmworkers' boycott was the Montgomery method in another field.

G. The Argument Over Remedy

The last argument of the era was about remedy. Johnson had posed it at Howard University in 1965: freedom alone was not enough, and the nation should seek equality as a result and not only as a right. Affirmative action turned that argument into policy. The revised Philadelphia Plan of 1969 required federal contractors to set goals for minority hiring. In Bakke in 1978, the Supreme Court struck down a medical school's set aside of sixteen seats by race and ordered Allan Bakke admitted, while allowing race to remain one factor among others in admissions. The movement had destroyed a legal system of racial caste in a decade. It left wealth, housing, and schools substantially where law alone could not move them. That unfinished agenda is what the next era inherited.

1. When did Congress pass the Fair Housing Act, and what two limits were written into its enforcement? The act had been debated for two years without passage. Its enforcement was written weakest of the three great acts. The Department of Housing and Urban Development could investigate a complaint and persuade, but it could not order a violator to stop. The person denied housing otherwise had to file a private lawsuit at personal expense. Require both limits; the gap between the announced right and the machinery is the section's point.

2. State the Kerner Commission's central conclusion, and what Johnson did with its recommendations. 3. Give the figures for Black elected officials, and name two mayoral victories. A few hundred Black Americans held elected office in the whole country in 1965. By 1970 the number stood near fifteen hundred, and it passed five thousand within the decade after 1970. Mayoral victories named in the reading: Carl Stokes in Cleveland in 1967, Richard Hatcher in Gary, Indiana in 1967, Maynard Jackson in Atlanta in 1973, and Coleman Young in Detroit in 1973. Any two, with years, earn the point. The Congressional Black Caucus was founded in 1971.

4. Contrast Swann and Milliken. In Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg (1971), a unanimous Court upheld busing as a tool for dismantling a segregated school system and ruled that desegregation plans could not be limited to the walk-in school, so one city and county district could be integrated across its whole map. In Milliken v. Bradley (1974), the Court held five to four that a desegregation remedy for Detroit could not sweep in suburban districts that had committed no proven violation. The line that decided the difference was the school district line: inside one district the court's remedial power ran, and across district lines it stopped, so schools could resegregate by address within the same metropolitan area.

5. Answer the lesson question from the reading. Accept any answer that separates the three parts and dates each one. Won: the Fair Housing Act of April 11, 1968 completing the three great statutes, and the rise in Black elected officials from a few hundred in 1965 past five thousand within the decade after 1970. Answered: George Wallace carrying five states in 1968, the southern strategy winning the presidency that November, and Milliken stopping Detroit's remedy at the city line in 1974. Undone: housing enforcement without orders behind it, the shelved Kerner recommendations, and wealth, housing, and schools left substantially where law alone could not move them. The strongest answers use the Bakke settlement of 1978, the quota struck down and race kept as one factor, as the era's closing statement of what remedy the country would and would not allow.
