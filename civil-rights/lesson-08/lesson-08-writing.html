Writing Task: The Reckoning

The prompt. This is the closing writing task of the unit. Write a developed essay of about 300 words answering the unit's essential question from this lesson's documents: how did the civil rights movement change American law and American life, and what did it leave unfinished? Your verdict must sort the record rather than list it. Name what the movement won, what answered its victories, and what remained undone in 1978, and judge which of the three runs deepest. State the strongest argument against your own verdict, at full strength, and answer it. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and The Reckoning File. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording. Keep the two halves of the record together wherever you use them: the Fair Housing Act's ban and its weak enforcement, Swann and Milliken as a pair, and Bakke's quota struck down beside race preserved as a factor.

Evidence Bank

1. On April 11, 1968, one week after King's assassination and after two years of failed debate, President Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act of 1968. Its Fair Housing Act title banned discrimination in the sale and rental of most housing, and its enforcement let the housing department investigate and persuade but not order, leaving private lawsuits at personal expense as the other route.

2. The Kerner Commission reported on March 1, 1968 that the nation was moving toward two societies, one black, one white, separate and unequal, and that white institutions created the ghetto, white institutions maintain it, and white society condones it. Johnson shelved its recommendations for large programs in jobs, housing, and education.

3. Black elected officials rose from a few hundred in the whole country in 1965 to near fifteen hundred by 1970 and past five thousand within the decade after. Black candidates won the mayor's office in Cleveland and Gary in 1967 and in Atlanta and Detroit in 1973, and Black members of the House founded the Congressional Black Caucus in 1971.

4. In 1968 George Wallace carried five southern states with nearly ten million votes, and Richard Nixon won the presidency on a southern strategy that courted white voters with law and order and slower enforcement. Each advance of the movement had organized its own opposition.

5. In Swann in 1971 a unanimous Court upheld busing as one tool of desegregation and ruled that plans could not be limited to the walk-in school. In Milliken in 1974 the Court held five to four that without an interdistrict violation and interdistrict effect, there is no constitutional wrong calling for an interdistrict remedy, and Detroit's plan stopped at the city line.

6. At Howard University in 1965, Johnson argued that the nation should seek not just equality as a right and a theory, but equality as a fact, and equality as a result. In Bakke in 1978 the Court struck down a medical school's set aside of sixteen seats by race and ordered Allan Bakke admitted, while allowing race to remain a plus in an applicant's file, one factor among others.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A verdict that answers the unit's essential question directly and judges which strand of the record runs deepest: what the movement won, what answered it, or what it left undone. Any of the three can earn full credit. An essay that lists the three without weighing them has summarized the unit rather than judged it. (Blueprint 15)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, drawn from at least two different parts of the record, the statutes, the commission, the officeholding, the backlash, the school cases, or the remedy argument. An essay built on 1968 alone cannot judge 1978. (Blueprint 15)
  • The strongest argument against the verdict stated at full strength and answered. Against a victory verdict: the Kerner program was shelved, Milliken let schools resegregate by address, and Bakke struck the quota, so the second half of the record reads as retreat. Against a backlash verdict: the three great statutes stand unrepealed, officeholding rose past five thousand, and Bakke preserved race as a factor, so the reaction limited the movement without reversing it. Against an unfinished verdict: the legal system of racial caste was destroyed outright, which no earlier decade had achieved, and a legal revolution left incomplete is not the same as a legal revolution denied. (Blueprint 15)
  • The paired records kept whole wherever they are used: the housing ban with its enforcement, Swann with Milliken, and Bakke's two holdings together. An essay in which any pair is split to make the verdict easier has failed the lesson's central discipline. (Blueprint 15)
  • The Howard standard engaged by name at least once: equality as a right and a theory measured against equality as a fact and equality as a result. (Blueprint 15)

Model opening for teacher reference. The movement changed American law completely and American life unevenly, and of the three strands in this record the unfinished one runs deepest, because the country accepted the movement's prohibitions and declined its remedies. The statutes of 1964, 1965, and 1968 stand, and the ballot they protected raised Black officeholders from a few hundred to past five thousand; yet the commission that named the remaining work in 1968 was shelved by its own president, the courts that opened Charlotte's schools in 1971 closed Detroit's suburbs in 1974, and the remedy argument that Johnson stated at Howard in 1965 was narrowed in Bakke to a plus in a file. A full model continues by answering the victory claim at full strength, that caste law was destroyed and never restored, and closes on the verdict restated.

Common weak answers. A verdict built on the signing photograph alone, in which April 1968 ends the story, cannot explain Milliken or Bakke. A backlash account that omits bank 3 cannot explain why the reaction never repealed a statute or emptied the offices the movement filled. A verdict of total failure that omits the unrepealed statutes repeats an error the record does not support: the argument after 1968 was over enforcement and remedy, and it was an argument the movement's opponents had to have because the law itself had been lost to them.
