Writing Task: Which Road to the Vote?

The prompt. It is 1913. You advise the suffrage movement's national leadership, and the budget can fund one strategy at full strength. Write a memorandum arguing that the movement should stake everything on EITHER the state-by-state road OR the federal amendment road. Use at least three of the evidence cards below, and answer the strongest argument for the road you reject.

The cards are summaries of the movement's situation in 1913, drawn from the lesson reading. Quote facts, not the cards: write the history in your own sentences.

Card 1: The Scoreboard

By 1913, women vote on equal terms with men in nine states, all in the West. Forty-one states have no full suffrage, and most referendums outside the West have failed, some repeatedly. New York's referendum campaigns have consumed years and fortunes without winning as of 1913.

Card 2: The Constitutional Bar

A federal amendment requires two-thirds of both houses of Congress, then ratification by three-fourths (36) of the states. The suffrage amendment has been introduced in every Congress since 1878 and has passed neither house since a Senate defeat in 1887. The Supreme Court ruled in 1875 that the existing Constitution does not protect women's voting.

Card 3: The Southern Wall

No southern state has granted full suffrage, and southern legislators oppose a federal amendment as federal interference with state control of elections, the same ground on which the Fifteenth Amendment is attacked. State campaigns in the South have made almost no progress in fifty years.

Card 4: The Momentum Argument

Every state victory enlarges the bloc of congressmen who owe their seats partly to women voters and who support the federal amendment for that reason. Washington (1910), California (1911), and Oregon, Arizona, and Kansas (1912) have just joined the suffrage column, and Illinois in 1913 has granted women presidential suffrage by statute, the first state east of the Mississippi to do so.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that picks one road and gives the strategic reason (reach versus feasibility), not a both-roads answer; the prompt's budget constraint exists to forbid hedging, though credit a memorandum that argues a sequence if it still ranks one road first and funds it.
  • At least three cards used accurately: Card 2's vote thresholds and Card 3's southern wall for the federal argument's difficulty; Cards 1 and 4 for the state argument's record and momentum; Card 3 cuts for the federal side, since state campaigns demonstrably stall in the South.
  • A real counterargument, answered rather than dismissed. The model answers: state-road advocates must answer Card 2/3 (decades of eastern and southern failure); federal advocates must answer Card 2 (36 states and two-thirds of Congress is how amendments go to die) and Card 4 (federal votes follow state wins).
  • Period discipline: nothing after 1913 used as knowledge. A reference to the eventual 1920 outcome in a 1913 memo costs the evidence point.

Historical note for the debrief. The movement, in the event, refused the choice: Catt's Winning Plan ran both, and Paul's militants made the federal road urgent while state wins in 1917 (New York) supplied the votes in Congress. Students who argued sequence over selection have the strongest historical warrant, which is worth saying aloud after grading hard on the ranking the prompt required.
