The Long Road to the Nineteenth Amendment

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A Movement Older Than the Era

The suffrage movement began at Seneca Falls in 1848, when Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott issued a Declaration of Sentiments modeled on the Declaration of Independence. After the Civil War it split over the Fifteenth Amendment: Susan B. Anthony and Stanton opposed any amendment that enfranchised Black men while excluding all women, while Lucy Stone's wing supported it as a step. The split lasted twenty years. Anthony tested the courts directly by voting in 1872; she was arrested, tried, and fined $100, which she never paid. In Minor v. Happersett (1875) the Supreme Court closed the judicial road entirely, ruling that voting was not a right of citizenship the Constitution protected for women. The two wings reunited in 1890 as the National American Woman Suffrage Association, and the argument turned from whether to how.

Two Roads

The state road ran through the West. Wyoming entered the Union in 1890 with women's suffrage in its constitution and refused congressional pressure to drop it. Colorado followed in 1893, Utah and Idaho in 1896. Western legislatures had fewer entrenched machines to defend, territories wanted settlers, and Populist farmers often backed the cause. But the state road was slow where populations were large: New York's referendum failed in 1915 before passing in 1917, and eastern campaigns consumed money for decades.

The federal road had been opened in 1878, when a women's suffrage amendment was first introduced in Congress. The Senate voted it down in 1887 and then ignored it for a generation. A constitutional amendment needed two-thirds of Congress and three-fourths of the states, a bar so high that many suffragists called it hopeless. Others answered that state-by-state victory would never reach the South or the big eastern states at all, and that only an amendment settled the question everywhere at once. The writing task for this lesson asks you to judge that argument.

The Opposition

The antis were organized, funded, and often led by women. The National Association Opposed to Woman Suffrage, founded in 1911, argued that most women did not want the vote, that the home was women's sphere, and that politics would coarsen women without cleansing politics. Behind the public argument stood interested money: brewers and distillers feared, correctly, that women voters would advance Prohibition, and urban machines feared voters they could not control through ward organizations. Southern legislators opposed a federal amendment as interference with state control of elections, the same ground on which the Fifteenth Amendment was attacked. The suffrage movement's own record carried a compromise it preferred not to advertise: some of its leaders courted southern support by arguing that white women's votes would protect white supremacy, and Black suffragists were repeatedly asked to march at the back or not at all. Lesson 8 takes up what that compromise cost.

The Final Push

Victory came from running both roads at once. Carrie Chapman Catt, leading NAWSA from 1915, built the "Winning Plan," coordinating state campaigns with a federal amendment drive and holding the party in power responsible for the result. Alice Paul pressed harder. Her Congressional Union organized a suffrage parade in Washington on March 3, 1913, the day before Wilson's inauguration, and from January 1917 her National Woman's Party stationed Silent Sentinels at the White House gates, the first group ever to picket a wartime president. Arrested for obstructing the sidewalk, the pickets were imprisoned at the Occoquan Workhouse, beaten on the "Night of Terror" of November 14, 1917, and force-fed when they hunger-struck. The publicity turned public opinion; Wilson announced his support for the amendment in January 1918 as a war measure.

Tennessee

The House passed the amendment in 1918; the Senate, after failing once, passed it on June 4, 1919. Ratification then became a state-by-state trench war, and by the summer of 1920 thirty-five states had ratified, one short. Tennessee's legislature took it up in August. The state house vote tied, then turned on a young legislator named Harry Burn, who switched after receiving a letter from his mother urging him to vote for suffrage. Tennessee ratified on August 18, 1920, and the Nineteenth Amendment was certified on August 26. Its operative sentence is one line: the right to vote shall not be denied or abridged on account of sex. It had taken seventy-two years from Seneca Falls.

Questions

1. What did the Supreme Court decide in Minor v. Happersett, and why did the decision force suffragists to win politically rather than legally?

2. Give two reasons the state road produced its first wins in the West.

3. Name the antis' public argument and the two interested industries or organizations that quietly supported them, with each one's motive.

4. Catt's Winning Plan and Paul's pickets are called the inside and outside strategies. Explain what each contributed that the other could not.

5. Why Tennessee? Explain what had to happen in the ratification count before one state legislator's switched vote could matter.

Teacher Answer Key

1. The Court held that suffrage was not a right of citizenship protected by the Constitution, so no lawsuit could win the vote for women nationally. Only constitutional amendment or state political action remained, which is why the movement's history after 1875 is a history of campaigns, not cases.

2. Any two: western states and territories had weaker, less entrenched political machines; territories and new states wanted to attract women settlers and families; Populist and farming constituencies often supported suffrage.

3. Public argument: women did not want the vote, the home was their sphere, and politics would coarsen women without improving government. Quiet support: the liquor industry (brewers and distillers), fearing women voters would advance Prohibition; and urban political machines, fearing a large bloc of voters outside ward control.

4. Catt's inside strategy supplied organization, state victories, and legislative pressure that made the amendment plausible and gave members of Congress a path to yes. Paul's outside strategy supplied moral pressure and publicity: pickets, arrests, and the Occoquan abuses made opposition costly and moved Wilson. Neither alone had both levers.

5. Ratification required thirty-six of forty-eight states. By August 1920 exactly thirty-five had ratified and the remaining unratified states were refusing, so Tennessee's vote was the thirty-sixth. Within Tennessee the house vote was tied until Harry Burn switched, making his single vote the one that completed national ratification.
