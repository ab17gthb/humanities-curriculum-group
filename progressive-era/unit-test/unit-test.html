Part 3: Source-Based Questions (14 points)

Source A. Theodore Roosevelt, speech on the "Man with the Muck-Rake," April 14, 1906

"In Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress you may recall the description of the Man with the Muck-rake, the man who could look no way but downward with the muck-rake in his hands; who was offered a celestial crown for his muck-rake, but who would neither look up nor regard the crown he was offered, but continued to rake to himself the filth of the floor. ... I hail as a benefactor every writer or speaker, every man who, on the platform or in book, magazine, or newspaper, with merciless severity makes such attack, provided always that he in his turn remembers that the attack is of use only if it is absolutely truthful."

2. (2 points) What warning does the figure of the Man with the Muck-rake carry, and how does it qualify the praise in the second quotation?

Part 3.

1. He welcomes severe attacks on corruption as the work of a benefactor, and he conditions that welcome on absolute truthfulness. One point each.

2. The figure can look only downward at filth and refuses the offered crown, so a writer who sees only corruption becomes a force for distortion and cynicism rather than reform. The praise holds for truthful exposure aimed at fixing evils, not for raking filth as an end in itself.

3. Roosevelt (about 27 percent) plus Taft (about 23 percent) totals about 50 percent of the popular vote, against Wilson's about 42 percent. The Republican-leaning vote was a majority, split between two candidates.

4. The plurality mechanism under winner-take-all state electoral votes. Wilson finished first in state after state because his opponents divided the majority, so about 42 percent nationally converted into 435 of 531 electoral votes.

5. It prohibits the United States and any state from denying or abridging a citizen's right to vote on account of sex. The second sentence gives Congress enforcement power through legislation, the standard enforcement clause also used in the Reconstruction amendments.

6. Four points: one for a clear evaluation (holds partly, or mostly false, either defended), one for accurate use of Source A (exposure praised but conditioned on truth, and Roosevelt warning against exposure as an end), one for using Source B or C to show a second mechanism (party organization and the split deciding 1912; or the amendment process of Congress plus thirty-six state ratifications deciding suffrage, a seventy-two-year political campaign rather than a publication event), and one for a unit fact beyond the sources (for example, the Jungle to the 1906 acts supports the claim, while the NAACP's litigation or the direct primary's machinery qualifies it).
