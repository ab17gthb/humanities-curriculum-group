The Muckrakers

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A Name Meant as an Insult

In April 1906, President Theodore Roosevelt gave a speech criticizing journalists who, in his view, did nothing but hunt for filth. He compared them to a character in Pilgrim's Progress, John Bunyan's allegory, who raked up muck from the floor and never looked up. Roosevelt meant the comparison to sting. The writers took it as a badge of honor instead. From then on they were the muckrakers, and the name fit their method. Dig into the dirt of American life, pile it where everyone could see it, and make looking away impossible.

Lesson 1 described four problems: work, the city, corruption, and corporate power. The muckrakers' contribution was to make those problems impossible to ignore. Reform laws needed votes, and votes needed readers who were angry. The muckrakers supplied the anger, one documented fact at a time.

The Magazines That Carried Them

Exposure needed a delivery system, and the new mass-circulation magazines provided it. In the 1890s, magazines such as McClure's cut their price to ten or fifteen cents, printed on inexpensive paper, and paid their bills with national advertising instead of subscriptions alone. Circulation climbed into the hundreds of thousands. That arithmetic mattered. A magazine earning money from half a million readers could pay a writer to spend months, even years, on one investigation.

The result arrived in November 1902, when McClure's carried Ida Tarbell's history of Standard Oil, Lincoln Steffens on corruption in St. Louis, and Ray Stannard Baker on railroads in the same run of issues. The editor, S. S. McClure, had assembled three investigations at once, and readers understood that the corruption in one city and the monopoly in one industry were parts of a single pattern.

Five Exposés That Moved the Country

Ida Tarbell and Standard Oil. Tarbell's father had been an independent oil man ruined by Rockefeller's combination, but her History of the Standard Oil Company, serialized in McClure's from 1902 to 1904, was not a memoir. It was a documented account of rebates, espionage, and coerced buyouts, built from court records and interviews. In 1911 the Supreme Court ordered Standard Oil broken into separate companies.

Lincoln Steffens and the cities. Steffens traveled from city to city and showed that corruption was not a few bad men but a system linking businessmen who paid bribes and officials who took them. His articles, collected as The Shame of the Cities (1904), made readers in honest-looking cities ask who was buying their own government.

Upton Sinclair and the meatpackers. Sinclair spent seven weeks in Chicago's Packingtown and wrote The Jungle (1906), a novel about an immigrant family destroyed by the meat industry. He wanted readers to demand justice for workers. Instead they fixated on the filth going into their food: rats, spoiled meat, and worse, ground into sausage. Sinclair later said he had aimed at the public's heart and hit its stomach instead. Within months Congress passed the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act, both in 1906.

David Graham Phillips and the Senate. His 1906 series "The Treason of the Senate" named individual senators and documented their ties to railroads and corporations. Because state legislatures chose senators, voters could not remove them directly. The series fed the campaign for direct election that produced the Seventeenth Amendment in 1913.

The generation before. The muckrakers had models. Jacob Riis's How the Other Half Lives (1890) had already shown photography's power to expose. Jane Addams wrote from inside the problem, reporting on Chicago's poor from Hull House, the settlement house she ran, in Twenty Years at Hull-House (1910). Exposure, in their hands, was reporting plus residence: stay, document, publish.

Why Exposure Worked, and Its Limits

Exposure worked for three reasons. The readers were the growing professional middle class, people who voted, organized, and wrote letters. The evidence was specific: named companies, dated documents, counted deaths, which made denial difficult. And the audience was national, so a scandal in Chicago created pressure in Washington.

The strategy had limits. Readers tired of outrage, and cheaper magazines competed with sensation rather than evidence. Some muckrakers drifted into exaggeration, which let their targets dismiss all of them. By the time war came in Europe in 1914, the magazines and their readers had moved on. But the laws, agencies, and amendments the exposés produced stayed, and the method, document the harm, name the responsible party, demand a specific fix, remains the template for investigative reporting.

Teacher Answer Key

1. President Theodore Roosevelt used it in an April 1906 speech, comparing critical journalists to the man with the muck-rake in Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress who never looked up. The writers accepted it because it described their method accurately and turned the insult into a public identity.

2. Magazines cut prices to ten or fifteen cents and funded themselves with national advertising, building circulations in the hundreds of thousands. That revenue let editors pay writers for months or years of investigation on a single subject.

3. Sinclair meant to win sympathy for meatpacking workers (the heart) but readers reacted to the contaminated food (the stomach). The Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act followed, both in 1906.

4. Tarbell: Standard Oil's methods (rebates, espionage, forced buyouts); result, the Supreme Court ordered the trust dissolved in 1911. Steffens: the system of municipal corruption linking bribe-paying businessmen and bribe-taking officials; result, national attention to city corruption and a model for investigating it as a system rather than individual scandal. Phillips is also acceptable: the Senate's corporate ties; result, momentum toward the Seventeenth Amendment (1913).

5. Any two: the middle-class readership voted and organized; the evidence was specific and documented, hard to deny; the national audience turned local scandals into federal pressure. Limit: reader fatigue, competition from sensational magazines, exaggeration by some writers, or attention shifting to the European war after 1914.
