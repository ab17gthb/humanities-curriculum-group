Be a Muckraker: Write Your Own Exposé

Directions: Tarbell, Steffens, and Sinclair changed laws by documenting a problem so specifically that the public could not ignore it. Your job is to do the same thing at your scale. Pick a real problem in your school, your town, or an industry you know. Gather the evidence first, then write the exposé.

Part 1: Test Your Target

The muckrakers chose targets that passed three tests. Yours must pass all three before you write.

  • Someone is hurt. A real group of people is worse off because of this problem.
  • Someone is responsible. A person, company, or office profits from the problem or is supposed to fix it and does not.
  • Evidence exists. You can document the problem with facts, numbers, photographs, interviews, or records, not just opinions.

Part 3: Write the Exposé

Follow the muckraker structure. Lead with the harm. Support every claim with your evidence. End with one specific demand: the law, rule, or practice that must change.

Headline:

Opening paragraph. Who is hurt, where, and since when. Start with a person or a scene, the way Sinclair started in Packingtown.

Evidence paragraphs. Present your three pieces of evidence in order of strength. Name names and give numbers.

Closing paragraph. State your one specific demand, and say who has the power to grant it.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Running the activity. Approve targets at the end of Part 1 before students gather evidence; the three tests on the page do most of the work. Reject targets that are gossip about named students or staff. Public problems (facilities, policies, local businesses, national industries) are the right scale. Require at least one piece of evidence a student gathered directly: a count, a photograph, an interview, or a measurement.

Full credit is awarded when the student:

  • Names a specific responsible party and a specific group that is hurt.
  • Supports the central claim with at least three distinct pieces of evidence, each with a stated source.
  • States the other side's defense fairly and answers it with evidence rather than insults.
  • Ends with one demand that a named decision-maker could actually grant.
  • Keeps the factual claims accurate. Exaggeration is the failure mode of real muckraking too; deduct for any claim the evidence does not support.

Connection. Grade against the lesson's method, not the student's writing polish: document the harm, name the responsible party, demand a specific fix. Students who finish early can compare their exposé to Sinclair's problem: did they aim at the heart or the stomach, and which did they hit?
