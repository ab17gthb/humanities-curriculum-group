Ballot Simulation: One Day at the Ballot Box

Directions: Welcome to Millbrook, a fictional mill town, on election day in 1908. Millbrook has adopted all four tools of direct democracy, and all four appear on today's ballot. Read each item, mark your vote, and write your reason. There are no trick answers. Your grade comes from the quality of your reasons.

Item 1: Proposition 4, the Factory Inspection Law (Initiative)

Citizens of Millbrook collected 2,300 signatures to place this law on the ballot after the legislature refused to consider it. The law requires state inspectors to visit every factory twice a year, sets fines for blocked fire exits, and charges factory owners a small annual fee to pay the inspectors.

For: The Millbrook mill fire killed nine workers last spring behind a locked door. Against: Owners say the fees will force the smallest mills to close.

☐ YES, enact the law     ☐ NO, reject it

Your reason, in one or two sentences:

Item 2: The Streetcar Franchise (Referendum)

The Millbrook council voted 5 to 4 to give the Union Streetcar Company a 30-year monopoly on the town's streetcar lines, at fares the company sets. Opponents gathered signatures to send the decision to the voters. The company promises new lines to the mill district within two years if the franchise stands.

☐ YES, approve the council's franchise     ☐ NO, repeal it

Your reason, in one or two sentences:

Item 3: The Recall of Mayor Thomas Brandt (Recall)

Mayor Brandt, elected two years ago, took campaign money from the Union Streetcar Company and then voted for its franchise. He has broken no law that a prosecutor can prove. Petitioners collected enough signatures to force this vote on whether he finishes his term.

His defense: The franchise was good policy, the donation was legal, and recalls should be reserved for crimes.

☐ RECALL the mayor     ☐ KEEP the mayor

Your reason, in one or two sentences:

Item 4: The Reform Party Primary for Governor (Direct Primary)

Until this year, Millbrook's party conventions picked the nominee in a closed room. This is the first primary, and the party's voters choose directly.

Candidate A, Helen Ostrander: A former state factory inspector. Promises factory laws and a tax on railroad property. Opposed by most of the party's donors. Candidate B, Marcus Webb: A railroad attorney endorsed by the convention bosses. Promises low taxes and "harmony with business."

Part 2: After the Tally

Your teacher will tally the class vote for each item on the board. Record the results, then answer.

1. Which item produced the closest vote in our class? What does the closeness tell you about that issue?

2. Which of the four tools was easiest to use well as a voter, and which demanded the most knowledge? Explain.

3. A Millbrook boss from 1890 would hate this entire ballot. Pick the one item he would hate most and explain why.

Teacher Guide

Setup. Run the four votes in order, tallying publicly after all ballots are in so later items do not anchor earlier ones. Ten minutes of reading and voting, ten of tally and discussion is the right pace. The packet works on paper as printed; no materials needed.

Scoring. There are no correct votes. Award full credit for reasons that cite a fact from the ballot item (the locked door, the 30-year term, the legal donation, the candidates' records). Deduct for reasons that restate the vote ("because it is good") without a fact. Part 2 question 3 has a strongest answer: Item 4, the primary, because it removes the boss's core function; accept Item 3 (recall) if the student argues it makes every official permanently accountable to voters.

Discussion prompts if time allows. Should a recall require proof of a crime, or is losing voters' confidence enough? Millbrook's franchise vote pits a monopoly's promise against competition: what information would a voter need that the ballot does not give? Connect back to the reading: every item on this ballot exists because reformers decided the intermediaries could not be trusted.
