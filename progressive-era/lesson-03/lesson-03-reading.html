Reforming Government

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

The Problem With the Machinery

The muckrakers exposed who was buying American government. The political reformers asked the next question: why was it so easy to buy? Their answer pointed at the machinery itself. Party bosses and convention delegates chose the candidates, so voters picked between men the machine had already approved. State legislatures chose United States senators, so a corporation needed to purchase only a few dozen legislators to own a senator. City councils answered to ward organizations. At every level, a small number of people stood between the voter and the decision, and those people could be reached, pressured, or bought.

The reformers' solution was direct democracy: move decisions from intermediaries to voters themselves. Between 1898 and 1918 most states adopted at least one of the new tools, and the states, in the phrase of the era, became laboratories where the tools were tested before the nation used them.

Four Tools of Direct Democracy

The initiative lets citizens write a proposed law themselves, gather signatures on a petition, and place it directly on the ballot for voters to approve or reject. The referendum sends a law, either one proposed by petition or one passed by the legislature, to the voters for a final decision. The recall lets voters remove an elected official before the term expires, by petition and a special vote. The direct primary takes the choice of a party's candidates away from bosses and conventions and gives it to the party's own voters in an election.

South Dakota adopted the statewide initiative and referendum first, in 1898. Oregon built the most complete system after 1902 and gave the tools their common name, the Oregon System. Wisconsin, under Governor Robert M. La Follette, passed the first statewide direct primary law in 1903. None of the tools removed legislatures. They gave voters a way to act when legislatures would not, and a way to threaten legislators who were considering not acting.

The Wisconsin Idea

La Follette's Wisconsin became the model state, and his method mattered as much as his laws. The Wisconsin Idea held that government should use trained experts the way a business used engineers. La Follette brought professors from the University of Wisconsin into state service to draft bills, staff commissions, and measure results. His railroad commission set rates on evidence instead of influence. Wisconsin taxed corporations more fairly, regulated railroads, and passed the direct primary, and delegations from other states traveled to Madison to study how it was done. La Follette left the governorship for the United States Senate in 1906 and carried the program national.

Reforming the Cities

Cities experimented fastest because their failures were visible. After a hurricane devastated Galveston, Texas, in 1900, the city replaced its mayor and council with a small elected commission, each member running one department. The commission government spread to hundreds of cities. A second invention, the council-manager plan, kept an elected council for policy but hired a professional manager to run daily operations, the way a school board hires a superintendent. Both plans tried to substitute administration for politics. Both also reduced the influence of immigrant and working-class voters who had used ward politics to win a share of power, a cost the reformers rarely advertised.

The Seventeenth Amendment

The largest machinery failure sat in Washington. Because state legislatures elected senators, Senate seats became prizes for rich men, and deadlocked legislatures sometimes left seats empty for years. David Graham Phillips's "Treason of the Senate," from Lesson 2, documented senators serving railroad and corporate interests. States began routing around the Constitution: Oregon and others let voters choose senators in a popular vote and pressured legislators to ratify the choice. In 1913 the Seventeenth Amendment made the workaround the law, establishing direct election of senators by the voters of each state.

The Same Machinery, a Moral Cause

Direct democracy tools were neutral; any organized cause could use them. The era's largest moral campaign, the drive to prohibit alcohol, used petitions, referenda, and pressure politics at national scale. The Woman's Christian Temperance Union and the Anti-Saloon League built the campaign for decades, town by town and state by state, until the Eighteenth Amendment prohibited the manufacture and sale of alcohol in 1919. Lesson 8 returns to Prohibition, because its results taught the country something about the limits of reform itself.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Candidates, senators, and city governments were all chosen through small groups of intermediaries (bosses, conventions, legislatures, ward organizations) who could be pressured or bought. Exposure alone could not fix a selection process that kept purchasable people in charge.

2. Initiative: citizens draft a law, petition it onto the ballot, and voters enact or reject it. Referendum: a law (proposed by petition or passed by the legislature) is sent to voters for final approval or repeal. Recall: citizens petition for a special vote to remove an elected official before the term ends.

3. The Wisconsin Idea was La Follette's program of governing through trained experts: university professors drafting legislation, evidence-based commissions such as the railroad commission, fairer corporate taxation, and the direct primary. Other states studied Wisconsin because it functioned as a working demonstration that the reforms operated in practice, not only in speeches.

4. States such as Oregon held popular votes for senator and pressured or pledged their legislatures to ratify the voters' choice, making legislative election a formality. Enough states did this that the constitutional amendment followed in 1913.

5. Commission and manager governments weakened ward politics, which had been the route by which immigrant and working-class voters won jobs, services, and representation. That group paid the cost of reforms sold as purely administrative improvements.
