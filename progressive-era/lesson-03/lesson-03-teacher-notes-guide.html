Reforming Government

Progressive Era · Lesson 3 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Progressive Era, Lesson 3 of 8. Lesson 2 ended with exposure: the muckrakers showed the country who was buying its government. This lesson asks the next question — why was American government so easy to buy in the first place? — and answers it structurally, with the machinery reforms of 1898 to 1919. It sets up everything that follows: the same tools reappear in the suffrage fight, in Wilson's program, and in Lesson 8, which returns to Prohibition and the limits of reform.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 1, The Problems Progressives Faced (the city boss and ward politics, met again here through Tweed and Galveston), and Lesson 2, The Muckrakers (David Graham Phillips's "The Treason of the Senate," which returns as the bridge to the Seventeenth Amendment). Inside the lesson, the reading and guided notes follow the deck's order exactly — the machinery problem, the four tools, the Wisconsin Idea, city reform and its cost, the Seventeenth Amendment, and the moral cause — and the activity, Ballot Simulation: One Day at the Ballot Box, puts all four tools on a single ballot. The quick check, Reform Terms, tests the six vocabulary definitions.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Lesson 1's city machine and ward politics, and Lesson 2's Phillips series on the Senate. No figure in the lesson needs outside knowledge: every date and name is taught as the documents state it — South Dakota 1898, Oregon after 1902, Wisconsin 1903, Galveston 1900 and 1901, the Seventeenth Amendment 1913, the Eighteenth Amendment 1919. Students met Boss Tweed in Lesson 1 and Phillips in Lesson 2; both return here in new roles.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.Civ.12 (citizens using and challenging laws and policies — the initiative, referendum, recall, and direct primary as tools for doing so), D2.His.9 (change over time — the machinery reforms of 1898–1919, from state experiments to constitutional amendments); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's accounts of the four tools, the Wisconsin Idea, and the two amendments), WHST.9-10.1 (the ballot simulation: a vote on each item, supported with a written reason).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to name and distinguish the four tools of direct democracy — initiative, referendum, recall, and direct primary — stating for each who starts it and what the voters decide; explain the Wisconsin Idea and why other states studied Wisconsin; state the hidden cost of commission and council-manager government and name the group that paid it; explain how states forced the direct election of senators before 1913 and what the Seventeenth Amendment established; and explain how the temperance movement used the same direct-democracy tools to win the Eighteenth Amendment.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening, the map, and the machinery problem (slides 1–6) 10 minutes; the four tools (slides 7–10) 12 minutes; the Wisconsin Idea (slides 11–13) 8 minutes; city reform and its cost (slides 14–16) 8 minutes; the Seventeenth Amendment and the moral cause (slides 17–21) 6 minutes; vocabulary and the turn to work (slides 22–24) 6 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. The activity is "Ballot Simulation: One Day at the Ballot Box"; the quick check is "Reform Terms"; the exit ticket has three parts — name the tool in each of three scenarios, explain why reformers distrusted legislatures to fix the Senate problem on their own, and judge which tool is strongest and which is most open to abuse.

Key vocabulary

initiative · referendum · recall · direct primary · commission government · city manager

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line "Progressive Era · Lesson 3"; the title "Reforming Government"; the standfirst — "Moving decisions from the bosses to the voters."

  • Picture: Joseph Keppler's 1889 cartoon The Bosses of the Senate, published in Puck: giant money bags labeled with the names of trusts looming over tiny senators at their desks, with the door marked as the people's entrance bolted shut.
  • Lesson 2 ended with exposure: the muckrakers showed the country who was buying its government. Lesson 3 asks the next question. Why was American government so easy to buy in the first place?
  • The reformers in this lesson answered that the problem was not only the buyers or the bought. The problem was the machinery: the procedures that decided who held office and how decisions got made.
  • Keep that picture in mind all hour, because the lesson is the story of how reformers tried to unbolt the door.
  • By the end, students should be able to name four tools of direct democracy, explain the Wisconsin Idea, state the hidden cost of city reform, and explain how the Seventeenth Amendment finally reached Washington.

Slide 2 · The essential question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type — "How did reformers redesign the machinery of democracy?"; the unit question in italic beneath it.

  • Today's lesson question sits at the top of the guided notes, with the unit question beneath it, exactly as on this slide: How did Americans respond to the problems created by industrialization, urbanization, and political corruption?
  • Tell students that each lesson answers it a different way. Lesson 1 answered with the problems themselves. Lesson 2 answered with exposure. Today's answer is structural: Americans rewired the machinery of government so that voters could act directly instead of begging intermediaries to act for them.
  • Ask: "What do you do when a mayor is corrupt?" Most students say vote him out. Then ask what you do when the process that produces every candidate is corrupt. That gap between the two answers is the whole lesson.
  • Six key terms today, all of them mechanisms, and an exit ticket that asks students to match each mechanism to a situation in a single town.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "Rewiring the machinery"; the six lettered rows — A, The machinery problem (bosses and legislatures stood between voters and decisions); B, Four tools (initiative, referendum, recall, direct primary); C, The Wisconsin Idea (experts and evidence inside government); D, City reform and its cost (new plans for running cities, and who paid for them); E, The 17th Amendment (the voters choose senators at last); F, A moral cause (temperance used the same tools, and won).

  • This slide is the map of the lesson, and it matches the guided notes section for section.
  • Section A states the diagnosis: the machinery problem. Section B unpacks the toolkit: initiative, referendum, recall, and the direct primary. Section C shows the proof of concept, the Wisconsin Idea, where one state ran the reforms as a working model.
  • Section D moves to the cities, where reformers invented commission government and the city manager, and where the hidden cost of reform shows up. Section E takes the toolkit to Washington and the Seventeenth Amendment. Section F closes with the temperance movement, a moral cause that picked up the same tools and carried them to the Eighteenth Amendment.
  • Three entries on the unit test blueprint live in this lesson: political reforms, the Seventeenth Amendment, and Prohibition.
  • Tell students to keep the map open on their notes; every heading they fill in sits on this slide.

Slide 4 · Section A: The machinery problem (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section A"; the title "The Machinery Problem"; the lines — "Every decision had a man in the middle, and the man in the middle could be bought."

  • Picture: Thomas Nast's portrait of Boss Tweed, drawn for Harper's Weekly in 1871: Tweed's head is a money bag with a dollar sign on it, captioned as the brains that achieved the Tammany victory.
  • Nast meant it literally: in the machine system, money did the thinking.
  • Open section A by returning to Tweed, whom students met in Lesson 1 as the type of the city boss. Today's question is narrower and more useful than whether Tweed was wicked. It is why the system kept producing Tweeds.
  • The reformers' answer was that between the voter and every decision stood a small group of intermediaries: bosses, convention delegates, legislators, ward men. Small groups can be reached, pressured, or bought, and as long as they stood there, exposure changed faces without changing outcomes.

Slide 5 · The people in between

On the slide: four bullets — Party bosses and conventions chose the candidates; State legislatures chose United States senators; City councils answered to ward organizations; At every level, a few people stood between the voter and the decision.

  • Work through the chain of intermediaries slowly, because reading question 1 and the first guided notes blanks rest on it.
  • Candidates were chosen by party bosses and convention delegates, so on election day voters picked between men the machine had already approved. Senators were chosen by state legislatures, so a corporation that wanted a friendly senator did not need to persuade a state's voters; it needed to purchase a few dozen legislators.
  • City councils answered to ward organizations, which delivered the votes that kept councilmen in office. At every level, a small number of people stood between the voter and the decision, and those people could be reached, pressured, or bought.
  • The reformers' conclusion, in the teacher key's phrasing, was that exposure alone could not fix a selection process that kept purchasable people in charge. The machinery itself had to change.
  • Have students complete section A of the notes before moving on.

Slide 6 · The reformers' answer: direct democracy

On the slide: three bullets — Direct democracy moved decisions to the voters themselves; Between 1898 and 1918, most states adopted at least one tool; The states became laboratories, testing tools for the nation.

  • The reformers' solution was direct democracy: move decisions from intermediaries to voters themselves. Between 1898 and 1918, most states adopted at least one of the new tools, and the states became, in the phrase of the era, laboratories where the tools were tested before the nation used them.
  • Two points deserve emphasis. First, the reform was additive, not revolutionary. None of the tools removed legislatures or abolished representative government. They gave voters a way to act when legislatures would not, and a way to threaten legislators who were considering not acting.
  • Second, the spread was fast because it was state by state. A reform that failed in one legislature could win in another state the next year, and each success became evidence for the next campaign.
  • The four tools themselves are the next section, and students should be ready to distinguish them, because the exit ticket depends on the distinctions.

Slide 7 · Section B: Four tools (divider)

On the slide: the section label "Section B"; the title "Four Tools"; the standfirst — "Four ways for voters to act when legislatures would not."

  • This section is the toolkit, and it is the most heavily tested part of the lesson. Four tools: initiative, referendum, recall, and direct primary.
  • Give students a shape for remembering them before the details land. The initiative creates law. The referendum decides law. The recall removes an official. The direct primary chooses the candidates.
  • Two of the tools move lawmaking to the voters, one moves removal to the voters, and one moves nominations to the voters. What unites all four is the bypass: each one routes around a body the reformers no longer trusted.
  • The next two slides take the tools in pairs, exactly as the guided notes present them, and the slide after that covers where each tool first appeared. Students should fill the lettered blanks in section B as each tool is introduced, not afterward.

Slide 8 · Initiative and referendum

On the slide: three bullets — The initiative lets citizens propose a law and put it on the ballot; The referendum sends a law to the voters for the final word; Both tools let voters make law without waiting for a legislature.

  • Two tools put lawmaking itself in voters' hands. The definition on the guided notes runs: initiative, citizens propose a law by petition and place it directly before the voters. Citizens write the law themselves, gather signatures on a petition, and place it directly on the ballot, where voters approve or reject it.
  • The definition for the second tool runs: referendum, a law is sent to the voters for final approval or rejection. A referendum can begin with a petition against a law the legislature passed, or the legislature itself can send a law down for a final decision.
  • The distinction students must hold is who starts the process and what is being decided. The initiative starts with citizens and produces a new law. The referendum is a yes or no verdict on a law that already exists or has been proposed.
  • Reading question 2 asks for exactly this distinction, tool by tool.

Slide 9 · Recall and the direct primary

On the slide: four bullets — The recall lets voters remove an official before the term expires; A petition comes first, then a special vote on a single question; The direct primary lets the party's voters choose its candidates; Nominations leave the convention hall.

  • The other two tools concern people rather than laws. The guided notes define the first this way: recall, voters remove an elected official before the term expires, by petition and special vote. Citizens who collect enough signatures force a special election on a single question: does this official finish the term?
  • The second definition runs: direct primary, an election in which a party's voters, not its bosses, choose the party's candidates. Of the four tools, the primary may have cut deepest, because the convention was where the machine's power lived. A boss who cannot choose the nominees has little left to sell.
  • Note the order on the guided notes: the recall is blank 3 and the direct primary is blank 4, and the first statewide primary law belongs to Wisconsin in 1903 under Governor Robert M. La Follette, whose state is the subject of the next section.

Slide 10 · Where the tools came from

On the slide: four bullets — South Dakota adopted the initiative and referendum first, in 1898; Oregon built the fullest system after 1902, the Oregon System; Wisconsin passed the first statewide direct primary in 1903; The tools also worked as a threat over legislatures.

  • The tools have birthplaces, and the guided notes test them. South Dakota adopted the statewide initiative and referendum first, in 1898. Oregon built the most complete system after 1902 and gave the tools their common name, the Oregon System. Wisconsin, under Governor Robert M. La Follette, passed the first statewide direct primary law in 1903.
  • Give students the pattern rather than three isolated facts: the innovations came from the states, especially the West and upper Midwest, and they spread state to state as each success was reported in the national press.
  • Close with the function that is easy to miss. The tools worked even when nobody used them. A legislature weighing a corrupt bargain now had to consider that angry voters could write their own law, reject the legislature's law, or end a career early.
  • The threat disciplined legislators who were considering not acting, which is how the reading puts it.

Slide 11 · Section C: The Wisconsin Idea (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section C"; the title "The Wisconsin Idea"; the lines — "One state showed the country how reform actually worked."

  • Picture: Robert M. La Follette of Wisconsin, the central figure of this section.
  • Wisconsin under La Follette became the model state of the reform era, and the reading insists that his method mattered as much as his laws. The Wisconsin Idea, the name his program carried, held that government should use trained experts the way a business used engineers.
  • Where the machine staffed government with loyalists collecting payment for votes delivered, La Follette staffed it with professors. Set up the contrast plainly for students: two theories of who should run a modern state. One says government is a prize to be distributed among the faithful. The other says government is a technical problem to be solved by people who know how.
  • The next two slides give the method and then the record it produced.

Slide 12 · Government by experts

On the slide: three bullets — Professors from the University of Wisconsin drafted the bills; Experts staffed the commissions and measured the results; Knowledge was treated as a public resource.

  • La Follette brought professors from the University of Wisconsin into state service to draft bills, staff commissions, and measure results. Each verb matters.
  • Drafting bills meant the laws were written by people who understood the industries being regulated, rather than by lobbyists handing text to friendly legislators. Staffing commissions meant the agencies that set railroad rates and supervised corporations were run by experts with tenure in their subjects. Measuring results meant a reform could be tested against evidence and adjusted, the way an engineer tests a bridge design.
  • The guided notes reduce this to three blanks in section C: trained experts, the University of Wisconsin, and evidence.
  • Have students notice the deeper claim underneath the method. The Progressives believed that the problems of an industrial society were complicated but solvable, and that knowledge, honestly applied, was a public resource like roads or schools.

Slide 13 · What Wisconsin did

On the slide: four bullets — A railroad commission set rates on evidence, not influence; Wisconsin taxed corporations more fairly; Wisconsin passed the direct primary, the first statewide law, in 1903; Delegations traveled to Madison to study the model.

  • The record in Wisconsin is what made other states pay attention. La Follette's railroad commission set rates on evidence instead of influence, hearing testimony and examining costs rather than trading favors. Wisconsin taxed corporations more fairly, regulated railroads, and passed the direct primary in 1903, the first statewide law of its kind.
  • Then came the reputation effect: delegations from other states traveled to Madison to study how it was done, treating Wisconsin as a working demonstration that the reforms operated in practice and not only in speeches. That phrase is nearly the teacher key's answer to reading question 3, so plant it firmly.
  • La Follette left the governorship for the United States Senate in 1906 and carried the program national, which points the lesson toward Washington. But first the cities, where experimentation ran fastest and where the cost of reform becomes visible.

Slide 14 · Section D: City reform and its cost (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section D"; the title "City Reform and Its Cost"; the lines — "Cities experimented fastest, because their failures were visible."

  • Picture: Galveston, Texas, at 18th and N Streets after the hurricane of 1900, from the Bain News Service collection at the Library of Congress.
  • Cities experimented fastest because their failures were visible: bad water, uncollected garbage, bought councils, and in Galveston's case a storm that devastated the city and discredited its government in a single night.
  • Section D covers two inventions that came out of the cities, commission government and the council-manager plan, and then a cost that attaches to both of them.
  • Warn students to listen for the cost as the inventions are introduced. It is the part of the story the reformers themselves rarely advertised, and reading question 5 and the last blanks in section D of the guided notes both turn on it.

Slide 15 · Galveston starts over

On the slide: four bullets — A hurricane devastated Galveston in 1900; The mayor and council were replaced by a small elected commission; Each commissioner ran one department; The plan spread to hundreds of cities.

  • After the hurricane devastated Galveston in 1900, the city replaced its mayor and council with a small elected commission, and the guided notes date the change to 1901. The definition students copy runs: commission government, a city run by a small elected commission, each member heading one department.
  • One commissioner might run police and fire, another finance, another streets and water. The appeal was accountability in a business form. Instead of a council where responsibility dissolved into committees and ward trading, a voter could name the exact official in charge of the water supply.
  • The commission government spread to hundreds of cities within the decade, which tells students how hungry cities were for an alternative to the ward system.
  • Note what the plan kept and what it removed. It kept elections. It removed the mayor, the council, and the ward map that came with them, and that removal is where the next slide's cost begins.

Slide 16 · The cost nobody advertised

On the slide: four bullets — The council-manager plan lets an elected council hire a professional manager; The plan works like a school board hiring a superintendent; Both new plans put administration in place of politics; Ward politics weakened, and with it a working-class share of power.

  • The second invention was the council-manager plan. The council stayed elected and kept policy, but it hired a professional to run daily operations, the way a school board hires a superintendent. The definition on the guided notes runs: city manager, a professional administrator hired by an elected council to run a city's daily operations.
  • Both plans tried to substitute administration for politics. Both also carried a cost the reformers rarely advertised. Commission and manager governments weakened ward politics, and ward politics, corrupt as it was, had been the route by which immigrant and working-class voters won jobs, services, and a share of power.
  • At-large commissioners and appointed managers answered to the whole city's electorate and to professional standards, which in practice often meant to middle-class reformers.
  • Reading question 5 asks students to name the cost and the group that paid it: immigrant and working-class voters. Connect this back to Lesson 1, where the machine's usefulness explained its survival.

Slide 17 · Section E: The Seventeenth Amendment (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section E"; the title "The Seventeenth Amendment"; the lines — "The largest machinery failure sat in Washington."

  • Picture: The United States Capitol as it looked around 1900.
  • The largest machinery failure sat in Washington. Under the Constitution as written, state legislatures elected United States senators, which made the Senate, the chamber designed to be insulated and deliberate, into the chamber most exposed to purchase.
  • A corporation that wanted a senator bought the legislature that chose him, and the price of a few dozen legislators was trivial next to the value of a friendly vote on railroad or tariff policy.
  • This section follows the same shape as section A: name the intermediaries, show why they could be bought, then watch the reformers build a bypass. The difference is that this bypass required amending the Constitution itself, the only one of today's reforms that did.

Slide 18 · The Treason of the Senate

On the slide: the Phillips photograph at right; three bullets — Senate seats became prizes for rich men; Deadlocked legislatures left seats empty for years; Phillips named the interest each senator served.

  • Picture: David Graham Phillips, whose 1906 magazine series The Treason of the Senate students met in Lesson 2.
  • Phillips documented, senator by senator, the railroad and corporate interests that members of the Senate served. His reporting landed because the structure made it plausible.
  • Senate seats became prizes for rich men, who could treat a legislature as a purchase. Deadlocked legislatures sometimes left seats empty for years, so a state could go unrepresented in one of its two Senate seats while politicians bargained.
  • The guided notes compress this into two blanks: legislatures, and seats left empty for years.
  • Emphasize the reformers' framing once more. The scandal was not simply that individual senators took direction from corporations. It was that the selection method guaranteed a Senate responsive to wealth, because wealth was what the method priced. Changing the men without changing the method had been tried for decades.

Slide 19 · 1913: direct election becomes law

On the slide: the big figure "1913"; the label — "the Seventeenth Amendment makes direct election of senators the law"; the note — "States like Oregon had already routed around the Constitution: voters chose, and legislatures ratified the choice."

  • 1913
  • States forced the issue before the Constitution moved. Oregon and others let voters choose senators in a popular vote and then pressured their legislators to ratify the choice, making legislative election a formality.
  • The workaround spread until the formal rule was an embarrassment: state after state was electing senators in everything but name. In 1913 the Seventeenth Amendment made the workaround the law, establishing direct election of senators by the voters of each state.
  • That last phrase is the final blank in section E of the guided notes. Reading question 4 asks how states forced the issue, and the answer is the Oregon method: popular votes plus pressure on legislators to ratify the result.
  • Name the larger pattern for students before moving on. When formal machinery blocks reform, reformers in this era repeatedly built an informal practice first and let the law catch up to the practice.

Slide 20 · Section F: A moral cause (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section F"; the title "A Moral Cause"; the lines — "The same tools, picked up by the era's largest moral campaign."

  • Picture: Frances Willard, who led the Woman's Christian Temperance Union for two decades and turned it into one of the largest organizations in the country.
  • Open the final section with the neutrality of the toolkit. Direct democracy tools were neutral; any organized cause could use them. Nothing in the initiative, the referendum, or the pressure campaign required the cause to be about government structure at all.
  • The era's largest moral campaign, the drive to prohibit alcohol, used petitions, referenda, and pressure politics at national scale, with exactly the discipline the political reformers had taught.
  • The temperance movement is where students see the tools escape their inventors' purposes, which is why this section exists and why the unit returns to Prohibition in Lesson 8.

Slide 21 · The drive against alcohol

On the slide: four bullets — The WCTU and the Anti-Saloon League built the campaign for decades; Petitions and referenda advanced the campaign town by town and state by state; In 1919, the Eighteenth Amendment prohibited alcohol; Lesson 8 returns to what Prohibition taught.

  • Two organizations built the prohibition campaign. The Woman's Christian Temperance Union, led for twenty years by Frances Willard, supplied the mass membership, organized town by town through churches and local unions. The Anti-Saloon League supplied the political machinery: single-issue pressure that rewarded friends and punished enemies regardless of party, applied at every level from the township to Congress.
  • The campaign ran for decades, town by town and state by state, stacking local victories until a national amendment was possible. In 1919 the Eighteenth Amendment prohibited the manufacture and sale of alcohol across the United States, a result the guided notes record in section F.
  • Set the closing note of the lesson carefully. Prohibition was the tools' greatest victory and, as Lesson 8 will show, the clearest demonstration of their limits, because a constitutional amendment could change the law faster than it could change behavior.

Slide 22 · Vocabulary: words for acting directly

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — initiative, referendum, recall. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test's matching section draws on them.

  • Say: "initiative: citizens propose a law by petition and place it directly before the voters"
  • Say: "referendum: a law is sent to the voters for final approval or rejection"
  • Say: "recall: voters remove an elected official before the term expires, by petition and special vote"
  • Drill the distinctions, not just the words. Ask who starts each process and what the voters decide.
  • The exit ticket opens with a single town using three tools at once: voters petition a park law onto the ballot, vote to remove their mayor, and choose a party's candidate for governor. The first of those is the initiative, and students who hesitate here usually fix the hesitation by remembering that the initiative is the only tool that begins with citizens writing a law.

Slide 23 · Vocabulary: words for the new machinery

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — direct primary, commission government, city manager. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: "direct primary: an election in which a party's voters, not its bosses, choose the party's candidates"
  • Say: "commission government: a city run by a small elected commission, each member heading one department"
  • Say: "city manager: a professional administrator hired by an elected council to run a city's daily operations"
  • The exit ticket's third scenario, choosing the party's candidate for governor, is the direct primary, and its second question asks why reformers distrusted legislatures to fix the Senate problem on their own.
  • The teacher key's point is the conflict of interest: legislatures were the body whose members benefited from choosing senators and were themselves the purchasable intermediaries, so asking them to surrender the power voluntarily asked the problem to solve itself.
  • The final exit ticket question asks which tool is strongest and which is most open to abuse; strong answers often name the initiative as strongest because it creates law, and the recall as most abusable because it can punish a single unpopular decision.

Slide 24 · Now your turn: ballot simulation and exit ticket

On the slide: the closing title "Ballot Simulation: A 1908 Mill Town"; the task line — One day at the ballot box in Millbrook. All four tools appear on the same ballot. Vote on each item, and write your reason; the rows — Item 1 (Initiative: a factory inspection law, petitioned onto the ballot); Item 2 (Referendum: the council's thirty-year streetcar franchise); Item 3 (Recall: Mayor Brandt, who took the streetcar company's money); Item 4 (Direct primary: a factory inspector against the bosses' candidate); the exit ticket line.

  • Close by handing the lesson to the activity, the ballot simulation set in Millbrook, a fictional mill town on election day in 1908. Millbrook has adopted all four tools, and all four appear on the same ballot, which is what makes the simulation a review disguised as a vote.
  • Item 1 is an initiative, a factory inspection law that citizens petitioned onto the ballot after a mill fire killed nine workers behind a locked door. Item 2 is a referendum on the council's thirty-year streetcar franchise. Item 3 is the recall of Mayor Brandt, who took the streetcar company's money and then voted for its franchise without breaking any law a prosecutor can prove. Item 4 is the town's first direct primary, a former factory inspector against a railroad attorney endorsed by the bosses.
  • Students mark each vote and write a reason; the grading is in the reasons, not the choices. Then tally the class and work Part 2.
  • Finish with the exit ticket from the guided notes: name the tool in each scenario, explain why reformers distrusted legislatures to fix the Senate problem, and judge which tool is strongest and which is most open to abuse.
  • Next lesson, reform moves from government machinery to the workplace and the consumer — the subjects the tools were built to reach.
