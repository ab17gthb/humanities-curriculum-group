Taft, the Split, and the Election of 1912

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

The Handpicked Successor

William Howard Taft won the election of 1908 with Theodore Roosevelt's active support, and Roosevelt sailed for Africa expecting his program to continue. Taft's record, judged by results, was not a betrayal. His administration filed roughly ninety antitrust suits, about twice Roosevelt's total, and the Mann-Elkins Act (1910) extended railroad regulation. But Taft was a judge by temperament, not a crusader. He proceeded case by case, disliked pressure politics, and seemed to progressives to defend every institution he touched. In politics, the appearance of retreat can do the damage of retreat itself.

The Breaks: Tariff and Pinchot

The first break came over the tariff. Taft had called Congress into special session in 1909 promising lower rates. The Payne-Aldrich Tariff that emerged lowered some rates and raised others, and Taft, trying to hold his party together, praised it in a speech as a good bill. Progressives had expected a fight and heard surrender.

The second break was personal and symbolic. Gifford Pinchot, Roosevelt's forester and a hero to conservationists, accused Secretary of the Interior Richard Ballinger of favoring corporate claims on Alaskan coal land. Taft cleared Ballinger and, in January 1910, fired Pinchot for insubordination. Pinchot carried the story straight to Roosevelt's circle. To conservationists, the firing proved the administration had changed sides.

The Insult

In 1911 Taft's Justice Department sued United States Steel, and its complaint mentioned a 1907 deal in which Roosevelt himself had approved the company's purchase of a competitor during a financial panic. Taft had authorized the suit without seeing the political charge inside it. Roosevelt read the complaint as a public accusation that he had been fooled or bought. Whatever friendship remained ended there, and Roosevelt began listening to the progressives urging him to run.

The Convention Fight

Roosevelt challenged Taft for the Republican nomination of 1912, and the new direct primaries from Lesson 3 gave him his weapon: in the states holding primaries, Republican voters chose Roosevelt overwhelmingly. But most delegates were still selected by state conventions controlled by Taft's organization, and at the national convention those delegates seated contested Taft delegations over Roosevelt ones. Taft took the nomination. Roosevelt's supporters walked out, and in August they formed the Progressive Party, nominating Roosevelt. Asked about his health, he said he felt as strong as a bull moose, and the party had its nickname.

Four Candidates, Two Reform Programs

The general election offered the widest choice of the era. Taft defended the Republican record and the courts. Eugene V. Debs, the Socialist, argued that public ownership of railroads and major industries was the only answer, and won about 6 percent of the vote, his party's best national showing. The real argument ran between the two reformers.

Roosevelt's New Nationalism accepted large corporations as permanent and proposed a strong federal government to regulate them: commissions with power over prices and practices, plus social welfare measures such as minimum wages and insurance against sickness and old age. Woodrow Wilson's New Freedom answered that regulated monopoly was still monopoly. Wilson wanted trusts broken up and competition restored, with government acting as referee to keep markets open rather than as manager of the corporations. Same diagnosis, corporate power. Opposite instruments, regulation versus restored competition.

The Result and Its Math

On October 14, 1912, a would-be assassin shot Roosevelt in the chest in Milwaukee. The bullet passed through his speech manuscript and glasses case; Roosevelt delivered the speech anyway. It changed nothing about the math. Wilson won with about 42 percent of the popular vote and 435 of 531 electoral votes. Roosevelt ran second with about 27 percent and 88 electoral votes, the strongest third-party showing in American history. Taft carried two states and 8 electoral votes. Combined, Roosevelt and Taft outpolled Wilson by more than a million votes. Divided, they elected him, and Lesson 6 takes up what Wilson did with the office the split handed him.

Questions

1. Taft's antitrust record was stronger than Roosevelt's by the numbers. Why did progressives still conclude he had betrayed the cause? Name two events.

2. How did the new direct primaries shape the fight of 1912, and why did Roosevelt lose the nomination despite them?

3. State the difference between the New Nationalism and the New Freedom in one sentence each.

4. Use the election results to explain the statement: "The Republicans won the argument and lost the presidency."

5. Which 1912 program, Roosevelt's or Wilson's, better fits the federal government Americans actually got over the following century? Defend your answer with one fact from the reading.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Politics ran on signals, not only results. The Payne-Aldrich Tariff (1909) looked like surrender on a promised reform, and the firing of Gifford Pinchot in the Ballinger affair read as siding with corporate interests against conservation. The U.S. Steel suit's implication about Roosevelt then made reconciliation nearly impossible. Any two of these three, explained, earn credit.

2. Primaries let Roosevelt prove Republican voters preferred him, which legitimized his challenge. He lost because most convention delegates were still chosen by Taft-controlled state organizations, and the convention seated contested Taft delegations, so the nomination machinery outweighed the primary results.

3. New Nationalism: accept big corporations and regulate them with a strong federal government, adding social welfare protections. New Freedom: break up monopoly power and restore competition, with government refereeing markets rather than managing corporations.

4. Roosevelt and Taft together outpolled Wilson by over a million votes, so a majority of voters chose a Republican of one kind or another. But split between two candidates, the Republican vote elected Wilson, who won 435 electoral votes with about 42 percent of the popular vote.

5. Either answer is defensible. Roosevelt's: modern regulation runs through permanent federal commissions supervising large corporations that still exist. Wilson's: antitrust enforcement aimed at competition remained a standing tool. Credit the defense, not the choice, provided it uses a reading fact (the commission model, the dissolution remedy, or the welfare measures in Roosevelt's program).
