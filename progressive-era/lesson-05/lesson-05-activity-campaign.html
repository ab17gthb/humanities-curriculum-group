1912 Campaign: Make the Case

Directions: You are the campaign team for one candidate in the election of 1912. Choose your candidate (circle one):   Roosevelt   Wilson   Taft   Debs. Build the case on this page, then deliver it by ONE of the two tracks at the bottom: a poster or a speech. Your facts must come from this unit. Invented facts lose points, the way they would have lost elections.

Part 1: The Case

Our slogan (ten words or fewer, and it must name what our candidate will do, not just who he is):

Plank 1: the problem we name, and our fix.

Plank 2: a second problem, and our fix.

Plank 3: the proof. One thing our candidate (or his record) has already done that shows he means it.

The attack line. Pick the opponent most dangerous to us and write the one sentence we will repeat against him. It must be true.

Part 2: Deliver It (choose one track)

Track A, the poster. Design your poster in the box: the candidate's name large, the slogan, one image idea described or drawn, and the three planks in short form. A voter walking past gets five seconds. Spend them well.

Track B, the speech. Write a two-minute speech (about 250 words) in the space below: open on a voter with a problem, walk the three planks, close on the attack line and the slogan.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit is awarded when the team:

  • States a slogan that names an action, not only an identity ("Regulate the trusts" rather than "Vote Roosevelt").
  • Gives three planks that match the actual candidate's platform from the comparison chart: no New Freedom planks on a Roosevelt poster.
  • Supports one plank with the candidate's real record (Roosevelt and conservation or trust suits, Taft and antitrust prosecutions, Wilson and his New Freedom program as stated, Debs and labor organizing).
  • Keeps the attack line factual. The richest true lines available: Wilson, "a minority president elected by a split"; Roosevelt, "he approved the U.S. Steel deal he now attacks"; Taft, "he praised the tariff he promised to cut"; Debs, "six percent is a protest, not a government."
  • For Track A, puts name and slogan at reading distance; for Track B, stays near 250 words with a person in the opening.

Debrief. Ask two teams of different candidates to read their attack lines aloud, then ask the class which attacks were fair. Close with the result from the reading: the two reform candidates agreed on the problem all fall and elected each other's opponent anyway.
