Roosevelt and the Square Deal

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

An Accidental President

Theodore Roosevelt became president in September 1901 because an assassin killed William McKinley. At 42 he was the youngest man ever to hold the office, and party leaders who had parked him in the vice presidency to get him out of New York politics discovered their mistake at once. Roosevelt believed the president should act as a steward of the people, doing anything the Constitution did not forbid. His domestic program, the Square Deal, had three sides: controlling corporations, protecting consumers, and conserving natural resources. Each side used the federal government in a way voters could watch.

First Side: Controlling the Corporations

Roosevelt's first test came against the Northern Securities Company, a holding company that controlled the competing railroads of the Northwest. In 1904, in Northern Securities Co. v. United States, the Supreme Court ordered the combination dissolved under the Sherman Antitrust Act, a law presidents had rarely used. The decision announced that no trust stood above federal reach, and Roosevelt's administration went on to file more than forty antitrust cases.

His aim was regulation more than destruction. Roosevelt distinguished large companies that competed fairly from those that cheated, and he preferred supervising the first kind to smashing them. The Bureau of Corporations (1903) investigated business conduct, and the Hepburn Act (1906) gave the Interstate Commerce Commission real power over railroads, including the authority to set maximum rates. The pattern was consistent: gather facts through an agency, then act on them in court or in Congress.

The Coal Strike: A Square Deal in Action

In May 1902, some 140,000 anthracite coal miners in Pennsylvania struck for higher pay and shorter hours. The mine owners refused even to meet the union, and by fall the price of coal was climbing with winter coming. Earlier presidents had sent troops against strikers. Roosevelt instead invited both sides to the White House, and when the owners still refused arbitration, he prepared a threat no owner could ignore: the Army would seize the mines and run them. The owners accepted arbitration. The miners won a 10 percent raise and a nine-hour day, though not formal recognition of their union.

The episode gave the Square Deal its meaning. The president had acted as a referee between capital and labor rather than as capital's agent, and both sides left with part of what they wanted. Labor leaders noticed. So did every corporation that might face the next dispute.

Second Side: Protecting the Consumer

The consumer side arrived through Lesson 2's chain of events. Upton Sinclair's The Jungle (1906) disgusted readers with the meatpacking industry, and Roosevelt, who had the book verified by his own investigators, pushed Congress to act on the evidence. The Meat Inspection Act required federal inspection of meat crossing state lines. The Pure Food and Drug Act banned impure and falsely labeled food and drugs. Both became law in 1906, and both put a permanent federal inspector between the producer and the public's table.

Third Side: Conservation

Roosevelt's chief forester, Gifford Pinchot, defined conservation as the wise use of resources: forests, water, and land managed by experts so they would serve the greatest good for the longest time. That meant use, not locking land away. Timber could be cut under rules, rivers dammed for irrigation under the Newlands Reclamation Act (1902), and grazing regulated, so the resources lasted.

The totals remain the largest in presidential history. Roosevelt set aside about 230 million acres of public land, including 150 national forests, five national parks, and 51 federal bird reservations. The Antiquities Act (1906) let him protect landmarks directly as national monuments, which he did 18 times, beginning with Devils Tower in Wyoming. Preservationists such as John Muir wanted some places left entirely untouched, and the argument between use and preservation runs through American land policy still.

What Roosevelt Changed

Roosevelt left office in 1909 having changed what Americans expected a president to do. The federal government now inspected food, supervised railroads, sued trusts, arbitrated strikes, and managed a continental estate of public land. The Square Deal did not settle the argument about how far federal power should reach, and it left some Americans almost entirely out, as Lesson 8 will show. But the referee's chair Roosevelt built at the center of economic life has been occupied by every president since.

Teacher Answer Key

1. The case tested whether the Sherman Antitrust Act reached holding companies and the largest combinations at all. A government win meant no trust was beyond federal power, which the administration proved by filing more than forty further antitrust cases.

2. Earlier presidents used federal force against strikers; Roosevelt acted as a neutral referee, inviting both sides to negotiate and pressuring the owners with a credible threat to seize and operate the mines. Miners won a 10 percent raise and a nine-hour day; they did not win formal recognition of their union.

3. Sinclair's novel The Jungle (1906) exposed meatpacking conditions; public disgust created pressure; Roosevelt verified the claims with his own investigators and pressed Congress; Congress passed the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act in 1906.

4. Managed use is shown by timber cut under federal rules in the national forests and by irrigation dams built under the Newlands Reclamation Act. Either fact, paired with the scale of the reserves (about 230 million acres administered by experts), supports the claim. Preservation alone would have barred the cutting and damming.

5. Accept any side defended with two reading facts. Consumer protection (federal food and drug inspection is now assumed), corporate control (antitrust and rate regulation as standing tools), and conservation (the public land estate and monument authority) all have defensible cases.
