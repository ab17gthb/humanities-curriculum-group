Wilson and the New Freedom

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A President With a Congress

Woodrow Wilson took office in March 1913 with something neither Roosevelt nor Taft had enjoyed: a Congress controlled by his own party. In his first annual message he named the enemy as a "triple wall of privilege": the tariff, the banking system, and the trusts. The New Freedom he had campaigned on promised to tear down all three walls and restore competition. What he built instead is more interesting than the promise, because governing kept pushing him toward the tools of the man he had beaten.

The Tariff Wall and the Income Tax

The Underwood Tariff (1913) cut average import duties from about 40 percent to about 25 percent, the first real reduction in a generation. But tariffs had been the federal government's main revenue, and cutting them opened a hole in the budget. The plug was ready: the Sixteenth Amendment, ratified in February 1913, which overturned the Supreme Court's 1895 decision in Pollock v. Farmers' Loan and gave Congress clear power to tax incomes. The first tax under it was modest, 1 percent on incomes above $3,000 with surtaxes rising to 7 percent on the largest incomes, and it touched few Americans directly. Its importance was structural. Federal revenue no longer depended on making imports expensive, and the government had a funding source that grew with the economy it was learning to regulate.

The Banking Wall

The panic of 1907 had shown the cost of a currency no one controlled: when banks failed, there was no lender of last resort, and a private banker, J. P. Morgan, organized the rescue himself. A congressional investigation, the Pujo Committee hearings of 1912 and 1913, documented a "money trust" of interlocked banks and corporations. Wilson's answer, the Federal Reserve Act (December 1913), split the difference between the two fears of the age. It created twelve regional Federal Reserve banks, owned by the member banks in each district, under a Federal Reserve Board in Washington appointed by the president. The system could expand or contract the currency with the needs of business, and it put the money supply under public supervision without making it a department of either Wall Street or the Treasury.

The Trust Wall

Against the trusts, Wilson first legislated like a New Freedom man. The Clayton Antitrust Act (1914) named specific practices and outlawed them: price discrimination meant to destroy competitors, tying contracts that forced buyers to take unwanted products, and interlocking directorates linking supposed rivals. It also declared that labor unions were not conspiracies in themselves and limited the injunctions courts could issue against strikes, language Samuel Gompers of the AFL hailed as labor's Magna Carta.

Then Wilson built a commission. The Federal Trade Commission Act (1914) created the FTC, a permanent body of experts empowered to investigate corporations and stop "unfair methods of competition." That is the New Nationalism's central tool, a Roosevelt-style commission supervising big business continuously instead of suing it occasionally, and Wilson reached for it within two years of defeating it. His later program confirmed the direction: the Federal Farm Loan Act (1916) built a federal credit system for farmers, and the Adamson Act (1916) set an eight-hour day for railroad workers by federal law.

What the New Freedom Became

Wilson's first term redrew the federal government's working machinery: a central banking system, an income tax, a trade commission, and clarified antitrust law all date from it, and all four still stand. The campaign argument of 1912 was never fully settled, because Wilson's own presidency voted both ways. He tore down the tariff wall as promised, then governed the economy through commissions and federal systems in a manner Theodore Roosevelt would have recognized as his own. Lesson 7 turns to the movement that watched these victories and asked when its turn would come.

Questions

1. What was the "triple wall of privilege," and which 1913 or 1914 law attacked each part of it?

2. Why did cutting the tariff require the Sixteenth Amendment? Explain the revenue problem and how the amendment solved it.

3. The Federal Reserve was designed to answer two opposite fears at once. Name both fears and the design feature answering each.

4. Which of Wilson's creations most resembles a Roosevelt tool, and why? Answer with the institution's name and its powers.

5. Wilson campaigned against Roosevelt's program and then used parts of it. Does that make his campaign dishonest, or does it show something about the difference between campaigning and governing? Defend a position with two facts.

Teacher Answer Key

1. The tariff, the banking system, and the trusts. Tariff: the Underwood Tariff (1913). Banking: the Federal Reserve Act (1913). Trusts: the Clayton Antitrust Act and the Federal Trade Commission Act (1914).

2. Tariffs were the federal government's principal revenue, so lower duties meant less money. The Sixteenth Amendment (ratified February 1913) authorized a federal income tax, overturning Pollock (1895), and the Underwood Act's new income tax replaced the lost customs revenue.

3. Fear of Wall Street control (a private money trust, documented by the Pujo Committee) answered by public supervision: a presidentially appointed Federal Reserve Board in Washington. Fear of pure government or political control answered by twelve regional banks owned by member banks and spread across the country rather than centralized in one institution.

4. The Federal Trade Commission (1914). It is a permanent expert commission that investigates corporations and can stop unfair methods of competition continuously, which is the New Nationalism's method of supervising big business rather than dissolving it case by case.

5. Accept either position defended with facts. Governing-explains-it answers cite the FTC, the Farm Loan system, or the Adamson Act as responses to problems that required continuous administration. Dishonesty answers cite the campaign's central promise (restored competition, no managed monopoly) against the commission built within two years. Credit the use of two specific facts over the verdict.
