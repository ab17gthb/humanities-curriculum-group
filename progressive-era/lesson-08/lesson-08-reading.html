The Limits of Progressivism

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

The Question of Who Counts

Every lesson in this unit has described reformers acting for "the people." This lesson asks who was included in that phrase. Progressivism rebuilt the relationship between Americans and their government, but its benefits were distributed along the era's existing lines, and on the largest question of American life, race, the era of reform was simultaneously an era of regression enforced by law. Judging Progressivism fairly means holding both records at once.

Progressivism and Race

While reformers were democratizing city governments, southern states were completing the legal structure of Jim Crow: poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, and white primaries stripped Black men of the vote the Fifteenth Amendment promised, and lynching enforced the system with terror while anti-lynching bills died in Congress. The federal government under Wilson did not merely look away. In 1913 his administration segregated the Treasury and Post Office departments, separating Black federal employees in offices where they had worked alongside white colleagues for decades. In 1915 the White House screened The Birth of a Nation, a film glorifying the Ku Klux Klan. The most democratic administration of the age, by its own description, ran a segregated government.

The Response

Black Americans answered with organization. Ida B. Wells had spent the 1890s documenting lynching as economic terror, not justice. W. E. B. Du Bois rejected Booker T. Washington's gradualism and, with William Monroe Trotter, launched the Niagara Movement in 1905, demanding full rights now. After a race riot in Springfield, Illinois, in 1908, Black leaders and white allies founded the NAACP in 1909, with The Crisis magazine under Du Bois carrying the case to the nation. The NAACP chose litigation as its weapon and began winning: Guinn v. United States (1915) struck down grandfather clauses, and Buchanan v. Warley (1917) blocked residential segregation ordinances. The organizations the era excluded built the machinery that would dismantle Jim Crow two generations later.

Immigrants and the Split Mind

On immigration the movement argued with itself. Settlement workers like Jane Addams defended immigrant cultures and fought for the newcomers in their neighborhoods. Other progressives, trusting expertise and alarmed by the new immigration from southern and eastern Europe, joined the restriction movement: the federal Dillingham Commission (1907 to 1911) produced volumes recommending restriction, and in 1917 Congress passed a literacy test for immigrants over President Wilson's veto. The era's faith in science had a darker branch: beginning with Indiana in 1907, states passed eugenic sterilization laws aimed at people officials deemed unfit, and called it reform.

Labor's Unfinished Business

Working people gained real protections in these years: hours limits for women upheld in Muller v. Oregon (1908), factory safety codes after Triangle, workers' compensation in most states, and the Clayton Act's language on unions. But the floor stayed low and the courts kept cutting. Congress's child labor law, the Keating-Owen Act, was struck down by the Supreme Court in 1918. There was no federal minimum wage, no protected right to organize, and no lasting ban on child labor. The reforms protected workers as objects of concern more reliably than they empowered workers as organizations, and the difference mattered.

The Last Reform: Prohibition

The era's final amendment was its boldest. The temperance movement of Lesson 3, the WCTU and the Anti-Saloon League, reached its goal when the Eighteenth Amendment was ratified in January 1919, banning the manufacture, sale, and transportation of alcohol; the Volstead Act supplied enforcement. Wartime grain conservation and the German names on America's breweries helped push it through. Prohibition differed in kind from the rest of the unit: instead of regulating an industry or empowering voters, it criminalized a behavior most Americans had considered ordinary. Enforcement overwhelmed authorities, illegal markets enriched organized crime, and in 1933 the Twenty-First Amendment repealed it, the only amendment ever repealed. Progressivism's arc runs from exposure to institution building, and it ends with a lesson in the limits of legislating morality.

Teacher Answer Key

1. The segregation of the Treasury and Post Office departments beginning in 1913, separating Black federal employees who had worked in integrated offices; and the 1915 White House screening of The Birth of a Nation, lending the presidency's prestige to a film glorifying the Klan. Both came from an administration claiming to deepen democracy, and both used federal authority to entrench the color line rather than merely tolerate it.

2. Litigation: take segregation and disfranchisement to the courts case by case. By 1917: Guinn v. United States (1915) voided grandfather clauses, and Buchanan v. Warley (1917) struck down a residential segregation ordinance.

3. Defense: settlement-house reformers such as Addams protected immigrant neighborhoods and opposed restriction. Restriction: the Dillingham Commission recommended limiting the new immigration, and Congress enacted a literacy test in 1917 over Wilson's veto. Eugenic sterilization laws (Indiana, 1907) show the restrictionist logic carried furthest.

4. Protection: the state imposed safety and hours rules on workers' behalf (factory codes after Triangle, Muller's upheld hours limits). Empowerment would mean workers' own institutions gained durable legal power, which largely did not happen: the child labor law was struck down in 1918, no federal minimum wage or protected organizing right existed. The reforms protected workers as objects of concern more reliably than they empowered workers as organizations.

5. The unit's other reforms regulated industries, structured markets, or widened political participation. Prohibition criminalized a widespread ordinary behavior and tried to legislate personal morality, which required policing millions of citizens rather than supervising corporations. Its enforcement failure and eventual repeal, unique among amendments, expose that limit.
