Teacher Answer Key

Part 1: Key Terms

Lesson 1: The Problems Progressives Faced

laissez-faire: the idea that government should keep its hands off business and the economy

tenement: a crowded, often unsafe multi-family apartment building in a city

political machine: an organization that controls a city or state government through favors, jobs, and votes

graft: the illegal use of political power for personal profit, such as kickbacks and bribes

trust: a combination of companies formed to control an industry and limit competition

Social Gospel: a movement teaching that Christian faith required improving social conditions

settlement house: a community center in a poor neighborhood offering classes, childcare, and help to residents

Lesson 2: The Muckrakers

muckraker: a journalist who investigated and exposed corruption, dangerous conditions, and corporate wrongdoing

exposé: a published report that reveals wrongdoing the public did not know about

mass-circulation magazine: a magazine sold cheaply to hundreds of thousands of readers and funded largely by advertising

serialized: published in installments over many issues

The Jungle (1906): Upton Sinclair's novel of the Chicago meatpacking industry, whose descriptions of filth in the food pushed Congress to act

Meat Inspection Act (1906): law requiring federal inspection of meat crossing state lines

public opinion: the views of the general public, which elected officials ignore at their risk

Lesson 3: Reforming Government

initiative: citizens propose a law by petition and place it directly before the voters

referendum: a law is sent to the voters for final approval or rejection

recall: voters remove an elected official before the term expires, by petition and special vote

direct primary: an election in which a party's voters, not its bosses, choose the party's candidates

Wisconsin Idea: governing through trained experts, with university professors drafting bills and staffing state commissions

commission government: a city run by a small elected commission, each member heading one department

city manager: a professional administrator hired by an elected council to run a city's daily operations

Lesson 4: Roosevelt and the Square Deal

Square Deal: Roosevelt's program of corporate control, consumer protection, and conservation

Sherman Antitrust Act: the federal law against business combinations in restraint of trade, used to dissolve the Northern Securities holding company in 1904

arbitration: settling a dispute through a neutral third party whose decision both sides accept

Hepburn Act (1906): law giving the Interstate Commerce Commission power to set maximum railroad rates

Pure Food and Drug Act (1906): law banning impure and falsely labeled food and drugs

conservation: the managed, expert-supervised use of natural resources so they last

Antiquities Act (1906): law letting the president protect landmarks as national monuments without Congress

Lesson 5: Taft, the Split, and the Election of 1912

Mann-Elkins Act (1910): law extending federal regulation of the railroads, passed under President Taft

Payne-Aldrich Tariff (1909): tariff law, promised as reform, that lowered some rates and raised others

Ballinger-Pinchot affair: the 1910 fight in which Taft fired forester Gifford Pinchot after Pinchot accused the Interior secretary of favoring corporate land claims

Progressive Party: the party Roosevelt's supporters formed in 1912, nicknamed Bull Moose

Eugene V. Debs: the Socialist candidate for president in 1912, who argued for public ownership of railroads and major industries

New Nationalism: Roosevelt's 1912 program of regulating large corporations through a strong federal government

New Freedom: Wilson's 1912 program of breaking up monopoly and restoring competition

Lesson 6: Wilson and the New Freedom

triple wall of privilege: Wilson's name for the three targets of his program, the tariff, the banking system, and the trusts

Underwood Tariff (1913): law cutting average import duties from about 40 percent to about 25 percent

Sixteenth Amendment (1913): amendment authorizing a federal income tax, overturning the Pollock decision

Federal Reserve Act (1913): law creating twelve regional Reserve banks under a federal board in Washington

Clayton Antitrust Act (1914): law naming and banning specific monopoly practices and protecting unions from conspiracy charges

Federal Trade Commission (1914): permanent expert commission empowered to stop unfair methods of competition

Adamson Act (1916): law setting an eight-hour day for railroad workers

Lesson 7: The Long Road to the Nineteenth Amendment

suffrage: the right to vote

Seneca Falls (1848): the convention that opened the women's rights movement, issuing a Declaration of Sentiments modeled on the Declaration of Independence

NAWSA: the National American Woman Suffrage Association, formed in 1890 by the reunited wings of the movement

Silent Sentinels: Alice Paul's pickets who stood at the White House gates from January 1917

Night of Terror (1917): the November night when guards at the Occoquan Workhouse beat the imprisoned suffrage pickets

Winning Plan: Carrie Chapman Catt's strategy of coordinating state campaigns with a federal amendment drive

Nineteenth Amendment (1920): amendment barring denial of the vote on account of sex

Lesson 8: The Limits of Progressivism

Jim Crow: the system of southern state laws enforcing segregation and stripping Black citizens of the vote

poll tax: a fee charged before a person could vote, used to keep Black and poor citizens from the polls

grandfather clause: a rule exempting voters whose grandfathers could vote, used to disfranchise Black voters while sparing illiterate whites

NAACP (1909): the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, founded to fight segregation and disfranchisement through the courts

Immigration Act of 1917: law imposing a literacy test on immigrants, passed over Wilson's veto

Eighteenth Amendment (1919): amendment banning the manufacture, sale, and transportation of alcohol

Volstead Act (1919): the federal enforcement law for Prohibition

Part 2. Sixteenth (1913): authorized a federal income tax. Seventeenth (1913): direct election of senators. Eighteenth (1919): banned the manufacture, sale, and transportation of alcohol. Nineteenth (1920): barred denying the vote on account of sex.

Part 3, grading notes. 1: work, city, corruption, corporate power, each with an era fact. 2: research, dramatize, publish to a mass audience; Jungle to the 1906 food laws, Phillips to the Seventeenth Amendment, or Tarbell to the 1911 dissolution. 3: recall, referendum, initiative. 4: corporations (Northern Securities/Hepburn), consumers (1906 food and drug laws), conservation (Antiquities Act monuments/forests). 5: tariff, Pinchot's firing, and the U.S. Steel suit broke the party; Roosevelt and Taft together outpolled Wilson but split the electoral vote. 6: tariff (Underwood), banks (Federal Reserve), trusts (Clayton and FTC). 7: Catt supplied organization and legislative pressure through the Winning Plan; Paul supplied publicity and moral pressure through pickets and the Occoquan scandal. 8: any two of race, immigration restriction, labor's low floor, Prohibition's coercion and repeal, each evidenced. 9: accept either thesis defended with accurate facts.
