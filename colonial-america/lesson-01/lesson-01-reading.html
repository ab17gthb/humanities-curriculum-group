Two Worlds Before Contact

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. A Hemisphere Full of People

Begin in the Americas in 1491, on their own terms, in the year before Columbus sailed. The hemisphere held perhaps fifty million people or more. That figure is a contemporary scholarly estimate, built from early records and archaeology, and the true number cannot be recovered. What the estimate establishes is scale. The Americas were not an empty wilderness. They were a lived in world of farmers and hunters, towns and empires, markets and trade routes, crowded with politics of its own.

Farming anchored most of that world. Maize, beans, and squash fed dense populations in Mesoamerica and the Andes and villages across the Eastern Woodlands. Hundreds of nations governed themselves and made war and peace with their neighbors.

B. Tenochtitlan on the Lake

The greatest city of that world stood on an island in Lake Texcoco, in present day Mexico. Tenochtitlan, the capital of the Aztec empire, held perhaps two hundred thousand people in 1500, more than London held at the same date, which makes it one of the largest cities in the world in its time. Causeways joined the island city to the lake shore, canals carried canoes through its streets, and aqueducts brought fresh water in from the hills.

The city fed itself in part from chinampas, raised garden plots built in the shallow lake. Its great market drew tens of thousands of traders on market days. At its center stood the Templo Mayor, the great temple precinct of an empire that drew tribute in food, cloth, feathers, and gold from peoples across Mesoamerica.

C. Cahokia and the League

Far to the north, on the Mississippi River near present day St. Louis, stood Cahokia, the largest city of the Mississippian culture. Cahokia had reached its height centuries before Columbus, between about 1050 and 1200, when its people raised some one hundred twenty earthen mounds around great plazas. The largest mound rose about one hundred feet. Traders brought copper, shells, and stone to Cahokia from across the continent, and its farmers worked the rich bottom land along the river.

In the northeast woodlands of present day New York, five Native nations, the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca, stood joined in the Iroquois Confederacy. Under the Great Law they kept peace among themselves and dealt jointly with outsiders, sending speakers to a council at Onondaga. Farming towns and trade routes covered the woodlands between these centers.

D. Europe on the Eve

Europe in 1491 was a continent of rival kingdoms. Spain had only just become one kingdom. In January 1492 its rulers, Ferdinand and Isabella, completed the Reconquista, the centuries of campaigns by Christian kingdoms to take the Iberian Peninsula from Muslim rulers, by accepting the surrender of Granada, the last Muslim kingdom in Iberia. The victory left their court with soldiers, officials, and a crusading habit of mind, and no war left at home.

Portugal had spent the century pushing down the coast of Africa in search of gold and a sea route to Asia. Its sailors worked in the caravel, a small, fast sailing ship able to sail against the wind and suited to long ocean voyages. Compass, astrolabe, gunpowder weapons, and the printing press completed the equipment of expansion.

E. Columbus Sails, Claims, and Reports

Christopher Columbus, a Genoese sailor sailing for Ferdinand and Isabella, crossed the Atlantic in the fall of 1492 with three small ships, seeking a sea route to Asia. In October he reached islands in the Caribbean. Columbus believed he had reached the edge of Asia, and he called the peoples he met Indians, a name taken from that error. He made four voyages in all between 1492 and 1504, claiming islands and coasts for Spain at each landfall.

On the voyage home in 1493 Columbus wrote a letter announcing his discoveries. Printed in Spain and then in Latin at Rome, it spread across Europe within months. He described fertile islands, gold, and peoples he called timid and generous, who exchanged valuable things for trifles. The letter was a report to the rulers who had paid for the voyage, and it read as an invitation to return.

F. The Columbian Exchange

What followed the voyages was an exchange between hemispheres that had been separated for thousands of years. The Columbian Exchange, the transfer of plants, animals, diseases, people, and ideas between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres that began in 1492, moved crops in both directions at once. Maize, potatoes, and tomatoes traveled east from the Americas and in time changed what Europe, Africa, and Asia ate and how many people their farms could feed.

Wheat, sugar, horses, cattle, and pigs traveled west. Horses in time remade life on the plains of North and South America. Sugar, planted in the Caribbean and Brazil, built an appetite for labor that would reshape the Atlantic world. No government directed the exchange, and no one could call it back.

G. Epidemic and Catastrophe

The deadliest part of the exchange traveled unseen. Europeans and Africans carried diseases, smallpox first among them, into populations that had little resistance to them. An epidemic, the rapid spread of a disease through a population with little resistance to it, could empty a town in a season, leaving too few healthy people to plant, harvest, or care for the sick, so hunger followed sickness.

Over the century after 1492, disease killed on a scale no army of the age could match. In many regions, by contemporary scholarly estimates, nine people in ten died, and the hemisphere's population fell by tens of millions. The empires and colonies of the lessons ahead were built in a hemisphere whose peoples had already been struck by this catastrophe. Indigenous nations were not erased by it. They rebuilt their towns, adapted their farming and their diplomacy, and resisted the newcomers from inside that loss.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the population estimate for the Americas in 1491, and why it is labeled an estimate. Perhaps fifty million people or more. The reading labels the figure a contemporary scholarly estimate because it is built from early records and archaeology rather than from any count taken at the time, and the true number cannot be recovered. Credit the figure, the label, and the reason. The point students must carry forward is scale: the hemisphere Columbus reached was fully peopled, and every later population figure in the unit is measured against this starting point.

2. Describe Tenochtitlan as the reading presents it. The capital of the Aztec empire, built on an island in Lake Texcoco in present day Mexico. It fed itself in part from chinampas, raised garden plots built in the shallow lake, alongside tribute in food drawn from across Mesoamerica. Its size: perhaps two hundred thousand people in 1500, more than London held at the same date, and one of the largest cities in the world in its time. Credit requires the setting, one food source, and the size measure with its comparison.

3. State what Cahokia was, and what the Iroquois Confederacy was. Cahokia was the largest city of the Mississippian culture, near present day St. Louis, a center of trade and ceremony known for its great earthen mounds, at its height between about 1050 and 1200, with some one hundred twenty mounds and its largest rising about one hundred feet. The Iroquois Confederacy was the league of five, later six, Native nations in present day New York, the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca of this lesson, joined under the Great Law to keep peace among themselves and deal jointly with outsiders. One was a city, the other a league, and the contrast is the answer: Indigenous political achievement in 1491 took more than one form.

4. State who paid for the voyage, what Columbus told them, and why the audience shapes how the letter must be read. Ferdinand and Isabella of Spain paid. Columbus told them he had found fertile islands at the edge of Asia, with gold and other riches, inhabited by peoples he described as timid and generous, exchanging valuable things for trifles. The audience shapes the letter because it was written to justify the money spent and to win support for further voyages. Its descriptions of rich land and unresisting peoples are a pitch to investors as well as a report, and students should read every claim in it with that purpose in view.

5. What did each world look like in 1491, and what single change after the meeting was the deadliest? Full credit answers hold all three parts. The Americas in 1491 held perhaps fifty million people, farming peoples and hunting peoples, the city of Tenochtitlan, the mound city of Cahokia in its Mississippian world, and leagues such as the Iroquois Confederacy governing their own affairs. Europe held rival kingdoms, a Spain fresh from completing the Reconquista in January 1492, and a Portugal already probing down Africa in caravels, with compass, printing, and gunpowder in hand. The deadliest change was epidemic disease, smallpox first among the diseases carried west, moving through populations with little resistance. The evidence for its scale is demographic: in many regions, by contemporary scholarly estimates, nine people in ten died over the following century, and the hemisphere's population fell by tens of millions. A paragraph that names the exchange's crops and animals without the disease toll, or the toll without the two worlds it struck, earns partial credit.
