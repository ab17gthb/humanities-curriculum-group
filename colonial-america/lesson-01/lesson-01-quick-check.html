Quick Check: The Letter and the Claim

Directions: Read the passage and the background, then answer the questions.

The passage. I took possession of all of them for our most fortunate King by making public proclamation and unfurling his standard, no one making any resistance. ... Besides, this Hispana abounds in various kinds of species, gold and metals. ... They manifest the greatest affection towards all of us, exchanging valuable things for trifles, content with the very least thing or nothing at all.

Source: Christopher Columbus, Columbus Reports on His First Voyage, 1493 (Gilder Lehrman Institute), as carried in the audited TPSP record. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

Background. Columbus sailed for Ferdinand and Isabella of Spain in the fall of 1492, seeking a sea route to Asia, and reached islands in the Caribbean in October. He believed he had reached the edge of Asia. The islands he claimed were already inhabited by peoples with their own governments, farming, and trade. Spain had completed the Reconquista in January 1492, and Portugal was already pushing down the coast of Africa in caravels. Columbus wrote the letter on the voyage home in 1493, and it was printed in Spain and in Latin at Rome and spread across Europe within months.

1. In the passage, what does Columbus say he did, and for whom? Answer in your own words, using one phrase from the passage as evidence.

2. Columbus describes the islanders as exchanging valuable things for trifles, content with the very least thing or nothing at all. Explain what picture of the islands' peoples this sentence builds for a reader in Spain, and what it invites that reader to support.

3. Using the background, give two facts about the world Columbus sailed from, or the world he sailed into, that the passage itself does not state.

4. A reader argues that the passage proves the islands were empty of any government worth the name. State the strongest answer to that argument from the background and the lesson.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What does Columbus say he did, and for whom? He says he took possession of the islands for the king of Spain, in his own phrase for our most fortunate King, by making public proclamation and unfurling his standard, no one making any resistance. Credit requires the act, the beneficiary, and a quoted phrase. (Blueprint 2)

2. What picture does the trifles sentence build, and what does it invite? It builds a picture of peoples who are generous, unresisting, and without a European sense of the value of their own goods, a picture in which valuable things can be had for almost nothing and land and labor stand open. It invites the reader in Spain, above all the rulers who paid for the voyage, to fund and man further voyages and settlement, on the promise of rich returns at little cost or risk. Students should connect the description to the letter's audience and purpose rather than treating it as neutral reporting. (Blueprint 2)

3. Give two facts the passage does not state. Any two from the background: Columbus sought a sea route to Asia and believed he had reached its edge; the islands were already inhabited by peoples with their own governments, farming, and trade; Spain had completed the Reconquista in January 1492; Portugal was already pushing down the African coast in caravels; the letter was printed and spread across Europe within months. One fact from each world, the Europe he sailed from and the Americas he sailed into, earns the strongest credit. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2)

4. State the strongest answer to the empty of government argument. The passage records a Spanish ceremony, not an Indigenous absence. The background states that the islands were already inhabited by peoples with their own governments, farming, and trade, and the lesson shows the hemisphere's political order at full strength, from the Aztec empire governing from Tenochtitlan to the five nations of the Iroquois Confederacy joined under the Great Law. No one making any resistance is Columbus's report of one ceremony on one beach; it cannot carry the weight of a claim about the governments of a hemisphere, and the peoples' own view of the ceremony is not in the letter at all. (Blueprint 1)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Question 2 is the hinge diagnostic: students who can read the trifles sentence as an argument aimed at investors have the sourcing the unit test will ask for. Question 4 predicts success on the writing task, because it requires the Americas of 1491 to be stated from evidence rather than assumed away.
