Writing Task: Which Change Remade the Americas?

The prompt. Answer the closing question of the lesson in a developed essay of about 300 words: which single change after 1492 did the most to remake the Americas? A strong answer does more than state a choice. It begins from what stood in the Americas in 1491, works through the changes the lesson records, and weighs the change it picks against the strongest rival. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading, stations, and documents. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording, except where a bank item quotes a document.

Evidence Bank

1. The Americas in 1491 held perhaps fifty million people or more, a contemporary scholarly estimate. Tenochtitlan, the capital of the Aztec empire on an island in Lake Texcoco, held perhaps two hundred thousand people in 1500, more than London, and Cahokia on the Mississippi had raised some one hundred twenty earthen mounds at its height centuries before. (Lesson reading, sections A, B, and C.)

2. Columbus took possession of the islands for Spain by ceremony, in his own words: I took possession of all of them for our most fortunate King by making public proclamation and unfurling his standard, no one making any resistance. His letter of 1493, printed across Europe within months, described fertile islands, gold, and peoples exchanging valuable things for trifles. (Christopher Columbus, Columbus Reports on His First Voyage, 1493, Gilder Lehrman Institute, as carried in the audited TPSP record.)

3. The Columbian Exchange moved crops in both directions. Maize, potatoes, and tomatoes traveled east and in time changed what Europe, Africa, and Asia ate. Wheat, sugar, horses, cattle, and pigs traveled west, and horses in time remade life on the plains of North and South America. (Lesson reading, section F.)

4. Sugar, planted in the Caribbean and Brazil, built an appetite for labor that pulled the Americas into the Atlantic slave trade, a forced movement of people that later lessons of this unit take up in full. (Lesson reading, section F, and activity, Station 5.)

5. Epidemic disease, smallpox first among the diseases carried west, moved through populations with little resistance to it. Over the century after 1492, in many regions, by contemporary scholarly estimates, nine people in ten died, and the hemisphere's population fell by tens of millions. (Lesson reading, section G.)

6. Indigenous nations were not erased by the catastrophe. They rebuilt their towns, adapted their farming and their diplomacy, and resisted the newcomers from inside that loss, and the league of five nations in the northeast continued to deal jointly with outsiders under the Great Law. (Lesson reading, sections C and G.)

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that names one change after 1492 and holds it up against the Americas that stood in 1491. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 supports any claim by fixing the baseline: a hemisphere of perhaps fifty million people, with cities and leagues of its own, is what every change acted on. (Blueprint 1) Bank item 2 supports the conquest and colonization claim: the claim staked in 1492 and advertised in print in 1493 opened the hemisphere to the empires of the next lessons. (Blueprint 2) Bank items 3 and 4 support the exchange claim: its crops fed growth on three continents, its animals remade the plains, and its sugar built the labor demand behind the slave trade. (Blueprint 2) Bank item 5 supports the disease claim, the strongest on scale: nine in ten dead in many regions, by contemporary scholarly estimates, is a demographic change no other item in the bank approaches. (Blueprint 2) Bank item 6 disciplines every claim: the peoples of the Americas rebuilt, adapted, and resisted, so no full credit essay writes the hemisphere as passive. (Blueprint 1)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive link is scale against baseline: students should argue about how much of the world of 1491 the chosen change reached, how fast, and how permanently, rather than listing changes in the order they happened.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest rival, such as the point that disease traveled on the ships colonization sent, so colonization stands behind the epidemic too, or the point that the exchange still feeds the world today, answered from the record rather than dismissed.

Model opening for teacher reference. The change that did the most to remake the Americas was epidemic disease, and its measure is the baseline it struck. The hemisphere of 1491 held perhaps fifty million people, governed from cities such as Tenochtitlan and leagues such as the Iroquois Confederacy, and within a century of the first crossing, by contemporary scholarly estimates, nine people in ten had died in many regions, a loss of tens of millions that no conquest of the age could have inflicted by arms. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the exchange and colonization evidence in lesson order, answering the argument that the ships stand behind the sickness from the record, and closing on the rebuilding and resistance of the nations that survived.

Common weak answers. Columbus alone (one sailor discovered America) skips both the world of 1491 and every change after the landfall. Crops only (the potato fed Europe) answers a question about Europe, not about the Americas. Disease stated without the baseline (many people died) skips the scale that makes the claim arguable, and a claim with no rival stated earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a weighing, not a verdict alone.
