Teacher Notes

Lesson 1 · Two Worlds Before Contact

These are the speaker notes from the slide deck, in slide order, set for printing. The deck itself carries the main ideas in a few fragments per slide; the teaching is here. Print this and keep it beside you while you teach. Each entry is headed by its slide number and shows what is on that slide, so you can find your place without flipping back to the screen. Vocabulary definitions match the student guided notes word for word.

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: Two Worlds Before Contact · The Americas and Europe in 1491, and the meeting that remade both

The image shows the printed map of Tenochtitlan issued at Nuremberg in 1524, a colorized woodcut published by Friedrich Peypus, probably after a drawing made by one of the men who sailed with Cortes. The original is held by the Newberry Library in Chicago. Hold the image in front of students before any content. Europe's first famous picture of an American city was a picture of a capital that held more people than London, drawn on an island in a lake, with streets, canals, and a great temple at its center. This lesson begins in 1491, one year before Columbus sailed, because the hemisphere he reached was already full of cities, leagues, farms, and politics. Students will meet that world first, then Europe, then the meeting itself and the exchange and the epidemics that followed it.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: What did the Americas and Europe each look like in 1491, and what changed when they met? · Unit question: How did the meeting of Europe, Africa, and the Americas remake all three worlds, and what kind of society grew up

Today's question has two halves, and students should hear both before the lesson begins. The first half asks for a portrait of two worlds in a single year, 1491: the Americas, with perhaps fifty million people, cities on lakes and rivers, and leagues of nations governing themselves, and Europe, with its rival kingdoms, a Spain finishing a centuries long war of reconquest, and ships and instruments ready for long ocean voyages. The second half asks what changed when those worlds met: a claim staked by ceremony, a letter printed across a continent, crops and animals moving in both directions, and disease moving west with a force no army could match. By the end of the hour students should be able to answer both halves from evidence, and to say why the lesson starts before Columbus rather than with him.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: Two worlds, in seven stretches · A · A hemisphere full of people · Perhaps fifty million, farming and governing · B · Tenochtitlan on the lake · An island capital larger than London · C · Cahokia and the league · A mound

This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section, so students can use the letters on screen to find their place in their notes all hour. Sections A, B, and C build the Americas on their own terms: a peopled hemisphere, its greatest city on Lake Texcoco, a mound city on the Mississippi raised centuries earlier, and a league of five nations in the northeast woodlands. Section D turns to Europe: rival kingdoms, a Spain unified and fresh from Granada, and Portugal probing down the African coast. Section E gives the meeting: Columbus's four voyages, his claims, and his printed letter of 1493. Sections F and G give the consequences in their true order of weight: the exchange of crops and animals, and then the epidemics, the deadliest cargo of all. Tell students the summary table in their guided notes asks them to rebuild exactly this map.

Slide 4 · Section A: A Hemisphere Full of People (divider)

On the slide: A Hemisphere Full of People · Perhaps fifty million people, in nations of their own.

Begin with scale, because students underestimate the Americas of 1491 when they meet the hemisphere only at the moment of contact. The reading gives the population as perhaps fifty million people or more, and it labels that figure a contemporary scholarly estimate, built from early records and archaeology, because no count was taken at the time and the true number cannot be recovered. Teach the label as part of the fact. An estimate honestly labeled is stronger evidence than a false precision, and students will meet labeled estimates throughout this unit. What the estimate establishes is beyond argument: two continents of farmers and hunters, towns and empires, markets and trade routes, governed by hundreds of nations with their own laws, diplomacies, and accounts of the past. Nothing in this lesson, and nothing in the unit, treats that world as empty, waiting, or still.

Slide 5 · A lived in world, not an empty one

On the slide: A lived in world, not an empty one · ▪ Perhaps fifty million people or more / ▪ An estimate, and labeled as one / ▪ Maize, beans, and squash fed millions / ▪ Hundreds of nations governed themselves / ▪ Trade routes

Work the fragments as a correction to the picture students bring into the room. Many arrive imagining a handful of scattered bands in an untouched wilderness. The record shows farming peoples whose three crops, maize, beans, and squash, fed dense populations in Mesoamerica and the Andes and villages across the Eastern Woodlands. It shows trade moving copper from the Great Lakes, shells from the Gulf, and stone from the plains along routes older than any European kingdom in the room's textbooks. And it shows government everywhere: councils, chiefs, kings, and leagues making war and peace and keeping accounts of the past. The point of the section is not admiration. It is accuracy about what contact struck. Every later figure in this unit, above all the death toll of Section G, is measured against the population and the political order established here.

Slide 6 · 50 million

On the slide: 50 million · people or more lived in the Americas in 1491, by contemporary scholarly estimate · The starting count of the unit · Every later figure is measured against it

Let the large figure carry the weight of Section A, and teach students to handle it the way historians do. Fifty million is not a census count. It is a contemporary scholarly estimate, assembled from tribute lists, early colonial records, carrying capacity, and archaeology, and scholars argue over a wide range around it. Say the range honestly and then say what does not depend on the argument: whether the hemisphere held forty million or seventy million, it held cities, empires, and leagues, and the catastrophe of Section G killed on a scale set by that starting population. Students who learn to say estimate when a figure is an estimate, and to state what a figure can and cannot prove, have learned a skill the whole course will use. The next two sections give the estimate faces: a capital city and a league.

Slide 7 · Section B: Tenochtitlan on the Lake (divider)

On the slide: Tenochtitlan on the Lake · The island capital in Lake Texcoco, / from the printed map of 1524.

The image is a detail of the 1524 Nuremberg map of Tenochtitlan, held by the Newberry Library in Chicago. Section B takes up the greatest city of the Americas in 1491, and students should meet it as a working capital, not as a ruin or a legend. Tenochtitlan stood on an island in Lake Texcoco, in present day Mexico, joined to the shore by causeways, cut by canals, and fed in part by chinampas, the raised garden plots built in the shallow lake. At its center stood the Templo Mayor, the great temple precinct of the Aztec empire, which drew tribute in food, cloth, feathers, and gold from peoples across Mesoamerica. The map students are looking at was printed in Europe three years after Spanish forces destroyed the city, a fact the activity asks them to weigh when they use the map as evidence.

Slide 8 · An island capital, larger than London

On the slide: An island capital, larger than London · ▪ Built on an island in Lake Texcoco / ▪ Perhaps 200,000 people in 1500 / ▪ Causeways, canals, and chinampas / ▪ A market of tens of thousands / ▪ The Templo Mayor at its cent

Teach the city through its systems, because systems are what the unit test asks students to describe. Water came first: an island city drank through aqueducts, moved goods by canoe through canals, and reached the shore on causeways that could be cut in war. Food came second: chinampas yielded crops through the year in the shallow lake, and tribute in food arrived from subject peoples across Mesoamerica. Exchange came third: the great market drew tens of thousands of traders on market days, by the counts Spanish visitors later reported. Power stood at the center, in the Templo Mayor precinct of an empire that taxed a continent. Hold the comparison steady for students: in 1500 this city held more people than London, and it was one of the largest cities in the world, built by a people Europeans had not known existed a decade earlier.

Slide 9 · Section C: Cahokia and the League (divider)

On the slide: Cahokia and the League · A city raised in earth, and five nations joined under one law.

Section C holds two different Indigenous achievements, and the contrast between them is the teaching point. On the Mississippi River stood Cahokia, a city of earthen mounds raised centuries before Columbus by the people of the Mississippian culture. In the northeast woodlands stood the Iroquois Confederacy, a league of five nations joined under the Great Law, still governing itself in 1491 and long after. One achievement was a place, built basket by basket in earth. The other was an agreement, built nation by nation in council. Students should leave the section able to state what each one was, in the vocabulary of the lesson, and to resist the habit of treating Indigenous peoples as one undifferentiated population. The hemisphere held empires, cities, leagues, towns, and villages, each with its own politics, and the record names them separately because they acted separately.

Slide 10 · The largest city of the Mississippian culture

On the slide: The largest city of the Mississippian culture · ▪ Near present day St. Louis / ▪ At its height between 1050 and 1200 / ▪ Some 120 earthen mounds / ▪ Its largest mound rose 100 feet / ▪ Trade drew copper, shells, and

Give Cahokia its dates plainly, because the dates carry a lesson inside the lesson. This city reached its height between about 1050 and 1200, centuries before Columbus, when its people raised some one hundred twenty earthen mounds around great plazas. The largest mound rose about one hundred feet in terraces and carried a great building on its top, and workers moved its earth basket by basket, without draft animals or metal tools. Cahokia's traders drew copper from the Great Lakes country, shells from the Gulf coast, and stone from the mountains and plains, and its farmers worked the rich river bottom. By 1491 the great city had long declined. Teach that decline as evidence too: Indigenous societies rose, changed, and fell for reasons of their own, long before Europeans arrived, which is what it means to say they had histories.

Slide 11 · 120

On the slide: 120 · earthen mounds raised at Cahokia around its great plazas · The largest rose about 100 feet · Moved basket by basket, without draft animals or metal tools

Pause on the labor inside the number, because the number alone can slide past students as a statistic. One hundred twenty mounds, the largest covering about fourteen acres at its base and rising about one hundred feet, represent organized work on a scale that requires government: people fed while they built, work assigned and sequenced, a center planned before it was raised. The builders had no draft animals, no wheeled carts, and no metal tools. Every basket of earth was dug, carried, and packed by human hands. When students ask what proves a society was organized, this is the shape of an answer: not a claim about a people, but a surviving work whose construction cannot be explained without coordination. Cahokia's mounds still stand beside the Mississippi, and the activity's third station asks students to state this evidence for the word city in their own sentences.

Slide 12 · Five nations, joined under the Great Law

On the slide: Five nations, joined under the Great Law · ▪ Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca / ▪ Peace kept among themselves / ▪ One council fire at Onondaga / ▪ A joint voice toward outsiders / ▪ Each nation governed its

Name the five nations aloud and have students repeat them, because the unit returns to this league for eight lessons and the names are the respect the record is owed. The Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca stood joined in the Iroquois Confederacy, under the Great Law, which ended war among them and kept a council fire at Onondaga, the central nation, where speakers settled questions that touched them all. Toward outsiders the league acted together, on war, peace, and trade, and its speakers dealt with neighbors as diplomats of a combined power. Inside, each nation governed its own towns and hunting grounds. That division, joint abroad and self governing at home, is what made the league durable, and it is why European officials in later lessons had to treat with the league as a power that could set terms rather than as a population to be managed.

Slide 13 · Section D: Europe on the Eve (divider)

On the slide: Europe on the Eve · Rival kingdoms, a finished reconquest, and ships ready for an ocean.

Turn the lesson to the other world now, and teach Europe with the same plainness used for the Americas. Europe in 1491 was a continent of rival kingdoms, and its western edge held the two states that would reach the Americas first. Spain had only just become one kingdom under Ferdinand and Isabella, and in January 1492 its rulers completed the Reconquista by accepting the surrender of Granada, the last Muslim kingdom in Iberia, ending centuries of campaigns by Christian kingdoms to take the peninsula. The victory left a court with soldiers, officials, and a crusading habit of mind, and no war left at home. Portugal, smaller and earlier at sea, had spent the century pushing down the coast of Africa toward a sea route to Asia. Section D asks students to inventory what Europe held in 1491: ambition, rivalry, ships, instruments, weapons, and print.

Slide 14 · The equipment of expansion, assembled

On the slide: The equipment of expansion, assembled · ▪ Spain unified, Granada taken in 1492 / ▪ The Reconquista completed / ▪ Portugal probing down Africa / ▪ The caravel, built for ocean voyages / ▪ Compass, gunpowder, and prin

Work the fragments as an inventory, and make students state what each item contributed. Unification contributed scale: one Spanish court could tax, charter, and claim at a level no divided kingdom could match. The completed Reconquista contributed men and habits: soldiers and officials practiced in conquest, settlement, and the government of a religious frontier, suddenly without a frontier at home. Portugal contributed the model and the rivalry: its African voyages proved Atlantic sailing, mapped winds and coasts, and pushed Spain to seek a western route rather than concede the eastern one. The caravel contributed the vehicle, a small, fast ship developed by the Portuguese in the 1400s, able to sail against the wind and suited to long ocean voyages. Compass, astrolabe, gunpowder weapons, and printing contributed reach and publicity: a voyage could be steered, fought for, and then read about across Europe within months.

Slide 15 · Section E: Columbus Sails, Claims, and Reports (divider)

On the slide: Columbus Sails, Claims, and Reports · Portrait said to be Christopher Columbus, / by Sebastiano del Piombo, 1519.

The portrait shows a man said to be Christopher Columbus, painted by Sebastiano del Piombo in 1519, thirteen years after Columbus died, and held by the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. No likeness of Columbus made from life survives, and the caption on screen says so by its wording: said to be. Section E takes up the sailor himself, a Genoese mariner who persuaded Ferdinand and Isabella of Spain to fund a western crossing to Asia. He sailed in the fall of 1492 with three small ships, reached islands in the Caribbean in October, and believed to the end of his life that he had reached the edge of Asia. He made four voyages between 1492 and 1504. What he did at each landfall, claim the land for Spain by ceremony, and what he wrote on the way home in 1493, are the two records this section reads closely.

Slide 16 · Four voyages, and a claim at each landfall

On the slide: Four voyages, and a claim at each landfall · ▪ A Genoese sailor sailing for Spain / ▪ Three small ships in the fall of 1492 / ▪ Caribbean landfall in October / ▪ He believed he had reached Asia / ▪ Possession taken

Teach the voyages as a sequence of arrivals in other people's homes, because that is what the record shows when it is read whole. The islands Columbus reached in October 1492 were inhabited, farmed, and governed by peoples with their own names for them. Columbus called the peoples Indians, a name taken from his error about where he was, and the error set the vocabulary of the next three centuries. At landfalls he took possession for the king of Spain by public proclamation and by unfurling the royal standard, a legal ceremony in Spanish form, performed before audiences who had no part in making it and no reason in their own law to accept it. Students should hold both facts at once: the crossing was a feat of navigation and endurance, and the claim it carried rested on a ceremony the claimed peoples never consented to.

Slide 17 · A letter printed across Europe

On the slide: A letter printed across Europe · ▪ Written on the voyage home, 1493 / ▪ Printed in Spain, then in Latin at Rome / ▪ Fertile islands and gold, described / ▪ Peoples called timid and generous / ▪ Valuable things excha

Read the letter as the quick check will test it: as a document with an author, an audience, and a purpose. Columbus wrote on the voyage home to the king and queen who had paid for the voyage, and the letter's descriptions answer their questions in order. The islands are exceedingly fertile, with farms, groves, and fields. Hispana abounds in gold and metals. The inhabitants are timid and full of terror at first, then guileless, honest, and very liberal of all they have, exchanging valuable things for trifles. And Columbus gave gifts, in his own words, so that they might become Christians, love the king and queen, and gather for Spain what Spain needed. Printed in Spain and then in Latin at Rome, the letter spread across Europe within months. It is the founding advertisement of the colonial age, and students should learn to read it as one.

Slide 18 · Section F: The Columbian Exchange (divider)

On the slide: The Columbian Exchange · Plants, animals, diseases, people, and ideas, moving both ways at once.

Section F gives the meeting its master fact, and it is the vocabulary term students must be able to state word for word by the end of the hour: the Columbian Exchange, the transfer of plants, animals, diseases, people, and ideas between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres that began with the voyages of Columbus in 1492. Teach the exchange as traffic, sorted by direction, because students remember a sorted system better than a list of wonders. Eastbound went American crops: maize, potatoes, tomatoes. Westbound went wheat, sugar, horses, cattle, and pigs. People moved too, at first in small numbers westward, then, as sugar built its appetite for labor, in the forced millions of the Atlantic slave trade, which Lesson 6 of this unit takes up in full. No government directed the exchange, and no one could call it back, which is why its effects outlasted every empire that rode on it.

Slide 19 · Crops east, animals west, nothing recalled

On the slide: Crops east, animals west, nothing recalled · ▪ Maize, potatoes, tomatoes traveled east / ▪ Wheat and sugar traveled west / ▪ Horses remade life on the plains / ▪ Sugar built an appetite for labor / ▪ Every crossing

Work the two directions separately and then together. Eastbound crops changed the Eastern Hemisphere slowly and permanently: maize, potatoes, and tomatoes raised the food supply of Europe, Africa, and Asia, and populations grew on American plants in places that never saw an American. Westbound cargo changed the Americas at ground level: wheat and cattle and pigs altered its fields, and horses, spreading across the plains of North and South America, remade hunting, travel, and war for the peoples who adopted them. Sugar is the hinge item of the list, and students should mark it: planted in the Caribbean and Brazil, it demanded labor on a scale that pulled the Americas into the Atlantic slave trade. Close with the property that defines the exchange as a system: it had no author and no off switch. Empires profited from it, resisted parts of it, and never controlled it.

Slide 20 · 9 in 10

On the slide: 9 in 10 · people died in many regions in the century after 1492, by contemporary scholarly estimate · Epidemic disease, smallpox first · The deadliest cargo of the exchange

Let this figure land in silence for a moment, because it is the largest fact in the lesson and one of the largest in the course. The diseases Europeans and Africans carried west, smallpox first among them, moved through populations that had little resistance to them. An epidemic could empty a town in a season, leaving too few healthy people to plant, harvest, or care for the sick, so hunger followed sickness and the dying compounded. Over the century after 1492 the hemisphere's population fell by tens of millions, and in many regions, by contemporary scholarly estimates, nine people in ten died. Teach the estimate label here as carefully as on the fifty million slide: scholars argue over regional counts, and the argument does not soften the order of magnitude. The next section states what this catastrophe does and does not mean.

Slide 21 · Section G: Epidemic and Catastrophe (divider)

On the slide: Epidemic and Catastrophe · The ground every later lesson of this unit stands on.

Section G closes the lesson where the unit itself begins: with the demographic catastrophe stated as the ground under everything that follows. The empires and colonies of the next seven lessons were built in a hemisphere whose peoples had already been struck by epidemic disease on a scale no army of the age could match. That fact explains much that students will otherwise misread: why Spanish forces prevailed against great empires, why English settlers found cleared fields and emptied towns and mistook them for wilderness, and why the labor demands of the colonies turned so quickly to Africa. State the limit as plainly as the fact. Indigenous nations were not erased. They buried their dead, rebuilt their towns, adapted their farming and their diplomacy, and resisted the newcomers from inside that loss, and this unit follows their decisions as carefully as it follows any governor's.

Slide 22 · Struck first by disease, and not erased

On the slide: Struck first by disease, and not erased · ▪ Smallpox first among the diseases / ▪ Whole towns sickened at once / ▪ Hunger followed sickness / ▪ Tens of millions died in a century / ▪ Nations rebuilt, adapted, resist

Close the teaching by separating the catastrophe from the conclusion students may be tempted to draw from it. The catastrophe is demographic and it is documented: disease ran ahead of settlement in many regions, striking peoples who had never met a European, and it returned in wave after wave across the century, so recovery was overtaken again and again. The false conclusion is disappearance. The peoples of the Americas survived the century in the tens of millions reduced to far fewer, and what they did next is this unit's continuing story: towns rebuilt on new sites, farming and diplomacy adapted to new neighbors and new goods, confederacies strengthened, and resistance offered from the first landfalls onward. When later lessons show Indigenous nations setting terms, supplying armies, and holding empires at bay for generations, students should remember this slide: those nations acted from inside a catastrophe, and acted still.

Slide 23 · Two worlds, met, and neither unchanged

On the slide: Two worlds, met, and neither unchanged · ▪ The Americas: cities, leagues, fifty million / ▪ Europe: one new kingdom, ships, print / ▪ A claim, and a letter that advertised it / ▪ An exchange no one directed / ▪ A ca

Build the synthesis as the answer to the lesson question, in the question's own order. In 1491 the Americas were a peopled world of farmers and hunters, the island capital of Tenochtitlan, the mound city of Cahokia in its Mississippian tradition, and the five nations of the Iroquois Confederacy governing under the Great Law. In 1491 Europe was a world of rival kingdoms, its newest unified state fresh from completing the Reconquista, its sailors equipped with the caravel, the compass, gunpowder, and print. The meeting brought a claim staked by ceremony and advertised by a printed letter, an exchange of plants, animals, diseases, people, and ideas that no government directed, and a demographic catastrophe that reshaped every society it touched. The next lesson takes up the first empire built on that ground: Spain's, raised by conquest on the ruins the epidemics helped prepare.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for the Americas in 1491

On the slide: Words for the Americas in 1491 · Columbian Exchange · plants, animals, and diseases moving between hemispheres after 1492 · Tenochtitlan · Aztec capital on an island in Lake Texcoco · Cahokia · Mississippian mound city n

These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them. Columbian Exchange: the transfer of plants, animals, diseases, people, and ideas between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres that began with the voyages of Columbus in 1492. Tenochtitlan: the capital of the Aztec empire, built on an island in Lake Texcoco in present-day Mexico, one of the largest cities in the world in 1500. Cahokia: the largest city of the Mississippian culture, near present-day St. Louis, a center of trade and ceremony known for its great earthen mounds. Iroquois Confederacy: the league of five, later six, Native nations in present-day New York, joined under the Great Law to keep peace among themselves and deal jointly with outsiders.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for Europe and the meeting

On the slide: Words for Europe and the meeting · caravel · small, fast Portuguese ship, able to sail against the wind · Reconquista · Christian campaigns to take Iberia, completed in 1492 · epidemic · rapid spread of disease in a popu

Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly. caravel: a small, fast sailing ship developed by the Portuguese in the 1400s, able to sail against the wind and suited to long ocean voyages. Reconquista: the centuries of campaigns by Christian kingdoms to take the Iberian Peninsula from Muslim rulers, completed in 1492, which shaped Spain's ambitions in the Americas. epidemic: the rapid spread of a disease through a population that has little resistance to it. Students should hold the chain these definitions make when read with the first vocabulary set: a ship and a finished reconquest carried one world across an ocean, an exchange began that no one directed, and an epidemic did the greatest part of the remaking. The activity and the writing task both ask students to judge that remaking from the documents.

Slide 26 · Now your turn: work the two worlds file

On the slide: Work the two worlds file · What did the Americas and Europe each look like in 1491, and what changed when they met? Work the five records in order, then judge which single change after 1492 did the most to remake the Ame

Close by returning to the lesson question and handing the lesson to the work. The activity, Two Worlds in 1491, runs five stations through the evidence: the Columbus letter of 1493 from the audited bank record, the printed map of Tenochtitlan of 1524, a labeled summary of Cahokia and the Mississippian world, a labeled summary of the Iroquois Confederacy, and a sorting record of the Columbian Exchange, ending in a ledger and a verdict on which single change after 1492 did the most to remake the Americas. The notebook page asks students to rebuild six pieces of the picture with evidence. The quick check, The Letter and the Claim, tests the letter's passage against the background of the two worlds. The writing task, Which Change Remade the Americas?, weighs the changes against the baseline of 1491. The exit ticket has three parts: the Columbian Exchange defined; the Great Law explained; and the lesson question answered with one fact about each world and the deadliest change at its proper scale.
