New England: Covenant and Community

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Plymouth: A Compact Signed Aboard Ship

In November 1620 the Mayflower anchored off Cape Cod, north of the Virginia lands its patent covered. Aboard were Separatist Pilgrims and other travelers called Strangers. Some Strangers argued that, landed outside the patent, they owed obedience to no one. Before anyone went ashore, forty-one men signed the Mayflower Compact, agreeing to "covenant and combine ourselves together into a civil Body Politick, for our better Ordering and Preservation" and to obey the "just and equal Laws" the colony would make for its "general Good." Men alone signed.

Half the company died in the first winter. The rest lasted in part because the Wampanoag, under their sachem Massasoit, chose alliance. The epidemic of 1616 to 1619 had killed most people in the coastal villages, Wampanoag among them, and Massasoit needed friends against the Narragansett, whom the sickness had spared. Tisquantum, a Patuxet man carried off by an English captain years before, who came home to an empty village, taught the Pilgrims corn planting in Patuxet's cleared fields. The alliance served both peoples, for their own reasons.

B. Massachusetts Bay: A City upon a Hill

Ten years later a larger migration crossed, some twenty thousand Puritans over the 1630s. They had not separated from the Church of England in name; they meant to build a model commonwealth for England to copy. Aboard the Arbella in 1630, Governor John Winthrop preached A Modell of Christian Charitie. God had made some rich, some poor, and bound them to one another: "wee must be knitt together, in this worke, as one man," surrendering "superfluities, for the supply of other's necessities." Then the warning: "wee shall be as a citty upon a hill. The eies of all people are uppon us." Fail, and God would withdraw his help and make them a byword.

C. The Town They Built

The community took a new shape. Families settled in tight farming towns around a meetinghouse, land granted broadly to households. Each town ran much of its own life in town meeting. Congregational churches examined members and chose ministers, supported by taxes on everyone. In 1647 the colony ordered towns of fifty households to keep a school, so children could read Scripture. Families were larger and longer lived than in the Chesapeake. Writing in 1634, Winthrop counted "about 4000 souls & upward" in good health. Of the Native peoples whose land Boston stood on, he wrote that "the natives, they are near all dead of the smallpox, so the Lord hath cleared our title to what we possess."

D. Watched by Their Neighbors

A covenant people kept watch on one another. A covenant bound a church or a whole community to live by God's laws and brought his judgment if they failed, so a neighbor's sin was never private. Churches examined candidates, admonished the straying, and expelled the unrepentant. Only male church members, the freemen, voted. Courts punished religious offenses as offenses against order. Dissent was not a difference of opinion. It read as a tear in the covenant itself.

E. The Dissenters: Williams and Hutchinson

Roger Williams, a Salem minister, taught that the king had no right to grant Native land he did not own, so the charter rested on theft, and that government could not compel worship. Banished in 1636, he bought land from the Narragansett sachems and founded Providence, Rhode Island, on soul liberty. Anne Hutchinson, a Boston midwife, taught at home meetings that salvation came by grace directly, not through works, and that most ministers preached a covenant of works. Tried at Newton in 1637, she demanded her charges: "I am called here to answer before you but I hear no things laid to my charge." The court banished her. She was killed in New Netherland in 1643.

F. Widening the Frame, and War

In 1639 the freemen of Windsor, Hartford, and Wethersfield adopted the Fundamental Orders of Connecticut, written without a royal charter, to "maintain and preserve the liberty and purity of the Gospel of our Lord Jesus which we now profess." Freemen chose governor and magistrates yearly, towns sent deputies to a General Court, and in that court, the Orders said, "shall consist the supreme power of the Commonwealth." Historians often call it the first written constitution in English America. Voting belonged to freemen alone.

Expansion ran over Native nations. In the Pequot War of 1637, English soldiers and Native allies surrounded the Pequot fort at Mystic at dawn, set it afire, and killed hundreds of Pequot men, women, and children fleeing the flames. In King Philip's War of 1675 and 1676, Metacom, Massasoit's son, built a coalition of Wampanoag, Narragansett, Nipmuc, and other peoples to strike back at fifty years of land loss. It was the deadliest war per capita in the colonies' history. Metacom was killed in 1676, captives were sold into Caribbean slavery, and Native power in southern New England was broken.

G. Salem, 1692

The watching community met its crisis at Salem Village in 1692. Afflicted girls named neighbors as witches, and accusations spread to church members in good standing. Sarah Good, a homeless beggar woman, was among the first accused. The colony's record charges her with "certaine Detestable arts called Witchcrafts and Sorceries," on testimony that neighbors who turned her out "Lost severall" cattle and "believe they Dyed by witchcraft." The court admitted spectral evidence, claims that an accused person's spirit tormented a victim, which the accused could not disprove. Nineteen were hanged, and Giles Corey, refusing to plead, was pressed to death. Then Increase Mather and other ministers attacked spectral evidence, the governor ended the court, and the colony repented.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the problem the Compact answered, quote the words of creation, and name who stood outside. The Mayflower had landed at Cape Cod, north of the Virginia territory its patent covered, and some passengers, the Strangers, argued that outside the patent they owed obedience to no authority. Before going ashore, forty-one men signed the Mayflower Compact, agreeing to "covenant and combine ourselves together into a civil Body Politick, for our better Ordering and Preservation" and to enact "such just and equal Laws" as served "the general Good of the Colony," which all promised to obey. Only adult men signed. Women, children, servants, and the Native peoples of the coast stood outside the government the compact created, however much its laws governed them. Source: Pilgrim settlers, "Mayflower Compact," 1620, Yale Avalon Project, from Francis Newton Thorpe, The Federal and State Constitutions, 1909, as carried in the audited Primary Source Project bank record. 2. Explain why Plymouth survived, naming Massasoit, Tisquantum, the epidemic, and Massasoit's reason. Half the company died in the first winter, and the rest survived in part through Wampanoag help. The epidemic of 1616 to 1619 had killed most people in the coastal villages, the Wampanoag among them, weakening Massasoit's people while largely sparing the Narragansett, their rivals. Massasoit therefore chose alliance with the English as a counterweight, for reasons of his own. Tisquantum, a Patuxet man carried off by an English captain years before, who came home to an empty village, taught the Pilgrims to plant corn in the cleared fields of Patuxet. (Blueprint 7)

3. Quote the two Winthrop sentences and explain what each required and threatened. Winthrop wrote, "Wee must be knitt together, in this worke, as one man," and, "For wee must consider that wee shall be as a citty upon a hill. The eies of all people are uppon us." The first sentence required mutual obligation: settlers must abridge themselves of their superfluities for the supply of others' necessities, rejoice and suffer together, and put the public work above private gain. The second made the colony a public example under watch. If the settlers dealt falsely with God in the work, he would withdraw his help and they would be made "a story and a by-word through the world." Source: John Winthrop, "A Modell of Christian Charitie," 1630, VIVA Pressbooks, American Literature I, seventeenth-century spelling retained, as carried in the audited bank record. (Blueprint 7)

4. Describe the discipline, then trace one dissenter through it. Church members examined and admitted candidates, admonished the straying, and expelled the unrepentant; only male church members voted as freemen; ministers were paid from taxes everyone owed; courts treated religious offenses as threats to order, so dissent read as a tear in the covenant. Roger Williams taught that the king could not grant Native land he did not own and that government could not compel worship; banished in 1636, he bought land from the Narragansett and founded Providence on soul liberty. Anne Hutchinson taught that salvation came by grace directly, not by works, and that most ministers preached a covenant of works; tried at Newton in 1637, she answered, "I am called here to answer before you but I hear no things laid to my charge," and, pressed, "Name one, Sir." She was banished, settled in Rhode Island, and was killed in New Netherland in 1643. Sources: Massachusetts Bay General Court, "Anne Hutchinson Trial Examination," 1637, East Tennessee State University, from John Winthrop's Short Story, 1644, as carried in the audited bank record. (Blueprint 8)

5. Answer the lesson question using Salem, with the evidence type, the count, and the ending. Salem's accusers and judges believed they were defending a covenant community against the devil working inside it, and the community's habits of watching, examining, and testifying turned accusation into evidence. The court admitted spectral evidence, claims that the accused person's spirit had tormented a victim in a form only the victim could see, which the accused could not disprove. Sarah Good's own record shows the standard at work, dead cattle and old quarrels sworn as witchcraft. Nineteen people were hanged and Giles Corey was pressed to death for refusing to plead. The crisis ended when Increase Mather and other ministers attacked spectral evidence, the governor dissolved the special court, and the colony repented. Sources: Essex County Court of Oyer and Terminer, "Records of the Trial and Execution of Sarah Good," 1692, Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive, as carried in the audited bank record. (Blueprint 8)
