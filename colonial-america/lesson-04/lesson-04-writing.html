Writing Task: Who Could Belong

The prompt. What kind of community did New England's settlers mean to build, and who could belong to it? Answer in a developed essay of about 300 words, argued from the records in The Covenant File. A strong answer does more than list the rules. It states what held the community together, it follows the boundary of belonging across at least three of the file's records, and it answers the strongest claim on the other side: that the discipline was simply the price of survival in a hard country, and no separate choice at all.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The Mayflower Compact, 1620: landed outside their patent at Cape Cod, forty-one men, Pilgrims and Strangers together, covenanted into a civil Body Politick and promised obedience to just and equal laws made for the general Good of the Colony. Women did not sign.

2. A Modell of Christian Charitie, 1630: Winthrop taught that the settlers must be knit together as one man, surrendering their superfluities for the supply of others' necessities, because they would be as a city upon a hill with the eyes of all people upon them.

3. Winthrop's Boston letter, 1634: the colony counted about 4,000 souls in good health, Of the Native peoples, Winthrop wrote that they were near all dead of the smallpox, and that the Lord had cleared the colonists' title to the land.

4. Anne Hutchinson's trial, 1637: Hutchinson, who taught that salvation came by grace and not by the ministers' covenant of works, opened her defense by saying she heard no things laid to her charge. The court banished her.

5. The Fundamental Orders of Connecticut, 1639: freemen of three river towns, with no royal charter, placed the supreme power of the Commonwealth in a General Court, for the stated purpose of preserving the liberty and purity of the Gospel. Voting belonged to freemen alone.

6. The wars: in 1637 English soldiers and Native allies burned the Pequot fort at Mystic and killed hundreds. In 1675 and 1676 Metacom's coalition struck back at fifty years of land loss; captives were sold into Caribbean slavery.

7. Sarah Good's trial record, 1692: a homeless beggar woman was charged with witchcraft on testimony that neighbors lost seventeen head of cattle after turning her out and believed the cattle died by witchcraft. The court admitted spectral evidence. Good was hanged on July 19, 1692.

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your claim, develop it with your evidence, answer the other side in full, and end by restating what the community was built to be and what its boundary cost.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that names both the community's design and its boundary, not a claim that the Puritans were strict or devout in general. (Blueprint 7, 8)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, drawn from at least three different records, including one founding document (bank 1, 2, or 5) and Salem (bank 7). Names, dates, and counts must be right: 1620 and forty-one signers; 1630; 1639; 1692 and seventeen head of cattle. (Blueprint 7, 8)
  • The boundary followed across the evidence: who signed, who voted, who paid, who was banished, who was never offered membership at all. An essay that describes the inside of the community without its outside cannot earn full credit under this prompt. (Blueprint 8)
  • The survival claim answered from the record. The strongest form of the answer grants what is true in it, that Plymouth in its first winter and the towns in wartime needed discipline and mutual labor to live, and then shows that the records contain choices survival does not explain: a charter challenged on the ownership of Native land, a woman tried for her theology in a settled town of 1637, a written constitution made without a king's leave, and a court in 1692 hanging a beggar woman on spectral evidence seventy years after the landing.

Model opening for teacher reference. New England's settlers meant to build a covenant community, a people bound to God and to one another under watch, and the boundary of that community was drawn as carefully as its center. At Plymouth the boundary was a signature: forty-one men covenanted into a body politic in 1620, and everyone else, wives, children, servants, and the Wampanoag on the shore, was governed without signing. At Massachusetts Bay the boundary was a church door: members were examined, freemen voted, and all paid for ministers whether they belonged or not. A full model continues in this pattern, carries the boundary through Connecticut, the wars, and Salem, answers the survival claim with the records survival cannot explain, and closes on the boundary itself rather than on praise or blame.

Common weak answers. A devotion claim (the Puritans were very religious, so they were strict) never names a mechanism of belonging and cannot use the compact or the Orders. A survival claim accepted whole (they had no choice) ignores Williams, whose land argument threatened the charter itself, and Hutchinson, tried in a fed and housed town. An essay that treats Salem as an unrelated madness rather than the community's own watching turned inward cannot earn full credit, because the prompt requires the boundary to be followed to 1692.
