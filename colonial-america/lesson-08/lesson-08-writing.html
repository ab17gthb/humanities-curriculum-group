Writing Task: Triumph, Warning, or Both

The prompt. This is the closing writing task of the unit. Write a developed essay of about 300 words answering the lesson question from this lesson's documents: how did Britain win North America, and what did the victory cost everyone who fought it? Judge the year 1763 itself. Was it a triumph, a warning, or both? Your essay must give the winning its full weight and must carry all three peoples who paid: Britain, the colonies, and the Ohio and Great Lakes nations. State the strongest argument against your own judgment, at full strength, and answer it. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and The War in Five Records. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording. Keep the paired records whole wherever you use them: the treaty map beside the nations who were left off its table, and the Proclamation's protection of Indigenous land beside its prohibition of colonial settlement.

Evidence Bank

1. In May 1754 Franklin published Join, or Die in the Pennsylvania Gazette, picturing the colonies as a snake cut into eight segments. That summer his Albany Plan proposed one general government for defense and Indian treaties, with power to lay general taxes. The Albany Congress adopted the plan, and every colonial assembly rejected it.

2. Washington's journal of his 1753 mission, published in 1754, read the Forks of the Ohio as a fort site with the absolute Command of both Rivers. In May 1754 he ambushed Jumonville's party; in July he surrendered Fort Necessity and signed a capitulation in French describing Jumonville's death as an assassination. In July 1755 Braddock's army was destroyed on the Monongahela, and frontier raids followed for two years, killing or capturing thousands of settlers by the estimates historians draw from the records.

3. Pitt took direction of the war in 1757, borrowed millions, sent regular regiments and the navy in force, promoted officers by merit, and promised repayment for the men and money colonial assemblies voted. Colonial soldiers served beside the regulars in large numbers. Louisbourg fell in 1758, Quebec was won in September 1759 with Wolfe and Montcalm both killed, and Montreal surrendered in 1760.

4. The Treaty of Paris of February 10, 1763 gave Britain Canada and all French territory east of the Mississippi except New Orleans, plus Florida from Spain, while Louisiana west of the river went to Spain. No Indigenous nation sat at the table, and no clause asked the peoples of the ceded country whether they accepted the transfer.

5. Britain's national debt nearly doubled during the war, and its ministers resolved that the colonies should pay toward their own defense. In the spring of 1763 the Ohio and Great Lakes nations, whose French counterweight was gone and whom Amherst had answered by ending gift giving and restricting powder, rose under leaders including Pontiac and took most of the interior forts within weeks.

6. In October 1763 the Proclamation forbade colonial settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains, reserved that country to the Indigenous nations as their hunting grounds, and ordered settlers already there to remove themselves forthwith. Settlers were already crossing the line the order drew.

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Open with your judgment, develop it from the documents, state and answer the strongest argument against it, and close with the judgment restated in one sentence.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment of 1763, triumph, warning, or both, stated directly and held through the essay. Any of the three can earn full credit if the costs for all three peoples are carried. An essay that celebrates the map or mourns the line without judging has summarized the lesson rather than answered it. (Blueprint 15)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, drawn from at least two different parts of the record, the union failure, the war's opening, Pitt's turn, the treaty, or the bills of 1763. An essay built on the treaty alone cannot judge the year that contained Pontiac's war and the Proclamation as well. (Blueprint 15)
  • The winning stated at full strength wherever the verdict leans toward warning: Canada taken, the Mississippi reached, France off the mainland, won by Pitt's money and regular power joined to colonial soldiers and assembly funding. A warning essay that shrinks the victory to enlarge the warning has failed the record. (Blueprint 15)
  • The strongest argument against the judgment stated at full strength and answered. Against a triumph verdict: the interior forts fell in the spring of 1763 and the victor's first peacetime act toward its colonists was a prohibition, so the triumph lasted one season. Against a warning verdict: no earlier war had transferred a continent at one signing, and the rising was contained within three years. Against a both verdict: the claim that both is an evasion unless the essay ranks the two, stating which ran deeper by the year's end and why. (Blueprint 15)
  • The Ohio and Great Lakes nations treated as actors with reasons of their own, the lost French counterweight, the ended gifts and restricted powder, and a country transferred without their consent, never as scenery behind the imperial contest. (Blueprint 15)

Model opening for teacher reference. The year 1763 was both a triumph and a warning, and the warning ran deeper, because the instruments of the victory were the causes of its undoing. Britain won by the fullest exertion the empire had ever made in America, Pitt's borrowed millions, regulars and sea power, and colonial men and money voted by thirteen assemblies, and the treaty of February 1763 recorded the largest transfer of territory in the century. Yet the same year presented the bills: a debt nearly doubled with the colonies marked to pay it, an interior in arms under Pontiac for reasons the British had supplied, and a Proclamation line that made trespassers of the veterans the victory had promised land. A full model continues by answering the triumph claim at full strength, that France never returned to the mainland, and closes on the judgment restated.

Common weak answers. A triumph essay built on bank 4 alone cannot explain why the victor spent the autumn of its victory forbidding its own subjects to settle the prize. A warning essay that omits bank 3 cannot explain how the warning's authors came to hold a continent. An essay in which the nations of Station 4 appear only as Pontiac's name, without the ended gifts, the restricted powder, or the unconsented transfer, repeats the treaty's own omission.
