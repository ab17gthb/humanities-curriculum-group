# Image Sources for Colonial America Lesson 8, Empire and War: The French and Indian War

All images are public domain period works. Downloads were made on October 22, 2026, and every downloaded file was opened and verified with PIL before use. Document copies are stored in the lesson's assets folder and img folder; deck versions are cropped to portrait (1046 by 1500) or to 16:9 for the title slide and stored in slides-build/img; no other alteration.

## 1. join-or-die.jpg (reading figure, activity Station 1; deck portrait crop join-port.jpg)

- Title: Join, or Die
- What it shows: A woodcut of a snake cut into eight segments, each labeled with the initials of a colony or region: S.C., N.C., V., M., P., N.J., N.Y., and N.E. Beneath the snake is the caption JOIN, or DIE.
- Creator: Benjamin Franklin (attributed), as the audited TPSP bank record and the Library of Congress item page carry the attribution
- Date: May 9, 1754, Pennsylvania Gazette
- Holder: Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division, item 2002695523
- Source URL: https://www.loc.gov/item/2002695523/ (image file as carried in the audited TPSP bank record: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/cph/3g00000/3g05000/3g05300/3g05315r.jpg)
- Public domain basis: The Library of Congress item page states "No known restrictions on publication" for this 1754 printed work, verified on the item's JSON record during this build.
- Verified: 516 by 640 JPEG as downloaded from the bank record's Library of Congress image URL. The resolution is the Library's service copy; the woodcut is reproduced at document size only.

## Sources quoted in this lesson, with editions

- Join, or Die description and transcript, reading context and activity Station 1: Benjamin Franklin (attributed), Join, or Die, 1754 (Library of Congress; image described), as carried in the audited TPSP bank record join-or-die-1754 in colonial.json. The bank record's visual description is quoted: a woodcut of a snake cut into eight segments with the segment labels and the transcript JOIN, or DIE. The edition the record's sourceNote names is the Library of Congress holding.
- Washington passage, activity Station 2: George Washington, The Journal of Major George Washington, Sent by the Hon. Robert Dinwiddie, Esq; His Majesty's Lieutenant-Governor, and Commander in Chief of Virginia, to the Commandant of the French Forces on Ohio (Williamsburg: William Hunter, 1754), as transcribed by Founders Online, National Archives (document Washington/01-01-02-0003-0002, Journey to the French Commandant: Narrative). The Forks passage is quoted verbatim from that transcription, with the edition's spelling (extreamly, timber'd) preserved. The capitulation of July 3, 1754 is paraphrased, not quoted, in the station's closing lines: the document was written in French, a language Washington could not read, and it described Jumonville's death as an assassination. No English wording of the capitulation is put in quotation marks anywhere in this lesson.
- Albany Plan passages, activity Station 3: The Albany Plan of Union, 1754, as transcribed by the Avalon Project at Yale Law School (avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/albany.asp), whose source line names Documents Illustrative of the Formation of the Union of the American States, Government Printing Office, 1927, House Document No. 398, selected, arranged and indexed by Charles C. Tansill. The preamble and Articles 1, 10, 15, and 16 are quoted verbatim from the Avalon transcription; cuts are marked with ellipses.
- Treaty passages used in paraphrase across the lesson: The Treaty of Paris, 1763, as transcribed by the Avalon Project at Yale Law School (avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/paris763.asp). Article IV (Canada ceded in full right, with all its dependencies, as well as the island of Cape Breton) and Article VII (the boundary fixed irrevocably by a line drawn along the middle of the River Mississippi, and everything on the left side of the river ceded except the town of New Orleans and its island) were read in the Avalon transcription and are paraphrased in the reading, guided notes, and keys; the treaty's terms as stated in this lesson follow those articles. The treaty is signed February 10, 1763, as its Avalon heading states.
- Proclamation passages, activity Station 5: The Royal Proclamation, October 7, 1763, as transcribed by the Avalon Project at Yale Law School (avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/proc1763.asp). The hunting grounds passage, the strictly forbid passage, and the remove themselves passage are quoted verbatim from the Avalon transcription, including its punctuation; cuts are marked with ellipses.
- Pontiac's Rebellion record, activity Station 4: a teaching summary written for this lesson and labeled as a summary on its face, not a quotation. Its facts are the standard record: Amherst's ending of gift giving and restriction of powder and shot sales after 1760, higher British prices and refused credit, settler movement across the mountains, exclusion of Indigenous nations from the Paris negotiations, the taking of most interior forts in the spring of 1763, and the sieges of Detroit and Fort Pitt. No speech is quoted for Pontiac or any other leader in this lesson, because no verbatim text by them is carried in the audited bank and none was verified from a named edition in this build.

## Figures stated as estimates

- Frontier casualties and captives, 1755 to 1757: the reading, key, and deck state that thousands of settlers were killed or carried into captivity, labeled in every appearance as the estimates historians draw from the records. No exact count is asserted anywhere in this lesson.
- Britain's national debt: stated as nearly doubled during the war, the standard summary figure, with no invented pound amount.
- No population figure is asserted for the Ohio Valley or for any Indigenous nation in this lesson.

## Gaps recorded honestly

- Fort Necessity and Great Meadows: photographs of the modern reconstructed fort at Fort Necessity National Battlefield circulate on Wikimedia Commons, including files in the Battle of Fort Necessity category, but they are modern photographs of a reconstruction, and no period image of the fort was verified in this build. The fort is taught in text, and no image of it is used, rather than dressing a modern reconstruction as a period record.
- Pontiac and the leaders of 1763: no verified public domain portrait of Pontiac exists from his lifetime; the familiar images are later artists' inventions. No portrait is used, and Pontiac is taught through the record of the rising, in text and in the labeled summary at Station 4.
- Braddock's defeat: period prints of the Monongahela battle circulate, but no holding archive file page stating a public domain basis and full catalog data was opened and verified in this build, so the battle is taught in text and through the frontier casualty estimates, labeled as estimates.
- The capitulation document: a facsimile of the signed Articles of Capitulation circulates through Canadian archival holdings, but its file page was not verified line by line in this build, so the capitulation is taught from Washington's published journal and the paraphrase stated above, not from a reproduced facsimile.
- No catalog number is stated anywhere in this lesson for any image beyond the Library of Congress item and digital ID numbers recorded above, which are taken from the Library's own item records and the Wikimedia Commons file pages that credit them.
