The Middle Colonies and the Atlantic Economy

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. A Dutch Colony Becomes New York

The middle colonies were the colonies of New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Delaware, known for fertile soil, busy ports, and peoples of many nations and faiths. The Dutch West India Company planted New Netherland on the Hudson River for the fur trade, and New Amsterdam grew into a trading port of many languages. Enslaved Africans labored in the town and on its farms. In 1664 an English fleet arrived, the Dutch director general surrendered, and New Amsterdam became New York. It was a proprietary colony, a colony granted by the king to an individual or group who owned its land, appointed its governor, and drew its rents and fees. The Dutch stayed on. Their farms, churches, and language remained, and their enslaved people remained enslaved, under new rulers.

B. Penn and the Holy Experiment

Pennsylvania began as a royal debt settled in land. In 1681 King Charles II chartered the colony to William Penn, the Quaker proprietor who received Pennsylvania's charter in 1681 and founded the colony as a holy experiment in government by his persecuted faith. Penn belonged to the Quakers, members of the Society of Friends, a Protestant group that rejected formal ministers and oaths, held women and men spiritually equal, and faced persecution in England and New England. Penn advertised his colony across Europe in English, Dutch, and German, promising land, an elected assembly, and liberty of worship. Quakers came first, then Germans, Welsh, and Scots-Irish settlers. Philadelphia, laid out in 1682, grew on grain and flour, and by 1750 it was the largest city in British America.

C. The Lenape and the Land

The land Penn sold was the Lenape homeland, and the Lenape were parties to its transfer, not scenery inside it. Lenape leaders had watched disease shrink their towns, Dutch and Swedish traders compete for their furs, and stronger neighbors press on their borders. Selling land to Penn brought trade goods and a counterweight to rivals. Penn held that land had to be bought from its Indigenous owners. He met Lenape leaders in council in meetings remembered at Shackamaxon in 1682. In his lifetime the treaties held. The standard did not outlive him. In 1737 the Walking Purchase used a doctored deed and trained runners to claim far more Lenape land than any council had agreed to sell, and Lenape families were pushed west.

D. Maryland: Toleration with Teeth

Maryland, to the south, was founded by the Calvert family as a refuge for English Catholics. By 1649 Protestants outnumbered the Catholic minority that had founded the colony. The assembly answered with the Maryland Toleration Act. Read whole, the act did two things at once. It ordered that no person professing to believe in Jesus Christ should be troubled, molested, or discountenanced for his or her religion, or compelled to another faith, as long as that person stayed loyal to the government. It also ordered death and the loss of all lands and goods for anyone who denied the Holy Trinity, fined reproachful speeches about the Virgin Mary and the apostles, and fined anyone who called a neighbor a reproachful religious name, from heretic and papist to puritan and Roundhead.

E. Flushing: Conscience from Below

Toleration in the middle colonies also rose from below. Flushing was an English town inside Dutch New Netherland, and in 1657 Director General Peter Stuyvesant ordered its people not to shelter Quakers. On December 27, 1657, town clerk Edward Hart and thirty residents signed a remonstrance and sent it to Stuyvesant anyway. They wrote that they could not condemn the Quakers and could not stretch out their hands against them. They grounded their refusal in their town charter from the Dutch States General and in what they called the law of love, peace and liberty. Stuyvesant arrested Hart and punished several signers. They signed knowing the risk. In 1663 the Dutch West India Company's directors overruled Stuyvesant and ordered him to let each person believe what he would.

F. Mercantilism and the Navigation Acts

An economy run from London held them together. Its theory was mercantilism, the economic theory that a nation's power grows by accumulating wealth, especially gold and silver, exporting more than it imports, and holding colonies as sources of raw materials and captive markets. Parliament wrote it into law in the Navigation Acts, the English laws, beginning in 1651, that required colonial trade to travel in English or colonial ships and required certain colonial goods to be sold only to England. The act of 1651 barred goods from Asia, Africa, and America from reaching England in foreign ships. The act of 1660 added enumerated goods, sugars, tobacco, cotton wool, indigo, ginger, and dyeing woods, which could be shipped only to England or another English colony. In practice, merchants smuggled, customs men looked away, and colonial shipowners prospered, because colonial ships counted as English.

G. One Atlantic Economy

Middle colony and New England farms sent grain, flour, fish, and timber south to the Caribbean sugar islands, worked by enslaved labor. Ships carried sugar and molasses north to colonial ports, manufactured goods west from Britain, and enslaved Africans from Africa, in the pattern called triangular trade, the pattern of trade across the Atlantic linking Europe, Africa, and the Americas, carrying manufactured goods, enslaved people, and colonial produce between them. Grain was not an enumerated good, so middle colony farmers sold into the Caribbean legally and grew rich inside the rules. The colonial population stood at about 250,000 in 1700 and about 2.5 million by 1775, both figures estimates, doubling roughly every twenty five years, by births more than by new arrivals.

Teacher Answer Key

1. New York's change of rulers. New York began as New Netherland, planted by the Dutch West India Company on the Hudson River for the fur trade, with New Amsterdam as its port. In 1664 an English fleet arrived, the Dutch director general surrendered, and the colony passed to an English proprietor, the king's brother. What stayed the same: Dutch farms, churches, and language remained, Dutch settlers stayed on under English rule, and enslaved Africans remained enslaved. The takeover changed the government and the flag, not the population. (Blueprint 9)

2. Penn's promises, the peoples who came, and Philadelphia by 1750. Penn advertised in English, Dutch, and German, promising land, an elected assembly, and liberty of worship. Quakers came first, then Germans, Welsh, and Scots-Irish settlers. Philadelphia, laid out in 1682, grew on grain and flour exports and by 1750 was the largest city in British America. Full credit names the charter year, 1681, and at least three peoples. (Blueprint 9)

3. Lenape reasons, Penn's standard, and 1737. Lenape leaders sold land for reasons of their own: disease had shrunk their towns, Dutch and Swedish traders competed for their furs, and stronger neighbors pressed their borders, so a sale to Penn brought trade goods and a counterweight to rivals from a buyer who paid. Penn's standard was that land had to be bought from its Indigenous owners, in council, as at Shackamaxon in 1682, and in his lifetime the treaties held. The Walking Purchase of 1737 broke the standard: Penn's sons and agents used a doctored deed and trained runners to claim far more land than any council had agreed to sell, and Lenape families were pushed west. (Blueprint 9)

4. The Toleration Act read whole. Protection: no person professing to believe in Jesus Christ was to be troubled, molested, or discountenanced for his or her religion, or compelled to another faith, provided the person stayed loyal to the government. Punishment, in the same law: death and confiscation of all lands and goods for denying the Holy Trinity, fines for reproachful speeches about the Virgin Mary and the apostles, and fines for calling a neighbor a reproachful religious name, the act listing terms from heretic and papist to puritan and Roundhead. Outside the protection: anyone who did not profess belief in Jesus Christ, and anyone who denied the Trinity. An answer that reports only the protection half has not read the act whole. (Blueprint 9)

5. The lesson question, answered. The theory was mercantilism, that national power grows by accumulating wealth and holding colonies as sources of raw materials and captive markets. Two rules: from 1651, goods from Asia, Africa, and America could not reach England in foreign ships; from 1660, enumerated goods including sugars, tobacco, cotton wool, indigo, ginger, and dyeing woods could be shipped only to England or another English colony. Two ways colonists lived inside the rules: merchants smuggled and customs officers looked away, colonial shipowners prospered because colonial ships counted as English, and grain, not being enumerated, went legally to the Caribbean and made middle colony farmers rich. The population figures, both estimates, run from about 250,000 in 1700 to about 2.5 million by 1775. (Blueprint 10)
