Writing Task: Helped to Grow, or Held in Place?

The prompt. Between 1651 and 1660 Parliament bound the colonies' trade in the Navigation Acts, and colonial merchants then spent a century living inside, around, and against those rules. Answer this question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Did mercantilism in practice help the colonies grow, or mainly hold them in place? Argue from the documents in the Atlantic Ledger. A strong answer separates what the law said from what colonists actually did, reckons with the enumerated list and the open grain trade at the same time, and ends with what the population figures, labeled as estimates, can and cannot prove. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each one supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson documents. Write the history in your own sentences. Quotations from the records belong inside quotation marks, spelled as the records spell them.

Evidence Bank

1. The Navigation Act of 1660 provided that no sugars, tobacco, cotton-wool, indigoes, ginger, fustic, or other dyeing wood from any English plantation could be shipped to any place other than another English plantation or England, Ireland, Wales, or Berwick upon Tweed, under forfeiture of the goods and the ship.

2. The acts also required that colonial trade travel in ships belonging to the people of England or of the plantations, with English masters and, by 1660, three fourths English mariners. Colonial ships counted as English, and colonial shipowners took over much of the carrying trade the Dutch lost.

3. Grain, flour, fish, and timber were not enumerated. Middle colony and New England farmers sold them legally to the Caribbean sugar islands, which needed food permanently, and Philadelphia grew on the grain trade until by 1750 it was the largest city in British America.

4. In practice, merchants smuggled goods past the customs houses, and customs officers often looked away. The law on paper and the trade on the water were never the same thing.

5. At Flushing in 1657 and in Maryland's act of 1649, the colonies show the other half of their condition: conscience and worship were argued over locally, by petitioners and assemblies, while trade was ruled from London.

6. The colonial population stood at about 250,000 in 1700 and about 2.5 million by 1775. Both figures are estimates. The growth came mainly from births, under the Navigation Acts, not outside them.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the question directly and rests on practice, not only on the statutes' text: what colonists could sell, in whose ships, and at whose prices. (Blueprint 10)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 establishes the control case in the acts' own words: enumeration, routing through England, forfeiture. (Blueprint 10) Items 2 and 3 establish the growth case: colonial hulls counted as English and took the carrying trade, and the unenumerated provisions trade fed the Caribbean legally and built Philadelphia. Item 4 supplies the practice that complicates both cases, smuggling and lax customs. Item 6 supplies the scale, population growth from an estimated 250,000 in 1700 to an estimated 2.5 million by 1775, and item 5 lets an essay widen the frame to the colonies' local control of conscience against London's control of trade. (Blueprint 9 and 10)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive distinction is between the trades the system reserved and the trades it left open: an essay that treats mercantilism as one uniform pressure, without separating enumerated from unenumerated goods, cannot judge the question.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest argument on the other side. For growth: that the planters of enumerated crops lost every foreign buyer and paid England's duties first, answered by noting which colonists the question asks about and which trades they held. For held in place: that colonial ships, the grain trade, and a tenfold population increase all happened inside the system, answered by asking who collected the duties and set the routes on the richest cargoes.

Model opening for teacher reference. Mercantilism in practice did both, and the cargo decided which. A Philadelphia grain merchant lived in the open half of the system: his wheat was not enumerated, his ship counted as English, and the sugar islands bought every barrel he could load, which is how his city became the largest in British America by 1750. A Virginia tobacco planter lived in the closed half: his leaf could land only in England or another English colony, under forfeiture of cargo and ship, and England's merchants and customs took their share before he saw a price. A full model continues in this pattern, weighs smuggling and lax enforcement as practice against the statutes, answers the counterclaim, and closes on the estimates, an estimated 250,000 people in 1700 and an estimated 2.5 million by 1775, growth the system permitted and profited from without ever having aimed at it.

Common weak answers. Quoting the acts and stopping there argues from the paper law and misses the lesson's word in practice. Treating population growth as proof the system helped confuses a background fact with a mechanism; item 6 says what it can prove and no more. Ignoring the enumerated list removes the strongest evidence the held in place side has, and an essay that does it has usually skipped Station 3 or Station 5 of the activity.
