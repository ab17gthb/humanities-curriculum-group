Guided Notes: The Middle Colonies and the Atlantic Economy

Lesson question: How did the middle colonies become the colonies of many peoples, and what held the Atlantic economy together?

Unit question: How did the meeting of Europe, Africa, and the Americas remake all three worlds, and what kind of society grew up in England's colonies?

Key Terms

middle colonies: the colonies of New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Delaware, known for fertile soil, busy ports, and peoples of many nations and faiths

Quakers: members of the Society of Friends, a Protestant group that rejected formal ministers and oaths, held women and men spiritually equal, and faced persecution in England and New England

William Penn: the Quaker proprietor who received Pennsylvania's charter in 1681 and founded the colony as a holy experiment in government by his persecuted faith

proprietary colony: a colony granted by the king to an individual or group who owned its land, appointed its governor, and drew its rents and fees

mercantilism: the economic theory that a nation's power grows by accumulating wealth, especially gold and silver, exporting more than it imports, and holding colonies as sources of raw materials and captive markets

Navigation Acts: the English laws, beginning in 1651, that required colonial trade to travel in English or colonial ships and required certain colonial goods to be sold only to England

triangular trade: the pattern of trade across the Atlantic linking Europe, Africa, and the Americas, carrying manufactured goods, enslaved people, and colonial produce between them

Summary table. New York: New Netherland, planted by the Dutch West India Company for the fur trade, passed to an English proprietor in 1664 without changing its population; Dutch settlers stayed and enslaved Africans remained enslaved. Pennsylvania and the Lenape: Penn's 1681 charter, advertising in three languages, and Philadelphia's rise to largest city in British America by 1750 rest on land bought from the Lenape in council in Penn's lifetime, a standard broken by the Walking Purchase of 1737. Toleration's two faces: Maryland's 1649 act protected Christians who professed belief in Jesus Christ and punished denial of the Trinity with death, while at Flushing in 1657 ordinary townspeople petitioned their own governor for the liberty he had denied Quakers and were overruled in their favor only in 1663. The Atlantic system: mercantilism and the Navigation Acts of 1651 and 1660 routed colonial trade through England, and colonists smuggled, shipped in colonial bottoms, and sold unenumerated grain to the Caribbean inside the same rules, as the population grew from an estimated 250,000 in 1700 to an estimated 2.5 million by 1775.

Exit ticket 1. The middle colonies held many peoples because a Dutch trading colony, a Quaker proprietor advertising across Europe, and rich wheat land drew Dutch, English, German, Welsh, and Scots-Irish settlers, alongside enslaved Africans, into the same ports and farms.

Exit ticket 2. Protection: a person professing belief in Jesus Christ could not be troubled, molested, or discountenanced for religion or compelled to another faith. Penalty: death and confiscation for denying the Holy Trinity (fines for reproachful religious names also earn credit). Outside the protection stood everyone who did not profess belief in Jesus Christ, and anyone who denied the Trinity.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that pairs the colonies' diversity with the Atlantic frame and uses two dates. The strongest answers run the Dutch colony's takeover in 1664, Penn's charter of 1681 and his Lenape purchases, the Toleration Act of 1649 and the Flushing Remonstrance of 1657 as toleration's two faces, and the Navigation Acts of 1651 and 1660 with the triangular trade as the economy that held the colonies together while the population grew from an estimated 250,000 in 1700 toward an estimated 2.5 million in 1775.
