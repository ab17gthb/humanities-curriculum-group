The Atlantic Ledger: Conscience and Cargo, 1649 to 1660

Directions: Work through the five stations in order. Each station holds one record from the middle colonies or the Atlantic economy, quoted from the edition named in its Source line, with cuts marked by ellipses, or labeled as a summary or a teaching table where the record is presented in that form. As you work, keep the ledger in Part 2: for each record, enter whose interest it served and whose it bound or cost. At the end, judge whether mercantilism in practice helped the colonies grow or mainly held them in place, and answer the strongest claim for the other side.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: The Flushing Remonstrance, December 27, 1657

Source: Edward Hart and 30 residents of Flushing, Flushing Remonstrance, 1657, as carried in the audited source record (Americans United). Flushing was an English town in Dutch New Netherland. Director General Peter Stuyvesant had ordered the town not to receive or entertain Quakers. Spelling follows the record. Cuts are marked by ellipses.

"Wee desire therefore in this case not to judge least we be judged, neither to condemn least we be condemned, but rather let every man stand or fall to his own Master. Wee are bounde by the law to do good unto all men, especially to those of the household of faith."

"The law of love, peace and liberty in the states extending to Jews, Turks and Egyptians, as they are considered sons of Adam, which is the glory of the outward state of Holland, soe love, peace and liberty, extending to all in Christ Jesus, condemns hatred, war and bondage."

"Therefore if any of these said persons come in love unto us, we cannot in conscience lay violent hands upon them, but give them free egresse and regresse unto our Town, and houses, as God shall persuade our consciences, for we are bounde by the law of God and man to doe good unto all men and evil to noe man."

State the refusal the signers are making, the three grounds they give for it, and what they risked by signing. Then state whose interest this record served and whose it bound.

Station 2: The Maryland Toleration Act, 1649, Read Whole

Source: Maryland General Assembly, Maryland Toleration Act, 1649, as carried in the audited source record (Yale Avalon Project). Maryland was a proprietary colony founded as a refuge for English Catholics. By 1649 Protestants outnumbered Catholics there. Spelling follows the record. Cuts are marked by ellipses. Read both clauses before you answer: the penalty comes first in the act, the protection after it.

"That whatsoever person or persons within this Province ... shall deny the holy Trinity the father sonne and holy Ghost, or the Godhead of any of the said Three persons of the Trinity or the Unity of the Godhead, or shall use or utter any reproachfull Speeches, words or language concerning the said Holy Trinity, or any of the said three persons thereof, shalbe punished with death and confiscation or forfeiture of all his or her lands and goods to the Lord Proprietary and his heires."

"... noe person or persons whatsoever within this Province ... professing to beleive in Jesus Christ, shall from henceforth bee any waies troubled, Molested or discountenanced for or in respect of his or her religion nor in the free exercise thereof within this Province ... nor any way compelled to the beleife or exercise of any other Religion against his or her consent, soe as they be not unfaithfull to the Lord Proprietary, or molest or conspire against the civill Governement established ... in this Province under him or his heires."

The act also fined anyone who called a neighbor, in a reproachful manner, "an heritick, Scismatick, Idolator, puritan, Independant, Prespiterian popish prest, Jesuite, Jesuited papist, Lutheran, Calvenist, Anabaptist, Brownist, Antinomian, Barrowist, Roundhead, Separatist," at ten shillings for every offence.

State exactly who the protection clause covers, exactly what the penalty clause punishes, and why a colony founded for Catholics would write both into one law. Then state whose interest this record served and whose it bound.

Station 3: The Navigation Acts, 1651 and 1660

Source: Navigation Act of 1651 and Navigation Act of 1660, in the Statutes of the Realm, as transcribed in the excerpts collection hosted by East Tennessee State University and checked against the same transcription at Founding.com. Spelling follows the transcription. Cuts are marked by ellipses.

1651. "... no Goods or Commodities whatsoever, of the Growth, Production or Manufacture of Asia, Africa or America, or of any part thereof ... shall be Imported or brought into this Commonwealth of England, or into Ireland, or any other Lands, Islands, Plantations or Territories to this Commonwealth belonging ... in any other Ship or Ships, Vessel or Vessels whatsoever, but onely in such as do truly and without fraud belong onely to the People of this Commonwealth, or the Plantations thereof, as the Proprietors or right Owners thereof; and whereof the Master and Mariners are also for the most part of them, of the People of this Commonwealth, under the penalty of the forfeiture and loss of all the Goods that shall be Imported contrary to this Act; as also of the Ship ..."

1660, on ships. "... no goods or commodities whatsoever shall be imported into or exported out of any lands, islands, plantations, or territories to his Majesty belonging ... in Asia, Africa, or America, in any other ship or ships, vessel or vessels whatsoever, but in such ships or vessels as do truly and without fraud belong only to the people of England or Ireland ... or are of the built of and belonging to any the said lands, islands, plantations, or territories, as the proprietors and right owners thereof, and whereof the master and three fourths of the mariners at least are English; under the penalty of the forfeiture and loss of all the goods and commodities ... as also of the ship or vessel ..."

1660, on enumerated goods. "... no sugars, tobacco, cotton-wool, indigoes, ginger, fustic, or other dyeing wood, of the growth, production, or manufacture of any English plantations in America, Asia, or Africa, shall be shipped, carried, conveyed, or transported from any of the said English plantations to any land, island, territory, dominion, port, or place whatsoever, other than to such other English plantations as do belong to his Majesty, his heirs and successors, or to the kingdom of England or Ireland, or principality of Wales, or town of Berwick upon Tweed, there to be laid on shore ..."

State the two rules these passages impose, the penalty for breaking them, and one group in the colonies the ship rule actually helped. Then state whose interest this record served and whose it bound.

Station 4: Penn's Invitation and the Lenape Purchase, a Summary Record

Source: Summary record written for this lesson, labeled as a summary. It rests on the Pennsylvania charter of 1681 and on William Penn's published invitation to settlers, printed in English in 1681 and in Dutch and German editions for continental readers. No sentence in this station is presented as Penn's exact words.

In 1681 King Charles II granted Pennsylvania to William Penn in payment of a royal debt owed to Penn's father. Penn was a Quaker, a member of a persecuted faith, and he set out to fill his colony with settlers. His printed invitation offered land for purchase, a government with an elected assembly, and freedom of worship, and it circulated where persecuted and land hungry readers lived, in England, the Netherlands, and the German states. Quakers came first, then Germans, Welsh, and Scots-Irish families. The land they bought was Lenape land. Penn's standing rule was purchase in council from the Lenape owners, and the councils remembered at Shackamaxon in 1682 set the pattern. Lenape leaders sold for reasons of their own: trade goods, and a buyer whose settlements could stand as a counterweight to stronger neighbors pressing on Lenape borders. Penn's rule held in his lifetime. His sons broke it in the Walking Purchase of 1737.

State what Penn offered settlers, how he spread the offer, and what the Lenape gained and risked in selling to him. Then state whose interest this record served and whose it bound.

Part 3: The Verdict

Did mercantilism in practice help the colonies grow, or mainly hold them in place? Answer in a developed paragraph. Use at least three stations, state what the rules took and what they left open, and answer the strongest claim for the other side in one or two sentences.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1. The refusal: the signers would not condemn Quakers, would not stretch out their hands against them, and would give peaceful visitors free coming and going in Flushing. Their three grounds: conscience under God, who alone judges; the law of love, peace and liberty, which they say extends in Holland even to Jews, Turks, and Egyptians; and their town patent and charter from the States General, which they were not willing to infringe. The risk was real: Stuyvesant arrested town clerk Edward Hart and punished several signers, and the vindication came only in 1663, when the Dutch West India Company's directors overruled him. Interest served: Quakers seeking entry, Flushing residents guarding their charter and consciences, and, in Holland's reputation, the Dutch state itself. Bound: Stuyvesant's campaign of religious enforcement, and every magistrate who would persecute peaceful worship. (Blueprint 9)

Station 2. Coverage, stated exactly: the protection reaches persons professing to believe in Jesus Christ who remain loyal to the Lord Proprietary and do not conspire against the civil government. The penalty clause punishes denial of the Trinity, or of the Godhead of any person of it, and reproachful speech about the Trinity, with death and confiscation of all lands and goods; separate clauses fine insults to the Virgin Mary and the apostles, and fine reproachful religious name calling at ten shillings an offence. Why both in one law: Maryland's Catholic founders had become a minority in their own colony, Protestants and Catholics had already fought, and the assembly bought peace by protecting Christian worship generally while punishing the speech and doctrine most likely to restart the fight. Interest served: the Calvert proprietorship and both Christian camps' safety. Bound: anyone outside Trinitarian Christianity, who received no protection at all, and every colonist's tongue. Toleration here is a grant from above with teeth, the opposite direction from Flushing. (Blueprint 9)

Station 3. Rule one, shipping: goods of Asia, Africa, and America could move to or from English lands only in ships truly belonging to the people of England or its plantations, with masters and, by 1660, three fourths of mariners English. Rule two, enumeration, from 1660: sugars, tobacco, cotton wool, indigo, ginger, fustic, and other dyeing wood from English plantations could be shipped only to another English plantation or to England, Ireland, Wales, or Berwick upon Tweed. Penalty: forfeiture of the goods and of the ship itself. The colonial group the ship rule helped: colonial shipowners and shipbuilders, above all in New England and the middle ports, because colonial ships and crews counted as English and could take the carrying trade the Dutch lost. Interest served: English shipping, English customs revenue, and colonial carriers. Bound: colonial producers of enumerated goods, who lost every foreign buyer, and Dutch merchants, the acts' first target. (Blueprint 10)

Station 4. Penn offered land for purchase, government with an elected assembly, and freedom of worship. He spread the offer in print, in English in 1681 and in Dutch and German editions aimed at continental readers. The Lenape gained trade goods and a settled buyer whose colonies could serve as a counterweight to stronger neighbors, and they sold under councils that Penn treated as binding sales. They risked what every seller of land to a growing colony risked: that each purchase would bring more settlers, more fences, and new pressure on the land that remained, a risk realized after Penn's death when his sons used the Walking Purchase of 1737 to take land no council had agreed to sell. Interest served: Penn's proprietorship, settlers seeking land and worship, and, in the short run, Lenape diplomacy. Bound: later Lenape generations, who inherited the sales, and Penn himself, whose rule of purchase limited how fast he could sell what he had not yet bought. (Blueprint 9)

Station 5. Controlled directly: the enumerated cargoes, tobacco and sugar above all, which had to land in England or another English plantation first, and every cargo's ship, which had to be English or colonial. Left free, within English or colonial hulls: grain, flour, fish, and timber to the Caribbean, none of them enumerated. That is why grain enriched middle colony farmers directly: they sold legally into the sugar islands' permanent food shortage at Caribbean prices. Tobacco enriched England first because the planter could not sell to the Dutch or French buyer who might pay more; the leaf passed through English ports, paid English duties, and employed English merchants before it reached its smoker. Interest served: Britain's customs and carrying trade, sugar islands fed cheaply, and mainland farmers with unenumerated crops. Bound: enumerated crop planters, and, at the center of the pattern, enslaved Africans, whose forced carriage is listed in the table as what it was. The trade routes of Station 5 are the subject Lesson 6 takes apart. (Blueprint 10)

The verdict. Full credit requires a judgment, three stations used accurately, and the other side answered. The growth case: the acts left the grain and provisions trade open, made colonial ships English in law and handed colonial carriers the Dutch trade, guaranteed enumerated planters a protected English market, and coincided with population growth from an estimated 250,000 in 1700 to an estimated 2.5 million by 1775; Philadelphia became British America's largest city inside the system, not outside it. The held in place case: enumeration cut planters off from foreign buyers and better prices, routed colonial wealth through English ports and duties, and treated the colonies in the acts' own words as plantations held for the kingdom's increase of shipping. The strongest synthesis a student can reach: mercantilism in practice did both at once, growing the colonies' shipping and grain wealth while reserving the richest trades' profits to England, and which half a colonist felt depended on what his farm or colony sold. Essays that argue only from the statutes' text without the practice, or only from growth without the enumeration, have used half the file.
