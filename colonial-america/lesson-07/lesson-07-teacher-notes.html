Colonial America · Lesson 7 · Governing, Working, and Arguing About God

These are the speaker notes from the slide deck, in slide order, set for printing. The deck itself carries the main ideas in a few fragments per slide; the teaching is here. Print this and keep it beside you while you teach. Each entry is headed by its slide number and shows what is on that slide, so you can find your place without flipping back to the screen. Vocabulary definitions match the student guided notes word for word.

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: Governing, Working, and Arguing About God · Assemblies, households, and the Great Awakening · Engraving of George Whitefield preaching in the open air, published in Harper’s Weekly, 1865.

Open with the engraving and with the shape of the lesson, because this hour is about a society rather than an event. By the 1730s the English colonies held about a million people who had built three working systems without waiting for London to design them. They governed themselves through elected assemblies that held the power of the purse. They worked and raised children inside households ordered by law, where a father’s authority covered wives, children, servants, and enslaved people. And they argued about God in churches that were about to be split by the greatest religious movement of the century. The engraving shows George Whitefield preaching in the open air, the scene the lesson ends with. The claim to carry through the hour is simple: in each of these three systems, ordinary colonists were practicing how to judge authority for themselves.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: How did colonists govern themselves, raise their families, and argue about God? · Unit question: How did the meeting of Europe, Africa, and the Americas remake all three worlds, and what kind of society grew up in Englan

Read the lesson question in its three parts, because the lesson answers all three and the writing task grades the connection among them. The first part is government: assemblies, a distant Crown, and a printer’s trial in New York. The second part is the household: coverture, children at work, apprentices and servants, and enslaved people building families inside an order that treated them as property. The third part is religion: settled churches, a revival that split them, and an Enlightenment arriving in the same decades through the same printing presses. The unit question runs beneath as the frame. This lesson describes the society the earlier lessons built, at the moment before the imperial wars of the next lesson tested it, and it asks what that society had already taught its people about authority.

Slide 4 · Section A: Government by Practice (divider)

On the slide: Government by Practice · Elected assemblies in every colony / The governor appointed; the money local

Section A is placed first because it states the political fact the rest of the lesson stands on. By the early 1700s every English colony in America had an elected assembly, a body of representatives chosen by landowning colonists to vote taxes and write laws. The painting shows a colonial assembly in session, the House of Burgesses in Williamsburg as the National Archives holds the image. Above each assembly stood a governor appointed by the Crown or by a proprietor, with a council of leading colonists to advise him. On paper the governor held the stronger hand: he could veto laws, dismiss the assembly, and command the militia. The section’s work is to show students why the paper overstated his power, and why the assembly’s control of money outweighed the governor’s commission over time.

Slide 5 · Assemblies and the power of the purse

On the slide: Assemblies and the power of the purse · ▪ Every colony: an elected assembly / ▪ Assemblies vote taxes and write laws / ▪ Assemblies vote the governor’s salary / ▪ Decade by decade, a larger share of governing

Work through the four fragments in order and attach the mechanism to each. The assembly was elected, which the governor was not, and its members answered to the landowning colonists who chose them. It voted the colony’s taxes and wrote its laws, the two ordinary instruments of government. The decisive fragment is the third. The governor’s salary, and the money for roads, forts, and officials, came to him only when the assembly voted it, so the man sent to govern began every year as a petitioner asking the governed for funds. Assemblies learned to delay salary bills, to cut them, and to attach conditions to them. Decade by decade, that yearly bargaining became a standing share of governing, and the men who sat in Williamsburg, Boston, and Philadelphia learned politics by practicing it, not by reading about it in London’s instructions.

Slide 6 · Section B: Salutary Neglect (divider)

On the slide: Salutary Neglect · The policy nobody announced, named a generation later by Edmund Burke.

Section B answers the obvious question Section A raises: why did Britain allow this to happen? The answer is not a grant and not a plan. Through much of the early 1700s the British government attended to wars and politics in Europe, to party struggles at home, and to the debts those wars left behind. No minister announced a policy of leaving the colonies alone, and no law granted them self-government. Distance did the rest of the work. An ocean crossing took weeks, instructions arrived late, and royal officials in America drew salaries voted by the assemblies they were sent to oversee. Edmund Burke gave the practice its name in Parliament a generation later. Teach students to hold both halves: the neglect was real in its effects and imaginary as a policy, since nobody in London had ever voted for it.

Slide 7 · How neglect worked in practice

On the slide: How neglect worked in practice · ▪ Britain attends to Europe: wars, parties, debt / ▪ No law grants self-government / ▪ Instructions cross the ocean in weeks / ▪ Officials draw salaries assemblies vote

Take the four fragments one at a time, because each one removes a possible supervisor. The first removes attention: Britain’s ministers had European wars and domestic politics enough to fill every cabinet meeting, and colonial administration sank down the agenda. The second removes legal design: because no statute created the arrangement, no statute supervised it, and the assemblies’ growing share of governing rested on custom rather than on a charter anyone in London reviewed. The third removes speed: a governor who wrote home for instructions waited weeks for an answer written to a situation already changed. The fourth removes independence: the royal official on the scene often held colonial property and always needed the assembly’s votes for his salary. The result was a generation of colonists for whom self-government was not a theory of rights. It was the ordinary way their towns and colonies ran.

Slide 8 · Section C: The Zenger Trial (divider)

On the slide: The Zenger Trial · A printer jailed in 1734 / A jury decides in August 1735

Section C moves from practice to principle, from how colonists governed to what rights they claimed while doing it. The photograph shows a colonial printing press of the kind John Peter Zenger worked, the press Stephen Daye brought to Massachusetts in the 1630s, photographed by the Library of Congress. Zenger printed the New-York Weekly Journal, which attacked New York’s royal governor, William Cosby, over his salary claims and his removal of judges. In November 1734 Zenger was jailed for seditious libel and held for about nine months awaiting trial. His newspaper kept publishing, run by his wife Anna from the jail’s neighborhood, which students should register: the prosecution had already failed to silence the press it was trying. The trial came in August 1735, and it produced the colonies’ most retold courtroom story.

Slide 9 · The trial of John Peter Zenger, 1735

On the slide: The trial of John Peter Zenger, 1735 · ▪ The charge: seditious libel against Governor Cosby / ▪ The law then: truth was no defense / ▪ Hamilton admits the printing, argues the truth / ▪ The jury acquits, against the

Narrate the trial through the four fragments, and use Station 1 of the activity for Hamilton’s words themselves. Zenger’s first lawyers were disbarred for challenging the judges’ commissions, so the defense fell to Andrew Hamilton of Philadelphia, the most famous lawyer in the colonies, an old man who traveled to New York for the case. He stunned the court by confessing that Zenger had printed and published the papers, giving away the only fact the prosecution had to prove. Then he argued the principle the law denied: that true statements could not be libel, and that the jury, not the judge’s instruction, should judge both the fact and the law of the case. The jury acquitted after a short deliberation. Crowds cheered the verdict in the street, and printers across the colonies printed the story.

Slide 10 · 9 months Zenger spent in jail awaiting trial

On the slide: 9 · months Zenger spent in jail awaiting trial · Arrested November 1734 · Tried and acquitted August 1735

Let the number stand as a measure of what the prosecution could still do, because the honest qualification of this lesson depends on it. Zenger was arrested in November 1734 and held about nine months before his trial, in part because the bail set for him was more than a printer could raise. During those months the government had its way with him completely: his shop was raided in effect, his earlier lawyers were disbarred, and he edited his newspaper through his wife from jail. The acquittal in August 1735 did not repay those nine months, and it did not change the law that had jailed him. Teach the stat as the counterweight inside the celebration. The colonies remembered the verdict as a landmark, and the same record shows a governor able to imprison a critic for three seasons on a charge a jury would not sustain.

Slide 11 · Section D: The Household (divider)

On the slide: The Household · The unit of work and discipline, where most colonists were governed.

Section D goes indoors, to the institution where most colonists actually experienced authority every day. A colonial household could hold a married couple, their children, apprentices learning a trade, indentured servants working out their terms, and enslaved people held as property, all eating, working, and sleeping under one roof and one legal head. The law gave the father authority over every person in that list. This section should be taught as a description of a working order, not as a backdrop. The household produced most of the colony’s food, cloth, and goods. It educated most children, nursed most of the sick, and disciplined most of the young. Its legal rules tell us who held standing in that order. Its daily work tells us who actually carried it, which was a wider set of people than the law credited.

Slide 12 · Coverture and the working reality

On the slide: Coverture and the working reality · ▪ A wife’s legal identity covered by her husband’s / ▪ No property, no contracts, no suits in her own name / ▪ Yet women ran farms, shops, and taverns, and taught / ▪ Widows regain

Teach the legal rule first and the working reality second, and insist that both are the record. Under coverture, a married woman’s legal identity was covered by her husband’s: she could not hold property in her own name, sign contracts, or sue in court, and her earnings belonged in law to him. Against that rule set the work the documents show. Women ran farms during long absences, kept shops and taverns, traded in their own neighborhoods, taught children, and managed the labor of servants and the enslaved. Station 5 of the activity gives students a widow’s will, Judith Johns of Goochland County in 1735, dividing her estate equally among her children. Widowhood restored the standing marriage had removed, and widows across the colonies used it to hold property, run trades, and direct their households’ goods by will.

Slide 13 · The people inside the household

On the slide: The people inside the household · ▪ Children worked young; many apprenticed out / ▪ Servants worked toward freedom dues and land / ▪ Enslaved people: families, faiths, and skills kept / ▪ New England longevity; Chesa

This slide names the household’s other members as people working inside the order, and the notes should keep their purposes in view. Children were put to work at tasks sized to them and many left home as apprentices or servants, carrying their labor to another household and learning a trade there. Indentured servants worked out terms of four to seven years toward freedom dues and a chance at land, and their bargains were enforced in courts that also punished them. Enslaved people had no term and no dues. Inside an order that treated them as property they built families across quarters and plantations, kept African faiths, languages, and skills, taught their children, and resisted in ways from slowed work to flight. Regional contrasts close the section: New England families lived longer and schooled their children in towns, while early deaths in the Chesapeake broke families often and left orphans to the courts.

Slide 14 · Section E: The Churches Before the Storm (divider)

On the slide: The Churches Before the Storm · Established churches, tax support, and a faith ministers feared had cooled.

Section E sets the religious scene the Awakening broke into, and it should be taught as a working establishment rather than a decline invented afterward. In New England, town taxes supported Congregational churches and their ministers, and attendance was expected of everyone in town. In the South, the Church of England was established by law across parishes so wide that one minister could barely serve them, and many families lived miles from any service. The middle colonies held the widest mix of faiths in the Atlantic world. By the 1730s ministers across these arrangements shared one worry: that the faith of the founding generations had cooled into habit, that sermons had grown learned and dry, and that commerce filled the week. Their disagreement was over the cure, stricter discipline or a faith felt in the heart.

Slide 15 · A faith of habit, and two cures proposed

On the slide: A faith of habit, and two cures proposed · ▪ Tax supported ministers; attendance expected / ▪ Wide southern parishes, one minister each / ▪ The worry: members attending from habit / ▪ Cure one: discipline · Cure two:

Work through the fragments and end on the choice, because the next section is that choice exploding. The establishment was real: ministers were paid from taxes, churches stood at the center of towns, and in New England a family’s standing and its churchgoing were hard to separate. The weakness was also real, and ministers themselves recorded it. Membership could be inherited, sermons could be essays read aloud, and a congregation could sit through both without anything in the hearers changing. The first cure proposed was the older one: catechize the children, discipline the wandering, and restore the practices of the founders. The second cure was new in scale if not in idea: preach for conversion, aim at the heart, and treat faith as something a person must feel and choose. In the mid 1730s that second cure found its preachers, its crowds, and its printing presses, all at once.

Slide 16 · Section F: The Great Awakening (divider)

On the slide: The Great Awakening · Northampton, 1734 and 1735 / Enfield, July 1741

Section F takes up the revival itself, beginning with its most careful mind. The portrait is Jonathan Edwards, the Northampton minister whose preaching and writing made him the leading theologian of the Awakening in New England. Edwards was no shouting itinerant. He was a Yale educated scholar who read his sermons in a level voice and kept exact records of his congregation’s spiritual state. In 1734 and 1735 a revival filled his town, as longtime churchgoers, young people first among them, came to him in distress and in joy over their souls. Edwards wrote the account of it that carried the news abroad. Teach the pairing that defines him: the most analytical preacher in the colonies produced the movement’s most terrifying sermon, and he did it on purpose, from a doctrine he could state in one sentence.

Slide 17 · Edwards and the sermon at Enfield

On the slide: Edwards and the sermon at Enfield · ▪ Faith is not habit or membership / ▪ Nothing but God’s pleasure keeps sinners out of hell / ▪ A spider over a fire; waters behind a dam / ▪ Congregations wept and cried out

Teach the sermon from Station 2 of the activity, where students read Edwards in his own words. His claim was that nothing keeps sinners out of hell at any moment but the mere pleasure of God: not health, not careful living, not a record of religious duty, all of which he weighed together as no stronger than a spider’s web against a falling rock. The images carried the doctrine into the body: God holding the sinner over the pit as a person holds a spider over a fire, wrath rising like waters behind a dam, a bow bent with its arrow aimed at the heart, the sinner hanging by a slender thread. Hearers at Enfield wept and cried out during the sermon, and the printed sermon, issued in Boston that year, became the most famous sermon in American history. Edwards’s purpose was conversion, and terror was his chosen road to it.

Slide 18 · Old Lights and New Lights

On the slide: Old Lights and New Lights · ▪ Revivals drew crowds across the colonies / ▪ Itinerants preached beyond their own parishes / ▪ Old Lights: disorder and false feeling / ▪ New Lights: a faith nobody felt is no faith

The revival’s spread forced every congregation to take a side, and the split is the section’s institutional fact. Itinerant preachers crossed parish lines to preach where they were invited, or where they were not, which broke the settled rule that a minister’s authority ran inside his own parish and no further. Hearers began traveling to hear favored preachers and staying home from their own. Defenders of the older order, called Old Lights, charged the revival with disorder, with judging ministers, and with mistaking bodily effects, fainting and crying out, for the work of God. Its supporters, the New Lights, answered that a faith nobody felt was no faith at all, and that a church of the unconverted was a worse disorder than a noisy meeting. Congregations divided, some split outright, and new churches formed. The argument taught laypeople to evaluate their ministers, which many of them continued to do.

Slide 19 · Section G: Whitefield, Print, and the Enlightenment (divider)

On the slide: Whitefield, Print, and the Enlightenment · Tours from Georgia to New England / The colonies’ first shared celebrity

Section G closes the lesson with the man who turned local revivals into one colonial event. The portrait is George Whitefield in 1745, painted by Joseph Badger and held by Harvard Art Museums. Whitefield was an English preacher, ordained in the Church of England, who arrived in the colonies in 1739 and toured from Georgia to New England in 1739 and 1740, returning again and again across his life. He had no American parish, no assembly’s salary, and no bishop’s permission for most of the pulpits he filled. What he had was a voice that carried across a field, a genius for publicity learned in the London theater world he had left, and printers happy to sell him. The section pairs him with the Enlightenment, because the two movements shared the same presses and taught the same habit by opposite roads.

Slide 20 · Whitefield on tour, 1739 and 1740

On the slide: Whitefield on tour, 1739 and 1740 · ▪ Churches when they opened; open air when they did not / ▪ Crowds in the thousands, beyond any town’s size / ▪ His journal, New York, November 1740: fainting, weeping / ▪ Franklin

Teach Whitefield from his own journal, which Station 3 of the activity prints from the Oxford Text Archive transcription. In New York on November 1, 1740, he preached twice to very crowded auditories, some hearers fainted in the evening, and the next day the whole congregation was alarmed, with shrieking, crying, weeping, and wailing heard in every corner. He recorded effects the way Edwards recorded doctrine, as evidence the work was real. His method scaled because print scaled it: newspapers announced his schedule town by town, his journals and sermons were printed and sold along the route, and Benjamin Franklin, who printed his works in Philadelphia and became his friend, once measured how far the voice carried and calculated the crowd could exceed thirty thousand hearers. A farmer in Georgia and a merchant in Boston could both claim to have heard the same man, which no colonial experience had offered before.

Slide 21 · What the Awakening taught beyond religion

On the slide: What the Awakening taught beyond religion · ▪ Hearers judged rival preachers for themselves / ▪ Itinerants challenged settled ministers, and survived / ▪ Baptists and Methodists multiplied, in the South above all / ▪

This slide collects the consequences the reading draws, and each fragment is a transfer of judgment downward. Ordinary hearers compared preachers, followed the ones who moved them, and let the others preach to empty benches, a consumer’s discipline applied to the pulpit. Itinerants who defied parish boundaries were denounced and sometimes prosecuted, and they kept preaching, which taught observers that a settled institution’s disapproval was survivable. Denominations that welcomed the revival grew fastest on it: Baptists and Methodists multiplied, especially in the South, where their preachers reached people the wide Anglican parishes had barely served. Black hearers, enslaved and free, heard a message that addressed their souls directly and formed churches and faiths of their own inside the movement’s openings. None of this was a political program. All of it was practice in deciding whose word to trust.

Slide 22 · 18,000 sermons Whitefield preached in his lifetime

On the slide: 18,000 · sermons Whitefield preached in his lifetime · An average of about 500 a year, across Britain and seven tours of the colonies

The figure comes from the Christian Classics Ethereal Library’s summary of Whitefield’s life, which counts more than 18,000 sermons across thirty four years of preaching, an average of about five hundred a year or ten a week. Hold the number up as a fact about labor and logistics rather than about fame. Each sermon was an event his audience walked or rode to, in an age without amplification, and most were versions of sermons he had delivered hundreds of times, improving in delivery with every repetition in the way Franklin admired. Seven crossings of the Atlantic stand inside the total. The colonies did not receive a distant celebrity’s reputation. They received the man himself, repeatedly, in town after town, and the repetition is why a shared colonial experience could form around one preacher before any shared politics existed.

Slide 23 · The Enlightenment beside the revival

On the slide: The Enlightenment beside the revival · ▪ Reason, observation, and natural law / ▪ Reaching the colonies through books, colleges, print / ▪ Franklin: experiments, writings, a wider world / ▪ Rivals over truth; partner

Close the content with the pairing the lesson insists on, stated as rivals and partners rather than as one tide. The Enlightenment prized reason, observation, and natural law over tradition and religious authority. It reached the colonies through imported books, through the colleges at Harvard, William and Mary, and Yale, and through the same newspapers and presses that carried Whitefield. Franklin is its local face: a printer who became an experimenter, testing electricity and publishing what he found for readers to judge. The rivalry was genuine. Edwards and the philosophes disagreed about where truth was found, in the awakened heart or in reasoned observation, and said so. The partnership was also genuine. Both currents told colonists that an inherited authority, a minister’s, a governor’s, a tradition’s, had to answer to their own judgment. That is the lesson’s conclusion, and the writing task argues it.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for self-government and revival

On the slide: Words for self-government and revival · salutary neglect · Britain leaving the colonies to govern themselves · Zenger trial · the 1735 trial; truth argued as a defense to libel · Great Awakening · the revival movement of

These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them. The first term is salutary neglect: the British practice through much of the early 1700s of leaving the colonies largely to govern themselves while Britain attended to wars and politics in Europe. The second is Zenger trial: the New York trial of 1735 in which printer John Peter Zenger was acquitted of seditious libel after his lawyer argued that true statements could not be libel, a landmark for freedom of the press. The third is Great Awakening: the religious revival movement that swept the colonies in the 1730s and 1740s, emphasizing personal faith and emotional preaching over church tradition and learning. The fourth is revival: a religious meeting or series of meetings meant to reawaken faith and convert sinners, the characteristic gathering of the Great Awakening.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for the two preachers and the other current

On the slide: Words for the two preachers and the other current · Jonathan Edwards · Northampton minister of the Enfield sermon, 1741 · George Whitefield · touring preacher of the open air crowds · Enlightenment · reason and observati

Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly. The first is Jonathan Edwards: the Massachusetts minister whose preaching, including the sermon Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God in 1741, helped ignite the Great Awakening in New England. The second is George Whitefield: the English preacher whose tours of the colonies during the Great Awakening drew open-air crowds in the thousands and made him the colonies' first shared celebrity. The third is Enlightenment: the European intellectual movement that prized reason, observation, and natural law over tradition and religious authority, reaching the colonies through books, colleges, and print. Keep the two preachers distinct in the notes. Edwards was the settled scholar who wrote the revival’s theology from one pulpit. Whitefield was the itinerant who carried it between colonies, and the Enlightenment is neither of them: it is the rival current that shared their presses.

Slide 26 · The record at a glance

On the slide: Governed, worked, argued · Governed · Assemblies under salutary neglect · The purse checked the governor · Printed · The Zenger verdict, 1735 · A jury refused; the law stood · Worked · The household under coverture · Law

This slide mirrors the final table in the guided notes, The Record at a Glance, and students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must. The five rows compress the argument of the hour. Governed: assemblies held the purse while Britain looked to Europe, and self-government became ordinary practice. Printed: a New York jury refused to convict a printer in 1735, changing no law and changing what colonists expected a jury and a newspaper could do. Worked: the household ran the colony’s production under a legal order that credited its head and subordinated wives, children, servants, and enslaved people, whose work and families filled the record anyway. Believed: revival preachers put every hearer in the position of judge. Reasoned: the Enlightenment asked the same of every reader. The next lesson takes this practiced, self-governing society into a war for the continent.
