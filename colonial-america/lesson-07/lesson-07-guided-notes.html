Guided Notes: Governing, Working, and Arguing About God

Lesson question: How did colonists govern themselves, raise their families, and argue about God?

Unit question: How did the meeting of Europe, Africa, and the Americas remake all three worlds, and what kind of society grew up in England's colonies?

Key Terms

salutary neglect: the British practice through much of the early 1700s of leaving the colonies largely to govern themselves while Britain attended to wars and politics in Europe

Zenger trial: the New York trial of 1735 in which printer John Peter Zenger was acquitted of seditious libel after his lawyer argued that true statements could not be libel, a landmark for freedom of the press

Great Awakening: the religious revival movement that swept the colonies in the 1730s and 1740s, emphasizing personal faith and emotional preaching over church tradition and learning

Jonathan Edwards: the Massachusetts minister whose preaching, including the sermon Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God in 1741, helped ignite the Great Awakening in New England

George Whitefield: the English preacher whose tours of the colonies during the Great Awakening drew open-air crowds in the thousands and made him the colonies' first shared celebrity

Enlightenment: the European intellectual movement that prized reason, observation, and natural law over tradition and religious authority, reaching the colonies through books, colleges, and print

revival: a religious meeting or series of meetings meant to reawaken faith and convert sinners, the characteristic gathering of the Great Awakening

Summary table. Assemblies under neglect: elected assemblies voted taxes, laws, and the governor's salary while Britain attended to Europe, a practice Burke later named salutary neglect. It taught colonists that governing was their ordinary work and that the power of the purse could check an appointed governor. The Zenger verdict, 1735: a jury acquitted a printer whose lawyer argued that true statements could not be libel, against the judge's instruction and without changing the law of seditious libel. It taught colonists that a jury of their neighbors could refuse to enforce a governor's prosecution and that print could criticize power. The household: fathers held legal authority over wives, children, servants, and enslaved people, and coverture erased a married woman in law, while women worked, traded, and taught, children labored and apprenticed out, and enslaved people built families and kept African faiths inside the order that subordinated them. It taught obedience inside a hierarchy and, in the daily work of the people inside it, how much of colonial life ran on labor the law did not credit. Revival and reason: the Great Awakening sent itinerants like Edwards and Whitefield to crowds who judged preachers for themselves, and the Enlightenment prized reason and observation, reaching the colonies through print. Together they trained colonists to question inherited authority in the pulpit, in print, and in the assembly.

Exit ticket 1. Salutary neglect is the British practice through much of the early 1700s of leaving the colonies largely to govern themselves while Britain attended to wars and politics in Europe. The power was the power of the purse. Assemblies voted taxes and the governor's salary, so the governor depended on them for money.

Exit ticket 2. Hamilton admitted that Zenger printed the papers and argued that true statements could not be libel. The jury acquitted against the judge's instruction. The verdict did not change the law because a jury's acquittal sets no precedent and repeals nothing. The law of seditious libel stood unchanged, and another printer could still be charged under it.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that names one practice from each field with its lesson. Strong answers join the assembly's power of the purse under salutary neglect, the household's legal hierarchy under coverture and the work of the women, children, servants, and enslaved people inside it, and the Awakening's revivals, where hearers judged preachers for themselves, into the lesson's conclusion that colonists had spent a generation practicing how to judge authority.
