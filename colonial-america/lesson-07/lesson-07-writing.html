Writing Task: Ready to Judge Authority

The prompt. Answer this question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Which force did most to make colonists ready to judge authority for themselves: self-government, print, or revival? Take one force as your verdict. A strong essay first states how that force worked in practice, in the reading's terms, before it argues that the force reached furthest. It also answers the strongest fact against the force it chooses. If you choose self-government, you must deal with the assemblies governing inside an empire that chose to neglect them, and with a household order that denied legal standing to wives and to the enslaved. If you choose print, you must deal with the Zenger verdict changing no law. If you choose revival, you must deal with the crowds going home to that same household order, and with Old Lights who heard disorder where New Lights heard faith. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. Every colony had an elected assembly voting taxes, laws, and the governor's salary. Governors depended on assemblies for money, and assemblies used the power of the purse to claim a larger share of governing decade by decade.

2. Through much of the early 1700s Britain attended to wars and politics in Europe and left the colonies largely to govern themselves. Burke later named the practice salutary neglect. No law granted the self-government the colonists practiced.

3. In August 1735 a New York jury acquitted printer John Peter Zenger of seditious libel after Andrew Hamilton argued that true statements could not be libel. The jury acted against the judge's instruction. The verdict changed no law and became a landmark for press freedom in practice and in memory.

4. Under coverture a married woman could not hold property, sign contracts, or sue. Women still ran farms, kept shops, traded, and taught, and widows could hold property and make wills, as Judith Johns of Goochland County did in 1735, dividing her estate equally among her children.

5. Jonathan Edwards preached at Northampton and, at Enfield in July 1741, preached Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God, teaching that nothing but God's pleasure kept sinners out of hell. Congregations wept and cried out, and congregations split between Old Lights and New Lights.

6. George Whitefield toured from Georgia to New England in 1739 and 1740, preaching in the open air to crowds in the thousands. Newspapers printed his schedules, sermons, and journals, and his November 1740 journal records crowded auditories, fainting, and a congregation alarmed in every corner.

7. Beside the revival, the Enlightenment prized reason, observation, and natural law and reached the colonies through books, colleges, and print. Rivals over where truth was found, revival and Enlightenment were partners in training colonists to question inherited authority.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • An accurate statement of how the chosen force worked in practice before any verdict about its reach. Self-government worked through the purse, bank items 1 and 2. Print worked through a defended newspaper and a reported tour, bank items 3 and 6. Revival worked through preaching that hearers judged for themselves, bank items 5 and 6. (Blueprint 13 and 14)
  • A verdict carried by documents. The self-government verdict rests chiefly on bank items 1 and 2, yearly practice under neglect. (Blueprint 13) The print verdict rests chiefly on bank items 3 and 6, Hamilton's argument and verdict, and the newspaper machinery around Whitefield. (Blueprint 13 and 14) The revival verdict rests chiefly on bank items 5 and 6, Edwards's sermon and Whitefield's recorded crowds, supported by bank item 7 on the two currents. (Blueprint 14)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately and in date order. Bank item 4, coverture and the Johns will, serves every verdict as the standing limit on all three forces, and strong essays use it rather than waiting for the prompt to assign it.
  • A direct answer to the strongest fact against the verdict chosen. A self-government essay that never mentions neglect or coverture has described a training ground without its walls. A print essay that never mentions that the Zenger verdict changed no law has skipped the hardest fact in its own file. A revival essay that never mentions the household order the crowds went home to, or the Old Lights, has argued enthusiasm rather than consequence.

Model opening for teacher reference. The strongest training was the one colonists practiced every year whether they noticed it or not. Preachers came and went and newspapers appeared weekly, but the assembly met on schedule, and every year the governor had to ask it for his salary and his government's money. Under a neglect no law had granted, that yearly bargaining taught the men who sat in thirteen assemblies that governing was their work and that an appointed ruler could be checked by the simple refusal to pay. A full model in this line concedes the limits directly, that the practice ran inside an empire that could have ended it and beside households where wives and enslaved people held no such standing, and argues that a habit practiced yearly by the colonies' own bodies outlasted both a single verdict and a passing revival.

Common weak answers. A verdict that colonists simply became rebellious earns partial credit at most, because the documents show practice and argument, not a mood. An essay that treats the Zenger verdict as a change in the law has reversed bank item 3. An essay that praises or condemns the Awakening without both halves of bank items 5 and 6, the sermon's claim and the crowds' recorded response, has read half the record.
