Quick Check: Self-Government and Revival

Directions: Read the document and the background, then answer the questions.

Document. Andrew Hamilton, defense summation in the trial of John Peter Zenger, New York, August 1735, printed from the audited TPSP bank record. Edition named in the bank record: the 19th-century State Trials reprint, via Constitution.org, of the narrative Zenger himself published in 1736.

"... it is not the cause of a poor printer, nor of New York alone, which you are now trying: No! It may, in its consequence, affect every freeman that lives under a British government on the main of America. It is the best cause; it is the cause of liberty ..."

"Power may justly be compared to a great river; while kept within its due bounds, it is both beautiful and useful; but when it overflows its banks, it is then too impetuous to be stemmed; it bears down all before it, and brings destruction and desolation wherever it comes."

Background. By the early 1700s every colony had an elected assembly that voted taxes, laws, and the governor's salary, while Britain, busy in Europe, practiced what Burke later called salutary neglect. In households, coverture covered a wife's legal identity with her husband's, though women worked, traded, and taught, and widows regained legal standing. In the 1730s and 1740s the Great Awakening swept the colonies: Edwards preached Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God at Enfield in 1741, Whitefield toured from Georgia to New England drawing open air crowds in the thousands, and the Enlightenment reached the colonies through books, colleges, and print.

1. In the first passage, whose cause does Hamilton say the jury is trying, and what does he name that cause?

2. In the river passage, state the condition under which power is useful and the result when that condition fails.

3. Using the background, define salutary neglect and name the assembly power that made self-government real in practice.

4. Using the background, answer both parts. (a) Name the preacher, the sermon, the place, and the year for the Awakening's most famous sermon. (b) State one thing the Great Awakening and the Enlightenment shared, although they were rivals over where truth was found.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Whose cause, and what does Hamilton name it? Not the cause of a poor printer, nor of New York alone. Hamilton says the verdict may affect every freeman living under a British government on the main of America, and he names it the cause of liberty. Both the widening and the name are required for full credit. (Blueprint 13)

2. State the condition and the result. Power kept within its due bounds is both beautiful and useful. When it overflows its banks it becomes too impetuous to be stemmed and brings destruction and desolation wherever it comes. (Blueprint 13)

3. Define salutary neglect, and name the assembly power. Salutary neglect is the British practice through much of the early 1700s of leaving the colonies largely to govern themselves while Britain attended to wars and politics in Europe. The power was the power of the purse. Assemblies voted taxes and the governor's salary, so governors depended on the assemblies they were sent to govern. (Blueprint 13)

4. (a) Name the preacher, sermon, place, and year. (b) State one shared thing. (a) Jonathan Edwards preached Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God at Enfield, Connecticut, in July 1741. All four facts are required for full credit. (b) Both the Awakening and the Enlightenment trained colonists to question inherited authority and judge it for themselves. The Awakening did it by sending hearers to judge rival preachers and their own spiritual state, spread by itinerants and print. The Enlightenment did it by prizing reason, observation, and natural law over tradition and religious authority. Either route, stated accurately, earns credit when the shared training is named. (Blueprint 14)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Questions 1 and 2 test close reading of Hamilton's own sentences, which the activity also uses. Questions 3 and 4 test the lesson's two blueprint lines in order: self-government under neglect and the Zenger verdict, then revival and the Enlightenment as rival teachers of the same habit of judgment.
