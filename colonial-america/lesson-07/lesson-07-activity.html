Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: Hamilton's Summation in the Zenger Trial, 1735

Source: Andrew Hamilton, defense summation in the trial of John Peter Zenger, New York, August 1735, printed from the audited TPSP bank record. Edition named in the bank record: the 19th-century State Trials reprint, via Constitution.org, of the narrative Zenger himself published in 1736. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

"I do (for my client) confess, that he both printed and published the two newspapers set forth in the information, and I hope in so doing he has committed no crime."

[...]

"... it is not the cause of a poor printer, nor of New York alone, which you are now trying: No! It may, in its consequence, affect every freeman that lives under a British government on the main of America. It is the best cause; it is the cause of liberty ..."

[...]

"Power may justly be compared to a great river; while kept within its due bounds, it is both beautiful and useful; but when it overflows its banks, it is then too impetuous to be stemmed; it bears down all before it, and brings destruction and desolation wherever it comes."

Hamilton begins by confessing the fact the prosecution had to prove. State what he confesses, and explain why a defense lawyer would give away the fact and fight on the principle instead.

In the river passage, when is power beautiful and useful, and what happens when it overflows its banks? Answer in Hamilton's own terms.

Station 2: Edwards, Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God, 1741

Source: Jonathan Edwards, "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God," preached at Enfield, Connecticut, July 8, 1741, printed from the audited TPSP bank record. Edition named in the bank record: VIVA Pressbooks, American Literature I, from the 1741 Boston printing. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

"There is nothing that keeps wicked men at any one moment out of hell, but the mere pleasure of God."

[...]

"... your healthy constitution, and your own care and prudence, and best contrivance, and all your righteousness, would have no more influence to uphold you and keep you out of hell than a spider's web would have to stop a falling rock."

[...]

"The God that holds you over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider or some loathsome insect over the fire, abhors you, and is dreadfully provoked ..."

[...]

"O sinner! consider the fearful danger you are in. ... You hang by a slender thread, with the flames of divine wrath flashing about it, and ready every moment to singe it and burn it asunder ..."

State Edwards's central claim in one sentence, using his own phrase for what alone keeps a sinner out of hell.

Edwards lists the things his hearers trusted to keep them safe: health, care and prudence, contrivance, righteousness. What does he say all of them together can do against a falling rock, and what is he asking his hearers to stop trusting?

Station 3: Whitefield's Journal, New York, November 1740

Source: George Whitefield, A Continuation of the Reverend Mr. Whitefield's Journal, from his leaving New-England, October 1740, to his arrival at Falmouth in England, as transcribed by the Text Creation Partnership and held by the Oxford Text Archive, Bodleian Libraries, University of Oxford. Entries for November 1 and 2, 1740, in New York. Spelling is as the edition carries it.

"Saturday, November 1. ... Preached twice as Yesterday to very crowded Auditories, and neither Time without Power. In the Evening Exercise, some fainted, and the Lord seemed to shew us more and more, that a Time for favouring New-York was near at Hand."

"Sunday, November 2. ... Immediately the whole Congregation was alarmed. Shrieking, Crying, Weeping and Wailing were to be heard in every Corner. Men's Hearts failing them for Fear, and many falling into the Arms of their Friends."

Teaching summary, not a quotation: Whitefield's tours were also print events. Newspapers carried his schedules and reports, his journals and sermons were printed and sold along his route, and Benjamin Franklin printed his works in Philadelphia. A reader who never heard him could follow the tour in print.

From the journal itself, name two physical effects Whitefield records in his hearers across these two days.

Whitefield was a visiting preacher with no parish of his own in New York, and settled ministers there had not invited the commotion he records. State whose authority a crowded, fainting congregation challenged, and how print carried that challenge to people who were never in the room.

Station 4: The Assembly's Purse, a Record of Practice

Source: This station is a teaching summary, written for this lesson from the standard record of colonial government. It is not a quotation. It states the practice the reading describes, in the order an assembly's year ran.

1. The governor opens the session and asks the assembly for money: his salary, pay for officials, and funds for roads, forts, and defense.

2. The assembly votes the taxes and writes the money bills. By custom in most colonies, money bills began in the elected assembly, not in the governor's council.

3. When a governor pressed a policy the assembly opposed, the assembly could delay his salary bill, cut it, or attach conditions to it.

4. Across the early 1700s, while Britain attended to Europe, assemblies in colony after colony turned this yearly bargaining into a standing share of governing.

Name the one request that brought the governor before the assembly every year, and state why that request gave the assembly a power the governor's commission did not give him.

Item 3 describes a weapon that works without a vote of defiance. Explain how delaying or conditioning a salary bill could move a governor, and why salutary neglect made the weapon stronger over time.

Station 5: The Will of Judith Johns, Goochland County, Virginia, 1735

Source: Last Will and Testament of Judith Johns, written April 3, 1735 and proved in the Goochland County court on May 20, 1735, as transcribed from the county record and consulted in the transcription carried at FamilySearch, "Last Will and Testament of Judith Johns." Spelling is as the transcription carries it. Judith Johns was a widow. Under coverture a married woman could not ordinarily make a will of property in her own name. A widow could.

"I give to my daughter Martha my Bed whereon I now ly with furniture & my trunk & wareing clothes & one mare and desire my son Robert may have the first fole that the mare brings that lives. All other of my Estate to be equally dyvided among all my children."

"I also appoint my son Joseph Executor of this my last will and Testament & also my will is that my Estate be not sold at publick saile."

The inventory of her estate taken that July listed, among its entries, 25 head of cattle, three spinning wheels, pots and an iron spit, pewter, tobacco hogsheads, and tobacco by weight. The household's wealth was work equipment and livestock, not coin.

State what Martha receives, what Robert is to receive, and how the rest of the estate is divided. Then state what kind of household economy the inventory shows.

This will exists because its maker was a widow. Explain what coverture would have prevented Judith Johns from doing while her husband lived, and what her two instructions about the estate, equal division and no public sale, were protecting.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: Hamilton's Summation

What does Hamilton confess, and why fight on principle? He confesses that Zenger printed and published the two newspapers named in the charge, the very fact the prosecution had to prove. He gives the fact away because it was not in dispute and could not be won. The contest he could win was the principle, whether truth could be a defense against libel. Conceding the printing cleared the courtroom of everything except that question and put the jury at the center of the case.

When is power beautiful and useful, and what happens when it overflows? In Hamilton's terms, power kept within its due bounds is both beautiful and useful, like a great river. When it overflows its banks it becomes too impetuous to be stemmed, bears down all before it, and brings destruction and desolation wherever it comes. Credit answers that keep the image's logic. Bounds are what make power useful, and the jury's verdict is presented as one of the banks.

Station 2: Edwards

State the central claim. Nothing keeps wicked men at any one moment out of hell but the mere pleasure of God. The phrase mere pleasure carries the claim. No human quality, effort, or arrangement holds the sinner up. Only God's unowed decision does.

What can health, prudence, contrivance, and righteousness do, and what must hearers stop trusting? Together they would have no more influence to keep a sinner out of hell than a spider's web would have to stop a falling rock. Edwards is asking his hearers to stop trusting their own safety arrangements, their health, their careful living, and their record of religious duty, as grounds for confidence about their state. The sermon aims to remove every ground of self assurance so that conversion, not improvement, becomes the hearer's errand.

Station 3: Whitefield's Journal

Name two physical effects from the journal. Any two of the following, in the journal's own record: hearers fainted in the evening exercise; the congregation was alarmed; shrieking, crying, weeping, and wailing were heard in every corner; men's hearts failed them for fear; many fell into the arms of their friends. The crowds themselves, described as very crowded auditories on both days, also earn credit.

Whose authority did the congregation challenge, and how did print carry it? The challenge ran against settled ministers, who held the pulpits, set the terms of membership, and in many places opposed the itinerants. A visiting preacher with no parish drew crowds that wept and fainted, and the hearers' response was itself a judgment on the ministry they ordinarily sat under. Print carried the challenge beyond the room, since newspapers reported the tours and the journals and sermons were printed and sold along the route, so readers who never heard Whitefield could judge for themselves.

Station 4: The Assembly's Purse

Name the yearly request, and state the power it created. The governor came to the assembly asking for money, beginning with his own salary and the funds of government. Because the assembly voted the taxes and wrote the money bills, the governor's need made him a petitioner before the men he was sent to govern. That dependence was a power no commission granted the assembly in words. It grew out of the yearly practice of supply.

How could delay or conditions move a governor, and why did neglect strengthen the weapon? A governor whose salary bill was delayed, cut, or conditioned felt the pressure in his own income and could end it only by bargaining. The weapon needed no defiance because it used the ordinary forms of business. Salutary neglect strengthened it because, year after year without close supervision from London, the bargaining hardened into custom.

Station 5: The Will of Judith Johns

State the bequests and the household economy. Martha receives her mother's bed with its furniture, her trunk, her wearing clothes, and one mare. Robert is to have the first foal the mare brings that lives. All the rest of the estate is to be divided equally among all the children. The inventory shows a working household economy: 25 head of cattle, three spinning wheels, cooking iron, pewter, and tobacco in hogsheads and by weight. The estate's value sat in livestock, tools for making cloth and food, and the cash crop, not in money.

What would coverture have prevented, and what were the two instructions protecting? While her husband lived, coverture covered Judith Johns's legal identity with his, so she could not hold property in her own name or dispose of it by a will of her own. Widowhood restored that standing, which is why this will exists. The equal division protected the younger children against a settlement that favored the eldest, and the bar on a public sale kept the household's working goods from being sold at forced prices and scattered, leaving the equipment and stock with the children who would work with them.

Part 2: Authority Claimed, Authority Questioned (Grading Guide)

Station 1 claims the authority of truth and of a jury of freemen, and questions the governor's prosecution and the law that made truth no defense. Station 2 claims God's absolute authority over every hearer, and questions the hearers' trust in their own health, prudence, and religious record, and by extension any comfortable ministry that let them keep that trust. Station 3 claims the authority of the preached word as the hearers themselves judged it, and questions settled ministers whose pulpits drew no such response. Station 4 claims the assembly's authority over supply, and questions the governor's claim to govern without bargaining. Station 5 claims a widow's authority to dispose of her household's goods and a mother's judgment about her children, exercised inside a legal order that gave that authority to widows and denied it to wives. Closing verdicts earn full credit when they argue from at least two stations and answer a counterclaim from the file. The self-government case rests on Station 4 and on Station 1's jury, colonists deciding in bodies they staffed. The print case rests on Stations 1 and 3, a newspaper defended in court and a tour carried by newspapers and printed journals. The revival case rests on Stations 2 and 3, hearers taught to judge their own state and their ministers. The strongest counterclaim is in the file itself. The Zenger jury changed no law, the assemblies bargained inside an empire that let them, and the revival's crowds went home to households ordered by coverture and slavery. The rubric credits the evidence and the counterclaim answered, not the force chosen.
