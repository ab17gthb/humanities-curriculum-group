Teacher Notes

Colonial America · Lesson 3 · Jamestown and the Chesapeake

These are the speaker notes from the slide deck, in slide order, set for printing. The deck itself carries the main ideas in a few fragments per slide; the teaching is here. Print this and keep it beside you while you teach. Each entry is headed by its slide number and shows what is on that slide, so you can find your place without flipping back to the screen. Vocabulary definitions match the student guided notes word for word.

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: Jamestown and the Chesapeake · A company venture, a starving winter, a chief’s corn, and the crop that remade a colony.

Open with the map. It is John Smith’s map of Virginia, drawn from his surveys of the Chesapeake in 1608, engraved by William Hole, and first published in 1612, the most accurate map of the bay made in its century. Everything this lesson tells happened inside the country the map charts, and the map carries the lesson’s first correction. The rivers are lined with the towns of the Powhatan peoples. Jamestown, founded in 1607, was a small English fort planted inside another ruler’s country, and it survived its first years on that ruler’s decisions. The lesson then follows the crop that ended the dependence. Tobacco made the colony pay, and tobacco demanded land and labor on terms that built the Chesapeake.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: How did a starving outpost become a tobacco colony, and what did tobacco demand? · Unit question: How did the meeting of Europe, Africa, and the Americas remake all three worlds, and what kind of society grew up in Engla

Read the lesson question aloud and separate its two halves, because the exit ticket and the writing task grade them separately. The first half is about survival and transformation: how a fort that nearly starved in the winter of 1609 and 1610 became, within a few years, a colony organized around one export crop. The second half is about consequences, and it is the harder half: what the crop demanded. Tobacco demanded fresh land as it wore out the soil, and that land belonged to the Powhatan peoples. It demanded labor in every season, and the search for that labor runs from indentured servants to the first Africans of 1619 to the decisive turn toward enslaved labor after 1676. Connect the question to the unit question beneath it. The society that grew up in the Chesapeake was built by those two demands.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: From a starving fort to a tobacco colony · A · A Company of Investors · The Virginia Company, its charter, and its ships · B · A Peninsula, and the Starving Time · A bad site, and the winter of 1609 and 1610 · C · The Po

This map follows the guided notes section by section. Sections A and B tell the founding as a business failure nearly completed: a joint-stock company’s fort, planted for defense on a sickly peninsula, reduced by the starving time from some five hundred people to about sixty. Section C restores the other government in the story, Wahunsenacawh’s paramount chiefdom, whose corn and whose diplomacy kept the fort alive. Tell students to track one question through every section: who paid the cost of each stage, and in what coin?

Slide 4 · Section A: A Company of Investors (divider)

On the slide: A Company of Investors · London, 1606: merchants pool their money, share the risk, and charter a colony as a business.

Teach the founding as a financial instrument before it is an adventure, because the instrument explains the colony’s later choices. Spain’s kings had paid for empire out of royal treasuries. England’s Virginia Company of London was a joint-stock company, chartered by King James I in 1606. Investors bought shares, pooled their money, and shared the costs, the risk of losing everything, and any profit. That structure set the colony’s clock. Investors who had risked money expected a return, and a colony that produced no return would lose its supply ships. The orders given the first fleet show the expectation plainly: find gold, find a passage to the Pacific, and find the Roanoke colonists lost twenty years before. Three ships carrying 144 men and boys sailed in December 1606 to execute a business plan on a shore none of the investors had seen.

Slide 5 · Shares, risk, and three ships

On the slide: ▪ The Virginia Company of London, chartered 1606 / ▪ Investors pool money and share costs, risks, profits / ▪ Orders: gold, a Pacific passage, the lost Roanoke colony / ▪ Three ships, 144 men and boys, December 1606

Carry both definitions exactly as the guided notes print them. A joint-stock company is this: joint-stock company: a business owned by investors who pool their money and share its costs, risks, and profits, the form used to fund the first English colonies. Jamestown is this: Jamestown: the first permanent English settlement in North America, founded in 1607 on the James River in Virginia by the Virginia Company. Then make the class sit with the passenger list’s shape, because the reading insists on it. The company of settlers held many gentlemen unaccustomed to labor and few farmers, carpenters, and fishermen in proportion to the work waiting. The venture had been stocked, in part, for the quick strike its investors imagined, gold and a passage west, rather than for the slow work of feeding a town through a winter. When students ask later why the colony starved with a river full of fish beside it, this slide is part of the answer. The plan, the site, and the company of men were all fitted to a colony that existed first on paper in London.

Slide 6 · Section B: A Peninsula, and the Starving Time (divider)

On the slide: A Peninsula, and the Starving Time · May 1607: a fort chosen against Spanish ships, on brackish water, in another ruler’s country.

The plate on this slide is Theodor de Bry’s engraving of the English arrival in Virginia, made after John White’s drawings and published in 1590, a generation before Jamestown, for Raleigh’s Roanoke voyages. Use it to fix the geography before the disaster. The settlers of 1607 built their fort on a low peninsula in the James River, far enough upriver to spot Spanish ships and near enough to deep water to moor their own. The same site gave them brackish drinking water, swamp air and disease, and soil and hunting grounds already used by the Paspahegh people, whose town stood nearby. Defense they had considered. Water, food, and neighbors they had not, or not enough. The section’s subject is what those choices cost in the colony’s third winter, and the numbers on the next slides should be taught without softening.

Slide 7 · The winter of 1609 and 1610

On the slide: ▪ A site defensible against ships, poor for living / ▪ Brackish water, disease, few farmers among gentlemen / ▪ Trade and supply fail together / ▪ The starving time: winter 1609 and 1610 / ▪ The survivors prepare to

Teach the starving time from the colony’s own accounts, at documentary weight. By the autumn of 1609 some five hundred colonists were in Virginia, swollen by a supply fleet whose flagship had wrecked on Bermuda. Through that winter the Powhatan trade that had fed the town was cut off, the settlers were penned inside their fort, and the accounts record what followed without flinching: horses, dogs, and rats eaten, and in documented cases the dead. In June 1610 the roughly sixty survivors boarded ships to abandon Jamestown altogether and met a relief fleet coming up the river, which turned them back. Precision is the point here, because legend prefers a rescue story. What saved Jamestown in 1610 was not a hero. It was a supply fleet that arrived days after the colony had given itself up.

Slide 8 · About 60: survivors of the starving time

On the slide: survivors of the starving time, spring 1610 · Of some five hundred colonists in the autumn of 1609, after a winter the colony’s accounts record in full

Stop on this number and make the class price it. A loss of nearly nine colonists in ten, in a single winter, in a settlement that had been reinforced that same year. The causes stack, and students should be able to stack them: a site chosen for defense with brackish water, a company of settlers short of farmers and laborers, a supply system broken by wreck and distance, and a trade lifeline with the Powhatan chiefdom cut at the moment of greatest need. Hold the number against the founding slide. The investors of 1606 had bought shares in a venture that by 1610 had consumed most of the people sent to run it. The colony’s continuation was a decision made in London to spend more, and in Virginia a fleet’s arrival, not a recovery the survivors had achieved on their own.

Slide 9 · Section C: The Powhatan Paramount Chiefdom (divider)

On the slide: The Powhatan Paramount Chiefdom · Some thirty peoples under Wahunsenacawh, farming towns along the rivers of Tsenacommacah.

The plate on this slide is Theodor de Bry’s engraving of a Virginia town, made after John White’s watercolor and published with Thomas Hariot’s report of 1590. It shows the kind of settled farming country the English entered in 1607: houses in order, fields under cultivation, ceremony and work in their places. Teach this section as the restoration of the story’s second government. The Powhatan paramount chiefdom joined some thirty peoples of the Chesapeake under Wahunsenacawh, the leader the English called Powhatan, who had built his power by inheritance and conquest and ruled from Werowocomoco, on the York River. His people farmed corn, beans, and squash, hunted and fished, and traded across the bay. The English were a few hundred hungry strangers in his country, and everything that follows in this section is his statecraft toward them.

Slide 10 · A chief weighs the English

On the slide: ▪ Corn traded, and corn withheld, as policy / ▪ English strength tested season by season / ▪ Tools and copper valued; Smith kept alive to make them / ▪ Use them against his enemies, or drive them out

The detail on this slide comes from Smith’s own map: Wahunsenacawh in state at Werowocomoco, as Smith was brought before him in the winter of 1607 and 1608. Read the meeting as diplomacy from the chief’s side. He feasted his prisoner, promised him liberty within four days, promised corn and venison for the colony, and asked in return that the English make him hatchets and copper. He wanted English metal goods and, possibly, English guns and numbers against his enemies. He also withheld corn when withholding served him, and the colony’s hunger rose and fell with his decisions. State the section’s judgment plainly, because the documents support it: Jamestown survived its first years because Wahunsenacawh allowed it to, while he weighed whether the strangers were worth more as clients than as a memory.

Slide 11 · Section D: John Smith and His Two Stories (divider)

On the slide: John Smith and His Two Stories · The portrait is Pocahontas in London in 1616, the year the rescue first enters Smith’s writing.

The engraving on this slide is Simon van de Passe’s portrait of Pocahontas, made in London in 1616, the only portrait of her from her lifetime. She had come to England as Rebecca Rolfe, wife of John Rolfe, and the Virginia Company presented her as proof that colonization and conversion worked. That same year Smith wrote his letter to Queen Anne introducing her at court, and in that letter, written to win royal favor for Pocahontas and for the company, the rescue story appears in his writing for the first time, eight years after the meeting it describes. Set the section’s rule before the texts. Smith is taught in this lesson as a source with an author selling himself, and the class will read his two tellings of the same winter side by side and decide what the difference between them is evidence of.

Slide 12 · 1608 against 1624

On the slide: ▪ 1608, A True Relation: a feast, a bargain, a friendly release / ▪ No rescue in the first account / ▪ 1616, letter to Queen Anne: the rescue first appears / ▪ 1624, General History: the rescue in full, on the stones

Work the comparison slowly, because it is the lesson’s exercise in reading a source against itself. In the General History of 1624 Smith tells the scene in full: two great stones brought before Powhatan, his head laid on them, clubs raised, and Pocahontas laying her own head on his to save him. In A True Relation of 1608, published within months of the meeting, the same visit ends with a feast, promises of corn, and a friendly send home, and Pocahontas does not appear in it at all. Between the two stand the letter of 1616, written for a queen, in a year when the company was displaying Pocahontas in London. Historians cannot settle whether Smith survived a ritual of adoption he misunderstood or improved his story as his audience grew. Students can settle the method: the later and more useful a story is to its teller, the harder it must be checked.

Slide 13 · 1608: the account with no rescue in it

On the slide: the account with no rescue in it · The rescue first appears in Smith’s writing in 1616 and in full in 1624, sixteen years after the meeting

Use this slide to fix the chronology in memory, because the whole argument of section D is three dates. Sixteen oh eight: the meeting, and the account written within months, feast and release, no rescue. Sixteen sixteen: the letter to Queen Anne, presenting Pocahontas at court, first written mention of her saving his life. Sixteen twenty-four: the General History, the full scene, stones, clubs, and the princess’s head laid on his. Then ask the corroboration question the bank record asks: what other kinds of evidence could test the scene, records from the Powhatan side, or other colonists’ accounts of the same weeks? There are none for this meeting, which is itself a finding. A claim resting on one author, told three ways across sixteen years, must be carried in the mind at exactly that weight, no more and no less.

Slide 14 · Section E: Tobacco Remakes the Colony (divider)

On the slide: Tobacco Remakes the Colony · From about 1612 one leaf gives the company its profit, and the colony its marching orders.

Introduce tobacco as the answer to the investors’ question, and keep the economics in front of the romance. For five years the colony had consumed money and people and returned nothing London could sell. John Rolfe’s experiments with a milder West Indian strain of tobacco, planted from about 1612, produced a leaf English smokers wanted in quantity. The Spanish had supplied that market from their colonies. Now Virginia could supply it, and the company finally had its commodity. Teach the turn in the colony’s purposes that followed. A settlement planted to find gold and a passage west became a plantation colony organized around a single crop, and every institution of Chesapeake life, its land hunger, its labor systems, its spread along the rivers, and its wars for ground, descends from that reorganization.

Slide 15 · One leaf, two hungers

On the slide: ▪ Rolfe plants a West Indian strain, from about 1612 / ▪ England’s market takes all Virginia can ship / ▪ Tobacco exhausts the soil within a few years / ▪ Settlement spreads along the rivers onto Powhatan land / ▪ H

Carry the definition exactly. A cash crop is this: cash crop: a crop grown for sale and profit rather than for the grower's own use, as tobacco was grown in the Chesapeake. Then work the two hungers as mechanisms, not slogans. The soil hunger: tobacco stripped a field’s fertility within a few years, so a planter needed several times the land he cropped in any year, fresh ground resting in reserve, and the colony’s footprint spread along every navigable river onto land the Powhatan peoples farmed and hunted. The labor hunger: tobacco needed hands at setting, topping, weeding, cutting, hanging, and packing, across the whole year, in numbers no company of gentlemen could supply. Land and labor together are the crop’s real product in this lesson. The leaf paid London. These two demands built Virginia, and the next two sections take them in order, the political year first.

Slide 16 · Section F: 1619: An Assembly and a Ship (divider)

On the slide: 1619: An Assembly and a Ship · One summer joins the first elected assembly in English America and the first Africans in its mainland colonies.

Frame 1619 as the hinge year of the colony’s history, and insist on the joining, because students will meet the two events in separate textbooks if the lesson does not bind them. In the summer of 1619 the reformed Virginia Company under its new leadership sent Governor Yeardley instructions to call an assembly, part of a bid to make the colony attractive to settlers by giving landowning colonists a voice in their laws. The assembly met at Jamestown at the end of July. Within weeks of its sitting, a ship reached Virginia carrying Africans for sale. The colony gained representative government and began its history of African slavery in the same season, out of the same company’s Virginia. Neither fact cancels the other, and the rest of American history is partly the working out of their coexistence.

Slide 17 · The summer of 1619

On the slide: ▪ The House of Burgesses meets at Jamestown / ▪ Landowning colonists choose representatives / ▪ Weeks later, a ship sells the colony some twenty Africans / ▪ Taken from a Portuguese slave ship in the Atlantic / ▪ Th

Carry the definition exactly. The House of Burgesses is this: House of Burgesses: the first elected assembly in English America, which met at Jamestown in 1619, where landowning colonists chose representatives to make the colony's laws. Then teach the second event with the honesty the record requires. The Africans who arrived in late August 1619 had been taken from a Portuguese slave ship bound for Mexico and were traded to the colony for provisions. Whether they were held as servants for a term, as some early Africans in Virginia plainly were, or as enslaved for life, the surviving records do not say clearly, and historians still argue the question from thin evidence. What is not argued is the sequel: over the next two generations Virginia’s assembly wrote slavery into statute, on the mother’s status, on baptism, on property, until the ambiguity of 1619 had been replaced by hereditary racial slavery in law.

Slide 18 · 20: Africans brought to Virginia in 1619

On the slide: Africans brought to Virginia in 1619 · Some twenty, taken from a Portuguese slave ship, the first recorded in England’s mainland colonies; their legal status is still argued by historians

Stop on this small number against the scale of what follows from it. Some twenty people, in a colony of about a thousand, arriving weeks after the first assembly sat. Teach the figure with its documentary frame: the count comes from the colony’s own reporting of the ship’s arrival, the people named in it had been baptized in Angola and carried across the Atlantic in a Portuguese slaver before an English privateer took them. Then state the long arc without inflating the first step. These twenty were the beginning of African slavery in England’s mainland colonies, not yet its legal system. Lesson 6 of this unit takes up the trade and the statutes whole. Here the point is the year: the future of Virginia was decided twice in one summer, in the assembly house and at the wharf, and students should leave able to say why both landings belong in the same sentence.

Slide 19 · Section G: Land, Labor, and Rebellion (divider)

On the slide: Land, Labor, and Rebellion · Headrights fill the colony with servants, the servants grow free and angry, and 1676 settles the labor question.

Close the lesson by following the labor supply to its crisis, because section G is where the crop’s second hunger is answered in full. After tobacco, Virginia’s scarce resource was people to work it, and the company answered with the headright, paying land for imported labor. For two generations the Chesapeake ran on young English servants who gambled their worst years against a start in a colony where many of them died in the first summer. The survivors who lived out their terms became the frontier population of the 1670s, land hungry, armed, and governed by men whose great estates blocked their way. The section’s story is how that population rose in 1676, what it did, and what the colony’s great men decided, in its ashes, about the labor they would buy afterward.

Slide 20 · Fifty acres a head

On the slide: ▪ The headright: fifty acres for every passage paid / ▪ Import twenty servants, gain a thousand acres / ▪ Servants bound four to seven years for passage / ▪ Freedom dues at the term’s end / ▪ Many die of seasoning f

Carry both definitions exactly. The headright system is this: headright system: the Virginia policy that granted fifty acres of land to any person who paid for an immigrant's passage to the colony, used to recruit laborers. An indentured servant is this: indentured servant: a laborer who agreed to work for a set number of years, usually four to seven, in exchange for passage to America and freedom dues at the end of the term. Then do the arithmetic aloud, because the headright is a machine and students should see it work. A passage cost a planter about the price of a servant’s first crop year. The passage bought two assets, the servant’s labor for the term and fifty acres forever. Import twenty servants and the estate gained a thousand acres along with twenty workers. Land, the colony’s wealth, flowed to the men who could import the most people, which is one reason the great planters of the later century were great. The servants’ side of the machine is the next fact: the seasoning, the first summer’s fevers, killed them in numbers the London recruiting pamphlets never printed.

Slide 21 · 1676: the colony burns its capital

On the slide: ▪ Frontier settlers and former servants follow Bacon / ▪ His declaration charges Berkeley with unjust taxes / ▪ And with shielding Indigenous peoples from retaliation / ▪ Jamestown burned, September 1676 / ▪ Bacon d

Carry the definition exactly. Bacon’s Rebellion is this: Bacon's Rebellion: the Virginia uprising of 1676 led by Nathaniel Bacon, in which frontier settlers and former servants attacked Indigenous peoples and burned Jamestown, pushing great planters toward enslaved labor. Then read the declaration as the station document it is, because Bacon’s own charges fix the rising’s politics. He accused Governor Berkeley of great unjust taxes raised for private favorites, and of protecting and emboldening the Indigenous peoples against the king’s loyal subjects, and he signed himself Generall by Consent of the people while calling the king’s governor a traitor. His men attacked Indigenous peoples, including peoples at peace with the colony, marched on Jamestown, and burned it in September 1676. Bacon died of illness that October and the rebellion collapsed. The planters’ lesson from the rising was about labor: servants who survived became free, armed, and land hungry. Enslaved Africans could be held for life, marked by sight, and barred from ever standing where Bacon’s men had stood. The turn toward enslaved labor, already under way, became decisive.

Slide 22 · The colony at a glance

On the slide: Company, crop, labor, and turn · The company · Joint-stock venture, chartered 1606 · A profit demanded; none returned for years · The hunger · Starving time, 1609 and 1610 · About 60 survive of some 500 · The chiefdom ·

This slide mirrors the closing table of the guided notes, The Colony at a Glance, and students should complete that table now from memory where they can. Check the table with the two questions that expose shallow answers. First, which rows show the colony being acted on, and which show it acting? The hunger and chiefdom rows belong to a colony surviving on fleets and on another ruler’s corn. The crop and turn rows belong to a colony making decisions, about land, about labor, and finally about whose labor would be bound for life. Second, which row reaches furthest past 1680? The turn: the labor settlement made after Bacon’s Rebellion shaped Virginia for the next two centuries. The table is the lesson in miniature: a business venture, a near death, a chief’s policy, a crop, and a choice about labor that outlasted everyone who made it.

Slide 25 · Answering the question

On the slide: ▪ Survival first: corn, discipline, and a relief fleet / ▪ Then the crop: Rolfe’s tobacco finds England’s market / ▪ The land demand: fresh soil, taken from Powhatan towns / ▪ The labor demand: servants first, then e

Run the synthesis as the writing task rehearsal, and build it from the class’s own answers before summarizing. Strong answers refuse the single hero version in both its forms. The outpost did not survive because Smith rescued it or because Pocahontas rescued Smith; it survived because Wahunsenacawh’s corn policy, hard discipline inside the fort, and a relief fleet in June 1610 carried it past the starving time. Nor did the crop simply save the colony once it arrived. Tobacco remade Virginia by making two demands no one could satisfy inside the colony’s own borders: fresh land every few years, which meant Powhatan land, and labor in every season, which meant people bound first for terms and after 1676 for life. Test the synthesis against its rival before closing: a colony is not only what saves it. It is also what its salvation goes on to require.
