Jamestown and the Chesapeake

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. A Company of Investors

England's first permanent colony in North America was not founded by a king. It was founded by a business. The Virginia Company of London was a joint-stock company, chartered by King James I in 1606. Investors bought shares and pooled their money, sharing the cost of the venture, the risk of losing everything, and any profit the colony might return. Spain's kings had paid for empire out of royal treasuries. England's merchants used a cheaper instrument. In December 1606 three ships carrying 144 men and boys sailed for Virginia, ordered to find gold, a passage to the Pacific, and the Roanoke colonists lost twenty years before.

B. A Peninsula, and the Starving Time

In May 1607 the settlers built their fort on a peninsula in the James River. The site could be defended against Spanish ships and was poor for almost everything else. The river water was brackish, the low ground bred disease, and the company held many gentlemen unaccustomed to labor and few farmers. Food ran short, and the colony governed itself badly. Then came the starving time, the winter of 1609 and 1610. Of some five hundred colonists, about sixty survived. The colony's own accounts record that the starving ate horses, dogs, and rats, and in documented cases the dead. The survivors were abandoning the town when a relief fleet reached them in June 1610 and turned them back.

C. The Powhatan Paramount Chiefdom

Jamestown stood inside another ruler's country. The Powhatan paramount chiefdom joined some thirty peoples of the Chesapeake under Wahunsenacawh, the leader the English called Powhatan, who had built his power by inheritance and conquest from his base at Werowocomoco. His people farmed corn, beans, and squash in towns along the rivers, and they hunted, fished, and traded across the bay. The English were a few hundred hungry strangers on his shore, and he treated them as a chief treats a useful and dangerous newcomer. He traded corn that kept them alive, tested their strength, withheld food when withholding served his purposes, and weighed whether the English might serve him against his enemies or had to be driven out.

D. John Smith and His Two Stories

The English leader who dealt with him most was John Smith, a soldier who imposed hard discipline on the settlers and traded, threatened, and fought for corn. Smith wrote about Virginia for the rest of his life, and he is best read as a source whose author is selling himself. His A True Relation, published in 1608, tells of his capture and his meeting with Wahunsenacawh as a feast and a friendly release, with no rescue in it. His letter to Queen Anne of 1616, written to present the chief's daughter Pocahontas at court, first tells of Pocahontas saving his life. His General History of 1624 tells the rescue in full, his head laid on the execution stones. The meeting may have held a ritual Smith misunderstood, or a story he improved as the years passed. The record cannot settle the question.

E. Tobacco Remakes the Colony

What saved the colony as a business was a leaf. John Rolfe planted a milder West Indian tobacco from about 1612, and the crop found a large and growing market in England. Tobacco became Virginia's cash crop, grown for sale and profit, and it remade everything it touched. The plant exhausted the soil within a few years, so planters always needed fresh land, and settlement spread along the rivers onto Powhatan land. It also demanded labor in every season, hands to plant, weed, cut, and cure. The colony that had nearly starved for want of food now organized itself around a crop no one could eat, and its hunger for land and workers set the terms of Chesapeake life.

F. 1619: An Assembly and a Ship

The year 1619 is the hinge of the colony's history. That summer the House of Burgesses, the first elected assembly in English America, met at Jamestown, and landowning colonists chose representatives to make the colony's laws beside the governor and his council. Weeks later an English privateer flying a Dutch flag arrived carrying some twenty Africans taken from a Portuguese slave ship, and the colony's governor bought them for provisions. They were the first Africans recorded in England's mainland colonies. What their legal status was, servant for a term or enslaved for life, the surviving records do not say clearly, and historians still argue the question from thin evidence. Slavery in law was built afterward, statute by statute, over the next two generations.

G. Land, Labor, and Rebellion

Tobacco's labor came first from England. Under the headright system, anyone who paid an immigrant's passage received fifty acres, so planters imported indentured servants by the thousands, laborers bound to work four to seven years for their passage and for freedom dues at the term's end. Many died of seasoning fevers in their first Virginia summer. Those who survived and took land on the frontier found the best acres held by great planters and the governor, Sir William Berkeley, unwilling to let them attack the Powhatan peoples. In 1676 Nathaniel Bacon led those frontier settlers and former servants in Bacon's Rebellion. They attacked Indigenous peoples, marched on Jamestown, and burned it before the rising collapsed with Bacon's death. Virginia's great planters drew the lesson. Bound labor that could not leave, could not claim land, and could be marked by sight was safer than servants who became free, armed, and angry. The turn toward enslaved African labor, already under way, became decisive.
