Writing Task: What Tobacco Demanded

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: How did a starving outpost become a tobacco colony, and what did tobacco demand? A strong answer separates the survival from the transformation. It explains first how the colony lived at all, on Powhatan corn, discipline, and a relief fleet, before it explains how Rolfe's tobacco made the colony pay, and then follows the crop's two demands, fresh land taken from the Powhatan peoples and labor that planters sought first in indentured servants and after 1676 decisively in enslaved Africans. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The Virginia Company of London was a joint-stock company chartered in 1606. Its investors pooled their money and shared the venture's costs, risks, and profits, and for years the colony returned no profit.

2. In the starving time, the winter of 1609 and 1610, about sixty of some five hundred colonists survived. The colony's accounts record the starving eating horses, dogs, and rats, and in documented cases the dead. A relief fleet turned the survivors back from abandoning Jamestown in June 1610.

3. Wahunsenacawh, ruling some thirty peoples from Werowocomoco, traded corn that kept the English alive, withheld food when withholding served him, and weighed whether the English might serve him against his enemies or had to be driven out. Smith's 1608 account of their meeting records a feast, a bargain for corn against hatchets and copper, and no rescue.

4. John Rolfe planted a milder West Indian tobacco from about 1612. Tobacco exhausted the soil within a few years, so settlement spread along the rivers onto Powhatan land, and the crop demanded hands at every stage in every season.

5. Under the headright system, fifty acres went to anyone who paid an immigrant's passage. Indentured servants came by the thousands on four to seven year terms, and many died of seasoning fevers in their first summer.

6. In 1619 the House of Burgesses met at Jamestown, and weeks later some twenty Africans arrived, the first recorded in England's mainland colonies, their legal status unclear in the records. In 1676 Bacon's Rebellion burned Jamestown, and afterward great planters turned decisively toward enslaved African labor.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the prompt, that the outpost survived on Powhatan corn and English relief before tobacco transformed it, and that tobacco's demands for land and labor set the colony's course. (Blueprint 5 and Blueprint 6)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 supports the venture's profit problem. (Blueprint 5) Bank item 2 supports the near death of the colony and its rescue. (Blueprint 5) Bank item 3 supports survival as Wahunsenacawh's policy, with the 1608 record read as it stands. (Blueprint 5) Bank item 4 supports the crop and its land hunger. (Blueprint 5) Bank items 5 and 6 support the labor sequence, headright and indenture first, then 1619 and the post 1676 turn to enslaved labor. (Blueprint 6) A full credit essay draws on at least two groups.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. An essay that lists the starving time, the crop, and the rebellion without saying which demand each fact answers has assembled the lesson's events without its argument, and should earn partial credit.
  • An ending that keeps the two judgments separate, as the lesson does: tobacco made Virginia pay, and the paying was done with Powhatan land and bound labor, first for terms and then for life.

Model opening for teacher reference. Jamestown survived its first years on decisions made at Werowocomoco. Wahunsenacawh fed the English when feeding served his diplomacy and withheld corn when it did not, and the colony that came through the starving time with about sixty survivors lived inside his country by his leave. Tobacco ended that dependence and replaced it with two hungers of the colony's own. Rolfe's crop exhausted the soil, so the land hunger fell on the Powhatan peoples town by town, and the labor hunger was filled first by servants who could one day go free and demand land themselves, until the men of 1676 burned the capital and taught the great planters to want laborers who could never do either. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the headright arithmetic and the hinge of 1619, then closing on the society tobacco had built by 1680.

Common weak answers. A lone founder answer (John Smith saved Jamestown) ignores the corn trade, Wahunsenacawh's policy, and the relief fleet, and repeats Smith's own advertising as fact. A rescue answer (Pocahontas saved Smith and so saved Virginia) rests on a story absent from the 1608 account and first written in 1616 for a queen. A crop only answer (tobacco saved the colony) stops at the profit and drops the demands, the land taken and the labor bound, which are the second half of the lesson question. Credit requires both records held at once: how the outpost lived, and what the crop that saved it went on to require.
