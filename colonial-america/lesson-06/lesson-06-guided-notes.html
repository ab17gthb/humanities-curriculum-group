Guided Notes: Slavery and the Slave Trade

Lesson question: How was the Atlantic slave trade organized, and what did enslaved people endure, build, and resist?

Unit question: How did the meeting of Europe, Africa, and the Americas remake all three worlds, and what kind of society grew up in England's colonies?

Key Terms

Middle Passage: the voyage across the Atlantic that carried enslaved Africans to the Americas, the middle leg of the Atlantic trade, made in crowded ships where disease and abuse killed many of those aboard

Atlantic slave trade: the forced migration of Africans across the Atlantic in slave ships, which carried some 12.5 million people from Africa between the 1500s and the 1800s, about 10.7 million of whom survived the crossing

chattel slavery: the system in which enslaved people were legally treated as property, bought, sold, and inherited, and in which their children were born enslaved for life

slave codes: the laws that defined enslaved people's legal status, denied them rights to courts, assembly, and arms, and punished resistance, tightened in the colonies across the late 1600s and early 1700s

task system: the labor system of the South Carolina and Georgia lowcountry in which enslaved workers were assigned a daily task and, when it was finished, had the remaining time for their own work

Stono Rebellion: the uprising of September 1739 near Charleston, South Carolina, in which enslaved Africans seized weapons and marched toward Spanish Florida, after which the colonies tightened their slave codes

Olaudah Equiano: the African writer who was kidnapped as a boy, enslaved, bought his freedom, and published in 1789 a narrative that described the Middle Passage and argued against the slave trade

Summary table. The trade: European ships carried guns, cloth, and metal to the Guinea coast, African rulers and merchants sold war captives and kidnapped people, and the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database estimates some 12.5 million people forced aboard and about 10.7 million surviving; inside it, the captives endured the Middle Passage, which Equiano's 1789 narrative described from the hold. The law: Virginia's 1662 statute made a child's status follow the mother's and its 1667 statute declared that baptism did not free, building chattel slavery, and slave codes denied the enslaved courts, assembly, and arms. The Chesapeake: tobacco worked in gangs, an enslaved population growing by birth from the early 1700s, and families built and held together against sale at an owner's death, debts, or divided estate. The lowcountry: rice grown on African knowledge, the day ordered by the task system, finished tasks leaving time for gardens, poultry, hunting, fishing, and markets, and the Gullah language and culture formed in a Black majority. Resistance and voice: day to day defiance and flight to Spanish Florida, the Stono Rebellion of September 1739 answered by the Negro Act of 1740, and Phillis Wheatley's Poems on Various Subjects published in London in 1773.

Exit ticket 1. The 1662 statute made every child born in Virginia bond or free according to the condition of the mother, so enslavement reproduced itself in each generation without any new capture. The 1667 statute declared that baptism did not alter bondage or freedom, closing the claim that Christian conversion freed a person. Together they made slavery hereditary and permanent in law.

Exit ticket 2. The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database estimates that some 12.5 million Africans were forced aboard slave ships between the 1500s and the 1800s and that about 10.7 million survived the crossing. The difference, about 1.8 million people, counts those who died on the Middle Passage.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that pairs an organization fact with a building or resistance fact. The strongest answers run from ships, coastal sales, statutes, and codes on one side to rice knowledge, families held against sale, the task hours turned to gardens and markets, flight, Stono, and Wheatley's published voice on the other.
