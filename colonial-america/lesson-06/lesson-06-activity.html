The System in Five Records

Directions: Work through the five stations in order. Each station holds one record of the Atlantic slave trade and the slavery it built. For each record, state the part of the system it shows: capture and the crossing, law, labor and control, resistance, or voice. You will build the full file in Part 2 and judge the records from it.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: Equiano in the Hold, 1789

Source: Olaudah Equiano, The Interesting Narrative, London, 1789. Text quoted verbatim from the audited TPSP record equiano-middle-passage; the edition named in that record is Documenting the American South, University of North Carolina, from the London 1789 first edition, original spelling and punctuation preserved. Cuts are marked with ellipses. The plate below is captioned with its own printed title and its makers' purpose, as this lesson uses it.

". . . The stench of the hold while we were on the coast was so intolerably loathsome, that it was dangerous to remain there for any time, and some of us had been permitted to stay on the deck for the fresh air; but now that the whole ship's cargo were confined together, it became absolutely pestilential. The closeness of the place, and the heat of the climate, added to the number in the ship, which was so crowded that each had scarcely room to turn himself, almost suffocated us. This produced copious perspirations, so that the air soon became unfit for respiration, from a variety of loathsome smells, and brought on a sickness among the slaves, of which many died, thus falling victims to the improvident avarice, as I may call it, of their purchasers. This wretched situation was again aggravated by the galling of the chains, now become insupportable; and the filth of the necessary tubs, into which the children often fell, and were almost suffocated. The shrieks of the women, and the groans of the dying, rendered the whole a scene of horror almost inconceivable. . . ."

File entries. What part of the system does this record show, and what in it could only come from a man who had been in the hold himself? Name two details a ship's plan or a merchant's account could not supply.

Equiano blames the deaths on the improvident avarice of the purchasers. Explain the accusation in your own words, and use the Brookes plate's own note, 454 allowed and 609 carried, as evidence for or against it.

Station 2: Virginia Makes Slavery Hereditary, 1662 and 1667

Source: Laws of Virginia, December 1662, Act XII, and September 1667, Act III. Text quoted verbatim from William Waller Hening, ed., The Statutes at Large, vol. 2, pp. 170 and 260, as transcribed by Virtual Jamestown, Virginia Center for Digital History, University of Virginia. Original spelling preserved.

"WHEREAS some doubts have arrisen whether children got by any Englishman upon a negro woman should be slave or ffree, Be it therefore enacted and declared by this present grand assembly, that all children borne in this country shalbe held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother, And that if any christian shall committ ffornication with a negro man or woman, hee or shee soe offending shall pay double the ffines imposed by the former act."

"WHEREAS some doubts have risen whether children that are slaves by birth, and by the charity and piety of their owners made pertakers of the blessed sacrament of baptisme, should by vertue of their baptisme be made ffree; It is enacted and declared by this grand assembly, and the authority thereof, that the conferring of baptisme doth not alter the condition of the person as to his bondage or ffreedome; that diverse masters, ffreed from this doubt, may more carefully endeavour the propagation of christianity by permitting children, though slaves, or those of greater growth if capable to be admitted to that sacrament."

File entries. What part of the system do these statutes show, and what two doors do they close? State each rule in plain modern words, naming whose status each rule settles.

The 1662 act broke with the English rule that a child's status followed the father's. Explain what that break did for slaveholders, using the act's own opening case, a child got by an Englishman upon a Negro woman.

Station 3: The Negro Act Defines the Chattel, South Carolina, 1740

Source: South Carolina, An Act for the Better Ordering and Governing Negroes and Other Slaves in This Province, No. 670, 1740, Sections I and XLV. Text quoted verbatim from David J. McCord, ed., The Statutes at Large of South Carolina, vol. 7 (Columbia, 1840), as transcribed in the South Carolina ETV Teaching American History transcription of the 1740 slave codes. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

"That all Negroes and Indians, (free Indians in amity with this government, and degrees, mulattoes, and mustizoes, who are now free, excepted,) mulattoes or mustizoes who now are, or shall hereafter be, in this Province, and all their issue and offspring, born or to be born, shall be, and they are hereby declared to be, and remain forever hereafter, absolute slaves, and shall follow the condition of the mother, and shall be deemed, held, taken, reputed and adjudged in law, to be chattels personal, in the hands of their owners and possessors . . ."

"And whereas, the having of slaves taught to write, or suffering them to be employed in writing, may be attended with great inconveniences; Be it therefore enacted by the authority aforesaid, That all and every person and persons whatsoever, who shall hereinafter teach or cause any slave or slaves to be taught to write, or shall use or employ any slave as a scribe in any manner of writing whatsoever, hereafter taught to write, every such person and persons, shall, for every such offense, forfeit the sum of one hundred pounds current money."

File entries. What part of the system does this record show? Section I uses the phrase chattels personal. State what the law declares enslaved people to be in the eyes of the law, and how long the declaration runs, using the section's own words for time.

Section XLV fines the teacher, not the learner. Explain why a legislature would punish a person who taught an enslaved person to write, and name what writing makes possible that the act's makers feared, in light of Station 4.

Station 4: Stono, and the Law That Answered It, 1739 and 1740

Source: The summary below is a teaching summary written for this lesson from the reading; it is not a period text. The statute passage is South Carolina's Negro Act of 1740, Section XXXVI, quoted verbatim from David J. McCord, ed., The Statutes at Large of South Carolina, vol. 7 (Columbia, 1840), as transcribed in the South Carolina ETV Teaching American History transcription of the 1740 slave codes. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

On Sunday, September 9, 1739, enslaved Africans near the Stono River, about twenty miles from Charleston, took guns and powder from a store, killed the storekeepers, and marched south under banners, calling out for liberty. The column grew as it went, toward Spanish Florida, whose governors had promised freedom to fugitives from the English colonies. The colony's militia caught the marchers the same day. Most were killed in the fighting or executed after it. The next year the assembly passed the Negro Act.

"And for that as it is absolutely necessary to the safety of this Province, that all due care be taken to restrain the wanderings and meetings of Negroes and other slaves, at all times, and more especially on Saturday nights, Sundays, and other holidays, and their using and carrying wooden swords, and other mischievous and dangerous weapons, or using or keeping of drums, horns, or other loud instruments, which may call together or give sign or notice to one another of their wicked designs and purposes . . ."

File entries. What part of the system does this record show? The marchers chose a Sunday and a direction. Explain each choice from the summary: why Sunday morning, and why south toward Florida.

Read Section XXXVI against the march. The section names drums, horns, meetings, and Saturday nights and Sundays. State what the assembly had understood about how the rebellion was organized, and what that understanding admits about enslaved people's lives under the task system.

Station 5: Wheatley's Poem, Printed Entire, 1773

Source: Phillis Wheatley, "On Being Brought from Africa to America," in Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral, London, 1773. Text quoted verbatim and printed entire, all eight lines, from the audited TPSP record wheatley-on-being-brought; the edition named in that record is Phillis Wheatley, "On Being Brought from Africa to America," 1773 (LitCharts). Wheatley was kidnapped from West Africa as a child and enslaved in Boston; her book of 1773 was the first published by an African American poet.

'Twas mercy brought me from my Pagan land,
Taught my benighted soul to understand
That there's a God, that there's a Saviour too:
Once I redemption neither sought nor knew.
Some view our sable race with scornful eye,
"Their colour is a diabolic die."
Remember, Christians, Negros, black as Cain,
May be refin'd, and join th' angelic train.

File entries. What part of the system does this record show? The poem opens by calling the kidnapping mercy. State what Wheatley says the crossing gave her, and then state what the last four lines accuse her Christian readers of, in the poem's own terms.

The final couplet runs, Remember, Christians, Negros, black as Cain, may be refin'd, and join th' angelic train. Explain how Wheatley uses the Christians' own doctrine as her argument, and why an enslaved poet in Boston in 1773 might choose that ground rather than another.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: Equiano in the Hold, 1789

What part of the system does the record show, and what could only come from a man who had been in the hold? The crossing: capture's destination and the Middle Passage itself. Only a survivor supplies the sensory record, the stench that made the hold dangerous to remain in, the heat and crowding that left each person scarcely room to turn, the galling of the chains, the filth of the necessary tubs into which children fell, and the sounds, the shrieks of the women and the groans of the dying. A plan shows stowage and a merchant's account shows prices and losses. Neither records what the air did to the people breathing it.

What does the improvident avarice accusation mean, and what does the Brookes note show? Equiano charges the purchasers with reckless greed: buyers demanded ships packed past what space and air could sustain because every captive landed alive was profit, so the deaths were a foreseeable cost of the packing, chosen by the men who profited. The Brookes plate's own note supports him from the trade's side of the record. Even after regulation, the ship was allowed 454 people at six feet by one foot four inches for each man, and before the act she had carried 609, stowed, in the note's words, spoonwise. A diagram printed by abolitionists and a narrative written by a survivor accuse the same practice from opposite ends of it.

Station 2: Virginia Makes Slavery Hereditary, 1662 and 1667

What two doors do the statutes close? The 1662 act settles the status of children: all children born in the colony are held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother, so a child of an enslaved woman is enslaved for life. The 1667 act settles the claim of faith: the conferring of baptism does not alter a person's condition as to bondage or freedom, so conversion and church membership cannot become a road to liberty. The first closes inheritance through the father; the second closes the church door as an exit.

What did the break with the father's rule do for slaveholders? Under English practice a child's status followed the father's, which would have freed the children Englishmen fathered on enslaved women. The 1662 act reversed the line for this one purpose. It made enslaved women's children enslaved from birth, whoever their fathers were, so the enslaved population reproduced itself as the owner's property, and it removed any legal consequence for the fathers, who owed the doubled fine only for fornication, not freedom or support for their children. Slavery after 1662 no longer needed a fresh capture or purchase for each worker. It inherited them.

Station 3: The Negro Act Defines the Chattel, South Carolina, 1740

What does Section I declare? Law, in its most complete form in this file. Section I declares the named peoples and all their issue and offspring, born or to be born, to be and remain forever hereafter absolute slaves, following the condition of the mother, and to be deemed, held, taken, reputed and adjudged in law to be chattels personal in the hands of their owners and possessors. The time word for full credit is forever hereafter, and the reach word is offspring: the statute claims generations not yet born.

Why fine the teacher? Because writing is a tool of movement and coordination. A person who can write can forge a ticket or a pass, read a notice or a newspaper, correspond across plantations, and keep accounts and records of his own. Section XLV prices that danger at one hundred pounds current money for every offense, charged to the person who teaches or employs the writer, the party the assembly could most easily identify and punish. Station 4 shows the fear underneath the fine: the Stono marchers had organized, gathered, and moved together, and a literate enslaved population could organize farther and quieter.

Station 4: Stono, and the Law That Answered It, 1739 and 1740

Why Sunday, and why south? Sunday was the day enslaved people were least watched and most able to gather, the day of markets, visits, and worship under the lowcountry's task system, and the day white families were at church; the marchers used the one opening the week gave them. South was Florida: Spanish governors had promised freedom to fugitives from the English colonies who accepted Catholic baptism, and St. Augustine stood within reach of a hard march. The column carried banners and called out liberty, recruiting as it went. Direction and day were strategy, not accident.

What had the assembly understood? Section XXXVI admits, in the act of forbidding it, that enslaved people had a social life of their own: meetings on Saturday nights, Sundays, and holidays, music on drums and horns that could call people together and carry signals, gatherings across plantation lines where news and plans could move. The rebellion was organized inside that life, and the assembly knew it, which is why the act aims at assembly itself rather than only at weapons. The passage is the slaveholders' own evidence that the people they held were a community, with leaders, communication, and purposes of their own.

Station 5: Wheatley's Poem, Printed Entire, 1773

What does the poem say the crossing gave her, and what do the last four lines accuse? Voice. Wheatley says the crossing, which she calls mercy, taught her benighted soul that there is a God and a Saviour, a faith she says she had neither sought nor known. The last four lines then turn on her readers: some view her sable race with a scornful eye and call its colour a diabolic die, and she answers from doctrine, that Negros, black as Cain, may be refin'd and join the angelic train. The accusation is that Christians who hold her race devilish deny what their own faith teaches about who can be saved.

How does Wheatley use the Christians' own doctrine? She argues from inside the shared text. If salvation is open to every soul, and if refinement and the angelic train await believers without distinction of colour, then scorn for the sable race is not Christian judgment but prejudice dressed in Christian words. The address, Remember, Christians, makes the readers the defendants. An enslaved poet in Boston in 1773 held no pulpit, press, or vote of her own; her book appeared in London because Boston publishers refused it. Doctrine was the ground her audience could not dismiss without dismissing itself, and she built the whole eight lines on it.

Verdict grading guide. Judgments may land on different records and earn full credit when defended from the texts. The strongest cases are usually made for Equiano, the file's only first person account of the crossing, without which the trade is tonnage and prices; or for the Virginia statutes, the file's hinge, without which the trade's victims might have been a generation rather than a hereditary caste; or for Wheatley, the only record in the file written by an enslaved person for print, whose eight lines do work no statute can do. A verdict for the Negro Act must show why the code's own words outrank the account of the crossing, and a verdict for Stono must reckon with the fact that its narrative here is a labeled teaching summary, while its statute passage is the assembly's record, not the marchers'. Any verdict that treats the summary in Station 4 as a period quotation has misread the source line.
