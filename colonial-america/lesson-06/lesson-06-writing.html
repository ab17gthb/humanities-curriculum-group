Writing Task: Endure, Build, Resist

The prompt. Answer the question in a developed essay of about 300 words: How was the Atlantic slave trade organized, and what did enslaved people endure, build, and resist? Argue the system before you judge the people inside it. A strong answer sets out the organization first, the ships and coastal sales, the statutes that made slavery hereditary, and the codes that policed it, then what enslaved people did inside that organization, the knowledge and families they built, the defiance and flight they sustained, and the rising and the printed voice that closed the era. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, drawn from both halves of the bank, and explain how each piece supports your judgment.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the System in Five Records activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database estimates that some 12.5 million Africans were forced aboard slave ships between the 1500s and the 1800s and that about 10.7 million survived the crossing. European ships carried guns, cloth, and metal goods to the Guinea coast, where African rulers and merchants sold war captives and kidnapped people to the traders. Sugar plantations in Brazil and the Caribbean took the largest share.

2. Olaudah Equiano, kidnapped as a boy and carried to Barbados and Virginia, published The Interesting Narrative in London in 1789. He described a hold so crowded that each person had scarcely room to turn, air unfit to breathe, and many dying, and he laid the deaths on the improvident avarice of their purchasers. (Audited TPSP record equiano-middle-passage.)

3. Virginia's statute of December 1662 declared that all children born in the colony would be held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother. Its statute of September 1667 declared that baptism did not alter a person's condition of bondage or freedom. (Hening, ed., The Statutes at Large, vol. 2, pp. 170 and 260.)

4. South Carolina's Negro Act of 1740 declared enslaved people and all their offspring, born or to be born, to be absolute slaves forever hereafter and chattels personal in the hands of their owners, and it fined any person one hundred pounds current money for teaching an enslaved person to write. (McCord, ed., The Statutes at Large of South Carolina, vol. 7, 1840.)

5. The lowcountry's rice wealth rested on knowledge enslaved people brought from West Africa's rice coasts, diking, flooding, and timing the crop. Under the task system, finished tasks left hours for garden plots, poultry, hunting, fishing, and market sales, and in the lowcountry's Black majority the Gullah language and culture took form.

6. In the Chesapeake, where the enslaved population grew by birth from the early 1700s, enslaved parents named children for kin in Africa and in Virginia and held marriages the law refused to recognize, under the standing threat that an owner's death, debts, or divided estate could sell a husband, wife, or child away.

7. In September 1739 enslaved Africans seized weapons near the Stono River and marched for Spanish Florida and its promised freedom. In 1773 Phillis Wheatley, kidnapped as a child and enslaved in Boston, published her Poems on Various Subjects in London, and her eight line poem On Being Brought from Africa to America answered Christians who viewed her race with a scornful eye from their own doctrine: Negros, black as Cain, may be refin'd, and join th' angelic train.

Plan Before You Write

My judgment (one sentence that answers both halves of the question directly):

My evidence picks (bank numbers, at least one from items 1 to 4 and at least one from items 5 to 7) and what each one proves:

The strongest evidence against my judgment, and how I will answer it:

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your judgment, present the organization of the trade and the law, present what enslaved people built and how they resisted, answer the strongest evidence against your judgment in one or two sentences, and end by restating what the record shows.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment that answers both halves of the prompt directly, how the trade was organized and what enslaved people endured, built, and resisted, rather than narrating one half. (Blueprint 11 and Blueprint 12)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, with at least one from each half. Bank items 1 through 4 carry the organization: the Database's estimates and the coastal trade, Equiano's hold, the Virginia statutes of 1662 and 1667, and the Negro Act's declaration and writing fine. (Blueprint 11) Bank items 5 through 7 carry the people: rice knowledge and the task hours, Chesapeake families under threat of sale, Stono, and Wheatley's poem of 1773. (Blueprint 12)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the judgment. The strongest essays name the difference between a system described and a people acting: the statutes and codes state what was done to enslaved people, while the rice coasts, the named children, the finished tasks, the march south, and the London book state what enslaved people did, and a full answer weighs the second record as evidence, not as decoration on the first.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest evidence on the other side, answered from the record rather than dismissed. An essay stressing the system's completeness must answer the Gullah language, the families, and the Stono column. An essay stressing resistance and building must answer the hereditary statutes and the Negro Act's reach into unborn generations.

Model opening for teacher reference. The Atlantic slave trade was organized as a system, ships and coastal markets feeding plantations, statutes making enslavement hereditary, and codes policing movement, assembly, and writing, and the people that system held answered it with the only property it could not confiscate outright, their knowledge, their kin, and their words. A full model continues in this pattern, carries the estimates and Equiano beside the 1662 and 1667 statutes and the Negro Act, carries rice and families beside Stono and Wheatley, answers the strongest opposing evidence, and closes on what the record shows.

Common weak answers. System only (the estimates, the statutes, the codes) describes the machinery without the people the lesson is about. People only (rice, families, Stono, Wheatley) makes the same error in reverse, and usually understates how completely the law claimed even unborn generations. A judgment that treats resistance as proof the system was weak, or the codes as proof resistance failed, earns partial credit, because the record holds both at full strength at once, which is what the Negro Act answering Stono in a single year shows.
