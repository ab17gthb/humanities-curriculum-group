Slavery and the Slave Trade

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. A Trade Built on Captives

The Atlantic slave trade, the forced migration of Africans across the Atlantic in slave ships, which carried some 12.5 million people from Africa between the 1500s and the 1800s, about 10.7 million of whom survived the crossing, ran on a chain of buyers and sellers. Those two figures are estimates from the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database. European ships carried guns, cloth, and metal goods to the Guinea coast of West Africa, where African rulers and merchants sold war captives and kidnapped people to the traders. Sugar plantations in Brazil and the Caribbean took the largest share; tobacco and rice plantations in England's colonies took much of the rest.

B. The Middle Passage in Equiano's Words

The Middle Passage, the voyage across the Atlantic that carried enslaved Africans to the Americas, the middle leg of the Atlantic trade, made in crowded ships where disease and abuse killed many of those aboard, lasted from about six weeks to three months. Olaudah Equiano, the African writer who was kidnapped as a boy, enslaved, bought his freedom, and published in 1789 a narrative that described the Middle Passage and argued against the slave trade, survived it as a child, carried to Barbados and Virginia. His Interesting Narrative, published in London in 1789, described a hold so crowded that each person had scarcely room to turn, air unfit to breathe, and sickness spreading among the captives. About 1.8 million died on the crossing, by the Database's estimates, deaths Equiano laid on the improvident avarice of their purchasers.

C. The Law That Made Slavery Hereditary

English law had no place at first for slavery for life, so Virginia's assembly built one. In December 1662 it declared that all children born in the colony would be held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother, so a child of an enslaved woman was enslaved for life. In September 1667 it closed a second door, declaring that baptism did not alter a person's condition of bondage or freedom. These statutes built chattel slavery, the system in which enslaved people were legally treated as property, bought, sold, and inherited, and in which their children were born enslaved for life. Colony after colony added the laws that defined enslaved people's legal status, denied them rights to courts, assembly, and arms, and punished resistance, tightened in the colonies across the late 1600s and early 1700s.

D. The Chesapeake: Tobacco and Families

In Virginia and Maryland, enslaved people worked tobacco in gangs from sunup to sundown, and skilled workers labored as carpenters, coopers, blacksmiths, and cooks whose work planters rented out for profit. By the early 1700s the Chesapeake's enslaved population grew by birth more than by new arrivals from Africa. Growth by birth meant families. Enslaved parents named children for kin in Africa and in Virginia, held marriages the law refused to recognize, and built family lines across plantations. An owner's death, debts, or divided estate could sell a husband, wife, or child away at any time.

E. The Lowcountry: Rice, Tasks, and a Black Majority

South Carolina and Georgia grew rich on rice, and the knowledge that made rice grow came from Africa. Enslaved people from West Africa's rice coasts knew how to dike tidal marshes, flood and drain fields, and time the crop, and planters paid higher prices for captives from those regions for that knowledge. The lowcountry day ran on the task system, the labor system of the South Carolina and Georgia lowcountry in which enslaved workers were assigned a daily task and, when it was finished, had the remaining time for their own work. Finished tasks left hours for garden plots, poultry, hunting, fishing, and produce sold in Charleston's markets. Enslaved people outnumbered white colonists, and in that majority a new language and culture took form, Gullah, joining African languages, faiths, and foods to American ground.

F. The North, and Resistance Every Day

Slavery was smaller in the northern colonies and present in all of them. Enslaved people worked farms in New England and the middle colonies, loaded ships in Boston, New York, and Newport, cooked and cleaned in households, and crewed vessels in the trade itself. Resistance ran beside the work every day: labor slowed, tools broken, sickness feigned, food taken that their labor had produced. They kept African names for their children, buried their dead by their own rites, and learned to read where they could. Some ran, to swamps and woods, to towns with free Black communities, and south to Spanish Florida, whose governors promised freedom to fugitives from the English colonies.

G. Stono, the Negro Act, and Wheatley's Voice

In September 1739, resistance in South Carolina turned to open revolt. The Stono Rebellion, the uprising of September 1739 near Charleston, South Carolina, in which enslaved Africans seized weapons and marched toward Spanish Florida, after which the colonies tightened their slave codes, began when enslaved men took guns and powder from a store near the Stono River and marched south under banners, calling out for liberty. The colony's militia caught them the same day, and most were killed in the fighting or executed after it. South Carolina answered in 1740 with the Negro Act, which forbade enslaved people to assemble, to move without a ticket, to earn money, or to be taught to write. Phillis Wheatley, kidnapped from West Africa as a child and enslaved in Boston, published her Poems on Various Subjects in London in 1773. Her poem On Being Brought from Africa to America, printed entire in this lesson's activity, holds gratitude for her faith and an accusation against Christians who viewed her race with a scornful eye in the same eight lines.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State how the Atlantic slave trade was organized, and give the Database's two estimates. European ships carried guns, cloth, and metal goods to the Guinea coast of West Africa, where African rulers and merchants sold war captives and kidnapped people to the traders. The largest share of captives went to the sugar plantations of Brazil and the Caribbean, with England's mainland colonies taking much of the rest for tobacco and rice. The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database estimates that some 12.5 million Africans were forced aboard slave ships between the 1500s and the 1800s and that about 10.7 million survived the crossing. Full credit requires the cargo, the sellers, the main destination, and both figures identified as the Database's estimates.

2. Explain what Equiano described in the hold, and what his phrase accuses the buyers of. Equiano described captives packed so closely that each had scarcely room to turn, air made unfit to breathe by heat and crowding, sickness spreading through the hold, and many dying. His phrase, the improvident avarice of their purchasers, accuses the buyers of reckless greed: they packed more people into each ship than the space and air could sustain because each surviving captive was profit, and the deaths followed from the packing rather than from accident. Credit requires both the conditions and the accusation, and noting that his account was published in London in 1789 to argue against the trade strengthens the answer.

3. Name the two Virginia statutes of the 1660s and state what each one did. The statute of December 1662 declared that all children born in the colony would be held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother, making slavery hereditary through the mother and breaking with the English rule that status followed the father. The statute of September 1667 declared that baptism did not alter a person's condition of bondage or freedom, closing conversion as a road to liberty. Together they built chattel slavery as the guided notes define it: enslaved people legally treated as property, bought, sold, and inherited, with their children born enslaved for life. The slave codes that followed denied the enslaved rights to courts, assembly, and arms and punished resistance.

4. Contrast the Chesapeake and the lowcountry. The Chesapeake grew tobacco, worked in gangs from sunup to sundown, with skilled trades beside the field work. Its enslaved population grew by birth from the early 1700s, and what enslaved people built there was family: named children, marriages the law refused to recognize, and kin lines across plantations, held together against sale at an owner's death or debt. The lowcountry grew rice on knowledge brought from Africa's rice coasts, and its day ran on the task system, a measured daily task after which the remaining time was the worker's own, used for garden plots, poultry, hunting, fishing, and market sales. In the lowcountry's Black majority, enslaved people built the Gullah language and culture.

5. Answer the lesson question using Stono and Wheatley together. Strong answers hold the system and the people in one paragraph. The trade was organized by merchants, ships, and laws, and its permanence was written in statutes that made children inherit their mothers' enslavement and in codes that answered every act of resistance. What enslaved people endured and risked appears at Stono: in September 1739 enslaved Africans seized weapons near Charleston and marched for Spanish Florida and its promised freedom, and South Carolina answered with the Negro Act of 1740, forbidding assembly, movement without a ticket, earning money, and being taught to write. What they built and said appears in Phillis Wheatley, kidnapped as a child, enslaved in Boston, and published in London in 1773, whose eight line poem turns the Christians' own faith against their scorn for her race. A paragraph that gives the machinery without Stono and Wheatley, or the two without the machinery, has answered half the question.
