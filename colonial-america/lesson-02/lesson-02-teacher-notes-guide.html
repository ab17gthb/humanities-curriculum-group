Teacher Notes

Lesson 2 · The Spanish and French Americas

A guide sheet for teaching the deck, in slide order. The slides carry the main ideas in a few sentences each; the teaching is here, in the form you can use while the slide is on the screen. Talking points are the content of the slide in bullets, with the numbers pulled out on their own lines so you can find and say them. Picture names what an image shows and what to point at. Ask is a question worth posing aloud, with the shape of a good answer. Note is enrichment: detail beyond the student reading, for the questions students ask you. Bracketed lines are delivery cues. Vocabulary definitions match the student guided notes word for word.

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: The Spanish and French Americas · Spain rules an empire of millions, France trades across a continent

[Have this slide on the screen as students come in.]

Picture: the map of Tenochtitlan published at Nuremberg in 1524, three years after the city fell, the first printed picture of the Aztec capital most Europeans ever saw.

  • Place the lesson after the last one. Lesson 1 ended with a hemisphere shattered by epidemic disease, its peoples rebuilding and resisting from inside the loss.
  • This lesson follows the two empires that moved into that world first and deepest.
  • Spain conquered the great empires of Mexico and Peru and built the largest colonial system in the Americas, ruling millions through law, labor drafts, and ranked birth.
  • France built something almost opposite in the north: a thin line of posts held together by trade and alliance, while running slave plantations in the Caribbean.
  • Students should leave able to compare the two systems, and to state the base both of them rested on.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: How did Spain build an empire on conquest, and how did France build one on trade? · The unit question stands behind it

[Read the question aloud. Students will need both halves for the exit ticket and the writing task.]

  • First half, how Spain built an empire on conquest: Cortes and Tenochtitlan in 1521, the silver of Potosi, the encomienda, and a society ranked by birth from peninsulares down to the enslaved.
  • Second half, how France built one on trade: Quebec in 1608, the fur trade, and alliances with Indigenous nations strong enough to set their own terms.

Note: warn students now against the easy version of the contrast, in which Spain is violent and France is gentle. The lesson ends in the French sugar islands, under a written slave code, and the contrast has to survive that ending to be worth anything.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: Conquest, silver, rank, trade, and the code, A through G

  • This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section. Students can use the letters on screen to find their place in their notes all hour.
  • Section A: the conquest of Tenochtitlan and the forces that actually won it. Section B: the empire built on the conquests, its silver, its royal fifth, and its great estates.
  • Section C: the encomienda and Las Casas. Section D: the ranked society of New Spain and the missions. Section E: France on the St. Lawrence and the fur trade.
  • Section F: the Dutch, Louisiana, and the sugar islands under the Code Noir. Section G draws the comparison the whole lesson builds toward.

Note: the summary table at the end of the guided notes is titled Two Empires at a Glance, and students will complete it from these seven sections.

Slide 4 · Section A: The Conquistadores (divider)

On the slide: The Conquistadores · A few hundred Spaniards march on the richest city in the Americas, and do not win alone

  • Set up the conquest as the lesson's first correction. Students usually arrive picturing Spanish soldiers defeating the Aztec empire by superior weapons.
  • The weapons were real, and so were the horses and steel, but the numbers never worked.
  • Cortes entered Tenochtitlan with about 500 men, in a city of perhaps 200,000 people.
  • What made the conquest possible was the politics of that empire. Peoples taxed and raided by the Aztecs, the Tlaxcalans above all, supplied the army that besieged the city.
  • Epidemic smallpox, arriving with the Europeans, did the rest inside the walls. Teach the conquest as an Indigenous war as well as a Spanish one.

Slide 5 · November 8, 1519: the entry into Tenochtitlan

On the slide: Bernal Diaz counted his company at 450 men · Towns along the lake, canoes without number · A great city rising from the water · Warnings from Tlaxcala fill their minds

Picture: the 1524 Nuremberg map of Tenochtitlan, drawn from a map sent home by the conquerors. The class read this map in Lesson 1 as a portrait of the city; today it is the conquerors' own picture of what they entered.

  • Bernal Diaz del Castillo was a foot soldier on the causeway that morning. He wrote his account decades later because official histories, he said, ignored the ordinary soldiers who had done the fighting.
  • Read his numbers against his description. The city before him held large towns along the lake, still larger ones rising from the waters, bridges, temples, and crowds of canoes beyond counting.
  • His own company, gazing at all of it, was a mere handful of men.
  • 450 men, in all, by Diaz's count.
  • The warnings Diaz records, from the peoples of Huexotzinco, Tlaxcala, and Tlalmanalco, tell the class that Indigenous states were already recruiting the Spanish into their own quarrels with the Mexica empire.

Ask: What can a force of 450 men do to a city of 200,000, and what can it not do? It cannot storm it. It can only enter as guests, allies, or prisoners, which is why the alliances decide the story.

Slide 6 · 450 men in Diaz's count

On the slide: 450 — Spaniards in the company Diaz counted · Against a capital of perhaps 200,000 people

[Let the large figure carry the slide. Pause on it.]

  • Hold this number in front of the class until its meaning settles. Four hundred and fifty is a large village or a small school. It is not an army capable of conquering an empire, and Diaz, who was there, gives the figure himself.
  • When Tenochtitlan fell in August 1521, after a siege of three months, the besieging force was overwhelmingly Indigenous: Tlaxcalan and other allied warriors in the tens of thousands.
  • They fought an empire whose tribute and captive taking they resented, for reasons the record gives in their own diplomacy.
  • Inside the city, smallpox killed defenders and leaders, including the emperor Cuitlahuac, at the moment the city most needed them.
  • Cortes then rebuilt Tenochtitlan as Mexico City, capital of the empire Spain would govern from the same streets.

Note: in Peru a decade later, Francisco Pizarro seized a second empire on the same pattern: Indigenous allies, an Inca civil war, and disease running ahead of the Spanish.

Slide 7 · Section B: Mines and Estates (divider)

On the slide: Mines and Estates · The woodcut shows Potosi and its silver mountain, from the Chronicle of Peru, printed in 1553

Picture: the first printed view of Potosi, a woodcut from Pedro Cieza de Leon's Chronicle of Peru of 1553, showing the silver mountain, the Cerro Rico, above the town that grew at its foot.

  • What Spain built on its conquests was not a string of trading posts but a governed empire of cities, courts, treasuries, and mines, the largest colonial system in the Americas.
  • Its engine was silver. The mountain at Potosi, in present day Bolivia, was found rich in silver in 1545.
  • Within a generation the town beneath it held more than 100,000 people, one of the largest cities in the world, fed and supplied from a wide countryside because nothing grows at that altitude.
  • Section B teaches the two institutions that carried the empire: the mine, taxed by the Crown before any owner was paid, and the hacienda, the great estate that fed the mining world.

Slide 8 · Silver out, food in: the working empire

On the slide: New Spain governed from Mexico City · Potosi and the Mexican mines drive the system · The Crown takes its fifth · Haciendas feed the towns and mines · Silver flows to Europe, and onward to China

  • Walk the system as a circuit, because students remember circuits.
  • New Spain, the viceroyalty governed from Mexico City, stretched from Panama to the present day American Southwest. A second viceroyalty ruled Peru.
  • At Potosi and in the Mexican silver districts around Zacatecas, drafted Indigenous laborers worked the mines in conditions that killed many of them.
  • The Crown took its fifth: 20 percent of all silver, at the mine mouth.
  • That made the Spanish state a partner in every shaft and gave it a direct interest in the labor supply.
  • Around the mines stood the hacienda, worked by Indigenous or enslaved labor, raising wheat, cattle, and sheep for mining towns that could not feed themselves.
  • Silver then moved outward: across the Atlantic to pay Spain's armies and creditors in Europe, and across the Pacific from Acapulco to Manila, where Chinese merchants traded silk and porcelain for the metal their economy prized.

Slide 9 · 100,000 people at Potosi

On the slide: 100,000 — people at Potosi within a generation of 1545 · A city larger than most in Spain, at 13,000 feet in the Andes

[Let the large figure carry the slide. Pause on it.]

  • Give students the scale of the mining city, because it overturns the picture of Spanish America as a frontier.
  • More than 100,000 people at Potosi in its great years, by some counts more than London held in the same decades, at nearly 13,000 feet, where frost rules every night and no grain ripens.
  • Every loaf, hide, and timber in the city arrived by llama train and mule from valleys days away, which is why the hacienda economy existed: the mine created a market that reorganized farming across half a continent.
  • The labor that dug the silver was drafted. Under the mita, adapted from an Inca labor tax, Indigenous communities sent a share of their men to the mines for set terms, and many did not return.

Ask: When students ask what the empire rested on, what is the concrete answer? Potosi: Indigenous labor, organized by the state, producing the metal the whole system ran on.

Slide 10 · Section C: The Encomienda and Las Casas (divider)

On the slide: The Encomienda and Las Casas · The labor system at the base of the empire, and the Spaniard who put its cost on the record

  • This section teaches the encomienda twice: once as the system's defenders described it, and once as its greatest Spanish critic recorded it. Students should hold the two descriptions apart.
  • In law, the encomienda was a grant of trust: a colonist received the tribute and labor of a district's Indigenous people, and in return owed them protection and Christian teaching.
  • In practice, as the reading states plainly, the grants were worked as forced labor, and communities already shattered by epidemic disease died under the demands.
  • Bartolome de las Casas, a settler turned Dominican friar, had held an encomienda himself before renouncing it.
  • For fifty years he argued at the Spanish court that Indigenous peoples were rational men and women whose rights bound the Crown. In 1552 he published the account students will read in the activity.

Note: teach him neither as a hero outside his world nor as a hypocrite inside it, but as evidence that the empire argued with itself, in its own courts, about what it was doing.

Slide 11 · Tribute and labor, owed by law

On the slide: A grant of a district and its people · Tribute in goods and gold, labor on demand · Protection and Christian teaching owed in return · In practice, forced labor on a dying population

Picture: a page of the Codex Mendoza, the tribute record painted by Indigenous artists for the Spanish Crown around 1541, listing what the Aztec empire's provinces had owed their rulers. Spain inherited that tribute machinery and redirected it.

  • Under the encomienda, the Crown granted a colonist the Indigenous people of a named district.
  • The grant did not give him their land in law. It gave him their tribute and their labor, in mines, fields, workshops, and building yards.
  • In return he was to protect them and have them taught the Christian faith. The bargain's terms were set entirely by the granting power, and the people bound by it had no seat at the making.
  • Communities ordered to deliver goods, gold, and workers were the same communities epidemic disease was reducing year by year, so demands fixed in one decade fell on a fraction of the people a decade later.
  • That arithmetic, more than any single cruelty, is what Las Casas carried to court, and it is why the encomienda became the empire's central argument with itself.

Slide 12 · 12 million: the figure Las Casas claimed

On the slide: 12 million — deaths Las Casas claimed in forty years · His figure for the Indies as a whole, printed in 1552

[Teach this figure with its label attached, because the label is part of the history.]

  • Las Casas wrote that of three million people on Hispaniola, scarce three hundred remained.
  • Above 12 million people had perished in forty years, he dared to affirm, in the Indies as a whole.
  • His numbers were arguments, built to force a king to act. Modern historians hold that he exaggerated them, and simplified a catastrophe driven chiefly by epidemic disease.
  • What is not in dispute is the direction and the scale: across the Caribbean and the mainland, populations fell by half, by three quarters, in many regions by nine in ten, within a century of contact.
  • His advocacy helped push the Crown to the New Laws of 1542, which limited the encomienda. Colonists resisted, the harshest clauses were withdrawn, and labor drafts continued in new forms.

Note: Las Casas also, early on, suggested African labor as a substitute for Indigenous labor, a position he later repented and condemned. State that turn plainly: it connects this lesson to the slave systems the unit takes up next.

Slide 13 · Section D: The Society New Spain Made (divider)

On the slide: The Society New Spain Made · A ranked order of birth, carried in law and custom, and missions on the empire's edges

  • Spain did not only extract from its American empire. It built a society there, with cities, universities, cathedrals, and a social order as deliberate as its tax system.
  • Every person in Spanish America held a rank fixed largely at birth.
  • Spaniards born in Spain stood at the top and held the great offices. Spaniards born in America, the creoles, owned most of the land and wealth and resented a ceiling they could see but not pass.
  • Between Spaniards and Indigenous peoples stood the growing mestizo population. At the bottom stood Indigenous communities and enslaved Africans, whose labor carried the whole structure.
  • On the empire's borders, where settlers were few, the mission did the work of expansion, gathering Indigenous peoples into supervised towns.

Note: teach the order as a system with daily consequences: rank decided office, dress, marriage, tax, and the court a person could appeal to.

Slide 14 · Ranked by birth

On the slide: Peninsulares, born in Spain, holding the high offices · Creoles, born in America, owning the land · Mestizos, between Spaniards and Indigenous peoples · Indigenous communities and enslaved Africans at the base

  • Take the four ranks in order and give each its grievance, because the grievances explain the politics of the next two centuries.
  • The peninsulares, officials born in Spain, arrived with royal appointments and answered to Madrid. American born Spaniards watched them take the viceroyalties, judgeships, and bishoprics.
  • The creoles owned the haciendas and mines and filled the city councils. Their resentment at exclusion from high office is the seed of the independence movements students will meet in a later course.
  • The mestizo population grew every generation and fit the order's categories worst, ranked between Spaniards and Indigenous peoples and pressing against both.
  • At the base stood Indigenous communities, taxed and drafted as corporate bodies, and enslaved Africans, held as property.
  • The lines were policed in law and custom, in marriage registers and tax lists, and they were also crossed, by wealth, by royal favor, and by the arithmetic of generations, which is why the order needed constant defending.

Slide 15 · Missions on the borders

On the slide: Religious settlements among Indigenous peoples · From Florida and Texas to California · Farming, crafts, and doctrine taught together · Towns gathered, and disease followed the gathering · Languages and kin ties kept alive inside the walls

  • Place the mission where it belongs, on the edges of the empire, in Florida, Texas, New Mexico, and eventually California, in country Spain claimed but could not garrison.
  • A mission was a religious settlement founded by Catholic orders, Franciscans above all, among Indigenous peoples, to convert them and remake their way of life.
  • Families were gathered into a supervised town around a church, taught farming and crafts alongside doctrine, and governed by a daily round of bells, work, and worship.
  • The missions extended Spanish language, livestock, and authority hundreds of miles beyond the settler towns, at a cost in soldiers the Crown never had to pay.
  • Their human cost fell on the gathered. Crowded towns raised epidemic disease to a killing rhythm, and mission populations fell at grievous rates even as new families were brought in.

Note: teach the second half of the story too. Indigenous residents used the missions on their own terms, kept languages, kin ties, and older practices alive inside them, fled when they could, and sometimes rose against them.

Slide 16 · Section E: France on the St. Lawrence (divider)

On the slide: France on the St. Lawrence · Champlain's drawing of his habitation at Quebec, the post he founded in 1608

Picture: Samuel de Champlain's own drawing of the habitation at Quebec: the fortified warehouse, dwellings, and garden he raised in 1608 on the St. Lawrence River. Everything about the French empire in the north follows from the size of this picture.

  • The post held a few dozen men. The colony it anchored held, for most of the century, a few thousand settlers strung along one river, in a country whose winters closed the river for half the year.
  • Such a colony could not conquer, tax, or draft the Indigenous nations around it, and it did not try. It traded.
  • Beaver pelts, gathered by Indigenous hunters across half a continent and carried to the French posts by Indigenous canoes, paid for the whole enterprise.
  • The trade's terms were set in council, with peoples who had other buyers and armies of their own.
  • Section E teaches the alliance system the fur trade required, and the two generations of war with the Haudenosaunee that the trade's rivalries produced.

Slide 17 · The exchange neither side could run alone

On the slide: Beaver pelts down the rivers to Quebec · Axes, kettles, knives, cloth, and firearms in return · Prices set in council, sealed with gifts · The hunting country stayed in Indigenous hands

  • Break the exchange down to its two columns, as the activity asks students to do.
  • Down the rivers came beaver pelts, the raw material of the felt hats all Europe wore, gathered by Montagnais, Algonquin, and Huron hunters in country the French had never seen and could not enter uninvited.
  • Up the rivers went manufactured goods: iron axes and kettles that outworked stone and clay, knives, cloth, and, increasingly, firearms.
  • Neither column could be supplied by the other side, which is the structure's whole meaning. France had no hunters, no canoes, and no knowledge of the interior. Its partners had no ironworks.
  • Prices were not posted but negotiated, in councils that opened with gifts and speeches and the renewal of the alliance itself, because in the diplomacy of the woodlands a trade agreement and a political alliance were the same document.
  • When beaver grew scarce in one district, the trade moved west, and France had to win new partners in new country, on terms those peoples accepted.

Slide 18 · Allies, marriages, and a two generation war

On the slide: Traders live in Indigenous towns and learn the languages · Intermarriage binds the trade to kinship · Shared war against the Haudenosaunee · Missions travel the same routes as the trade

  • The alliance system ran on people, not paper. French traders wintered in Indigenous towns, learned the languages, and married into the nations, so that trade partnerships became kinship, the strongest bond the woodlands recognized.
  • Champlain set the pattern early by joining his partners' wars, and the enemy that choice made was the Haudenosaunee, the Five Nations south of Lake Ontario.
  • Their own trade ambitions ran through Dutch posts on the Hudson, and the hunting country the beaver wars of the middle century were fought over.
  • France and the Haudenosaunee remained enemies, with truces, for two generations.
  • Jesuit missionaries traveled the traders' routes to the same towns, learned the same languages, and built missions among the Huron and other nations, with results the course meets again: converts, divisions inside nations, and records written in Indigenous languages.
  • Through all of it, French survival was renewed annually, in councils where Indigenous leaders set terms a governor could accept or refuse but not dictate.

Slide 19 · Section F: The Dutch, Louisiana, and the Sugar Islands (divider)

On the slide: The Dutch, Louisiana, and the Sugar Islands · A second fur colony falls to England, and France's richest America turns out to run on enslaved labor

  • This section widens the map and then breaks the lesson's easy contrast, deliberately.
  • The Dutch built their own fur colony on the Hudson, New Netherland, with its capital at New Amsterdam, and held it for forty years before an English fleet took it in 1664 and renamed it New York.
  • France answered England's growing presence by planting Louisiana in 1699 at the mouth of the Mississippi, a colony meant to hold the river, the Gulf, and the road west. Both stories belong to the northern pattern of posts and trade.
  • Then the section turns south, to the America that paid France's colonial bills: the sugar islands of the Caribbean, Saint Domingue above all, where plantations worked by enslaved Africans produced wealth the fur trade never approached.
  • There France did not ally and negotiate. It legislated slavery, in the Code Noir of 1685, and students will read the code's own articles in the activity.

Slide 20 · New Netherland falls, Louisiana planted

On the slide: The Dutch on the Hudson from the 1620s · Furs through Iroquois country, farms along the river · England seizes the colony in 1664: New York · France plants Louisiana in 1699 to hold the Mississippi

  • The Dutch West India Company planted New Netherland in the 1620s for the same beaver that built New France, trading through Iroquois country at Fort Orange and farming the Hudson valley from New Amsterdam at the river's mouth.
  • The colony was always thinly peopled, and in 1664 an English fleet took it without a battle the settlers were willing to fight.
  • Renamed New York for the king's brother, it kept its Dutch families, churches, and place names, a reminder that empires changed flags faster than they changed populations.
  • France watched England consolidate the coast and answered at the continent's other door.
  • In 1699 French colonists planted Louisiana at the mouth of the Mississippi, claiming the river system, checking Spain's Gulf settlements, and giving the fur trade a southern outlet.
  • Louisiana stayed poor, garrisoned, and dependent on enslaved African labor and Indigenous alliances at once, a colony that belonged to both of the lesson's patterns and prospered at neither.

Slide 21 · The Code Noir, 1685: slavery in French law

On the slide: Article II, baptism and Catholic instruction for the enslaved · Article XII, children of enslaved parents are enslaved · Article XXXVIII, flight punished with mutilation and branding · Article LIX, freed slaves granted the rights of the freeborn

  • Read the four articles as a system, the way the activity sets them in front of students.
  • Article II required every enslaved person in the French islands to be baptized and instructed in the Roman Catholic faith, bringing the church inside the slave system as its registrar.
  • Article XII made the system hereditary: children born from marriages between slaves were slaves, so status passed through birth and no generation could age out of it.
  • Article XXXVIII policed the system's edge: an enslaved person on the run for one month was to have his ears cut off and be branded with a fleur de lys on one shoulder, with worse for a second flight.
  • Article LIX then held out the system's one door: freed slaves were granted the same rights, privileges, and immunities as the freeborn.
  • On Saint Domingue, the richest sugar colony in the world, this code was working law. The empire of alliances in the north and the empire of the code in the islands were the same French empire, flying the same flag.

Ask: Read together, do these four articles protect the enslaved, or control them? Have students answer from the text of the articles only; the activity's verdict turns on it.

Slide 22 · Section G: Two Ways of Holding America (divider)

On the slide: Two Ways of Holding America · Rule against alliance, and the base both empires shared

  • Close the lesson by making the comparison do its full work, in both directions.
  • By 1700 Spain had built the stronger holding by every measure of permanence: millions of subjects, cities larger than Spain's own, a revenue in silver, and a society that reproduced itself across generations.
  • France had built the wider holding at the smaller cost: a few thousand settlers, a web of alliances reaching to the Mississippi and the Great Lakes, and an empire that survived because it served the interests of the nations inside it.
  • Then set both holdings on the record's base. Spain's conquests were made by Indigenous armies and its wealth dug by Indigenous and African labor.
  • France's trade was gathered and defended by Indigenous partners, and its richest colonies ran on enslaved Africans under a written code.
  • The forms differed completely, and students should be able to describe the difference exactly. The dependence did not differ, and students should be able to state that exactly too.

Slide 23 · Two empires at a glance

On the slide: How it came · What it lived by · Whose labor · Indigenous nations, Spain against France in four rows

  • This slide mirrors the final table in the guided notes, Two Empires at a Glance. Students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • How it came: Spain by conquest in 1519 to 1521, carried by Indigenous allies and disease; France by settlement for trade, Quebec in 1608.
  • What it lived by: Spain by silver, tribute, and hacienda produce; France by furs in the north and sugar in the islands.
  • Whose labor: Spain commanded Indigenous drafts and enslaved Africans; France traded with Indigenous partners and enslaved Africans on the plantations.
  • How it treated Indigenous nations: Spain ruled them as ranked subjects and gathered them into missions; France courted them as allies because it could not rule them.

Note: grade the table generously on wording and strictly on the labor row. An entry that leaves enslaved labor out of the French column has missed Section F, and with it the lesson's closing judgment.

Slide 24 · Words for the Spanish empire

On the slide: conquistador · encomienda · mestizo · New Spain

[Have students copy the definitions exactly as they appear in the guided notes.]

  • conquistador: a Spanish military leader who conquered Indigenous peoples in the Americas in the 1500s, seeking land, gold, and glory for Spain and himself
  • encomienda: the Spanish system that granted a colonist the right to demand tribute and labor from Indigenous people in a given area, in return for supposed protection and Christian teaching
  • mestizo: a person of mixed Spanish and Indigenous ancestry, ranked in the caste order of Spanish America between Spaniards and Indigenous peoples
  • New Spain: Spain's viceroyalty in North America and the Caribbean, governed from Mexico City, which at its height stretched from Panama to the present-day American Southwest
  • Teach the four as one system's parts. The conquistador made the conquest, the encomienda organized the labor the conquest delivered, the mestizo population grew inside the ranked society the system built, and New Spain was the government that held it all.

Slide 25 · Words for the borderlands and the trade

On the slide: mission · fur trade · hacienda

[Have students copy the definitions exactly as they appear in the guided notes.]

  • mission: a religious settlement founded by Catholic orders among Indigenous peoples in the Spanish and French colonies to convert them and remake their way of life
  • fur trade: the exchange central to French and Dutch colonization, in which Europeans traded manufactured goods to Indigenous hunters for beaver pelts and other furs
  • hacienda: a large estate in Spanish America worked by Indigenous or enslaved labor, producing food and goods for nearby towns and mines
  • Keep the geography in view for review. The mission stood on the borders of the Spanish and French colonies alike, the fur trade ran the French and Dutch north, and the hacienda anchored the Spanish countryside, so the three terms together map the two empires' working institutions.

Slide 26 · The lesson in one view

On the slide: Conquered · Ruled · Traded · Enslaved, the lesson in four rows

[Close the argument before the work begins. Make students say the four rows aloud in order, because the sequence is the lesson.]

  • Conquered: Tenochtitlan fell in 1521 to a siege carried by Indigenous allies and smallpox as much as by the five hundred Spaniards who entered it.
  • Ruled: Spain governed its prize through silver and the royal fifth, the encomienda and the drafts that followed it, missions on the borders, and a society ranked by birth from peninsulares to the enslaved.
  • Traded: France held the north with a few thousand settlers because the fur trade let it grow rich on pelts gathered by Indigenous partners, at prices set in councils it did not control.
  • Enslaved: the same France wrote the Code Noir in 1685 and ran its richest colonies on enslaved African labor.
  • Two empires, opposite in form, and one shared base in land taken and labor coerced.

Slide 27 · Now your turn

On the slide: Answer the question · Activity, notebook, quick check, and writing for the lesson · Exit ticket

  • The activity, Two Empires, One Hemisphere, puts five stations in front of students: Diaz entering Tenochtitlan, Las Casas on the destruction, summaries of the encomienda and the New Laws and of the fur trade, and the Code Noir's own articles. It ends with a comparison table and a verdict on which empire's way of holding America proved stronger by 1700.
  • The notebook page charts the two empires across five questions. The quick check, The Causeway and the Code, tests the 1524 map, the forces that took Tenochtitlan, the governor who could not command his partners, and the code's three rules.
  • The writing task turns the lesson question into an essay argued from the evidence bank, closing with the student's judgment on whether the contrast between the empires survives the sugar islands.
  • Exit ticket, three parts: the encomienda defined with Las Casas named; the fur trade explained with its numbers; and the lesson question answered in two or three sentences.

Note: the next lesson turns to the English, and to a starving outpost on the James River.
