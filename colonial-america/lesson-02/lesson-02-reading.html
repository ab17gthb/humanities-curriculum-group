The Spanish and French Americas

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Conquistadores and the Fall of Tenochtitlan

In 1519 Hernan Cortes sailed from Cuba with about 500 men and marched toward the richest city in the Americas. He was a conquistador, a Spanish military leader who conquered Indigenous peoples in the 1500s, seeking land and gold. The empire he challenged ruled from Tenochtitlan, a lake city larger than any in Spain, and collected tribute across central Mexico.

Cortes did not conquer that empire alone. The Tlaxcalans and other peoples who resented Aztec tribute joined him in the tens of thousands, for reasons of their own, and smallpox killed defenders inside the city. After three months of siege, Tenochtitlan fell in August 1521, and Cortes rebuilt it as Mexico City. In Peru, Francisco Pizarro used Indigenous allies, Inca civil war, and disease to seize a second empire.

B. An Empire of Mines and Estates

What Spain built was the largest empire in the Americas. New Spain, the viceroyalty governed from Mexico City, stretched from Panama to the present day Southwest, and a second viceroyalty ruled Peru. Silver was its engine. At Potosi in the Andes, a mountain of silver found in 1545 drew a city of more than 100,000 people, where drafted Indigenous laborers worked in conditions that killed many of them.

On the land, Spaniards built the hacienda, a large estate worked by Indigenous or enslaved labor, producing food and goods for nearby towns and mines. Their owners became the lasting power in the countryside. The Crown took its fifth of every mine's silver, and Potosi silver flowed onward to Europe and across the Pacific to China.

C. The Encomienda and Las Casas

To reward conquerors and control the conquered, the Crown used the encomienda, the system that granted a colonist the right to demand tribute and labor from Indigenous people in a given area, in return for supposed protection and Christian teaching. In practice the grants meant forced labor, and communities already shattered by disease died under the demands.

Bartolome de las Casas, a friar who had himself held an encomienda, argued at court that Indigenous peoples held rights the Crown was bound to protect. His Brief Account of the Destruction of the Indies, published in 1552, described the killing in numbers his readers could not forget, and his advocacy helped push the Crown to the New Laws of 1542, which limited the encomienda. Colonists resisted, and labor drafts continued. Las Casas once suggested African labor as a substitute, a position he later repented.

D. The Society New Spain Made

Spanish America ordered its people by birth. At the top stood peninsulares, born in Spain, and below them creoles, Spaniards born in America, who owned most land and resented exclusion from high office. Below them stood mestizos, people of mixed Spanish and Indigenous ancestry, ranked between Spaniards and Indigenous peoples, and at the bottom stood Indigenous communities and enslaved Africans, whose labor carried the structure.

On the empire's edges, Catholic orders built the mission, a religious settlement founded among Indigenous peoples to convert them and remake their lives. Missions from Florida to California gathered Indigenous families into supervised towns and spread Spanish authority where no garrison could hold. Disease followed, and the towns lost people at grievous rates, while residents kept languages, kin ties, and older practices alive inside them.

E. France on the St. Lawrence: An Empire of Trade

France came by a different road. Samuel de Champlain founded Quebec in 1608, and the colony stayed small, a thin line of farms and posts along the St. Lawrence. Its wealth was the fur trade, the exchange central to French and Dutch colonization, in which Europeans traded manufactured goods to Indigenous hunters for beaver pelts. Beaver hats brought high prices in Europe, and a few thousand settlers grew rich on pelts from half a continent.

A trade empire could not command the peoples it traded with, so France allied with them. French traders married into Indigenous nations, learned their languages, and bound the commerce with councils, gifts, and shared war against common enemies, above all the Haudenosaunee, whose trade ambitions made the two powers enemies for two generations. French survival depended on Indigenous partners who set terms of their own.

F. The Dutch, Louisiana, and the Sugar Islands

The Dutch followed the same trade to the Hudson River, founding New Netherland in the 1620s. England seized it in 1664 and renamed it New York, but Dutch families and commerce remained. In the far south, France planted Louisiana at the mouth of the Mississippi in 1699, to hold the river and check Spain.

France's richest America was neither Canada nor Louisiana. On Saint Domingue and the sugar islands, plantations worked by enslaved Africans produced sugar worth far more than the fur trade. There the Code Noir of 1685, issued by Louis XIV, wrote slavery into French law, requiring Catholic baptism for the enslaved, making children of enslaved mothers enslaved, and setting mutilation as the punishment for running away.

G. Two Ways of Holding America

By 1700 the two empires had built opposite instruments. Spain ruled millions of Indigenous subjects and extracted silver and tribute through ranked law and coerced labor. New France held a continent on paper with a few thousand settlers and alliances renewed council by council, because its trade required Indigenous partners too strong to rule.

Neither empire stood on its own strength alone. Spain's conquests were made with Indigenous armies and sustained by Indigenous and African labor, and France's trade was gathered and defended by Indigenous partners. Both empires rested on land taken and labor coerced, and both had to reckon with Indigenous nations that remained powers in their own country.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Name the two forces besides Spanish weapons, and the year. Indigenous allies and epidemic disease. The Tlaxcalans and other peoples who resented Aztec tribute joined Cortes in the tens of thousands, fighting for reasons of their own against an empire whose tribute demands they resented. Smallpox, carried into the city during the fighting, killed defenders inside besieged Tenochtitlan. The city fell in August 1521, after a siege of three months, and Cortes rebuilt it as Mexico City.

2. What was the encomienda in law and in practice? In law, the encomienda granted a colonist the right to demand tribute and labor from Indigenous people in a given area, in return for supposed protection and Christian teaching. In practice it was worked as forced labor, and communities already shattered by disease died under the demands. Bartolome de las Casas, himself a former encomienda holder, attacked the system at the Spanish court. His Brief Account of the Destruction of the Indies (1552) put the killing on the record, and his advocacy helped push the Crown to the New Laws of 1542, which limited the encomienda. Colonists resisted the laws, and labor drafts continued in new forms.

3. Describe the ranked order from top to bottom. Peninsulares, born in Spain, stood at the top. Below them stood creoles, Spaniards born in America, who owned most of the land and resented exclusion from high office. Below them stood mestizos, people of mixed Spanish and Indigenous ancestry, ranked between Spaniards and Indigenous peoples. At the bottom stood Indigenous communities and enslaved Africans, whose labor carried the whole structure.

4. Why did France depend on alliances? New France held only a few thousand settlers in a thin line of farms and posts along the St. Lawrence, far too few to command the peoples whose country held the furs. Its colony lived by the fur trade, in which Europeans traded manufactured goods to Indigenous hunters for beaver pelts. France therefore bound the trade with marriages into Indigenous nations, councils, gifts, and shared war, and its survival depended on Indigenous hunters, guides, and allies who set terms of their own. Its great enemy in the trade was the Haudenosaunee, with whom it fought for two generations.

5. How did Spain build an empire on conquest, and how did France build one on trade? Full credit answers hold the contrast and the shared base together. Spain conquered populous empires with small Spanish forces carried by Indigenous allies and disease, then ruled millions through New Spain, the encomienda and later labor drafts, haciendas, missions, and a ranked caste order, extracting silver at Potosi. France planted few settlers and lived by the fur trade, which required Indigenous partners, so it built alliances, intermarriage, and shared war, above all against the Haudenosaunee. Full credit answers then close with the reading's verdict: in the sugar islands France rested on enslaved labor under the Code Noir of 1685, so both empires rested in the end on land taken and labor coerced. Answers that present France as peaceful, with no account of the Code Noir or the sugar plantations, earn partial credit.
