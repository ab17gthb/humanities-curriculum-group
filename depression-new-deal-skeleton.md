# The Depression and New Deal Unit: Assignment Skeleton

_Sixth built unit for the Knoweth curriculum and planner site, sitting in course order after the Twenties and before World War II. Modeled on the Progressive Era pilot, the Gilded Age, Imperialism and World War I, and the Twenties units. Every lesson has the same working plate so teachers can pick pieces without losing the line of the unit._

## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- How did the New Deal change the relationship between Americans and their federal government, and what did it leave undone?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's core plan is 18 instructional days. The unit runs from the winter of 1929 to 1930 through the end of the 1930s. It opens with the Depression already under way, because the Twenties unit owns the crash itself and closes by separating the crash from the Depression. This unit teaches the bridge the Twenties lesson pointed to: bank failures, deflation, and collapsing demand turning a market collapse into a decade of collapse. It ends with the New Deal's legacy and limits, and it does not open the war mobilization that ended mass unemployment; the World War II unit owns that, and the last lesson names the boundary plainly.

**The plate in this build**

1. **Reading**: 850 to 900 words with five questions and a teacher key
2. **Guided notes**: keyed to the reading and lesson, with the vocabulary set verbatim and a teacher key
3. **Activity**: stations, jigsaw, map work, simulation, debate, or structured chart work in the locked print design
4. **Notebook page**: a single organizer students keep
5. **Quick check**: a short formative tied to the blueprint
6. **Writing task**: a paragraph or short essay with a scaffold and a teacher key or rubric

TPSP source analyses and photo sets are linked where the audited bank already carries them. They are not rebuilt merely to fill a folder.

**Six state-neutral era topics**

1. Collapse and human cost
2. From Hoover to Roosevelt: the changing federal role
3. Relief, recovery, and reform: the New Deal programs
4. Opposition, the critics, and the courts
5. Labor, exclusion, and the unfinished agenda
6. Legacy: the new American state and its limits

## Lesson 1: The Human Cost of the Depression

**Lesson question:** How deep did the Depression cut, and how did ordinary families live through it?

**Reading scope:** The collapse after the crash in plain sequence: bank failures and lost savings, deflation and falling prices, industrial production cut nearly in half, unemployment reaching about one worker in four in 1933, and farm prices so low that producing food cost more than it returned. Then the human record built from that collapse: breadlines and soup kitchens run by charities and cities, shantytowns called Hoovervilles, eviction and the loss of homes, families doubled up and marriages and births postponed, hoboes and teenage riders on freight trains, and the Dust Bowl drought on the southern Plains driving farm families west on Route 66. The reading is careful with numbers and labels contemporary estimates as estimates. It closes by posing the question the whole unit answers: whether a collapse this size was a private misfortune to be waited out or a public emergency the government had a duty to answer.

**Activity concept:** The cost ledger stations. Students work through an unemployment series, a bank failure account, a Hooverville photograph and caption, a Dust Bowl migrant account, and a family budget before and after a wage cut, then build a ledger of the Depression's costs sorted into money lost, homes lost, and plans lost, each entry tied to a document.

**Vocabulary:** Great Depression, deflation, bank run, unemployment, breadline, Hooverville, Dust Bowl

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Farm Security Administration photographs and migrant accounts where the bank carries them. **LINK**.

## Lesson 2: Hoover and the Limits of Voluntarism

**Lesson question:** What did President Hoover do about the Depression, and why did his program fall short of the collapse?

**Reading scope:** Hoover's beliefs first, because his policy followed from them: rugged individualism, the fear that federal relief would weaken character and unbalance the budget, and voluntarism, the plan of asking business, charities, and local government to carry relief by cooperation rather than compulsion. Then what he actually did, which was more than the caricature allows: the Federal Farm Board, public works spending, the Hawley-Smoot Tariff and its costs, the Reconstruction Finance Corporation lending to banks and railroads in 1932, and the Emergency Relief and Construction Act of the same summer, his one step toward federal relief loans to states. Then the limits: charity and city budgets exhausted, wages cut under voluntary pledges, the RFC lending at the top while families failed at the bottom, and the Bonus Army of veterans camped in Washington in 1932 and driven out by troops. The lesson closes with the election of 1932 and the long interregnum winter, when the banking system failed state by state while two presidents waited and watched.

**Activity concept:** The voluntarism test. Students staff a 1931 relief desk and process five cases drawn from the documents, a laid-off machinist, a failed bank depositor, a drought county, a city relief office out of funds, and a Bonus Army veteran, deciding for each case what Hoover's program offered, what it refused, and what happened, then judge the program against the size of the collapse.

**Vocabulary:** voluntarism, rugged individualism, Reconstruction Finance Corporation, public works, Hawley-Smoot Tariff, Bonus Army, interregnum

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP material on Hoover, the Bonus Army, and the 1932 campaign where the bank carries it. **LINK**.

## Lesson 3: Roosevelt and the First Hundred Days

**Lesson question:** How did Roosevelt's first hundred days change what Americans expected their government to do in a crisis?

**Reading scope:** The inauguration of March 4, 1933, with the banking system shut or failing in state after state, and the first inaugural address and its claim that the only thing to fear was fear itself, treated as a policy instrument and not only a phrase. The bank holiday proclaimed March 6, the Emergency Banking Act passed in a day on March 9, the reopening of sound banks, and the first fireside chat on March 12 explaining the banking system to millions of listeners in plain language, after which deposits flowed back in. Then the Hundred Days as a legislative season: Congress passing bill after bill, the Agricultural Adjustment Act for farm prices, the Tennessee Valley Authority for a whole river region, the National Industrial Recovery Act, the Glass-Steagall separation of banking and the federal guarantee of deposits, and the end of Prohibition set in motion. The reading keeps two judgments in view at once: speed and confidence were real achievements, and most programs were experiments whose results nobody could yet know.

**Activity concept:** Hundred Days drafting desk. Student groups receive one crisis file each, banking, farm prices, a river valley, industrial collapse, and unemployment, and draft the one-page bill they would send to Congress in March 1933, then compare their drafts with the law actually passed and mark what Congress added, cut, or left out.

**Vocabulary:** bank holiday, Emergency Banking Act, fireside chat, Hundred Days, Glass-Steagall Act, Agricultural Adjustment Act, Tennessee Valley Authority

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP First Inaugural Address and First Fireside Chat where the bank carries them. **LINK**.

## Lesson 4: Relief, Recovery, and the Alphabet Agencies at Work

**Lesson question:** What did the New Deal agencies actually do in people's lives, and how far did their help reach?

**Reading scope:** The alphabet agencies as work, one by one, with the distinction the lesson is built on: direct relief, money or goods given to people in need, against work relief, paid jobs on public projects. Harry Hopkins and the Federal Emergency Relief Administration sending federal money through the states. The Civilian Conservation Corps putting a quarter million young men into camps within months, planting trees and building park roads for thirty dollars a month, most of it sent home. The Civil Works Administration hiring four million people in the winter of 1933 to 1934 and then being shut down as too costly. The Public Works Administration under Harold Ickes building slowly and carefully: dams, bridges, schools, and courthouses that still stand. The National Recovery Administration and its codes and Blue Eagle, the experiment in planning industry from Washington. The reading ends by measuring reach honestly: millions were helped and hired, the agencies were segregated or uneven in many places, and by 1934 unemployment still stood far above any level the country had known before 1929.

**Activity concept:** Agency case files. Each group receives a case file of one American, a jobless young man in Ohio, a widowed mother in Chicago, a drought farmer in Oklahoma, a laid-off teacher in Mississippi, and a construction engineer in Oregon, and matches the case to the agency built for it, states what the agency would actually provide, and records who in the file no agency reached and why.

**Vocabulary:** direct relief, work relief, Civilian Conservation Corps, Federal Emergency Relief Administration, Civil Works Administration, Public Works Administration, National Recovery Administration

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP material on the CCC, the TVA, and New Deal work programs where the bank carries it. **LINK**.

## Lesson 5: The Critics: Long, Townsend, Coughlin, and the Court

**Lesson question:** Why did the New Deal draw attack from every direction by 1935, and what did the attacks reveal about the country?

**Reading scope:** The charge that the New Deal did too little, carried by three mass movements with radios and membership lists. Huey Long of Louisiana and Share Our Wealth, with its caps on fortunes and its promise of a homestead and income for every family, and its network of clubs enrolling millions before Long's assassination in September 1935. Dr. Francis Townsend and his old-age revolving pension, two hundred dollars a month to every citizen over sixty who spent it within the month, carried by petitions and Townsend clubs that pressed Congress from every district. Father Charles Coughlin, the radio priest whose broadcasts reached tens of millions, moving from early support of Roosevelt to the National Union for Social Justice and open war on the administration. Then the charge that the New Deal did too much: the American Liberty League of wealthy men and former Democrats, and the Supreme Court, which in 1935 and 1936 struck down the National Industrial Recovery Act in the Schechter case and the Agricultural Adjustment Act in United States v. Butler, and read its limits into the Constitution. The reading closes with the pressure these attacks put on Roosevelt from both directions at once, which is the setup for the Second New Deal.

**Activity concept:** The pressure map. Students work from a Share Our Wealth pamphlet passage, a Townsend Plan petition, a Coughlin broadcast account, a Liberty League statement, and the Schechter holding, then map each attack by its source, its charge, its proposed cure, and the voters it could move, and judge which pressure Roosevelt had the most reason to answer in 1935.

**Vocabulary:** Share Our Wealth, Townsend Plan, old-age pension, demagogue, American Liberty League, Schechter v. United States, United States v. Butler

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP material on Long, Townsend, Coughlin, and the Schechter decision where the bank carries it. **LINK**.

## Lesson 6: The Second New Deal

**Lesson question:** How did the Second New Deal of 1935 move beyond the first, and who was it built to protect?

**Reading scope:** The legislative season of 1935, driven by the critics of Lesson 5 and by the plain fact that recovery had not arrived. The Works Progress Administration under Hopkins, putting about eight and a half million people to work across its life on roads, schools, airports, and, through its arts projects, plays, murals, guidebooks, and oral histories. The Social Security Act of August 1935, the unit's most durable statute: old-age insurance paid from payroll taxes, unemployment insurance run with the states, and aid to dependent children and the blind, and its founding exclusions, agricultural and domestic workers left out, an exclusion that fell hardest on Black workers and on women. The National Labor Relations Act, the Wagner Act, guaranteeing workers the right to organize and bargain through unions of their own choosing and creating the board to enforce it. The Revenue Act of 1935, the wealth tax, raising rates at the top. The reading treats 1935 as the year the New Deal stopped being only an emergency program and became a settlement: the federal government accepting permanent duties for old age, unemployment, and labor rights.

**Activity concept:** The settlement chart. Students work through the WPA project record, the Social Security Act's titles, a Wagner Act case summary, and a wealth tax table, then build the settlement chart themselves: each permanent duty the federal government accepted in 1935, who it covered, who it left out, and how it was paid for.

**Vocabulary:** Works Progress Administration, Social Security Act, old-age insurance, unemployment insurance, payroll tax, National Labor Relations Act, collective bargaining

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Social Security Act excerpts and WPA material where the bank carries it. **LINK**.

## Lesson 7: Labor Rising and the Recession of 1937

**Lesson question:** Why did 1937 bring both labor's greatest victories and the New Deal's hardest year?

**Reading scope:** The Wagner Act in workers' hands: the Committee for Industrial Organization organizing whole industries rather than crafts, the sit-down strike as the new tactic, and the Flint sit-down of the winter of 1936 to 1937, where General Motors workers held the plants for forty-four days and won recognition of the United Auto Workers. Union membership climbing from under three million in 1933 toward nine million by the decade's end. The Memorial Day Massacre of 1937, where Chicago police killed ten strikers at Republic Steel, as the measure of what organizing still cost. Then the turn: Roosevelt's court packing plan of February 1937, proposed after his landslide reelection, read by much of the country as an attack on the Court itself, defeated in the Senate even as the Court upheld the Wagner Act and Social Security and began sustaining New Deal laws. And the Roosevelt recession of 1937 to 1938, when federal spending cuts and new payroll taxes pulled money out of a weak economy and unemployment climbed back toward nineteen percent, teaching the administration the cost of balancing the budget too soon.

**Activity concept:** Two ledgers of 1937. Groups work the Flint settlement documents, a Memorial Day account, the court packing message, and a recession production series, then keep two ledgers side by side, what labor won in 1937 and what the New Deal lost, and close by judging which ledger ran longer.

**Vocabulary:** sit-down strike, Congress of Industrial Organizations, United Auto Workers, collective bargaining (carried from Lesson 6), court packing, Roosevelt recession, deficit spending

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP material on Flint, the court packing fight, and the 1937 recession where the bank carries it. **LINK**. Note: "collective bargaining" is defined in Lesson 6 and carried into this lesson's notes verbatim; this lesson's own new terms are six.

## Lesson 8: What the New Deal Changed and What It Did Not

**Lesson question:** What did the New Deal change for good, what did it leave undone, and how should it be judged?

**Reading scope:** The changed country first. The federal government as a permanent presence in economic life: bank deposits insured, securities markets regulated, old-age and unemployment insurance running, labor rights enforced, the Tennessee Valley electrified, minimum wage and maximum hours law from 1938. The physical country rebuilt in part: dams, bridges, schools, parks, and airports in daily use. The political settlement: the New Deal coalition of white southerners, northern city voters, union members, farmers, and Black voters moving toward the Democratic Party, holding national power for a generation. Then the limits, stated as plainly: the Depression was not ended. Unemployment never fell below fourteen percent in any year of the decade, and full employment arrived only with war spending. The exclusions written into the settlement, agricultural and domestic workers outside Social Security at the start, segregated CCC camps and uneven relief, redlining in federal housing loans, and Mexican repatriation carried out under local and federal pressure in the early Depression years. The reading closes by framing the judgment historians still argue: measured against the collapse it answered, the New Deal was relief and reform on a scale the country had never attempted; measured against full recovery and equal treatment, it stopped short, and the war, not the New Deal, finished the Depression.

**Activity concept:** The verdict brief. Students receive the unit's evidence sorted into four files, the changed state, the physical record, the recovery numbers, and the exclusions, and prepare a one-page verdict brief answering the lesson question with a claim, three pieces of evidence, and the strongest counterargument answered.

**Vocabulary:** New Deal coalition, Fair Labor Standards Act, safety net, redlining, repatriation, Securities and Exchange Commission, Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP premium DBQ #5, "Did the New Deal end the Great Depression?" (DBQ180), is the natural writing extension here. **LINK** for the planner picker, not rebuilt.

## Changes from the working map

- The unit opens with the Depression under way rather than with the crash, because the Twenties unit owns the crash and teaches the crash-versus-Depression distinction in its last lesson. Teachers who run the units back to back get one continuous line from October 1929 into this unit's first lesson.
- The critics and the Court share one lesson rather than splitting, because the two-front pressure of 1935 is the direct cause of the Second New Deal, and the lesson sequence should make that cause visible.
- The court packing fight is taught in Lesson 7 rather than Lesson 5, on chronology: it belongs to 1937, after the 1936 landslide, and it sits beside the recession of the same year. Lesson 5's Court material is the 1935 and 1936 decisions striking New Deal laws.
- The unit does not open war mobilization. The last lesson states that war spending, not the New Deal, brought full employment, and leaves the mobilization itself to the World War II unit.
