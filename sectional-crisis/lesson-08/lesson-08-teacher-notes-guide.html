Total War and Appomattox

Sectional Crisis and Civil War · Lesson 8 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Sectional Crisis and Civil War, Lesson 8 of 8 — the unit's closing lesson. Lesson 7 ended on emancipation transforming the war. This lesson runs the war's last year: Grant's coordinated campaigns of 1864 fixing the Confederate armies in place while Sherman destroyed the resources behind them, the election of 1864 refusing an armistice, Congress and the petitioning women carrying slavery's end into the Constitution, Lee's surrender at Appomattox on April 9, 1865, and Lincoln's assassination five days later. The lesson stops at April 1865, where the unit stops — with the freed people's future standing exactly where the war left it, unanswered on purpose. The Reconstruction unit opens there.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 6 (Grant's predecessors' caution as the foil; Scott's Anaconda completed in substance), Lesson 7 (the Proclamation's war-powers limits answered by the Thirteenth Amendment), and Lesson 2 (slavery named as the war's cause — now by the president himself, in the Second Inaugural). The activity, The Ending File, pairs Lincoln's verdict against Sherman's letter, Annie Davis's question, the League's address, and the Homestead Act.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: McClellan's removal (Lesson 6); the Proclamation and its limits (Lesson 7); and the geography of the two theaters at survey level. Casualty and signature figures are taught as the usual estimates and counts, as the documents state them. Following the unit's binding image discipline, the lesson uses no photograph of the dead and no battle photograph at all; its war is carried in portraits, the McLean house photograph, and the documents. No image of Richmond's ruins or of the assassination is used, by the SOURCES record's stated judgment.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (a surrender's terms and an inaugural's argument read as the war's settlement documents), D2.His.9 (the war's ending as coordinated cause and effect over a year, not one battle), D2.His.16 (settled and left standing held apart — the unit's closing distinction), D2.Civ.12 (an amendment carried while the war still ran); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.8, WHST.9-10.1 (Lincoln's verdict judged from at least three documents, with the other side's strongest claim answered).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to state Grant's doctrine (armies, not cities, all at once) and its Overland price, stated as the usual estimate; to state Sherman's theory in his own words ("war is cruelty; you cannot refine it") and the March's measure (some 62,000 men, a corridor some 60 miles wide), with the human accounting carried; to explain how the election of 1864 refused the armistice the Chicago platform demanded; to hold the Union's other wartime work — Homestead and Morrill (1862), the League's some 400,000 signatures, the Thirteenth Amendment's passage on January 31, 1865; to state Appomattox's terms; to read the Second Inaugural's three moves; and to close the unit in two columns — settled, and left standing.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; Grant (slides 4–6) 7 minutes; Sherman (slides 7–10) 9 minutes; the election (slides 11–12) 5 minutes; the other wartime work (slides 13–16) 8 minutes; Appomattox (slides 17–19) 6 minutes; the Second Inaugural (slides 20–21) 5 minutes; April 1865 and the close (slides 22–29) 5 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes (final table: The Ending at a Glance), and The Ending File (five stations). The quick check is "The Woe Due"; the writing task is "The Verdict of the Second Inaugural"; the exit ticket has three parts — Grant's doctrine and the Appomattox terms, the amendment's date, and the lesson question answered from both settlements.

Key vocabulary

Ulysses S. Grant · total war · William Tecumseh Sherman · March to the Sea · Thirteenth Amendment · Appomattox Court House · Second Inaugural Address

  • Picture: the McLean house at Appomattox Court House, Virginia, photographed by Timothy H. O'Sullivan in April 1865, the month of the surrender that took place in its parlor. Open with the house rather than a battle scene, because this lesson is about an ending, not a battle.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "How did the war end, and what did its ending settle?"
  • The question has two halves, and the lesson grades both.
  • The first half is mechanical: how did the war end? Not by one battle or one surrender paper, but by coordinated pressure applied for a year, an election that refused an armistice, and a retreat cut off at a Virginia crossroads village.
  • The second half is the unit's closing argument: what did the ending settle? The Union's indivisibility and slavery's destruction, say the documents. What it left standing — the freed people's claims to land, safety, and the vote — is stated as plainly as what it settled, because the next unit opens exactly there.
  • Keep both halves in view all hour. Every document in the lesson answers one of them, and the exit ticket grades both.

On the slide: the title "The ending, in seven stretches"; the seven section rows, A–G.

  • The map follows the guided notes section by section, so students can use the letters on screen to find their place in their notes all hour.
  • Section A gives Grant command and his doctrine; section B follows Sherman through Atlanta and Georgia; section C puts the war to a vote in 1864; section D reads the wartime work of Congress and of the women who petitioned.

Slide 4 · Section A: Grant takes command (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "March 1864: the armies, not the cities, and all at once."

  • Begin with the appointment, because the ending of the war was designed before it was fought.
  • In March 1864 Lincoln made Grant general in chief of all Union armies, ending the war's long search for a commander who would use the whole strength of the Union at once.
  • His doctrine reversed the practice of his predecessors, who had chased capitals and fought one army at a time while the others rested. Grant would fight every Confederate army simultaneously, so no front could send help to another.
  • He put his headquarters with the Army of the Potomac in Virginia, and sent Sherman west.
  • Say: "Ulysses S. Grant: the Ohio born general who won at Fort Donelson, Shiloh, Vicksburg, and Chattanooga, was made general in chief in March 1864, and directed the coordinated campaigns that wore the Confederacy down, accepting Lee's surrender at Appomattox and later serving two terms as president."

Slide 5 · The general in chief

On the slide: the Grant portrait at right; four bullets — command of all Union armies, March 1864; the objective, the Confederate armies; every army moving at once; Grant staying in Virginia, Sherman going west.

  • Picture: Grant, from the Brady studio's wet collodion portrait, held by the Library of Congress. Grant was forty-one when he took command of all Union armies in March 1864; the portrait is captioned without a precise year, as the SOURCES record directs.
  • Teach the record as a qualification for the doctrine. Grant had won in the west by moving against armies and supply lines together — at Donelson on the rivers, at Vicksburg on the Mississippi, and at Chattanooga on the railroads.
  • Lincoln's judgment in promoting him was that the man who had taken an army's surrender at Vicksburg would not stop at taking a city.
  • Students should hold the sequence Fort Donelson, Shiloh, Vicksburg, Chattanooga as the argument Grant carried into Virginia in the spring of 1864.

Slide 6 · The Overland ledger, and Petersburg

On the slide: the Grant portrait at right; four bullets — the Wilderness, Spotsylvania, Cold Harbor; some 65,000 Union casualties in six weeks, by the usual estimates; Grant sliding south after each battle; Petersburg besieged, June 1864 to April 1865.

  • Work the ledger plainly, because the cost is part of the doctrine's meaning.
  • Grant fought Lee in May and June of 1864 in a near-continuous battle, and the Union army paid casualty figures the country had never seen — by the usual estimates, some sixty-five thousand men in six weeks.
  • Earlier commanders had retreated after such losses to refit. Grant slid around Lee's flank and kept going south, which is why the North's newspapers at first called him a butcher and then understood that the arithmetic had changed: Lee could not replace his men, and Grant could.
  • The siege of Petersburg, the rail center feeding Richmond, then fixed Lee in the trenches for nine months while Sherman ranged free in Georgia.

Slide 7 · Section B: Sherman and total war (divider)

On the slide: the Sherman portrait at right; the section label and title; the caption — "William Tecumseh Sherman, photographed by Mathew Brady, circa 1865."

  • Picture: Sherman, photographed by Mathew Brady circa 1865, from the Library of Congress copy. His face in the portrait, lined and unsmiling, was the face the Confederacy learned to read as resolution.
  • Section B gives the war its second engine. While Grant fixed Lee in Virginia, Sherman campaigned through the Confederacy's interior — first to Atlanta, the rail and manufacturing center of the Deep South, and then across Georgia itself.
  • His theory matched Grant's and extended it: armies were the objective, but armies ate, rode railroads, and drew their will from the society behind them, so the society's resources were military targets too.
  • The next two slides take his own statement of that theory and then the march that carried it out.
  • Say: "William Tecumseh Sherman: the Ohio general who took Atlanta in September 1864, cut loose from his supply lines to march to the sea, and carried total war through Georgia and the Carolinas, writing to Atlanta's mayor that war is cruelty and cannot be refined."
  • Say: "total war: the strategy of making war on the enemy's resources and will as well as its armies, destroying railroads, crops, mills, and stores so that the society sustaining the armies felt the war in its households, carried to its fullest by Sherman in Georgia and the Carolinas."

Slide 8 · Atlanta, and the letter of September 12

On the slide: four bullets — Atlanta taken, September 1864; civilians ordered out of a military post; to Mayor Calhoun, "War is cruelty"; "You cannot refine it."

  • The occasion of the letter carries as much weight as its sentence.
  • Sherman had ordered Atlanta's remaining civilians to leave the city he meant to hold as a military post, and Mayor James M. Calhoun protested the order's hardship.
  • Sherman answered that the hardship was the war itself, not his order, and that those who brought war into the country deserved its curses.
  • Students read the letter's central paragraphs at Station 5 of the activity, in a named transcription, and judge its argument there.

Slide 9 · The March to the Sea

On the slide: the Sherman portrait at right; four bullets — cut loose from supplies, November 1864; some 62,000 soldiers living off the country; railroads, mills, and stores destroyed; Savannah reached in December.

  • Add the human accounting the reading insists on.
  • The march fed the army and broke Georgia's capacity to supply Lee — and it also stripped farms whose owners were mostly women, children, old men, and enslaved people, thousands of whom followed the columns seeking freedom and food the army could not always give.
  • Sherman then turned north through the Carolinas in 1865, where Columbia burned as his army passed, a fire whose authors the record still argues over.
  • Total war shortened the war. The lesson does not ask students to call it clean.
  • Say: "March to the Sea: Sherman's campaign of November and December 1864, in which some sixty two thousand Union soldiers marched from Atlanta to Savannah living off the country and destroying the railroads, mills, and stores in a corridor sixty miles wide, presenting Savannah to Lincoln as a Christmas gift."

Slide 10 · 60 miles wide, the corridor of destruction

On the slide: the big figure "60"; the label — "miles wide, the corridor of destruction"; the italic note — Sherman's march across Georgia, November and December 1864.

  • Pause on the width, because it converts a march into a strategy.
  • A column on one road consumes the farms along it. Sherman marched in parallel columns spread across a front some sixty miles wide, foraging parties working the farms between the roads, so that a band of Georgia from Atlanta to the sea was eaten out and its railroads wrecked beyond quick repair.
  • The figure is the march's own measure in the reports and the histories, stated as the usual figure rather than a surveyed line.
  • Ask: "What was a corridor that wide meant to prove to the people inside it?" The answer Sherman intended was that the Confederate government could not protect its own heartland, and that continuing the war would bring this to every state that sustained it.

Slide 11 · Section C: The election of 1864 (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "An armistice on the ballot, in the middle of a war."

  • Section C states the war's political fact: the Union held a presidential election on schedule in the fourth year of a civil war, and the war itself was the issue.
  • The Democratic convention at Chicago in August 1864 wrote a platform declaring the war a failure and demanding an immediate armistice — a ceasefire that would have left the Confederacy in possession of its territory while commissioners negotiated.
  • The party then nominated George B. McClellan, the general Lincoln had removed from command in 1862, who accepted the nomination while distancing himself from the armistice plank.
  • Through the summer of Petersburg's trenches and the casualty lists, Lincoln privately expected defeat and prepared to cooperate with a successor he believed would lose the war at the table.

Slide 12 · Atlanta votes, and the soldiers vote

On the slide: four bullets — Lincoln reelected, November 1864; a clear popular majority, an overwhelming electoral margin; soldiers in the field voting to finish the war; the armistice refused by the voters themselves.

  • Work the timing before the result, because the result turned on six weeks.
  • Atlanta fell on September 2; Union victories in the Shenandoah Valley followed; and the northern public that had counted the Overland campaign's cost all summer could suddenly see the war being won.
  • Lincoln carried every northern state but three and won the electoral college by a margin no one had predicted in August. The soldiers' vote, cast in the field under state laws arranged for it, ran heavily for the commander in chief.
  • Teach the consequence rather than the celebration: an armistice government would have negotiated with the Confederacy as a fact. The electorate refused it, and the Confederacy's last strategy — outlasting northern will — died in November rather than April.

Slide 13 · Section D: The Union's other wartime work (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Farms and colleges chartered, and an amendment carried, while armies fought."

  • Section D widens the frame on purpose, because the war's settlement was not made only by armies.
  • With the seceded states' members absent, the wartime Congress passed in 1862 the program the South had blocked for a generation — land for settlers and land-grant colleges among it — and the records of that work sit in the activity beside the generals' letters.
  • The section's second campaign belongs to women who held no vote and no office. The Women's Loyal National League turned the right of petition into an engine for a constitutional amendment, and the House answered on January 31, 1865.
  • That passage is named here as a wartime event, and no further, by the unit's design. The Homestead Act and the Morrill Land-Grant Act are the two statutes the reading names, and the activity prints the Homestead text itself at Station 4.

Slide 14 · Homesteads and colleges, 1862

On the slide: four bullets — the Homestead Act, 160 acres to a settler; five years of farming, and the land was yours; the Morrill Act, land to fund state colleges; passed by a Congress the South had left.

  • Read the statute's terms at Station 4 of the activity, where section 1 of the Homestead Act is printed from the audited bank record.
  • Any head of a family or adult citizen — or an immigrant who had declared an intention to become one — who had never borne arms against the United States could enter a quarter section of public land.
  • State the record's own shadow plainly: the public land the act gave away was Native land, taken through treaties and war — a point the bank record's headnote makes and the lesson keeps.
  • The Morrill Land-Grant Act of the same summer granted federal land to fund colleges of agriculture and the mechanical arts. Together the two acts chartered the farms and the universities of the postwar country while the war for the Union was still undecided.

Slide 15 · The petition campaign, and January 31, 1865

On the slide: four bullets — the Women's Loyal National League, 1863; petitions for an amendment abolishing slavery; some 400,000 signatures, by the usual count; the House passing the Thirteenth Amendment, January 31, 1865.

  • Teach the boundary the unit draws around the amendment. The League, founded by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony, made the petition the weapon of women who could neither vote nor fight, and its address is Station 3 of the activity.
  • The amendment's passage is taught here as a wartime event — one date inside the war's last winter. Its ratification fight, and the settlement built on it after the war, belong to the next unit, and this lesson does not open them.
  • Say: "Thirteenth Amendment: the constitutional amendment, passed by Congress on January 31, 1865, and ratified that December, that abolished slavery and involuntary servitude everywhere in the United States, except as punishment for a crime, the first of the three Reconstruction amendments and the only one adopted while the war still ran."

Slide 16 · 400,000 signatures for abolition

On the slide: the big figure "400,000"; the label — "signatures for abolition, by the usual count"; the italic note — the Women's Loyal National League petition campaign, 1863 to 1865.

  • Pause on the number, stated as the campaign's usual count, because it measures a form of power the Constitution left open to the voteless.
  • The League set out to gather a million names and presented its rolls to Congress in installments, Senator Charles Sumner carrying the first great roll into the Senate in February 1864.
  • Four hundred thousand signatures represented canvassing in every loyal state, organized by women who could not cast a ballot for any member they petitioned.
  • When the House passed the Thirteenth Amendment in January 1865, members on both sides credited the petitions with stiffening the vote.
  • Students should connect the figure to Station 3, where the League's own address argues that while slavery exists anywhere there can be freedom nowhere.

Slide 17 · Section E: Appomattox (divider)

On the slide: the McLean house photograph at right; the section label and title; the caption — "The McLean house, photographed by Timothy H. O'Sullivan, April 1865."

  • Picture: the McLean house at Appomattox Court House, photographed by Timothy H. O'Sullivan in April 1865, from the Library of Congress copy.
  • Section E runs the war's last week. Wilmer McLean, in whose parlor the surrender was signed, had moved to Appomattox to escape the war after its first great battle was fought across his earlier farm at Manassas — a coincidence the record documents, which students may hear as the war's bookend, provided it is taught as coincidence and not as fate.
  • The next two slides take the retreat and then the terms, in that order, because the terms were written for an army that had been run to ground, not for one that had been talked into stopping.
  • Say: "Appomattox Court House: the Virginia village where, on April 9, 1865, Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to Grant on terms that paroled the soldiers home with their horses for the spring planting, the surrender that effectively ended the Civil War."

Slide 18 · Richmond falls, and Lee's way runs out

On the slide: four bullets — Petersburg lines breaking, April 2, 1865; Richmond evacuated and burning, entered April 3; Lee marching west, hoping for supplies; Union cavalry and infantry closing the retreat.

  • Work the week as a closing trap, day by day.
  • Grant's spring offensive finally broke the Petersburg lines on April 2, and Lee told Jefferson Davis the capital must be evacuated that night. Richmond's departing authorities set fires to warehouses and arsenals, and the fires spread through the business district before Union troops entered on April 3 and put them out.
  • Lee marched west along the Appomattox River expecting rations waiting at Amelia Court House that were not there, losing a day his army did not have.
  • Union cavalry got across his front near Appomattox Court House while infantry closed behind him. On the morning of April 9 Lee's last attack failed to break the cavalry line, and infantry stood behind it. He sent word to Grant that he would meet him.

Slide 19 · The terms in the McLean parlor, April 9

On the slide: the McLean house photograph at right; four bullets — soldiers paroled home, not imprisoned; officers keeping their sidearms; horses taken home for the spring planting; Grant stopping the celebrating salutes.

  • Teach the terms as Lincoln's policy executed, not as improvisation.
  • Lincoln had instructed his commanders at City Point in March to let the Confederates down easy, and Grant's terms did exactly that: no treason trials implied, no prison camps, and an army sent home to plant. Grant also ordered rations issued to Lee's starving men from Union stores.
  • When Union guns began a victory salute, Grant stopped it, holding that the Confederates were fellow countrymen again and there would be no rejoicing over a fallen foe.
  • The other Confederate armies surrendered in the weeks that followed, on the Appomattox model.

Slide 20 · Section F: The verdict of the Second Inaugural (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "March 4, 1865: slavery the offense, and the war its woe."

  • Section F slows down on a speech of some seven hundred words, because the speech is the war's own verdict on the unit's essential question, and the activity prints it in full as its first station.
  • Lincoln delivered it on March 4, 1865, at his second inauguration, with the war visibly ending and its cost beyond any figure his audience needed to be given.
  • He used the occasion — the most ceremonial a president commands — not to celebrate the coming victory but to name the war's cause and apportion its guilt, and he apportioned it to his own section as well as to the enemy.
  • The next slide reads its argument in three moves.
  • Say: "Second Inaugural Address: Lincoln's address of March 4, 1865, which named American slavery as the offense and the war as its woe, read the conflict as judgment on both sections, and closed by calling the country to bind up its wounds, delivered five weeks before his assassination."

Slide 21 · The offense, the woe, the obligation

On the slide: the Lincoln portrait at right; four bullets — one eighth of the population were colored slaves; "somehow the cause of the war"; both reading the same Bible, neither prayer answered fully; "with malice toward none, with charity for all."

  • Picture: Lincoln, photographed by Alexander Gardner on February 5, 1865, a month before the address — one month before the Second Inaugural Address, as the SOURCES record dates the sitting.
  • Read the three moves in order. First the cause, stated as common knowledge: the slave interest was somehow the cause of the war.
  • Second the judgment: if slavery is an offense that must needs come, the war is the woe due for it, charged to North and South together.
  • Third the obligation, which the closing sentence lays out as work — binding wounds and caring for the soldier, his widow, and his orphan.

Slide 22 · Section G: April 1865, and what stood open (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "The assassination, and the claims the war left unanswered."

  • Section G closes the unit in the month the war ended, and it closes deliberately on what the ending did not do.
  • On April 14, five days after Appomattox, John Wilkes Booth shot Lincoln at Ford's Theatre, and the president died the next morning. Jefferson Davis was captured in Georgia in May, and the remaining Confederate armies surrendered by June.
  • The two great questions the war had been fought over stood settled — the Union indivisible and slavery destroyed as a legal system. But a settlement on paper is not a settlement in a life, and this section measures the distance between the two in one letter written by an enslaved woman in Maryland, which students read in full at Station 2 of the activity.

Slide 23 · The president killed, and Annie Davis's question

On the slide: four bullets — Booth at Ford's Theatre, April 14, 1865; Lincoln dead on the morning of April 15; Annie Davis, August 1864, "if we are free"; Maryland freeing her by its own constitution, November 1864.

  • Tell the assassination plainly and briefly, as the record gives it: a famous actor and Confederate sympathizer shot the president in his box at the theatre, and Lincoln died across the street the next morning, the war's last famous casualty.
  • Then set beside it the letter that measures the settlement's edge. Annie Davis, enslaved at Belair, Maryland, wrote to Lincoln in August 1864 that it was her desire to be free, to go and see her people on the Eastern Shore, and asked him to let her know if we are free, and what she could do.
  • The Emancipation Proclamation had not named loyal Maryland. Her state freed her that November by its own new constitution — a freedom no federal measure had given her when she asked.

Slide 24 · Settled, and left standing

On the slide: four bullets — settled, no state could leave the Union; settled, slavery destroyed as a legal system; left standing, land for the freed people; left standing, safety, and the vote.

  • Close the lesson's argument in its own two columns, because the unit test and the writing task both grade the distinction.
  • Settled beyond reopening: the Union was perpetual in fact as Lincoln had argued it was in law, and slavery as a system of property in human beings was destroyed — its destruction written into the Constitution by the amendment Congress passed in January 1865.
  • Left standing in April 1865, exactly where the war left them: whether the freed people would hold land of their own, whether they would be safe in their homes and persons, and whether they would vote.
  • Those questions were not answered at Appomattox, in the Second Inaugural, or in any document of this unit. They are the questions the next unit opens with, and the lesson ends by naming them rather than resolving them.

Slide 28 · The ending at a glance

On the slide: the title "Pressure, vote, surrender, verdict, open claims"; the five rows — pressure (Grant and Sherman, 1864; armies fixed, resources destroyed); vote (the election and the petitions; no armistice, an amendment carried); surrender (Appomattox, April 9, 1865; paroles, sidearms, horses for planting); verdict (the Second Inaugural; slavery the offense, the war its woe); open (April 1865; land, safety, and the vote unanswered).

  • This slide mirrors the guided notes table, The Ending at a Glance, and students should complete that table now — from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • The five rows compress the hour and close the unit. Pressure: Grant fixed the Confederate armies while Sherman destroyed the resources behind them. Vote: the electorate refused an armistice in 1864, and the petition campaign carried an amendment through the House.
  • Surrender: Lee's army went home on parole at Appomattox. Verdict: Lincoln named slavery the offense and the war its woe, charged to both sections. Open: the freed people's land, safety, and vote stood unanswered.
  • The guided notes table uses these same five rows, so the chart in the notebooks and the summary on screen cannot drift apart.
