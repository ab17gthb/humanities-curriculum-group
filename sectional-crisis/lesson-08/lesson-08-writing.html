Writing Task: The Verdict of the Second Inaugural

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words, judging one claim: the Second Inaugural holds that the war was slavery's punishment, a woe given to North and South for a shared offense. Judge whether the lesson's record supports Lincoln's verdict, or whether the war is better explained as a political failure's last resort. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your judgment.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading, stations, and documents. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank wording, except where a bank item quotes a document.

Evidence Bank

1. Grant, made general in chief in March 1864, made the Confederate armies the objective and moved every Union army at once. The Overland campaign cost, by the usual estimates, some sixty five thousand Union casualties in six weeks, and ended in the nine month siege of Petersburg. (Lesson reading, section A.)

2. Sherman to Mayor Calhoun, September 12, 1864: "War is cruelty, and you cannot refine it... But you cannot have peace and a division of our country." The only peace he offers Atlanta is to stop the war by admitting it began in error. (Activity, Station 5, Wikisource transcription of the letter.)

3. The March to the Sea, November and December 1864: some sixty two thousand soldiers lived off the country and destroyed railroads, mills, and stores in a corridor some sixty miles wide; Savannah was presented to Lincoln as a Christmas gift. (Lesson reading, section B.)

4. The Democratic platform of 1864 declared the war a failure and demanded an armistice. After Atlanta fell, Lincoln was reelected in November, with Union soldiers voting heavily to finish the war. An armistice was on the ballot and was rejected. (Lesson reading, section C.)

5. Lincoln, Second Inaugural, March 4, 1865: one eighth of the whole population were colored slaves, and "all knew that this interest was somehow the cause of the war"; if American slavery is an offense that must needs come, the war is "the woe due to those by whom the offense came," given to both North and South. (Activity, Station 1, audited TPSP record.)

6. Annie Davis, enslaved in Belair, Maryland, wrote to Lincoln on August 25, 1864, to ask if she was free, because the Emancipation Proclamation had not named her loyal state; Maryland freed her by its own constitution that November. On April 9, 1865, Grant paroled Lee's army home, and on April 14 Booth shot Lincoln. (Activity, Station 2, audited TPSP record; lesson reading, sections E and G.)

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment argued from the record, not from the address alone. Lincoln's verdict must be tested against the lesson's military and political evidence before it is accepted or rejected. (Blueprint 15)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 carries the war's mechanism, coordinated pressure and its cost. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 2 carries Sherman's explanation, error and pride answered by obedience to the laws. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 3 carries total war as the verdict's evidence or its problem, depending on the essay's judgment. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 4 carries the political test, an armistice offered and rejected at the ballot box in 1864. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 5 carries Lincoln's verdict itself, slavery named as cause and offense. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 6 carries the settlement's edge, Davis unreached by proclamation and the war closing in surrender terms and assassination. (Blueprint 15)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the judgment. A punishment essay should show why the war fell on both sections and why its scale answers to slavery's two hundred and fifty years, in Lincoln's arithmetic. A political failure essay should show the chain of decisions, compromise repealed, secession chosen, armistice refused, that made the war the last resort of a politics that could no longer hold the question.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest claim of the other side. A punishment answer must answer Davis, whose letter shows the woe falling on the enslaved as well as the enslavers. A political failure answer must answer Lincoln's naming of slavery as the cause both sections knew, and Sherman's refusal of any peace with division.

Model opening for teacher reference. Lincoln's verdict survives the record that tests it hardest. The war was not an accident of failed politics alone, because every political failure in the unit ran through one interest: the platform fights, the secession declarations, and Sherman's own letter all return to slavery and the division made to protect it. Its punishment fell, as the address says, on both sections, the North paying in the Overland campaign's casualty lists for a Union that had compromised with slavery for seventy years. The verdict's limit is written by Annie Davis, who bore the woe without sharing the offense, and a full model in this line closes on her letter and on the claims April 1865 left standing.

Common weak answers. Lincoln was right because he was president (an appeal to authority) ignores the record the prompt requires. The war was only about states' rights (a single cause the documents contradict) skips the Second Inaugural's naming of slavery and the secession record of the earlier lessons. An essay with no answer to the other side's strongest claim earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a weighing, not a verdict alone.
