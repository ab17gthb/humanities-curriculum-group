Guided Notes: Total War and Appomattox

Lesson question: How did the war end, and what did its ending settle?

Unit question: How did compromise over slavery finally fail, and what did the war it caused settle about slavery and the Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Ulysses S. Grant: the Ohio born general who won at Fort Donelson, Shiloh, Vicksburg, and Chattanooga, was made general in chief in March 1864, and directed the coordinated campaigns that wore the Confederacy down, accepting Lee's surrender at Appomattox and later serving two terms as president

total war: the strategy of making war on the enemy's resources and will as well as its armies, destroying railroads, crops, mills, and stores so that the society sustaining the armies felt the war in its households, carried to its fullest by Sherman in Georgia and the Carolinas

William Tecumseh Sherman: the Ohio general who took Atlanta in September 1864, cut loose from his supply lines to march to the sea, and carried total war through Georgia and the Carolinas, writing to Atlanta's mayor that war is cruelty and cannot be refined

March to the Sea: Sherman's campaign of November and December 1864, in which some sixty two thousand Union soldiers marched from Atlanta to Savannah living off the country and destroying the railroads, mills, and stores in a corridor sixty miles wide, presenting Savannah to Lincoln as a Christmas gift

Thirteenth Amendment: the constitutional amendment, passed by Congress on January 31, 1865, and ratified that December, that abolished slavery and involuntary servitude everywhere in the United States, except as punishment for a crime, the first of the three Reconstruction amendments and the only one adopted while the war still ran

Appomattox Court House: the Virginia village where, on April 9, 1865, Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to Grant on terms that paroled the soldiers home with their horses for the spring planting, the surrender that effectively ended the Civil War

Second Inaugural Address: Lincoln's address of March 4, 1865, which named American slavery as the offense and the war as its woe, read the conflict as judgment on both sections, and closed by calling the country to bind up its wounds, delivered five weeks before his assassination

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher

Teach this lesson last in the Sectional Crisis and Civil War unit, after Lesson 7 on emancipation and the transforming war of 1863, and before the review day and the unit test. It closes the unit in April 1865 and stops there on purpose. The Thirteenth Amendment's passage by the House on January 31, 1865, is named only as a wartime event; presidential Reconstruction, the Freedmen's Bureau, and the later amendments belong to the next unit and are not taught here. It pairs with the lesson activity, The Ending File, whose five stations print the Second Inaugural Address in full, Annie Davis's letter, the Women's Loyal National League address, the Homestead Act's first section, and Sherman's letter to the mayor of Atlanta, and with the writing task, The Verdict of the Second Inaugural. Students need Lesson 6 on the war's first seventeen months and Lesson 7 on the Emancipation Proclamation, Black soldiers, Gettysburg, and Vicksburg. Grant's coordinated campaigns, Sherman's march, and the Appomattox terms are new here. Students will learn how the war ended in coordinated pressure and a negotiated surrender, what the ending settled about the Union and slavery, and what it left unanswered for the freed people. The lesson matches Common Core literacy standards for reading and writing about primary sources (RH.1, RH.2, RH.6, and RH.8 in the grades 9 to 10 and 11 to 12 bands) and the C3 Framework indicators for evaluating sources and communicating conclusions (D2.His.13 and D4.2). Timing is one class period of about fifty minutes for the reading and guided notes, with the activity in a second period. Materials are the printed reading, guided notes, and activity, and the slide deck with its printed teacher notes.

Summary table. Grant and Sherman, 1864 and 1865: Grant made the Confederate armies the objective and moved every Union army at once, fought through the Overland campaign to the Petersburg siege, while Sherman took Atlanta, wrote that war is cruelty and cannot be refined, and marched to the sea destroying the resources in a sixty mile corridor. Together they settled that the Confederacy could not outlast the Union, and left its households carrying the war's cost. The vote and the Congress: the Democrats demanded an armistice in 1864, Atlanta's fall carried Lincoln to reelection, and Congress chartered homesteads and land grant colleges while the women's petition campaign drove the Thirteenth Amendment through the House on January 31, 1865. Appomattox and the Second Inaugural: Richmond fell on April 3, Lee surrendered on April 9 on terms that paroled his men home with their horses, and Lincoln's address of March 4 had already named slavery as the war's cause and the war as its woe. April 1865, and what stood open: Booth killed Lincoln on April 14 and 15, Davis was captured in May, and the freed people's claims to land, safety, and the vote stood exactly where the war left them.

Exit ticket 1. Grant made the Confederacy's armies, not its cities, the objective. At Appomattox his soldiers were paroled home rather than imprisoned (also accept officers keeping their sidearms, or men taking their horses home for the spring planting).

Exit ticket 2. The House of Representatives passed the Thirteenth Amendment on January 31, 1865, and sent it to the states. The reading names the passage as a wartime event only.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer naming both settlements and one open claim. The war settled that no state could leave the Union and that slavery as a legal system was destroyed. Left unanswered in April 1865 stood the freed people's claims to land, to safety, and to the vote (any one named earns the point).
