Writing Task: What Did Each Side Think Would Win the War?

The prompt. In April 1861 both governments expected a short war, and each held a theory of how it would be won. Answer this question in a developed essay of about 300 words: What did each side think would win the war, and what did the first seventeen months teach them? Argue from the documents in the First War File. A strong answer states both theories with the arithmetic under the Union's, follows the evidence of 1861 and 1862 through at least two campaigns or records, and ends by judging which theory the evidence supports, including what Antietam changed. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each one supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson documents. Write the history in your own sentences. Quotations from the records belong inside quotation marks, spelled as the records spell them.

Evidence Bank

1. The Anaconda Plan, in summary: in May 1861 General Winfield Scott proposed a naval blockade of the southern ports and a column of some eighty thousand men with gunboats down the Mississippi River, expecting the enclosed Confederacy to collapse under the pressure. Newspapers named the plan for the snake that kills by constriction. Lincoln adopted its parts in substance, the blockade of April 1861 and the river campaigns of 1862.

2. The population map, audited bank record, U.S. Coast Survey, 1861: "A map of the Southern states shaded by county to show the percentage of enslaved people in the population, based on the 1860 census. Darker shading marks counties where enslaved people were the largest share of the population, especially along the Mississippi River and across the Black Belt of Alabama, Mississippi, and Georgia." The Union held about 22 million people and the Confederacy about 9 million, more than a third of them enslaved.

3. Ballou, Letter to Sarah Ballou, July 14, 1861, audited bank record: "I have no misgivings about, or lack of confidence in, the cause in which I am engaged, and my courage does not halt or falter." And: "I am willing, perfectly willing, to lay down all my joys in this life, to help maintain this Government, and to pay that debt." Ballou was killed at First Bull Run a week later. The record notes that the letter's authenticity is disputed.

4. Lincoln, Proclamation on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus, September 24, 1862, audited bank record: "That the writ of habeas corpus is suspended in respect to all persons arrested, or who are now, or hereafter during the rebellion shall be, imprisoned in any fort, camp, arsenal, military prison, or other place of confinement by any military authority, or by the sentence of any court-martial or military commission." The same order made persons discouraging enlistments or resisting the draft subject to martial law.

5. The Antietam record, audited bank record, September 1862, its photograph described and not reproduced: "A photograph of dead Confederate soldiers lying piled together in a sunken ditch at Antietam, Maryland. The bodies are clustered in the ditch bed among broken fence rails and scattered debris." Mathew Brady exhibited Gardner's photographs in New York in October 1862 as The Dead of Antietam. The battle of September 17, 1862, cost some 22,700 casualties by the usual reckoning and ended Lee's invasion of the North.

6. First Bull Run and the campaigns of 1862, in summary: on July 21, 1861, before excursion crowds from Washington, the Confederate army won at Manassas and the Union army dissolved into a rout, ending the ninety day war. In 1862 Lee drove McClellan from Richmond in the Seven Days and won Second Bull Run, while Grant took Forts Henry and Donelson and Farragut took New Orleans, and Shiloh cost some twenty four thousand casualties by the usual estimates.

Write the Essay

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the prompt, stating the Union theory of strangulation on superior numbers and the Confederate theory of endurance rewarded by recognition, rather than a narrative of battles without theories. (Blueprint 11)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Item 1 establishes the Union theory and its adoption in substance. (Blueprint 11) Item 2 establishes the arithmetic and the geography of slavery the theory targeted. (Blueprint 11) Item 6 establishes the short war's death at Bull Run on July 21, 1861, and the divided record of 1862, Lee's eastern victories against the western river and port campaign and Shiloh's cost. (Blueprint 11, 12) Item 4 establishes the home front the long war required, restraint suspended by the order of September 24, 1862. (Blueprint 12) Item 5 establishes cost at Antietam and the exhibition that carried it home, with the photograph described and not reproduced, as the bank record presents it. (Blueprint 12) Item 3 establishes devotion, with its authenticity caveat credited when a student names it. (Blueprint 11)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive distinction is between a battle list and a theory test: an essay that recounts who won each fight, without asking what each result did to blockade, river, recognition, and will, has used half the file.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest evidence for the other theory. For the endurance claim, Lee's victories of 1862 and Antietam's cost: the victories were won on Confederate ground and did not open a port, raise the blockade, or bring a European flag in, while the invasion meant to win recognition ended on September 17, 1862. For a claim that the Union theory had already won by 1862, the habeas record itself is the correction: a government suspending the writ nationwide in September 1862 was administering a war it had not finished.

Model opening for teacher reference. Each side entered the war with a theory fitted to its resources. The Union, holding about 22 million people against about 9 million, adopted Scott's theory in substance: blockade the coast, take the Mississippi, and let constriction do what a single battle could not. The Confederacy, holding cotton and its own ground, chose endurance: defend, outlast northern patience, and let Britain's need for cotton bring recognition. A full model continues in this pattern, kills the short war at Bull Run on July 21, 1861, weighs Lee's eastern victories against New Orleans and the Tennessee forts, reads the habeas order of September 24, 1862, as the long war's home front price, answers the endurance claim directly, and closes on Antietam, where the invasion ended on September 17, 1862, and Lincoln found the standing, five days later, to widen the war's purpose toward emancipation.

Common weak answers. Treating McClellan's caution as the Union's theory confuses a commander with a strategy: the theory was Scott's, and it advanced in the west while McClellan hesitated in the east. Treating the Antietam photographs as if students had seen them reproduced misses the record's own presentation: the lesson studies the audited description and the exhibition's effect, and an essay should write about the record that way. Quoting Ballou without his date or his death a week later at Bull Run spends the letter's force, and ignoring the record's authenticity note, when the essay leans on the letter, weakens its sourcing.
