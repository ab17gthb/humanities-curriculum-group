The War Begins: Sumter to Antietam

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Fort and the Answer

On April 12, 1861, Confederate batteries in Charleston harbor opened fire on Fort Sumter, the federal fort whose relief had become the crisis of Abraham Lincoln's first month in office. The garrison held out for thirty four hours and surrendered on April 14. Lincoln answered the next day by calling for seventy five thousand volunteers to serve ninety days. Virginia seceded, and Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina followed, bringing the Confederacy to eleven states. Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri stayed in the Union, held by occupation and maneuver, and in Kentucky by a neutrality both sides courted until it broke.

B. Two Peoples, Two Theories of Victory

The war ran on arithmetic neither side could change. The Union held about 22 million people. The Confederacy held about 9 million, and more than a third of them were enslaved people whose labor sustained the southern war effort. The North held most of the country's factories, railroads, and shipping. The South held cotton, a long coastline, and its own ground to defend. General Winfield Scott, the Union's aged commanding general, proposed the Anaconda Plan, a naval blockade of the southern coast and a drive down the Mississippi River to strangle the Confederacy's trade and split it in two. The Confederacy chose the opposite theory. It would defend, outlast northern patience, and win European recognition on the strength of King Cotton, the belief that Britain's mills needed southern cotton too much to let the South fall, a leverage that collapsed as Britain drew cotton from India and Egypt.

C. First Bull Run and the Army That Had to Be Built

The first great battle tested both theories and flattered neither. On July 21, 1861, the armies met near Manassas, Virginia, along Bull Run, while excursion crowds from Washington rode out with picnic baskets to watch the war end in an afternoon. The Confederate army won the field, the Union army dissolved into a rout, and the watchers fled with the soldiers. First Bull Run ended the hope of a ninety day war. Both governments now had to build real armies. Lincoln gave the eastern army to George B. McClellan, a brilliant organizer who built the Army of the Potomac into the country's largest trained force, and then, again and again, declined to spend it.

D. The War Widens: East and West in 1862

In the spring of 1862 McClellan crept toward Richmond by the Virginia peninsula. In the Seven Days battles of late June and early July, Robert E. Lee, newly in command in Virginia, drove him away from the capital, and in August Lee won again at Second Bull Run. The west told a different story. Ulysses S. Grant took Forts Henry and Donelson in Tennessee in February, and Farragut's fleet seized New Orleans, the Confederacy's largest city and port, in April. At Shiloh, in Tennessee, two days in April cost some twenty four thousand casualties by the usual estimates, a number no American battle had approached. In March the ironclads Monitor and Virginia fought to a draw at Hampton Roads and ended the wooden warship's century in a morning.

E. The Liberties the War Suspended

The war reached into the Constitution as well as onto battlefields. Lincoln suspended the writ of habeas corpus along the lines of military operations, so men arrested by the army could be held without a hearing before a judge. When Chief Justice Roger B. Taney ruled in the Merryman case in 1861 that only Congress could suspend the writ, Lincoln did not comply. Federal troops arrested Maryland legislators that September to keep Maryland in the Union. On September 24, 1862, facing resistance to the new militia draft, Lincoln proclaimed the suspension nationwide and made rebels, draft resisters, and anyone guilty of a disloyal practice subject to martial law. The government claimed the measures saved the Union. Its critics answered that a Union saved by suspending its own charter was being changed by the saving.

F. Antietam: The Bloodiest Day

In September 1862 Lee carried the war into Maryland, hoping victory on northern soil would win European recognition. A copy of his orders, lost by a Confederate officer, reached McClellan and told him Lee's army was divided. On September 17, along Antietam Creek near Sharpsburg, the armies fought the single bloodiest day in American history, some 22,700 casualties by the usual reckoning. The battle was a tactical draw and a strategic Union victory, because the invasion ended and Lee withdrew into Virginia. Alexander Gardner photographed the unburied Confederate dead days later, and Mathew Brady exhibited the photographs in New York that October, where civilians stood before pictures of the dead themselves. This lesson teaches that record through the audited description of it rather than through the photographs, which are not reproduced here. Five days later, on September 22, Lincoln issued the Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation.

G. A Letter Home and the Lessons of Seventeen Months

Against the strategies stands one soldier's account of why he marched. Sullivan Ballou, a major in the 2nd Rhode Island Volunteers, wrote his wife Sarah on July 14, 1861, a week before First Bull Run, that he had no misgivings about the cause and was willing to lay down all his joys in this life to help maintain the government. He was killed at Bull Run, and the letter, never mailed, was found in his trunk. Seventeen months had taught both sides the same lessons. The short war was a fiction, cotton had not brought Europe in, and the blockade was closing as Scott had said. On Antietam's field Lincoln had found the ground from which slavery itself could be struck, where the next lesson begins.

Teacher Answer Key

1. The fort and the answer. Confederate batteries opened fire on Fort Sumter on April 12, 1861, and the garrison surrendered on April 14 after thirty four hours. Lincoln answered on April 15 by calling for seventy five thousand volunteers to serve ninety days. The four states that seceded after the call were Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina, bringing the Confederacy to eleven states. (Blueprint 11)

2. The arithmetic and the two theories. The Union held about 22 million people. The Confederacy held about 9 million, more than a third of them enslaved. Scott's Anaconda Plan proposed a naval blockade of the southern coast and a drive down the Mississippi River to strangle the Confederacy's trade and split it in two. The Confederacy's opposite theory was to defend, outlast northern patience, and win European recognition on the strength of King Cotton. The cotton leverage collapsed as Britain drew cotton from India and Egypt instead. (Blueprint 11)

3. First Bull Run. The battle was fought on July 21, 1861, near Manassas, Virginia, along Bull Run. Excursion crowds from Washington rode out expecting to watch the war end in an afternoon. The Confederate victory and the Union rout ended the hope of a ninety day war and taught both governments to build real armies. McClellan then built the Army of the Potomac into the largest trained force the country had ever fielded, and then declined, again and again, to spend what he had built. (Blueprint 11)

4. The liberties suspended. Lincoln suspended the writ of habeas corpus, first along the lines of military operations. Taney ruled in the Merryman case in 1861 that only Congress could suspend the writ, and Lincoln did not comply. The proclamation of September 24, 1862, suspended the writ across the country and made rebels and insurgents, their aiders and abettors, persons discouraging enlistments, draft resisters, and anyone guilty of any disloyal practice subject to martial law and trial by courts martial or military commission. (Blueprint 12)

5. The lesson question, answered. The Union thought its numbers, industry, and navy would win, applied as Scott proposed, by blockade and by splitting the Confederacy along the Mississippi. The Confederacy thought endurance would win, that defense on its own ground plus cotton's hold on Europe would exhaust northern patience and bring recognition. Seventeen months taught both sides that the short war was a fiction, that armies had to be built and replaced at a cost counted at Shiloh in tens of thousands, that cotton had not brought Europe in, and that the blockade and the river were closing as Scott had said. Antietam was fought on September 17, 1862, and five days later, on September 22, Lincoln issued the Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation. (Blueprint 11, 12)
