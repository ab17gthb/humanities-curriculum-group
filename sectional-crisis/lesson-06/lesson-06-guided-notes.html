Guided Notes: The War Begins: Sumter to Antietam

Lesson question: What did each side think would win the war, and what did the first seventeen months teach them?

Unit question: How did compromise over slavery finally fail, and what did the war it caused settle about slavery and the Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Fort Sumter: the federal fort in Charleston harbor whose provisioning became the crisis of Lincoln's first month in office, bombarded by Confederate batteries on April 12, 1861, and surrendered on April 14, the act that opened the Civil War

First Bull Run: the war's first great battle, fought near Manassas, Virginia, on July 21, 1861, before excursion crowds from Washington, whose Confederate victory and Union rout ended the hope of a short war and taught both governments to build real armies

Anaconda Plan: General Winfield Scott's strategy of 1861, to strangle the Confederacy by naval blockade of its coast and a drive down the Mississippi that would split it in two, derided as slow by a public demanding a quick blow and adopted in substance as the war became long

blockade: the naval closure of an enemy's ports to trade, proclaimed by Lincoln against the Confederacy in April 1861 and enforced by a navy built from almost nothing to some six hundred vessels, the instrument that strangled the cotton trade on which Confederate finance rested

habeas corpus: the constitutional writ by which a prisoner may demand to be brought before a judge and shown the cause of detention, which Lincoln suspended along the war's military lines from 1861 and Congress authorized in 1863, so that military arrests could proceed without civil trial

George B. McClellan: the organizer who built the Army of the Potomac and commanded it through the Peninsula campaign and Antietam, a brilliant administrator whose caution before the enemy Lincoln finally exhausted, removing him in November 1862

Antietam: the battle of September 17, 1862, near Sharpsburg, Maryland, where McClellan, holding a copy of Lee's lost orders, fought the Army of Northern Virginia to a standstill in the single bloodiest day in American history, ending the first invasion of the North and giving Lincoln ground to issue the Emancipation Proclamation

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher. This is the sixth lesson of the Sectional Crisis and Civil War unit. Teach it after Lesson 5, on the debates, John Brown, the election of 1860, and secession, and before Lesson 7, on emancipation, because Antietam's field is the ground the Emancipation Proclamation stands on. It pairs with the lesson reading, which the notes follow section by section, with the First War File activity, and with the lesson writing task. Students need prior knowledge of secession and the Confederate government from Lesson 5, and of the border states named there. The lesson matches the Common Core literacy standards for history and social studies, on citing textual evidence, determining central ideas, and writing arguments from sources, and the C3 Framework's indicators on causation, sourcing, and gathering evidence. Students will learn what Fort Sumter set in motion, the two rival theories of victory and the arithmetic under them, how First Bull Run and the campaigns of 1862 tested those theories, what the suspension of habeas corpus claimed and cost, and why Antietam, a draw on the field, changed the war. Timing: one period for the reading and notes, one for the activity. Materials: the printed lesson packet and the slide deck.

Summary table. Sumter and the call: Fort Sumter was bombarded on April 12, 1861, and surrendered on April 14, Lincoln called for seventy five thousand ninety day volunteers on April 15, and Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina seceded, while Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri stayed. The two theories: the Union, with about 22 million people against about 9 million, more than a third enslaved, adopted Scott's Anaconda Plan of blockade and a Mississippi drive in substance, while the Confederacy defended and waited on King Cotton, whose leverage collapsed as Britain turned to India and Egypt. Bull Run and McClellan: the Confederate victory of July 21, 1861, before excursion crowds ended the short war hope, and McClellan built the Army of the Potomac and declined to spend it. The war in 1862: Lee drove McClellan from Richmond in the Seven Days and won Second Bull Run, while Grant, Farragut, and Shiloh's cost, some twenty four thousand casualties by the usual estimates, showed the west moving and the price rising. Antietam: on September 17, 1862, with Lee's lost orders in hand, McClellan fought Lee to a standstill at some 22,700 casualties by the usual reckoning, the invasion ended, and on September 22 Lincoln issued the Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation.

Exit ticket 1. Scott's Anaconda Plan proposed strangling the Confederacy by a naval blockade of its coast and a drive down the Mississippi that would split it in two. The Confederacy's opposite theory was to defend on its own ground, outlast northern patience, and win European recognition on the strength of King Cotton.

Exit ticket 2. The writ was habeas corpus. Chief Justice Roger B. Taney ruled in the Merryman case in 1861 that only Congress could suspend it. Lincoln did not comply, and the suspension continued and widened, reaching the whole country in the proclamation of September 24, 1862.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that states both theories and two lessons with two dates. The strongest answers state that the Union trusted its numbers, industry, and navy applied by blockade and river, that the Confederacy trusted endurance and cotton, and that by Antietam on September 17, 1862, the short war of the ninety day call of April 1861 was disproved, cotton had failed, and the war's purpose was widening toward emancipation, announced in preliminary form on September 22, 1862.
