The Antislavery Argument at Full Voice

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Rendition Becomes a Spectacle

The Fugitive Slave Act stopped being a statute and became a street scene. In April 1851 Thomas Sims was carried through Boston in chains and put on a ship for Savannah. That October a Syracuse crowd broke William Henry, called Jerry, out of custody and sent him to Canada. In September, at Christiana in Pennsylvania, the Maryland slaveholder Edward Gorsuch came with a federal warrant and died at William Parker's door. Resisters answered with the higher law doctrine, that no statute could make a person property. In May 1854 Anthony Burns was arrested in Boston and marched to a revenue cutter between lines of federal troops while church bells tolled and buildings stood draped in black. Rendition had become a public spectacle the republic staged against itself.

B. The Novel That Answered

Harriet Beecher Stowe had lived eighteen years in Cincinnati, across the Ohio River from Kentucky, listening to the slave narratives the movement published, Josiah Henson's account in particular. In 1851 she began publishing Uncle Tom's Cabin in the National Era and in March 1852 it appeared as a book. The usual estimates, stated as estimates, put its first year sales near three hundred thousand copies in the United States. Stowe built her argument from the slave narrative's evidence and aimed it at the Christian mother's conscience. Again and again the book returns to one wound, families parted at the auction block or by a master's debts. It also shows slavery corrupting the enslaver's household as surely as the enslaved one. The reader was asked first to feel what separation did inside a home.

C. Banned, Answered, and Carried Everywhere

The South answered the book by banning it and by shelving replies. Within a few years southern writers had published a shelf of anti-Tom novels arguing that slavery was kind and the North cruel. Stowe answered her critics with evidence. In 1853 she published A Key to Uncle Tom's Cabin, a documentary volume setting out the slave narratives, newspaper reports, and cases behind her scenes. Abroad the book was translated into language after language. It is often told that President Lincoln, greeting Stowe in 1862, called her the little woman who wrote the book that made the great war. The greeting is an anecdote, printed decades later and unverifiable, and this lesson states it as a story everyone repeats, not as a fact anyone can check.

D. Douglass at Rochester

Frederick Douglass spoke at Rochester on July 5, 1852, invited to celebrate the nation's birthday. He answered that the birthday was not the slave's. The Fourth of July, he told his audience, was theirs, not his, a day that revealed to the enslaved man the gross injustice and cruelty to which he was the constant victim. Douglass audited the founding's promises against the slave's present. He turned the founders' declaration on a slaveholding nation and scorched the churches that blessed the arrangement. He said his subject needed scorching irony, not convincing argument, fire rather than light. The speech did not end in despair. Douglass closed by drawing hope from the Declaration itself, holding that its principles would yet be turned against slavery.

E. Two Texts of One Speech

Sojourner Truth spoke at a women's rights convention at Akron, Ohio, in May 1851. Born enslaved in New York and freed under that state's gradual emancipation, she traveled as a preacher for abolition and women's rights. At Akron she argued from her own labor that women deserved equal rights. The speech survives in two texts, and the difference is this lesson's source problem. Marius Robinson, the convention's secretary, printed his report in the Anti-Slavery Bugle of June 21, 1851, weeks after the speech. Twelve years later Frances Dana Gage published her own version. Gage gave the speech its famous repeated refrain and rendered Truth in a southern dialect, though Truth was born in New York and spoke Dutch as a child.

F. The Enslaved Woman's Own Book

Harriet Jacobs wrote the argument the movement's men could not make for her. Enslaved in North Carolina, she hid for seven years in a crawl space above her grandmother's storeroom, within earshot of her own children. She escaped to the North and wrote. In 1861 she published Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl under the name Linda Brent, the first slave narrative by an American woman published in the United States. Jacobs told northern women readers directly what slavery did to enslaved women and girls. Her book opened by showing a child who did not know she was enslaved learning at six that she was a piece of merchandise. The witness here is not a visitor to slavery. She is its subject, writing in her own changed name.

G. Feeling, Judgment, Witness, Action

By 1852 the antislavery argument had assembled its instruments, and each asked something different of its reader. The novel asked for feeling, slavery carried into the parlor and judged at the hearth. The oration asked for judgment, the nation's own promises read back to it by a man it had enslaved. The narrative asked for witness, a life set down in the first person, with sponsors' documents authenticating her account. The rescues asked for action, crowds putting their own bodies between a fugitive and a commissioner. Against all four, the parties still promised the question could be managed by compromise. The writers of 1851 and 1852 had already carried it into the country's households. What the parties would do with that argument is the next lesson's subject.

The crowds proved that rendition had become a public spectacle the republic staged against itself. (Blueprint 4)

3. State the South's answer, Stowe's reply, and the standing of the Lincoln greeting. The South banned the novel and answered it with a shelf of anti-Tom novels defending slavery. In 1853 Stowe published A Key to Uncle Tom's Cabin, a documentary volume setting out the narratives, reports, and cases behind her scenes. The story that Lincoln greeted her as the little woman who made the great war is an anecdote, printed decades later and unverifiable, with no contemporary record. It is stated in this lesson as a story everyone repeats, not as a fact anyone can check, and students should treat it the same way. (Blueprint 3)

4. State the two Truth texts and what the later one added. Marius Robinson, the convention's secretary, printed his report of the Akron speech in the Anti-Slavery Bugle of June 21, 1851, weeks after Truth spoke. Frances Dana Gage, who had presided at the convention, published her version twelve years later, in 1863. Gage's version added the speech's famous repeated refrain and rendered Truth in a southern dialect, though Truth was born in New York and spoke Dutch as a child. Robinson's near contemporary report is the text a historian may quote with confidence. (Blueprint 4)

5. What did the writers ask their readers to feel, to judge, and to do? Full credit answers assign each instrument its ask and support it from the reading. The novel asked for feeling: Stowe carried slavery into northern parlors and made families parted at the auction block a household argument. The oration asked for judgment: Douglass at Rochester on July 5, 1852 told his audience the Fourth was theirs, not the slave's, and read the founding's promises back against the slave's present. The narrative asked for witness: Jacobs, after seven years hidden in a crawl space above her grandmother's storeroom, published Incidents in 1861 as Linda Brent, telling what slavery did to women in her own voice. The rescues asked for action, from the Syracuse crowd to the Christiana defense. (Blueprint 3 and 4)
