The Antislavery Argument at Full Voice

Sectional Crisis and Civil War · Lesson 2 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Sectional Crisis and Civil War, Lesson 2 of 8. It follows Lesson 1 directly: the Compromise's enforcement clause put federal officers on northern streets, and this lesson follows the answer those streets produced. Between 1851 and 1861 a novelist, an orator, a preacher, and a woman writing under a changed name built an argument in four forms — the novel, the oration, the speech, and the narrative — and each form asked something different of its reader. The lesson is also the unit's source-care lesson: one of its most famous speeches survives in two texts that do not match, and one of its most repeated stories cannot be verified at all.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 1 (the Fugitive Slave Act whose renditions open this lesson, and the higher-law answer named here), Jackson and Reform Lesson 7 (the petition and print machinery now turned to narrative and fiction), and this unit's Lesson 3 (Kansas-Nebraska, testing the parties' promise that the question could still be managed). The activity, The Antislavery File, pairs Stowe's concluding remarks and Douglass's oration against the two Truth texts and Jacobs's Incidents.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: The Fugitive Slave Act's machinery from Lesson 1 (affidavit, commissioner, posse) and the higher-law doctrine's name; the slave narrative as a genre (students met Douglass's tradition in earlier units at survey level); and the Fourth of July as the republic's founding celebration — Douglass's audit assumes they feel its weight. No familiarity with Stowe's plot is assumed; the lesson teaches the novel as an instrument, not a story to summarize.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (two conflicting texts of one speech weighed as sources — the lesson's centerpiece), D2.His.9 and D2.His.10 (an argument's forms and audiences over time), D2.His.6 (an unverifiable anecdote labeled as such), D2.Civ.14 (a statute disobeyed on a stated higher-law ground); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.6, RH.9-10.9 (Robinson against Gage), WHST.9-10.1 (the three asks as an essay).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to sequence the four rendition cases of 1851–1854 and state the higher-law doctrine; to explain Stowe's construction (narrative evidence inside a domestic novel) and handle the 300,000 figure as an estimate; to sort the responses to the novel by grade of evidence, down to the Lincoln anecdote labeled unverifiable; to state Douglass's audit and his "scorching irony" reasoning; to compare Robinson's 1851 text with Gage's 1863 version and name the one a historian may quote; and to state Jacobs's standing — seven years hidden, the first slave narrative by an American woman published in the United States.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; rendition spectacle (slides 4–6) 7 minutes; the novel and its reach (slides 7–11) 11 minutes; Douglass (slides 12–14) 8 minutes; the two Truth texts (slides 15–17) 8 minutes; Jacobs (slides 18–20) 5 minutes; assembly, vocabulary, and exit (slides 21–27) 6 minutes. Teach the Truth sections slowly — they are the lesson's source problem. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes (whose summary table is The Argument at a Glance), and The Antislavery File (five stations). The notebook page charts the five forms and the two texts; the quick check is "Two Texts of One Speech"; the exit ticket has three parts — the novel defined with its dates, the two Truth texts with the quotable one named, and the lesson question in two or three sentences.

Key vocabulary

Uncle Tom's Cabin · Harriet Beecher Stowe · slave narrative · higher law · Sojourner Truth · Harriet Jacobs · Anthony Burns

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title; the standfirst — "A novel, an oration, a speech in two texts, and a narrative."

  • Picture: Frederick Douglass in the daguerreotype Samuel J. Miller made between 1847 and 1852, held by the Art Institute of Chicago.
  • Open with the writers, not the politicians: Lesson 1 closed with a compromise whose enforcement clause put federal officers on northern streets, and this lesson follows the answer those streets produced.
  • Between 1851 and 1861 a novelist, an orator, a preacher, and a woman writing under a changed name built an argument in four forms, and each form asked something different of its reader.
  • Students should leave able to name each form, state its ask, and handle the lesson's source problem — two surviving texts of Sojourner Truth's Akron speech — with care about who wrote each one down and when.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the rule; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "What did the antislavery writers of 1851 and 1852 ask their readers to feel, to judge, and to do?"
  • Today's question has three verbs, and students will need all three for the exit ticket and the writing task: feel, judge, and do.
  • Preview the mapping without giving the lesson away: the novel worked on feeling, carrying slavery into homes that had never seen it; the oration worked on judgment, reading the nation's own promises back to the people celebrating them; the narrative worked as witness; and the rescues demanded action with the body.
  • Then add the caution the lesson builds in: an argument at full voice still has to be checked. One of its most famous speeches survives in two texts that do not match, and one of its most repeated stories — Lincoln greeting Stowe — cannot be verified at all. Full voice and full care go together in this lesson.

Slide 4 · Section A: Rendition Becomes a Spectacle (divider)

On the slide: the Burns engraving at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Anthony Burns, whose 1854 rendition filled Boston with troops and mourning crowds."

  • Picture: Anthony Burns in the portrait engraving published in Boston in 1855, held by the Library of Congress. Keep Burns in view while section A is introduced, because his case is the section's proof.
  • The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 was written as procedure — an affidavit, a commissioner, a certificate. In practice it became theater.
  • Each rendition put marshals, troops, chains, and crowds into northern streets, and each crowd went home having seen slavery's officers at work in a free state.
  • Section A follows four cases in three years — Sims, Jerry, Christiana, and Burns — rising in scale until a whole city turned out in mourning dress to watch one man marched to a ship. Keep that pairing of law and theater in mind all hour.

Slide 5 · Four renditions, 1851 to 1854

On the slide: four bullets — Sims carried through Boston in chains, April 1851; Jerry rescued by a Syracuse crowd, October 1851; Christiana, Gorsuch killed at Parker's door, September 1851; Burns marched to a cutter between troops, 1854.

  • Work the four cases as a rising sequence, since students should feel the escalation the reading builds.
  • Thomas Sims was seized in Boston in April 1851, held in a courthouse wrapped in chains, and shipped back to Savannah — the city's first rendition under the new law.
  • That September, at Christiana in Pennsylvania, Edward Gorsuch arrived with a federal warrant for men he claimed as his property and was killed at William Parker's door, where Black families had gathered to defend their homes.
  • In October a Syracuse crowd broke William Henry, called Jerry, out of custody during his hearing and sent him to Canada.
  • Close with Burns, whose full case gets the next slide. The pattern to name: the law's enforcement kept producing the movement's evidence, in public, on northern ground.

Slide 6 · Burns, and the higher law answer

On the slide: the Burns engraving at right; four bullets — arrested in Boston, May 1854, tried before a commissioner; church bells tolled, buildings draped in black; resisters invoking a higher law above the statute; rendition becoming a spectacle staged against itself.

  • Give Burns his full weight, because the vocabulary list carries him.
  • Anthony Burns, escaped from Virginia, was arrested in Boston in May 1854 and tried before a federal commissioner while the courthouse stood draped in black and church bells tolled.
  • A rescue attempt failed, and on June 2 he was marched to a revenue cutter between lines of federal troops and marines, with tens of thousands watching in silence.
  • He was remanded to slavery, and Boston later raised money to purchase his freedom.
  • Teach the resisters' doctrine beside the spectacle — the higher law, named by Senator William H. Seward in 1850, held that a law of God and nature above the Constitution condemned slavery and the Fugitive Slave Act, and northern resisters used it to justify disobeying a law they held no legislature could rightfully make.
  • Say: "higher law: the doctrine Senator William H. Seward named in 1850, that a law of God and nature above the Constitution condemned slavery and the Fugitive Slave Act, the argument northern resisters used to justify disobeying a law they held no legislature could rightfully make."
  • Say: "Anthony Burns: the Virginia fugitive arrested in Boston in May 1854 and remanded to slavery after a rendition that filled the city with federal troops and mourning crowds, whose case became the Fugitive Slave Act's most famous northern spectacle."

Slide 7 · Section B: The Novel That Answered (divider)

On the slide: the Stowe portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Harriet Beecher Stowe, painted by Alanson Fisher in 1850."

  • Picture: Alanson Fisher's 1850 portrait of Harriet Beecher Stowe, held by the National Portrait Gallery.
  • Section B turns from the street to the parlor.
  • Stowe had lived eighteen years in Cincinnati, across the Ohio River from a slave state — teaching, raising a family, listening to fugitives and travelers, and reading the slave narratives the movement published, Josiah Henson's account among them.
  • When the Fugitive Slave Act made her northern neighbors into slavery's potential officers, she answered with the form she commanded: a novel, serialized in an antislavery newspaper and built, scene by scene, out of the narratives' evidence.
  • Teach the construction before the plot: this book was an argument assembled from testimony. The portrait dates from 1850, the year the Fugitive Slave Act passed into law.
  • Say: "Harriet Beecher Stowe: the Cincinnati teacher and writer, daughter of Lyman Beecher and sister of Henry Ward Beecher, who wrote Uncle Tom's Cabin from the slave narratives and from Josiah Henson's account in particular, and later published a documentary key defending her novel's facts."

Slide 8 · Uncle Tom's Cabin, 1851 and 1852

On the slide: the Stowe portrait at right; five bullets — serialized in the National Era in 1851; published as a book in March 1852; built from the slave narratives, Henson among them; its recurring wound, families parted at the block; aimed at the Christian mother's conscience.

  • Read the novel as an instrument, the way the reading presents it.
  • Its serialization in the National Era through 1851 built an audience week by week, and the book of March 1852 reached readers no pamphlet could.
  • Stowe's method did as much work as her story: she took the slave narrative's evidence — its separations, auctions, and pursuits — and set it inside a domestic novel northern families already knew how to read.
  • The recurring wound is separation: a mother sold from her child, a husband sold down the river, a family scattered by a master's debts.
  • She also aimed the argument inward at the enslaver's household, showing slavery corrupting the people who ran it as surely as it destroyed the people under it. The ask was feeling first, because feeling was the door her readers had left open.
  • Say: "Uncle Tom's Cabin: Harriet Beecher Stowe's antislavery novel, serialized in 1851 and published as a book in March 1852, the best selling American novel of its century, which carried the slave narratives' evidence into northern parlors and made slavery's cruelties a household argument."
  • Say: "slave narrative: the first person account of slavery by a formerly enslaved person, published with sponsors' authenticating documents, the genre that supplied the antislavery movement with its evidence and its witnesses from the 1840s onward."

Slide 9 · 300,000 copies in the first year

On the slide: the big figure "300,000"; the label — "copies in the first American year, by the usual estimates"; the italic note — best-selling American novel of its century; serialized 1851, a book in March 1852; estimates stated as estimates.

  • Hold this number up as an estimate, because the lesson models how to handle figures.
  • No complete sales ledger survives; the usual estimates put the first year near three hundred thousand copies in the United States, with British and translated sales far larger, many of them pirated.
  • Even discounted, the scale is the fact: no American novel had reached so many readers so fast, and it reached them in their homes, read aloud in parlors and kitchens.
  • Stowe understood what the number meant: the argument had not been voted on or preached at a crowd; it had been bought, borrowed, and wept over at the hearth — which is why the South answered it as a political act, and why this lesson counts the novel as an instrument, not a diversion.

Slide 10 · Section C: Banned, Answered, and Carried Everywhere (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "A shelf of anti-Tom novels, a documentary Key, and one story that cannot be checked."

  • Section C follows the book into the world and teaches students to sort its effects by their evidence.
  • The South's answer is documented: the novel was banned, its sellers prosecuted in places, and southern writers produced a shelf of anti-Tom novels arguing that slavery was kind and northern wage labor cruel.
  • Stowe's counter-answer is also documented: A Key to Uncle Tom's Cabin in 1853, a documentary volume setting out the narratives, cases, and newspaper reports behind her scenes.
  • The third item is different in kind: the story that Lincoln greeted Stowe as the little woman who made the great war was printed decades later, with no contemporary record — and this lesson states it as an anecdote, unverifiable, on its face.

Slide 11 · The answer, the Key, and the anecdote

On the slide: four bullets — banned across the South, answered by anti-Tom novels; 1853, A Key to Uncle Tom's Cabin defends the facts; translated into language after language; the Lincoln greeting, an anecdote printed decades later.

  • Teach the three responses as three grades of evidence.
  • The anti-Tom shelf — novels like Aunt Phillis's Cabin of 1852 — is real and readable; its existence measures the wound Stowe had opened, since a book that changed nothing needs no answer.
  • The Key of 1853 is Stowe's own method turned outward: she cited Josiah Henson's narrative for Tom's fidelity and set case beside scene, inviting critics to check her — which students should recognize as the same discipline this unit asks of them.
  • Then the anecdote. It was first printed long after 1862, it flatters everyone's sense of the book's power, and precisely because it is satisfying it must be labeled: a story everyone repeats, not a fact anyone can check.
  • Students will meet this sorting again with the Truth texts.

Slide 12 · Section D: Douglass at Rochester (divider)

On the slide: the Douglass daguerreotype at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Frederick Douglass, in Samuel J. Miller's daguerreotype."

  • Picture: Frederick Douglass in Samuel J. Miller's daguerreotype, made between 1847 and 1852.
  • Section D moves from the parlor to the platform.
  • Douglass, who had escaped slavery in Maryland in 1838 and built the movement's leading Black newspaper in Rochester, was invited by the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society to give the city's Independence Day address.
  • He spoke on July 5, 1852, in Corinthian Hall, before an audience that had celebrated the Fourth the day before.
  • What he did with their celebration is the section's subject: he accepted their premises, praised their founders' principles, and then audited the republic's practice against them in the hearing of the people who claimed to own both.
  • By 1852 he had also broken with the Garrisonian abolitionists over the Constitution — a fight this lesson names and does not teach.

Slide 13 · What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?

On the slide: the Douglass daguerreotype at right; four bullets — Rochester, July 5, 1852; the Fourth yours, not mine, you rejoice, I must mourn; your celebration a sham, your liberty hollow mockery; the founding's promises audited against the slave's present.

  • Set the oration's opening move plainly.
  • Douglass divides the holiday before he judges it: this Fourth of July is yours, not mine, and to the slave it reveals, more than all other days in the year, the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim.
  • From there the speech reads the celebration item by item from the slave's side — its boasted liberty an unholy license, its prayers and hymns and thanksgivings a thin veil over crimes that would disgrace a nation of savages.
  • Note the discipline in the attack: Douglass does not reject the founding. He praises the signers as statesmen and patriots and claims their principles as the slave's inheritance — which is what makes the audit unbearable for hearers who agreed with every premise he started from.

Slide 14 · Scorching irony, not convincing argument

On the slide: four bullets — not light, but fire; not the shower, but thunder; the nation's conscience must be roused; its hypocrisy must be exposed; the speech closes in hope, on the Declaration.

  • Douglass tells his audience what instrument he is using and why, and students should catch the reasoning.
  • At a time like this, he says, scorching irony, not convincing argument, is needed; the nation needs not the gentle shower but thunder — its feeling quickened, its conscience roused, its hypocrisy exposed.
  • Argument assumes missing information. Douglass judged that his hearers lacked nothing but the will to apply what they knew, so he aimed at conscience rather than information.
  • Then mark the ending, because students often miss it: the speech closes in hope. Douglass draws encouragement from the Declaration's principles and from the antislavery movement's rise, holding that the same ideals his audience celebrated would yet be turned against slavery itself. Judgment first, then hope, in that order.

Slide 15 · Section E: Two Texts of One Speech (divider)

On the slide: the Truth photograph at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Sojourner Truth, photographed about 1870."

  • Picture: Sojourner Truth in the photograph of about 1870 that she sold at her lectures to support her work.
  • Section E is the lesson's deliberate source problem, and it should be taught slowly.
  • Truth spoke at the women's rights convention at Akron, Ohio, in May 1851, arguing from her own enslaved labor that women deserved equal rights.
  • No recording and no manuscript in her hand exist, because Truth could not write. What exists are texts made by other hands: a secretary's report printed within weeks, and a presiding officer's recollection published twelve years later.
  • Everything students think they know about this speech depends on which text they are reading, and the section's two slides take them in order.
  • Say: "Sojourner Truth: the New York woman born enslaved as Isabella Baumfree, freed under that state's gradual emancipation, who renamed herself in 1843 and traveled as an itinerant preacher for abolition and women's rights, speaking at Akron in 1851."

Slide 16 · Robinson's text, printed June 21, 1851

On the slide: the Truth photograph at right; four bullets — Marius Robinson, the convention's secretary; printed in the Anti-Slavery Bugle within weeks; the pint and the quart, let her pint be filled full; Eve, Lazarus, and woman setting the world right.

  • Read Robinson's report as the near-contemporary record it is.
  • Robinson edited the Anti-Slavery Bugle and served as the convention's secretary, and his account appeared on June 21, 1851, weeks after the speech.
  • In it Truth argues from her body: she has plowed and reaped and husked and chopped and mowed, can carry and eat as much as any man, and answers the intellect objection with the pint and the quart — that a woman's smaller measure should still be filled full.
  • She argues from the Bible her hearers cited against her: Eve turned the world upside down, so let woman set it right side up again, and Jesus never spurned woman from him.
  • There is no repeated refrain in this text, and its plainness is part of its credibility. This is the text a historian may quote with confidence.

Slide 17 · Gage's version, published in 1863

On the slide: four bullets — Frances Dana Gage, who had presided at Akron; adds the repeated refrain the world remembers; renders Truth in a southern dialect; Truth born in New York, speaking Dutch as a child.

  • Now set Gage beside Robinson, document against document, as the activity's Stations 3 and 4 require.
  • Frances Dana Gage, who had presided over the Akron convention, published her recollection in 1863, twelve years after the speech.
  • Her version is more dramatic, and it gave the speech its famous repeated refrain and its southern dialect. Both additions fail the biographical test: Truth was born enslaved in New York as Isabella Baumfree, grew up speaking Dutch, and never lived in the South.
  • Gage's text is not worthless; it is evidence of how the speech was remembered and remade, and its refrain has inspired generations. But fame is not provenance.
  • Teach the verdict directly: quote Robinson for what Truth said, and cite Gage, when cited at all, as Gage's 1863 version.

Slide 18 · Section F: The Enslaved Woman's Own Book (divider)

On the slide: the Jacobs photograph at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Harriet Jacobs, in the Gilbert Studios photograph of 1894."

  • Picture: Harriet Jacobs in the Gilbert Studios photograph of 1894, the only known photograph of her, taken in her eighties.
  • Section F closes the sequence of forms with the witness who paid most for her testimony.
  • Jacobs was enslaved in Edenton, North Carolina. To resist her enslaver's pursuit she hid for seven years in a crawl space above her grandmother's storeroom — nine feet by seven and three feet high at its peak — drilling small holes to watch her children play below, unable to speak to them.
  • She escaped to the North in 1842, worked as a nursemaid, and spent years writing the book this section reads, published in 1861 as the war began.
  • The photograph is dated wherever it appears in this lesson, following the unit's practice with late portraits.

Slide 19 · Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl, 1861

On the slide: the Jacobs photograph at right; four bullets — published under the name Linda Brent; the first slave narrative by an American woman published in the United States; a child learning at six that she is merchandise; what slavery did to women, in the subject's own voice.

  • Open the narrative where Jacobs opens it, with the discovery.
  • She was born a slave and did not know it until six years of happy childhood had passed; at her mother's death she learned by the talk around her that she was a slave, and that she had all along been a piece of merchandise, trusted to her parents for safe keeping and liable to be demanded back at any moment.
  • Her father, a skilled carpenter, had several times offered his earnings to purchase his children and never succeeded.
  • Teach the book's standing: it is the first slave narrative by an American woman published in the United States, written for northern women readers in particular, and it makes the argument about slavery and womanhood that the movement's male speakers could not make for her — because she alone could testify to it.
  • Say: "Harriet Jacobs: the North Carolina woman who escaped enslavement after seven years hiding in a crawl space above her grandmother's storeroom, and published Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl in 1861 under the name Linda Brent, the first slave narrative by an American woman published in the United States."

Slide 20 · Seven years hidden

On the slide: the big figure "7"; the label — "years hidden in a crawl space above a storeroom"; the italic note — Harriet Jacobs, within earshot of her children; escaped north in 1842; published her narrative in 1861.

  • Spend the silence this number earns.
  • Seven years is most of a childhood: Jacobs's son and daughter grew from small children toward adolescence below a ceiling their mother watched through a bored hole, and she could not speak to them, cough freely, or stand upright in her hiding place.
  • She chose the space over the alternatives her enslaver offered, and her choice is the narrative's credential: no reader could call her testimony secondhand.
  • When the book appeared in 1861, under the name Linda Brent and with an abolitionist editor's sponsorship, it carried that credential into northern homes as the war began.
  • Students should hold the number beside the novel's sales figure from section B: Stowe reached the most readers, and Jacobs had paid the most to testify. The writing task asks them to weigh reach against witness.

Slide 21 · Section G: Feeling, Judgment, Witness, Action (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Four instruments, assembled by 1852, and the parties still promising to manage the question."

  • Section G assembles the lesson, and students should be able to run the four instruments in order without their notes by its end.
  • The novel asked for feeling: slavery carried into the parlor and judged at the hearth, by a writer who built fiction from testimony and defended her facts in a documentary key.
  • The oration asked for judgment: the nation's promises read back to it by a man it had enslaved.
  • The narrative asked for witness: a life set down in the first person, authenticated by sponsors because polite readers doubted the teller. The rescues asked for action: crowds and committees on northern streets.
  • Over all four, the parties still promised that the slavery question could be managed by compromise. The next lesson tests that promise in Kansas.

Slide 22 · What each form asks

On the slide: four bullets — the novel, feel what separation does inside a home; the oration, judge the celebration against the chains; the narrative, witness a life in the first person; the rescue, act, on the ground of a higher law.

  • Land the lesson in this four-line balance, and make students copy it into the summary table.
  • Each form also carries its own standard of proof, which is the lesson's hidden curriculum.
  • The novel proved itself by documentation — Stowe's Key answering charge with citation. The oration proved itself by the audience's own premises — the Declaration turned against its celebrants. The narrative proved itself by sponsorship and detail — a named woman writing under a changed name for safety.
  • The speech at Akron proves the rule by its exception: when the recorder is late and inventive, confidence falls.
  • Feeling, judgment, and witness each ask for trust, and this lesson has taught students how to grant it carefully, text by text, rather than all at once.

Slide 23 · The Argument at a Glance

On the slide: the title "What each instrument asks of its reader"; the four rows — the novel (Uncle Tom's Cabin, 1851 and 1852; feel: families parted, households corrupted); the oration (Douglass at Rochester, July 5, 1852; judge: the Fourth audited from the slave's side); the narrative (Jacobs, Incidents, 1861; witness: a life told by its subject); the speech (Truth at Akron, two texts; act, and ask who wrote the words down).

  • This slide mirrors the summary table in the guided notes, The Argument at a Glance, and students should complete that table now — from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • The four rows are the lesson in order: the novel, Stowe's book of 1852, built from the narratives and aimed at the mother's conscience, asking for feeling; the oration, Douglass at Rochester, turning the Fourth against its celebrants, asking for judgment; the narrative, Jacobs in 1861, after seven years in hiding, asking for witness.

  • Grade the table generously on wording and strictly on the asks: feeling, judgment, witness, and action are the lesson's vocabulary of purpose.

Slide 26 · The argument assembled

On the slide: four rows — Felt (Stowe carried slavery into the parlor and made it a household argument); Judged (Douglass read the founding's promises back to their celebrants); Witnessed (Jacobs set down a life the movement's men could not testify to); Checked (Robinson against Gage, an anecdote labeled an anecdote).

  • Use this slide to close the argument before the work begins, and make students say the four rows aloud in order, because the sequence is the lesson.
  • Felt: a novelist in Cincinnati built slavery's evidence into a domestic novel, and the country bought it by the hundreds of thousands.
  • Judged: an orator in Rochester refused the role of grateful guest and audited the nation's birthday from the slave's side of it.
  • Witnessed: a woman who had hidden seven years in a crawl space published her own account in her own changed name.
  • Checked: the same lesson that amplifies these voices refuses to repeat a presidential anecdote as fact and refuses a famous refrain as a certain quotation. Full voice and full care are the same standard, applied to friends of the cause as strictly as to its enemies.

Slide 27 · Now your turn: answer the question
