Writing Task: Feeling, Judgment, Witness

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: What did the antislavery writers of 1851 and 1852 ask their readers to feel, to judge, and to do? A strong answer assigns each form of the argument its ask, the novel, the oration, and the first person narrative, supports each assignment from the bank, weighs which form the documents show reaching furthest by 1861, and handles the two Truth texts with care about who wrote each one down and when. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The Fugitive Slave Act produced public renditions: Thomas Sims shipped from Boston in chains in April 1851, William Henry rescued at Syracuse that October, Edward Gorsuch killed at Christiana that September, and Anthony Burns marched through a mourning Boston between federal troops in 1854. Resisters answered with the higher law doctrine.

2. Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin, serialized in 1851 and a book in March 1852, was built from the slave narratives and from Josiah Henson's account in particular. It made families parted at the auction block its recurring wound, aimed at the Christian mother's conscience, and sold by the usual estimates near three hundred thousand copies in its first American year.

3. The South banned the novel and answered it with a shelf of anti-Tom novels. Stowe replied in 1853 with A Key to Uncle Tom's Cabin, a documentary volume defending her facts. The story of Lincoln greeting her as the little woman who made the great war is an anecdote printed decades later and unverifiable.

4. Douglass at Rochester on July 5, 1852 told his audience the Fourth was theirs, not the slave's, that the celebration was to him a sham and its liberty a hollow mockery, and that the subject needed scorching irony, not convincing argument. He closed in hope drawn from the Declaration itself.

5. Truth at Akron in May 1851 survives in two texts: Robinson's report in the Anti-Slavery Bugle of June 21, 1851, arguing through the pint and the quart, and Gage's version of 1863, which added the famous refrain and a southern dialect, though Truth was born in New York and spoke Dutch as a child.

6. Jacobs hid seven years in a crawl space above her grandmother's storeroom, escaped, and published Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl in 1861 as Linda Brent, the first slave narrative by an American woman published in the United States, opening with a child learning at six that she was a piece of merchandise.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the prompt and takes a position on reach. Any of the three forms earns full credit when the reach judgment is argued from the bank rather than asserted. (Blueprint 3 and 4)
  • The three asks assigned correctly and supported. Feeling belongs to the novel: bank item 2 carries Stowe's evidence base, her recurring wound, her audience, and her sales. Judgment belongs to the oration: bank item 4 carries Douglass's answer at Rochester, his sham and hollow mockery charges, and his choice of scorching irony. Witness belongs to the narrative: bank item 6 carries Jacobs's hiding, her 1861 book, and her opening discovery. (Blueprint 3 and 4)
  • Source care with Truth. A full credit essay that quotes Truth quotes Robinson's 1851 report, or quotes Gage only while naming it as Gage's 1863 version. Bank item 5 supplies both provenances and the dialect problem. An essay that prints the famous refrain as Truth's certain words without provenance has failed the lesson's method, whatever its other merits. (Blueprint 4)
  • Facts handled at their standing. The Lincoln greeting appears, if at all, as an anecdote printed decades later and unverifiable (bank item 3), and the novel's sales appear as the usual estimates. Precision about what can be checked is part of the grade. (Blueprint 3)

Model opening for teacher reference. By 1852 the antislavery writers had stopped asking the parties for permission. Stowe carried slavery into the parlor and asked the Christian mother reading there to feel a family parted at the auction block as a wound in her own house, and three hundred thousand buyers in the first year suggest the parlor answered. Douglass stood up in Rochester on the nation's birthday and asked his hearers to judge their own shouts of liberty against the slave's chains, choosing scorching irony because the facts were already theirs. Jacobs, writing last and from furthest inside, asked her readers to witness: a child of six learning she was merchandise, in a book by the woman she became. A full model continues in this pattern through the rescues and the higher law ground beneath them, and closes on the parties still promising to manage a question the writers had already moved into the country's households.

Common weak answers. The summary answer that lists the writers without assigning asks has described the lesson instead of answering it. The fame answer that builds its Truth paragraph on the refrain without provenance repeats the error Station 4 exists to correct. An essay that treats the Lincoln greeting as a documented meeting has traded a checkable record for a good story, and bank item 3 is the correction.
