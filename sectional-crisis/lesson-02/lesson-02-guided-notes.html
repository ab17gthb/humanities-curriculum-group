Guided Notes: The Antislavery Argument at Full Voice

Lesson question: What did the antislavery writers of 1851 and 1852 ask their readers to feel, to judge, and to do?

Unit question: How did compromise over slavery finally fail, and what did the war it caused settle about slavery and the Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Uncle Tom's Cabin: Harriet Beecher Stowe's antislavery novel, serialized in 1851 and published as a book in March 1852, the best selling American novel of its century, which carried the slave narratives' evidence into northern parlors and made slavery's cruelties a household argument

Harriet Beecher Stowe: the Cincinnati teacher and writer, daughter of Lyman Beecher and sister of Henry Ward Beecher, who wrote Uncle Tom's Cabin from the slave narratives and from Josiah Henson's account in particular, and later published a documentary key defending her novel's facts

Sojourner Truth: the New York woman born enslaved as Isabella Baumfree, freed under that state's gradual emancipation, who renamed herself in 1843 and traveled as an itinerant preacher for abolition and women's rights, speaking at Akron in 1851

Harriet Jacobs: the North Carolina woman who escaped enslavement after seven years hiding in a crawl space above her grandmother's storeroom, and published Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl in 1861 under the name Linda Brent, the first slave narrative by an American woman published in the United States

slave narrative: the first person account of slavery by a formerly enslaved person, published with sponsors' authenticating documents, the genre that supplied the antislavery movement with its evidence and its witnesses from the 1840s onward

higher law: the doctrine Senator William H. Seward named in 1850, that a law of God and nature above the Constitution condemned slavery and the Fugitive Slave Act, the argument northern resisters used to justify disobeying a law they held no legislature could rightfully make

Anthony Burns: the Virginia fugitive arrested in Boston in May 1854 and remanded to slavery after a rendition that filled the city with federal troops and mourning crowds, whose case became the Fugitive Slave Act's most famous northern spectacle

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher

Teach this lesson second in the Sectional Crisis and Civil War unit, directly after Lesson 1 on the Compromise of 1850 and the Fugitive Slave Act, whose enforcement scenes this lesson opens with and whose resisters supply its writers their subject. It pairs with the lesson activity, The Antislavery File, whose five stations the writing task draws on, and its two Truth stations should be taught after section E of the reading so students meet the source problem in the documents themselves. Students need Lesson 1's account of the Fugitive Slave Act at working level, the idea of a slave narrative from the Jackson and Reform unit, and Douglass's standing as the movement's leading orator from that unit. Content: students will trace how the antislavery argument was made between 1851 and 1861 in a novel, an oration, two rival texts of one speech, and a woman's narrative, and will practice asking who wrote a famous text down and when. Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies (cite textual evidence, determine central ideas, integrate and evaluate multiple sources, write arguments from evidence) and the C3 Framework (evaluate sources and use evidence, develop claims, communicate conclusions). Timing: one period of about 50 minutes for the reading and these notes; the activity takes a second period. Materials: the lesson reading, these notes, and the slide deck if projected.

Summary table. The novel: Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin, serialized in 1851 and a book in March 1852, built from the slave narratives and aimed at the Christian mother's conscience; it asks the reader to feel slavery as families parted and households corrupted. The oration: Douglass at Rochester on July 5, 1852, answering that the Fourth was the audience's, not the slave's, and auditing the founding's promises against the slave's present; it asks the reader to judge a celebration. The narrative: Jacobs's Incidents of 1861, written as Linda Brent after seven years in hiding; it asks the reader to witness what slavery did to women, in the subject's own voice. The rescue and the speech: the Syracuse and Christiana resisters acted on a higher law, and Truth at Akron argued from her own labor, surviving in Robinson's 1851 report and Gage's 1863 version; they ask for action and for care about who recorded the words.

Exit ticket 1. Uncle Tom's Cabin is Harriet Beecher Stowe's antislavery novel, which carried the slave narratives' evidence into northern parlors and made slavery's cruelties a household argument. It was serialized in 1851 and published as a book in March 1852.

Exit ticket 2. The two texts are Marius Robinson's report, printed in the Anti-Slavery Bugle of June 21, 1851, and Frances Dana Gage's version, published in 1863. A historian may quote Robinson's with confidence because it was written down and printed within weeks by the convention's secretary, while Gage wrote twelve years later and added a refrain and a dialect Truth did not use.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that assigns each form its ask. The novel asks the reader to feel, the oration to judge the nation's promises against the slave's present, and the narrative to witness a life in the first person. Strong answers add that the rescues asked for action.
