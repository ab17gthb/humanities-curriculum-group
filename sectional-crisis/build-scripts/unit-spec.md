## Vocabulary (use verbatim everywhere: guided notes, decks, study materials)

### Lesson 1 (blueprint 1, 2)
- Compromise of 1850: the package of five measures enacted in September 1850 that admitted California as a free state, organized Utah and New Mexico without a slavery restriction, settled the Texas boundary and assumed its debt, ended the slave trade in the District of Columbia, and enacted a far stricter Fugitive Slave Act, passed bill by bill under Douglas after Clay's single omnibus failed
- Fugitive Slave Act: the law of September 18, 1850, that required the return of people accused of escaping slavery on an owner's affidavit, denied the accused a jury trial and the right to testify, paid federal commissioners ten dollars for remanding a prisoner and five for freeing one, and made aiding a fugitive a federal crime
- Henry Clay: the Kentucky senator who had built the compromises of 1820 and 1833 and returned to the Senate in 1849, at seventy two, to frame the Compromise of 1850, the last settlement of his long career as the Union's peacemaker, dying two years after its passage
- Daniel Webster: the Massachusetts senator and orator whose Seventh of March speech in 1850 pleaded for the Compromise in the Union's name, breaking with his antislavery supporters, who mourned him in Whittier's poem "Ichabod" as a fallen champion of freedom
- personal liberty laws: the statutes northern states passed to obstruct the Fugitive Slave Act, forbidding state officers to help in renditions, denying the use of state jails, and granting accused fugitives the jury trials and counsel that federal law denied them
- Underground Railroad: the informal network of routes, safe houses, and guides, Black and white, by which enslaved people escaped the South for the free states and Canada, carrying thousands north across the middle decades of the century despite the federal law against aiding them
- Harriet Tubman: the Maryland woman born enslaved as Araminta Ross, who escaped in 1849 and returned south again and again to guide some seventy enslaved people to freedom, served the Union army as a scout, spy, and nurse, and became the most famous conductor on the Underground Railroad

### Lesson 2 (blueprint 3, 4)
- Uncle Tom's Cabin: Harriet Beecher Stowe's antislavery novel, serialized in 1851 and published as a book in March 1852, the best selling American novel of its century, which carried the slave narratives' evidence into northern parlors and made slavery's cruelties a household argument
- Harriet Beecher Stowe: the Cincinnati teacher and writer, daughter of Lyman Beecher and sister of Henry Ward Beecher, who wrote Uncle Tom's Cabin from the slave narratives and from Josiah Henson's account in particular, and later published a documentary key defending her novel's facts
- Sojourner Truth: the New York woman born enslaved as Isabella Baumfree, freed under that state's gradual emancipation, who renamed herself in 1843 and traveled as an itinerant preacher for abolition and women's rights, speaking at Akron in 1851
- Harriet Jacobs: the North Carolina woman who escaped enslavement after seven years hiding in a crawl space above her grandmother's storeroom, and published Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl in 1861 under the name Linda Brent, the first slave narrative by an American woman published in the United States
- slave narrative: the first person account of slavery by a formerly enslaved person, published with sponsors' authenticating documents, the genre that supplied the antislavery movement with its evidence and its witnesses from the 1840s onward
- higher law: the doctrine Senator William H. Seward named in 1850, that a law of God and nature above the Constitution condemned slavery and the Fugitive Slave Act, the argument northern resisters used to justify disobeying a law they held no legislature could rightfully make
- Anthony Burns: the Virginia fugitive arrested in Boston in May 1854 and remanded to slavery after a rendition that filled the city with federal troops and mourning crowds, whose case became the Fugitive Slave Act's most famous northern spectacle

### Lesson 3 (blueprint 5, 6)
- Kansas-Nebraska Act: the law of May 30, 1854, written by Senator Stephen A. Douglas, which organized Kansas and Nebraska as territories, left slavery in them to popular sovereignty, and repealed the Missouri Compromise line of 1820 that had barred slavery from the northern Louisiana Purchase lands
- popular sovereignty: Douglas's doctrine that the people of a territory should decide for themselves whether to permit slavery there, offered as a democratic alternative to congressional rule over the territories and tested to destruction in Kansas
- Bleeding Kansas: the name the country's newspapers gave the Kansas Territory's guerrilla war of 1855 and 1856, when proslavery and free state settlers, armed by sponsors in Missouri and New England, fought over two rival governments and left some two hundred dead
- border ruffians: the name free state settlers gave the Missourians who crossed the border in organized bands to vote in Kansas elections, intimidate free state voters, and sack the town of Lawrence in May 1856
- Lecompton Constitution: the proslavery constitution drafted at Lecompton in 1857 and offered to Kansas voters in a referendum arranged so that even rejecting it protected slavery already in the territory, whose fraud split President Buchanan from Douglas and delayed Kansas statehood until 1861
- John Brown: the Connecticut born abolitionist who, convinced slavery would end only in blood, led the Pottawatomie killings in Kansas in 1856 and the raid on the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry in 1859, and was hanged by Virginia on December 2, 1859
- Charles Sumner: the Massachusetts senator whose two day speech "The Crime Against Kansas" in May 1856 denounced slavery and its southern defenders in personal terms, for which Congressman Preston Brooks beat him nearly to death at his Senate desk

### Lesson 4 (blueprint 7, 8)
- Republican Party: the party organized in 1854 from the wreckage of the Kansas-Nebraska fight, joining ex-Whigs, Free Soilers, and anti-Nebraska Democrats on the single plank that Congress must bar slavery from the federal territories, the first successful wholly northern party in the country's history
- free labor: the northern creed that labor was honorable, that the laborer had a right to rise by his work to property and independence, and that slavery degraded work and monopolized the common lands, the economic faith under the Republican Party's promise of free soil
- John C. Frémont: the explorer and army officer called the Pathfinder, the Republican Party's first presidential nominee in 1856, who carried eleven of the sixteen free states and showed both sections that a purely northern party could nearly win the presidency
- Dred Scott v. Sandford: the Supreme Court decision of March 6, 1857, in which Chief Justice Taney held that Black people could not be citizens of the United States, that residence on free soil had not freed Dred Scott, and that Congress had never had power to bar slavery from the territories
- Roger B. Taney: the Maryland chief justice who succeeded John Marshall in 1836 and wrote the Dred Scott opinion, attempting to settle the slavery question by constitutional decree and instead voiding the Republican platform and deepening the crisis he meant to end
- Abraham Lincoln: the Illinois lawyer and former one term congressman who rose on the Kansas-Nebraska fight to lead his state's Republicans, debated Douglas across Illinois in 1858, and was elected the sixteenth president in 1860 on the platform of barring slavery from the territories
- House Divided speech: Lincoln's address of June 16, 1858, accepting the Republican nomination for the Senate, which argued that the government could not endure permanently half slave and half free and charged that the Nebraska repeal and the Dred Scott decision were parts of one design to make slavery national

### Lesson 5 (blueprint 9, 10)
- Lincoln-Douglas debates: the seven open air debates between Abraham Lincoln and Stephen A. Douglas across Illinois in the Senate campaign of 1858, three hours each before crowds in the tens of thousands, which made Lincoln a national figure and fixed the terms of the slavery argument
- Freeport Doctrine: Douglas's answer at Freeport in August 1858, that a territory could keep slavery out despite the Dred Scott decision by refusing to pass the local police laws slavery needed to survive, the answer that saved his Senate seat and forfeited the South
- Harpers Ferry: the Virginia town at the meeting of the Potomac and Shenandoah rivers, site of the federal arsenal John Brown seized on October 16, 1859, hoping to arm an enslaved rising, and of the engine house where marines under Robert E. Lee captured him two days later
- secession: the doctrine and act by which eleven slaveholding states, beginning with South Carolina on December 20, 1860, declared their union with the United States dissolved and withdrew, holding that the Union was a compact from which a state could withdraw as it had entered
- Crittenden Compromise: Senator John J. Crittenden's last settlement proposal of December 1860, which would have written the Missouri line into the Constitution as an unamendable amendment protecting slavery south of it forever, rejected by the Republicans on Lincoln's instruction and failing in January 1861
- Confederate States of America: the government organized at Montgomery, Alabama, in February 1861 by the first seven seceded states, later eleven, with a constitution modeled on the federal one that named slavery's protection in its text where the original had not
- Jefferson Davis: the Mississippi senator and former secretary of war chosen president of the Confederate States in February 1861, who directed the Confederacy's war for four years and was captured in Georgia in May 1865 after Richmond fell

### Lesson 6 (blueprint 11, 12)
- Fort Sumter: the federal fort in Charleston harbor whose provisioning became the crisis of Lincoln's first month in office, bombarded by Confederate batteries on April 12, 1861, and surrendered on April 14, the act that opened the Civil War
- First Bull Run: the war's first great battle, fought near Manassas, Virginia, on July 21, 1861, before excursion crowds from Washington, whose Confederate victory and Union rout ended the hope of a short war and taught both governments to build real armies
- Anaconda Plan: General Winfield Scott's strategy of 1861, to strangle the Confederacy by naval blockade of its coast and a drive down the Mississippi that would split it in two, derided as slow by a public demanding a quick blow and adopted in substance as the war became long
- blockade: the naval closure of an enemy's ports to trade, proclaimed by Lincoln against the Confederacy in April 1861 and enforced by a navy built from almost nothing to some six hundred vessels, the instrument that strangled the cotton trade on which Confederate finance rested
- habeas corpus: the constitutional writ by which a prisoner may demand to be brought before a judge and shown the cause of detention, which Lincoln suspended along the war's military lines from 1861 and Congress authorized in 1863, so that military arrests could proceed without civil trial
- George B. McClellan: the organizer who built the Army of the Potomac and commanded it through the Peninsula campaign and Antietam, a brilliant administrator whose caution before the enemy Lincoln finally exhausted, removing him in November 1862
- Antietam: the battle of September 17, 1862, near Sharpsburg, Maryland, where McClellan, holding a copy of Lee's lost orders, fought the Army of Northern Virginia to a standstill in the single bloodiest day in American history, ending the first invasion of the North and giving Lincoln ground to issue the Emancipation Proclamation

### Lesson 7 (blueprint 13, 14)
- contraband: the name General Benjamin Butler gave at Fort Monroe in May 1861 to enslaved people who escaped to Union lines, held as property seized from the enemy rather than returned, the legal fiction under which hundreds of thousands reached freedom before any proclamation freed them
- Emancipation Proclamation: Lincoln's executive order, issued in preliminary form on September 22, 1862, and in final form on January 1, 1863, which declared free the enslaved people in the states and parts of states then in rebellion, left slavery untouched in the loyal border states, and opened the Union army and navy to Black enlistment
- United States Colored Troops: the regiments of Black soldiers raised in the Union army from 1863, some 180,000 men by the war's end, who fought at Port Hudson, Milliken's Bend, and Fort Wagner and campaigned for and won equal pay in June 1864
- 54th Massachusetts: the first Black regiment raised in the North, recruited in 1863 under Governor John Andrew and Colonel Robert Gould Shaw, whose assault on Fort Wagner that July, at the cost of Shaw and nearly half the regiment, answered the claim that Black men would not fight
- Gettysburg: the battle of July 1 to 3, 1863, in Pennsylvania, where the Army of the Potomac under Meade stopped Lee's second invasion of the North in the war's greatest battle, costing the two armies some fifty thousand casualties and ending the Confederacy's hope of winning on northern soil
- Vicksburg: the Mississippi River fortress whose surrender to Grant on July 4, 1863, after a six week siege, gave the Union the whole Mississippi, split the Confederacy in two, and, falling the day after Gettysburg ended, made that Independence Day the war's turning point
- Gettysburg Address: Lincoln's two minute dedication of the soldiers' cemetery at Gettysburg on November 19, 1863, which restated the war as a test of the proposition that all men are created equal and rededicated the living to the dead men's unfinished work

### Lesson 8 (blueprint 15)
- Ulysses S. Grant: the Ohio born general who won at Fort Donelson, Shiloh, Vicksburg, and Chattanooga, was made general in chief in March 1864, and directed the coordinated campaigns that wore the Confederacy down, accepting Lee's surrender at Appomattox and later serving two terms as president
- total war: the strategy of making war on the enemy's resources and will as well as its armies, destroying railroads, crops, mills, and stores so that the society sustaining the armies felt the war in its households, carried to its fullest by Sherman in Georgia and the Carolinas
- William Tecumseh Sherman: the Ohio general who took Atlanta in September 1864, cut loose from his supply lines to march to the sea, and carried total war through Georgia and the Carolinas, writing to Atlanta's mayor that war is cruelty and cannot be refined
- March to the Sea: Sherman's campaign of November and December 1864, in which some sixty two thousand Union soldiers marched from Atlanta to Savannah living off the country and destroying the railroads, mills, and stores in a corridor sixty miles wide, presenting Savannah to Lincoln as a Christmas gift
- Thirteenth Amendment: the constitutional amendment, passed by Congress on January 31, 1865, and ratified that December, that abolished slavery and involuntary servitude everywhere in the United States, except as punishment for a crime, the first of the three Reconstruction amendments and the only one adopted while the war still ran
- Appomattox Court House: the Virginia village where, on April 9, 1865, Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to Grant on terms that paroled the soldiers home with their horses for the spring planting, the surrender that effectively ended the Civil War
- Second Inaugural Address: Lincoln's address of March 4, 1865, which named American slavery as the offense and the war as its woe, read the conflict as judgment on both sections, and closed by calling the country to bind up its wounds, delivered five weeks before his assassination
