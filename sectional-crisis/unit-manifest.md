# Sectional Crisis and Civil War: Unit Manifest

**Essential question:** How did compromise over slavery finally fail, and what did the war it caused settle about slavery and the Union, and what did it leave open?

**Course position:** The fifth unit of the US History course (courseId u5), built fourteenth in the build order. It opens with the Compromise of 1850, the settlement the Jackson and Reform unit deliberately did not teach, and closes at the war's end in April 1865 with Appomattox and Lincoln's assassination. The Missouri Compromise and the gag rule era are prior knowledge here, taught in the Early Republic and Jackson and Reform units and named in prose only. The Thirteenth Amendment's passage by the House on January 31, 1865, is named in Lesson 8 as a wartime event and no further; the Reconstruction amendments' passage debates, the Freedmen's Bureau, and presidential Reconstruction are not taught here, because they open the Reconstruction unit. The era is taught with its peoples in the frame throughout: fugitives and the committees that defended them, an enslaved woman writing the president to ask whether she was free, Black soldiers fighting for equal pay as well as for the Union, the women who petitioned emancipation into a constitutional amendment, and the enslaved people who freed themselves by walking to the Union lines in numbers no proclamation ordered. One image discipline governs the whole unit and is stated in the skeleton's frame: Gardner's Antietam photographs of the Confederate dead are taught through the audited bank record's description and the documented effect of their exhibition, and are not reproduced in any student document, deck, or teacher notes document, the same practice the Civil Rights unit applied to photographs of the dead.

**Built:** 2026-10-22. 51 documents, 247 printed pages. 8 slide decks, 219 slides, 30,502 words of speaker notes, 8 printable teacher notes documents (74 pages).

**Topics (6):** 1 The last settlement: compromise, the fugitive law, and the antislavery answer · 2 Kansas and the broken parties: Nebraska, the Republicans, and the Court · 3 The break: argument, raid, election, and secession · 4 The war for the Union: strategy, stalemate, and cost to 1862 · 5 The war remade: emancipation, 1863, and the Address · 6 The verdict of arms: total war, Appomattox, and the Second Inaugural.

## Lesson 7: Emancipation and the War Transformed, 1863

Contraband and confiscation, the Proclamation read with its limits on the page, freedom as the enslaved executed it, Black soldiers, Gettysburg and Vicksburg, the Address, and the draft riots. Lesson question: "How did emancipation change what the war was for, and for whom?"

- Deck: 27 slides, speaker notes 138 to 187 words a slide (4,327 words), four giant stat slides, teacher notes document 10 pages.
- Honest flags, the unit's largest: the skeleton's first draft described Robinson's Pictures of Slavery and Anti-Slavery as abolition's photographic evidence. The bank record presents the book as the opposite, a Philadelphia writer's 1863 defense of slavery as a divine institution. The bank's Men of Color record carries no image of the broadside, so none is reproduced and the station follows the bank's presentation, its central appeal quoted and its source line saying so. No period image of the 54th Massachusetts in action exists and the famous Fort Wagner prints are later artists' imaginings, so none is used; the United States Colored Troops are pictured by Company E, 4th U.S. Colored Infantry at Fort Lincoln (Library of Congress cwpb.04294), captioned for exactly what it is. Fort Pillow and the draft riots are stated plainly, in one sentence each, and never lingered on.
