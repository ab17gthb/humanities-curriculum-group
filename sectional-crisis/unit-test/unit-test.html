Part C: Sources (10 points)

Answer each question in complete sentences, citing the sources by letter.

Source A

The Congress of the United States, Section 14 of the Kansas-Nebraska Act, May 30, 1854. From U.S. Congress, 'Kansas-Nebraska Act,' 1854 (Avalon Project, Yale Law School). Cuts are marked with ellipses in brackets.

“[...] That the Constitution, and all Laws of the United States which are not locally inapplicable, shall have the same force and effect within the said Territory of Nebraska as elsewhere within the United States, except the eighth section of the act preparatory to the admission of Missouri into the Union approved March sixth, eighteen hundred and twenty, which, being inconsistent with the principle of non-intervention by Congress with slaves in the States and Territories, as recognized by the legislation of eighteen hundred and fifty, commonly called the Compromise Measures, is hereby declared inoperative and void; [...] it being the true intent and meaning of this act not to legislate slavery into any Territory or State, nor to exclude it therefrom, but to leave the people thereof perfectly free to form and regulate their domestic institutions in their own way, subject only to the Constitution of the United States [...]”

A1. (1 point) The act declares the eighth section of the act for the admission of Missouri inoperative and void because it is inconsistent with what principle, as the passage names the principle?

A2. (2 points) State what the act says is not its intent to do about slavery in a territory or state, and state what it leaves the people of a territory free to do instead.

Source B

The South Carolina Secession Convention, the Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession, 1860. From South Carolina Secession Convention, 'Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession,' 1860 (Avalon Project, Yale Law School). Cuts are marked with ellipses in brackets.

“[...] We assert that fourteen of the States have deliberately refused, for years past, to fulfill their constitutional obligations, and we refer to their own Statutes for the proof. [...] An increasing hostility on the part of the non-slaveholding States to the institution of slavery, has led to a disregard of their obligations, and the laws of the General Government have ceased to effect the objects of the Constitution. [...] Thus the constituted compact has been deliberately broken and disregarded by the non-slaveholding States, and the consequence follows that South Carolina is released from her obligation. [...] They have encouraged and assisted thousands of our slaves to leave their homes; and those who remain, have been incited by emissaries, books and pictures to servile insurrection. [...] A geographical line has been drawn across the Union, and all the States north of that line have united in the election of a man to the high office of President of the United States, whose opinions and purposes are hostile to slavery. [...] It has announced that the South shall be excluded from the common territory, that the judicial tribunals shall be made sectional, and that a war must be waged against slavery until it shall cease throughout the United States.”

B1. (2 points) State what the declaration asserts fourteen of the states have deliberately refused to do, and state the proof the declaration points to for the charge.

B2. (2 points) State the consequence the declaration draws from the compact having been deliberately broken, and name one purpose it says the incoming sectional party has announced.

Source C

President Abraham Lincoln, the Emancipation Proclamation, January 1, 1863. From Abraham Lincoln, 'Emancipation Proclamation,' 1863 (Avalon Project, Yale Law School). Cuts are marked with ellipses in brackets.

“[...] That on the first day of January, in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and sixty-three, all persons held as slaves within any State or designated part of a State, the people whereof shall then be in rebellion against the United States, shall be then, thenceforward, and forever free; [...] the Executive Government of the United States, including the military and naval authority thereof, will recognize and maintain the freedom of such persons, and will do no act or acts to repress such persons, or any of them, in any efforts they may make for their actual freedom. [...] And I further declare and make known, that such persons of suitable condition, will be received into the armed service of the United States to garrison forts, positions, stations, and other places, and to man vessels of all sorts in said service. [...] And upon this act, sincerely believed to be an act of justice, warranted by the Constitution, upon military necessity, I invoke the considerate judgment of mankind, and the gracious favor of Almighty God. [...]”

C1. (1 point) The proclamation declares free the persons held as slaves in any state whose people shall then be in what condition?

C2. (2 points) State what the Executive Government will recognize and maintain for the people declared free, and state what service such persons of suitable condition will be received into.

Part D: Essay

The essay is scored on the six part holistic scale below, whichever prompt the student chose.

Score 5. A clear judgment is stated and held through the essay. At least three pieces of specific evidence from the unit are used accurately, the strongest point on the other side is stated fairly and answered, and the writing is organized in paragraphs with a conclusion that follows from the evidence.

Score 4. A clear judgment is stated and mostly held. Two or three pieces of specific evidence are used accurately, the other side is stated and answered, and the organization holds, with minor lapses in precision or development.

Score 3. A judgment is stated but drifts, or the evidence is specific in places and general in others. The other side is mentioned but answered thinly, or the answer to it is asserted rather than argued.

Score 2. The essay retells the secession story or the war story without a judgment that the evidence is made to serve, or the judgment is stated but supported by only one accurate piece of evidence. The other side does not appear.

Score 1. The response is a fragment, a single point restated, or evidence is used inaccurately in ways that change its meaning. Little of the prompt is answered.

Score 0. The response is blank, off the prompt, or copies the prompt without answering it.

For Prompt 1, strong evidence includes South Carolina's declaration charging the northern states with refusing their constitutional obligations on fugitives, nullifying the acts of Congress by their own statutes, encouraging enslaved people to leave their homes, and electing a president hostile to slavery; Stephens's Cornerstone Speech naming the new government's foundation as the truth of racial inequality and slavery as the Black man's natural condition; the Kansas-Nebraska repeal and the violence over slavery's extension; the Dred Scott decision voiding every political settlement of the territorial question; and Lincoln's Second Inaugural naming American slavery as the offense of which the war was the woe. Evidence on the other side includes the secession documents' own language of compact and constitutional rights, the North's stated war aim of Union in 1861, and the tariff and states rights arguments the era also carried; a strong essay weighs what the seceding states said when they left against what later memory preferred to say for them. (Blueprint 10)

For Prompt 2, strong evidence includes the Proclamation's limits, freedom declared in the rebellious states and slavery untouched in the loyal border states; the contraband record, in which the enslaved made emancipation a fact by reaching Union lines before any proclamation; Black enlistment, some 180,000 soldiers in the United States Colored Troops and the equal pay fight won in June 1864; the Thirteenth Amendment completing abolition in law everywhere in the United States; Appomattox and the Union preserved as one government; and the Second Inaugural naming slavery, not disunion alone, as the war's offense. Evidence on the other side includes Lincoln's opening pledge to save the Union with or without freeing the enslaved, the Proclamation framed as military necessity, and the freed people's land, safety, and vote left unsettled in April 1865. Either prompt can earn full credit argued from either side of its question, provided the judgment is earned from the evidence and the other side is answered. (Blueprint 15)
