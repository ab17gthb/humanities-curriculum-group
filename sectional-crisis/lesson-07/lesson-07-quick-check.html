Quick Check: Freedom Declared, Freedom Fought For

Directions: Read the passage, then answer the questions.

Lincoln, Emancipation Proclamation, January 1, 1863, in part. "That on the first day of January, in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and sixty-three, all persons held as slaves within any State or designated part of a State, the people whereof shall then be in rebellion against the United States, shall be then, thenceforward, and forever free; and the Executive Government of the United States, including the military and naval authority thereof, will recognize and maintain the freedom of such persons."

Source: Abraham Lincoln, Emancipation Proclamation, 1863, as carried in the audited Primary Source Project bank record (Abraham Lincoln, 'Emancipation Proclamation,' 1863 (Avalon Project, Yale Law School).). Quoted from the bank excerpt.

1. State the declaration. Name who is declared free, by what description of the place they are held, and the two things the Executive Government, including its military and naval authority, promises to do.

2. State the limits. Name the four loyal slave states the proclamation did not touch and the other areas it exempted, and state the critics' charge in one sentence.

3. Work the soldiers' record. State the force organized in 1863 and the number who served by the war's end, the broadside of March 1863 and what its quoted appeal promised enlistment would silence, and the pay a Black soldier received against a white soldier's and when Congress equalized it.

4. Work the turning of 1863. Give the Gettysburg battle's dates and combined cost, the fortress that surrendered on July 4 and what its fall did to the Confederacy, and the date and restated purpose of Lincoln's cemetery address.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the declaration, with the description and the two promises. All persons held as slaves within any state or designated part of a state whose people were then in rebellion against the United States are declared then, thenceforward, and forever free. The Executive Government, including the military and naval authority, promises to recognize and to maintain the freedom of such persons. (Blueprint 13)

2. State the limits, with the four states, the exemptions, and the charge. The proclamation did not touch Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri, the loyal slave states, and it exempted Tennessee and the parts of Louisiana and Virginia under Union occupation. The critics' charge: it freed people where the Union could not yet enforce freedom and stood aside where it could. (Blueprint 13)

3. Work the soldiers' record, with the force, the broadside, and the pay. The United States Colored Troops were organized in 1863, and some 180,000 Black men served by the war's end, roughly a tenth of the Union army. Douglass's 'Men of Color, To Arms!' broadside of March 1863 promised that rising to arms would forever silence the tongue of calumny, of prejudice, and of hate, and that the men who rose would be regarded as men. Black soldiers were paid ten dollars a month against a white soldier's thirteen, and charged for clothing from the smaller sum, until Congress granted equal pay in June 1864. (Blueprint 13)

4. Work the turning, with Gettysburg, Vicksburg, and the Address. Gettysburg was fought on July 1 to 3, 1863, and cost the two armies together some fifty thousand casualties. Vicksburg surrendered to Grant on July 4, 1863, after a six week siege, giving the Union the whole Mississippi River and splitting the Confederacy in two. On November 19, 1863, dedicating the soldiers' cemetery at Gettysburg, Lincoln restated the war as a test of the proposition that all men are created equal and rededicated the living to the unfinished work and a new birth of freedom. (Blueprint 14)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Questions 1 to 3 carry blueprint entry 13 and test whether students can read the proclamation's own description of who is freed, hold its limits beside its promises, and attach the soldiers' record to it. Question 4 carries blueprint entry 14. A student who writes that the proclamation freed all enslaved people in the United States has the slogan of emancipation rather than its record, and the unit test will price the limits.
