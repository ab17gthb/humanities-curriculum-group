Emancipation and the War Transformed, 1863

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Freedom Walked to the Lines First

Long before any proclamation, enslaved people began freeing themselves. From May 1861, thousands escaped to Union lines at Fort Monroe, in Virginia, where General Benjamin Butler refused to return them and called them contraband of war, property seized from an enemy in arms. The fiction let the army shelter and employ them while Washington still called the war one for Union only. Congress followed where the fugitives had led. A first Confiscation Act came in 1861, and in April 1862 Congress ended slavery in the District of Columbia, paying loyal owners for the people it freed. Lincoln still waited. He had the border states to hold and no victory to speak from. Antietam, on September 17, 1862, gave him the ground, and on September 22 he issued a preliminary proclamation.

B. The Proclamation and Its Limits

The final Emancipation Proclamation, issued on January 1, 1863, declared free every enslaved person in the states and parts of states then in rebellion, and it ordered the government, its army, and its navy to recognize and maintain that freedom. It said nothing about the loyal slave states, Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri, and it exempted Tennessee and the parts of Louisiana and Virginia already under Union occupation. The critics' charge followed at once: the proclamation freed people where the Union could not yet enforce freedom and stood aside where it could. It struck at the labor system the rebellion fought with, it opened the army and navy to Black enlistment, and it made British or French recognition of a slaveholding republic far harder.

C. Freedom as the Enslaved Executed It

A proclamation printed in Washington freed no one by itself. What carried it into fact was the movement of enslaved people toward the Union armies, News of January 1 traveled by word of mouth through the enslaved community faster than any army marched. Enslaved families left plantations as Union forces came near, offered their labor to the armies, and gave soldiers information about the country. The Confederacy's labor force withdrew by the month. The numbers cannot be fixed exactly, and honest accounts state them as estimates: by the war's end, some half a million enslaved people had reached Union lines. However narrowly the proclamation was drawn as law, emancipation was becoming a military fact.

D. Black Soldiers Under Arms

The Militia Act of July 1862 authorized Black enlistment, and the proclamation turned authorization into recruitment. The army organized the United States Colored Troops in 1863, and by the war's end some 180,000 Black men had served, roughly a tenth of the Union army. Frederick Douglass lent recruitment its most famous broadside in March 1863, calling men of color to rise now and fly to arms. The service these men were offered was not equal. Black soldiers were paid ten dollars a month against a white soldier's thirteen, and were charged for their clothing, until Congress granted equal pay in June 1864. Their risk was also unequal: at Fort Pillow, in April 1864, Confederate troops killed many Black soldiers after the fort had been taken and resistance had ended.

E. The 54th Massachusetts at Fort Wagner

The first Black regiment raised in the North proved the point at a price. The 54th Massachusetts was recruited in the spring of 1863 under Governor John Andrew and Colonel Robert Gould Shaw, and Black men came from across the North to join it, two of them sons of Frederick Douglass. On July 18, 1863, the regiment led the assault on Fort Wagner, the earthwork guarding Charleston harbor, across open sand. Shaw was killed on the parapet, and nearly half the regiment was killed, wounded, or missing. The fort was not taken that night. What the assault settled was the claim that Black men would not fight, which northern doubters stopped repeating after Fort Wagner. The regiment then refused its pay for eighteen months rather than accept less.

F. Gettysburg, Vicksburg, and the Address

In May 1863 Lee won at Chancellorsville but lost Stonewall Jackson, mortally wounded there. In June Lee invaded the North again, and Meade's Army of the Potomac met him at Gettysburg, in Pennsylvania. The three days, July 1 to 3, stopped the invasion and cost the two armies some fifty thousand casualties. On July 4, the day after Gettysburg ended, Vicksburg surrendered to Grant after a siege of six weeks. The Union now held the whole Mississippi, and the Confederacy was split in two. On November 19, at the dedication of the soldiers' cemetery at Gettysburg, Lincoln restated, in about two minutes, the war as a test of the proposition that all men are created equal, and he rededicated the living to the unfinished work of the men buried there.

G. The Draft Riots, Robinson's Defense, and the New Arithmetic

The year's strain showed in New York. The federal draft fell hardest on working men who could not pay the three hundred dollars that hired a substitute. When the lottery resumed on July 13, rioting followed for four days. Mobs burned draft offices, then turned on Black New Yorkers, lynching men and burning the Colored Orphan Asylum after its children were got out. Troops sent from the Gettysburg campaign restored order. That year John Bell Robinson of Philadelphia published Pictures of Slavery and Anti-Slavery, defending slavery as God's institution from the Bible. The Union now fought with the formerly enslaved as soldiers, laborers, and guides, while the Confederacy fought with its labor force leaving the field. Whether freedom was now the war's purpose or still its instrument is this lesson's question.

1. State the road to the proclamation. Name the place and the general where enslaved people first reached Union lines in numbers, the name Butler gave them and what it meant, the two congressional steps of 1861 and April 1862, and the battle and dates that let Lincoln issue the preliminary proclamation.

2. State the proclamation and its limits. Give its date, what it declared and what it ordered the government and its armed forces to do, the four loyal slave states it did not touch and the other areas it exempted, and the critics' charge and the supporters' three part answer.

3. Work the soldiers' record. State the act and year that authorized Black enlistment, the force organized in 1863 and the number who had served by the war's end, the pay Black soldiers received against a white soldier's and when Congress equalized it, and what happened at Fort Pillow in April 1864.

4. Work the turning of 1863. State the battle and commander lost in May, the Pennsylvania battle's dates, armies, and combined cost, the river fortress that surrendered on July 4 and to whom and what its fall did to the Confederacy, and the date, place, and restated purpose of Lincoln's cemetery address.

5. In a short paragraph, answer the lesson question. Name two ways emancipation changed what the war was for, using the proclamation's own terms and the record of Black soldiers, and two facts that complicate the change, using the proclamation's limits and either the draft riots or Robinson's book. Judge whether, by the end of 1863, the change or the complication runs deeper in the record.
