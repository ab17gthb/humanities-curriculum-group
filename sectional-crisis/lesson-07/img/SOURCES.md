## Subjects for which no likeness is used, recorded honestly

- Robert Gould Shaw and the 54th Massachusetts at Fort Wagner: no period photograph of the regiment in action exists, and the famous later prints of the Fort Wagner assault are artists' imaginings made decades after the war. None is used. The lesson shows the United States Colored Troops through the Fort Lincoln company photograph above, captioned for exactly what it is.
- Fort Pillow and the New York draft riots: no image is used for either event. Both are stated in plain words in the reading, the activity, and the deck notes, and no image of violence is used as decoration anywhere in this lesson.
- The 'Men of Color, To Arms!' broadside: the bank record presents the broadside as a description with its central appeal quoted (its image is described, not reproduced, in the record). The lesson follows the bank's presentation and reproduces no broadside image.

## Quoted texts and their editions

All period quotations in this lesson are drawn programmatically from the audited Primary Source Project bank file sectional-civil-war.json during the build, so no bank text was retyped. Each document's Source line names the edition the bank record's sourceNote names:

- Abraham Lincoln, Emancipation Proclamation, January 1, 1863, bank record emancipation-proclamation-1863, whose sourceNote names the Avalon Project, Yale Law School. The bank record is an excerpt whose cuts are marked [...] and are printed as the bank carries them. Quoted at Activity Station 1 and, in part, in the quick check. The proclamation's limits are stated on the page with the station: it did not name Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, or Missouri, and it exempted Tennessee and the parts of Louisiana and Virginia under Union occupation.
- Frederick Douglass and other African American leaders, 'Men of Color, To Arms! To Arms!', 1863, bank record douglass-men-of-color-to-arms-1863, whose sourceNote names the Gilder Lehrman Institute, the original held by the Library Company of Philadelphia, the image described. Used at Activity Station 2 exactly as the bank presents it, a description of the broadside with its central appeal quoted word for word.
- Abraham Lincoln, Gettysburg Address, November 19, 1863, bank record gettysburg-address-1863, whose sourceNote names the Avalon Project, Yale Law School, Bliss copy. Printed in full at Activity Station 3, exactly as the bank carries it, including the bank text's double hyphens.
- John Bell Robinson, Pictures of Slavery and Anti-Slavery, 1863, bank record robinson-pictures-of-slavery-1863, whose sourceNote names the 1863 first edition, Internet Archive. Used at Activity Station 4 as the bank presents it: Robinson was a Philadelphia writer defending slavery as a divine institution imposed by God, arguing from Noah's curse on Canaan. Quoted from the bank text with one additional cut, marked [...] and disclosed in the station's Source line. Note: the unit skeleton's phrase describing this record as abolition's photographic evidence does not match the bank record; the bank record governs, and the lesson follows the bank.
- The New York draft riots record, Activity Station 5, is a labeled teaching summary built from the public record of July 1863 (the Enrollment Act of March 1863 and its three hundred dollar commutation, the lottery of July 11 and its resumption on July 13, four days of rioting, the burning of the Colored Orphan Asylum after its children were removed, the usual estimate of about one hundred and twenty dead stated as an estimate, and order restored by federal troops including regiments sent from the Gettysburg campaign). It is labeled a teaching summary on its face, and no words in it are presented as any participant's own.
- No period text is quoted from memory anywhere in this lesson.

## Image gaps

- No public domain period image of the 54th Massachusetts itself was used, for the reason stated above; the Fort Lincoln company photograph stands in its place, captioned honestly.
- No image of the contraband camps at Fort Monroe is used; a Commons file titled for Fort Monroe proved on inspection to be a small wood engraving printed on an envelope, not a photograph of the camps, and it was not used rather than miscaptioned.
