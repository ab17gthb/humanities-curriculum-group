Writing Task: Failing, or Working as Designed?

The prompt

Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: What did the Kansas-Nebraska Act repeal, and what did popular sovereignty look like when settlers tried it? Take a position on the doctrine's record in Kansas. Was Bleeding Kansas popular sovereignty failing, or was it popular sovereignty working exactly as its design allowed? A strong answer states the doctrine first from the act's own words, then follows it through at least two Kansas tests, the elections, the violence, or Lecompton, and judges whether the outcomes broke the doctrine or followed from it. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim. The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and activity. Write in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The Kansas-Nebraska Act, May 30, 1854: the eighth section of the Missouri act of 1820 declared inoperative and void, and the people of a territory left perfectly free to form and regulate their domestic institutions in their own way, subject only to the Constitution.

2. The Appeal of the Independent Democrats, January 1854: the bill arraigned as a gross violation of a sacred pledge and part of an atrocious plot, and the Missouri prohibition described as a solemn compact regarded for more than thirty years as inviolable American law.

3. The election of March 1855: thousands of Missourians rode into Kansas, voted, and rode home; the proslavery legislature they seated expelled its free state members, free state settlers built a rival government at Topeka, and President Pierce recognized the proslavery legislature.

4. May 1856: a proslavery posse sacked Lawrence on May 21, Preston Brooks beat Charles Sumner nearly to death at his Senate desk on May 22, and John Brown's party killed five proslavery settlers at Pottawatomie Creek on May 24 and 25.

5. Sumner, The Crime Against Kansas, May 1856: the ballot box plundered, the cry "I am an American citizen" interposed in vain, and force openly employed in compelling Kansas to slavery for the sake of political power.

6. The Lecompton referendum, 1857: voters offered the constitution with slavery or without it, slavery already present protected under both, and no way to reject the constitution; Buchanan endorsed it, Douglas broke with him over the fraud, and Congress refused Kansas admission under it.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the lesson question: what the act repealed, and a position, failing or working as designed, on what popular sovereignty looked like in Kansas. (Blueprint 5 and Blueprint 6)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 supports the repeal and the doctrine in the act's own words. (Blueprint 5) Bank item 2 supports the northern charge that a settled compact was broken. (Blueprint 5) Bank item 3 supports the first election's fraud and the two governments. (Blueprint 6) Bank item 4 supports the violence of May 1856 in its sequence. (Blueprint 6) Bank item 5 supports the contemporary accusation and its motive claim. (Blueprint 6) Bank item 6 supports the Lecompton fraud and Douglas's break with Buchanan. (Blueprint 5 and Blueprint 6)
  • A full credit essay must use item 1 or item 6; a verdict on the doctrine without the act's own words or the referendum its author disowned is an opinion without its documents.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the position. An essay that lists the repeal, the election, and the sack of Lawrence without judging whether those events broke the doctrine or followed from its design has assembled the Kansas record without its argument, and should earn partial credit.
  • An ending that states the balance sheet: the doctrine promised to take slavery out of Congress, and in Kansas it produced two governments, a sacked town, a beaten senator, a massacre, and a small war, with the question unsettled.

Model opening for teacher reference. Popular sovereignty did not fail in Kansas for want of trying. It failed on paper first. The act that sent the question west named no rule for who counted as the people of a territory, set no day on which they could decide, and appointed no umpire for a disputed count, and Kansas ran its first election inside those three silences. Missourians supplied the voters, a rival government supplied the protest, and when the ballots would not settle it, Lawrence, the Senate floor, and Pottawatomie supplied the rest. A design that cannot tell a fair vote from a stolen one is not betrayed by the theft. It is described by it.
