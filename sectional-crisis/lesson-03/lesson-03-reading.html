The Kansas-Nebraska Act and Bleeding Kansas

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Railroad Behind the Repeal

Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois chaired the Senate committee on territories, and he wanted the country west of Missouri organized. Douglas wanted a transcontinental railroad built west from Chicago, through country with no territorial government. Every railroad proposal needed southern votes. Southern senators named their price for organizing Kansas and Nebraska. The land lay north of the line drawn in 1820, where the Missouri Compromise barred slavery forever. If the territories were to be organized at all, that bar would have to go, and Douglas agreed to remove it. He did not present the bargain as a surrender. He presented it as a principle, and the principle had a name his speeches had carried for years.

B. The Act and Popular Sovereignty

The Kansas-Nebraska Act became law on May 30, 1854. It organized Kansas and Nebraska as territories. It declared the eighth section of the Missouri act of 1820 inoperative and void. The act stated its intent: not to legislate slavery into any territory or state, nor to exclude it therefrom, but to leave the people thereof perfectly free to form and regulate their domestic institutions in their own way, subject only to the Constitution. This was popular sovereignty, Douglas's doctrine that a territory's people should decide slavery for themselves while Congress stood aside. The act never said when they could decide, in the territorial years or only at statehood, and Kansas would test that silence.

C. The Storm and the Broken Parties

The northern answer began before the act had even passed. In January 1854 six members of Congress, among them Senators Salmon P. Chase and Charles Sumner, issued the Appeal of the Independent Democrats. It arraigned the bill as a gross violation of a sacred pledge and as part of an atrocious plot to open a vast free region to slavery. Mass meetings, sermons, and newspaper campaigns followed the appeal through the spring. The Whig Party, already strained by the Compromise of 1850, collapsed within two years, unable to hold its northern and southern wings. The Democratic Party survived but split by section. An anti-Nebraska coalition gathered ex-Whigs, Free Soilers, and anti-Nebraska Democrats into one camp, and the next lesson names the party they built.

D. Kansas, the Doctrine's Laboratory

Kansas opened to settlement in 1854, and both sides understood that its people would now vote slavery up or down. Emigrant aid societies in New England raised money to send free state settlers west. Missouri lay directly across the border, and Missourians crossed in organized thousands. In the territorial election of March 1855, thousands of Missourians rode in, voted, and rode home, and the free state settlers gave them the name border ruffians in that season. The proslavery legislature they elected expelled the few free state members and passed laws protecting slavery. Free state settlers answered with a rival government at Topeka. Two governments now claimed one territory, and President Franklin Pierce recognized the proslavery legislature as the lawful one.

E. Violence in Sequence

In May 1856 the argument turned to open violence. On May 21 a proslavery posse sacked Lawrence, destroying its newspaper presses and burning its hotel. In Washington, Sumner was concluding a two day speech, The Crime Against Kansas, denouncing slavery in personal terms. On May 22 Congressman Preston Brooks of South Carolina beat Sumner with a cane at his Senate desk and nearly killed him. Massachusetts kept the seat empty; Brooks resigned, was reelected, and was honored across the South. On the night of May 24 and 25, at Pottawatomie Creek, John Brown led four of his sons and two other men in taking five proslavery settlers from their cabins and killing them. The sack, the caning, and the killings answered one another in a single week, and this lesson states all three with the same plainness.

F. A Small War and an Armed Truce

Through the summer and fall of 1856 Kansas fought a guerrilla war. Free state and proslavery bands raided farms, burned cabins, and fought small actions along the creeks and border towns. The country's newspapers named the territory Bleeding Kansas, and the dead of 1855 and 1856 are usually estimated near two hundred. Neither government in the territory could protect its own people. In September 1856 a new territorial governor, John Geary, disbanded the rival militias and restored an armed truce. The question the doctrine was supposed to settle locally came out of the territory's first trial exactly as it had gone in, with slavery's place in Kansas still contested and both sides now armed, organized, and practiced in violence.

G. Lecompton and the Balance Sheet

In 1857 a proslavery convention at Lecompton drafted a constitution offered to Kansas voters in a referendum arranged so that even rejecting it protected slavery already in the territory. Free state voters boycotted the referendum as a fraud. President James Buchanan endorsed the Lecompton Constitution. Douglas broke with his own president, arguing the rigged referendum betrayed popular sovereignty itself. Congress refused admission under Lecompton, and Kansas entered the Union as a free state in January 1861, under a constitution written at Wyandotte. Popular sovereignty had promised to take slavery out of Congress and let neighbors settle it. In Kansas it produced two governments, a sacked town, a beaten senator, five men killed at Pottawatomie, and a small war, and the question stood no nearer settled than it had in 1820.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Why did organizing Kansas and Nebraska require repealing the Missouri Compromise line, and what bargain stood behind the repeal? The territories lay north of the line of 1820, where the Missouri Compromise barred slavery forever, so organizing them under popular sovereignty required declaring that bar void. Behind the repeal stood Douglas's railroad: he needed southern votes to organize the territories and build a transcontinental railroad west from Chicago, and the South's price was the removal of the bar.

2. State the act's own statement of its intent, and state the question the act left unanswered about when a territory could decide. The act declared its true intent and meaning was not to legislate slavery into any territory or state, nor to exclude it therefrom, but to leave the people thereof perfectly free to form and regulate their domestic institutions in their own way, subject only to the Constitution. It never said when the people could decide, during the territorial years or only when framing a state constitution.

3. What did the Appeal of the Independent Democrats charge, and what happened to the two national parties after the act passed? The Appeal arraigned the bill as a gross violation of a sacred pledge and as part of an atrocious plot to open a vast free region to slavery. The Whig Party collapsed within two years, unable to hold its northern and southern wings, and the Democratic Party survived but split by section, while an anti-Nebraska coalition gathered the repeal's opponents.

4. How did Kansas come to have two governments, and which one did Washington recognize? Missourians crossed the border in organized thousands and carried the March 1855 election by riding in, voting, and riding home; the proslavery legislature they elected expelled its free state members. Free state settlers answered with a rival government at Topeka. President Pierce recognized the proslavery legislature as the lawful government.

5. State the three acts of violence of May 1856 in their order, and state what the Lecompton referendum was arranged to protect even if voters rejected the constitution. On May 21 a proslavery posse sacked Lawrence and destroyed its presses and hotel. On May 22 Congressman Preston Brooks beat Senator Charles Sumner nearly to death at his Senate desk for his Crime Against Kansas speech. On the night of May 24 and 25 John Brown's party took five proslavery settlers from their cabins at Pottawatomie Creek and killed them. The Lecompton referendum was arranged so that even a vote against the constitution protected slavery already in the territory.
