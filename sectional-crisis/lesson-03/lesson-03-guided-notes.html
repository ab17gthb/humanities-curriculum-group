Guided Notes: The Kansas-Nebraska Act and Bleeding Kansas

Lesson question: What did the Kansas-Nebraska Act repeal, and what did popular sovereignty look like when settlers tried it?

Unit question: How did compromise over slavery finally fail, and what did the war it caused settle about slavery and the Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Kansas-Nebraska Act: the law of May 30, 1854, written by Senator Stephen A. Douglas, which organized Kansas and Nebraska as territories, left slavery in them to popular sovereignty, and repealed the Missouri Compromise line of 1820 that had barred slavery from the northern Louisiana Purchase lands

popular sovereignty: Douglas's doctrine that the people of a territory should decide for themselves whether to permit slavery there, offered as a democratic alternative to congressional rule over the territories and tested to destruction in Kansas

Bleeding Kansas: the name the country's newspapers gave the Kansas Territory's guerrilla war of 1855 and 1856, when proslavery and free state settlers, armed by sponsors in Missouri and New England, fought over two rival governments and left some two hundred dead

border ruffians: the name free state settlers gave the Missourians who crossed the border in organized bands to vote in Kansas elections, intimidate free state voters, and sack the town of Lawrence in May 1856

Lecompton Constitution: the proslavery constitution drafted at Lecompton in 1857 and offered to Kansas voters in a referendum arranged so that even rejecting it protected slavery already in the territory, whose fraud split President Buchanan from Douglas and delayed Kansas statehood until 1861

John Brown: the Connecticut born abolitionist who, convinced slavery would end only in blood, led the Pottawatomie killings in Kansas in 1856 and the raid on the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry in 1859, and was hanged by Virginia on December 2, 1859

Charles Sumner: the Massachusetts senator whose two day speech "The Crime Against Kansas" in May 1856 denounced slavery and its southern defenders in personal terms, for which Congressman Preston Brooks beat him nearly to death at his Senate desk

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher

Teach this lesson third in the unit, after the antislavery argument at full voice and before the Republican Party and Dred Scott, in one class period of about 50 minutes. It pairs with the lesson activity, whose five stations carry the act, the Appeal, the Forcing Slavery cartoon, Sumner's speech, and the Lecompton record, and with the unit writing task. Students need the Missouri Compromise of 1820 and the Compromise of 1850 from the earlier lessons, named here as prior knowledge. The lesson matches the Common Core literacy standards for history and social studies on citing textual evidence and determining central ideas, and the C3 Framework on civics and history. Students will learn what the act repealed, what popular sovereignty promised, and what it produced in Kansas, from the fraudulent election of 1855 through the violence of 1856 to Lecompton. Materials: the reading, these notes, and the activity stations.

Exit Ticket Key

1. Popular sovereignty is Douglas's doctrine that the people of a territory should decide for themselves whether to permit slavery there, offered as a democratic alternative to congressional rule over the territories and tested to destruction in Kansas.

2. The act, dated May 30, 1854, repealed the Missouri Compromise line of 1820 and left slavery in Kansas and Nebraska to popular sovereignty.

3. For failing: its first election was decided by voters who rode in from Missouri and rode home, and it produced two governments and a small war. For working as designed: the act set no residency test, no timetable, and no umpire, so imported voting and armed settlement were moves the design left open. Credit either judgment argued from the record.
