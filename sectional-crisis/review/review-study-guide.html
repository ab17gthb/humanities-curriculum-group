Part 3: The Unit as a Whole

Compromise over slavery failed in stages, and each stage narrowed the ground the next settlement could stand on. The Compromise of 1850 held the Union together by enacting its terms bill by bill, but its enforcement clause, the Fugitive Slave Act, put federal commissioners, posses, and troops on northern streets and taught a generation of northerners slavery as a presence in their own towns. The antislavery writers gave that experience its argument, Stowe carrying the slave narratives' evidence into northern parlors, Truth and Jacobs bearing witness in their own persons, Douglass auditing the founding's promises against the slave's present. The Kansas-Nebraska Act then repealed the Missouri line that had bounded slavery's spread for thirty four years, and popular sovereignty, offered as a way to take the question out of Congress, produced in Kansas two governments, a sacked town, a beaten senator, and a small war. A new party rose on the single promise to bar slavery from the territories, and the Supreme Court answered by voiding that promise and denying Black citizenship outright. The debates of 1858 fixed the argument's terms, Brown's raid convinced the South that abolition meant invasion, and in 1860 a president pledged only against slavery's extension was elected without one southern electoral vote. The seceding states said in their own declarations what the break was about: the North's refusal to return fugitives, its hostility to slavery, and a government they held had been made destructive of slavery's protection, and their vice president named slavery the new government's cornerstone.

The war the break caused began as a war for the Union and became a war against slavery because slavery was the rebellion's labor system and its cause. Sumter forced the choice, Bull Run ended the hope of a short war, and the rival strategies, blockade and the Mississippi against endurance and King Cotton, ran the contest toward the longer arithmetic of the North's numbers, factories, and navy. The enslaved moved first: walking to the Union lines under the contraband fiction, they made emancipation a military fact before it was a policy, and Antietam gave Lincoln the standing to make it policy. The Proclamation of January 1, 1863, freed the enslaved in the rebellious states, left the loyal border states untouched, and opened the army's ranks, and some 180,000 Black soldiers thereafter fought for the Union that had enslaved many of them, for equal pay as well as for victory. Gettysburg and Vicksburg in July 1863 ended the Confederacy's hope of winning on northern soil or holding its western half, and the Gettysburg Address restated the war as a test of the proposition that all men are created equal. Grant's coordination and Sherman's march then wore the Confederacy down by arithmetic and by destruction, until Lee's surrender at Appomattox on April 9, 1865, on terms that sent his soldiers home with their horses for the spring planting.

What the war settled can be stated in two sentences, and Lincoln stated its verdict in the Second Inaugural five weeks before his death. The Union is indivisible: no state may dissolve it by its own act, and the question secession raised was answered by arms and has not been reopened by law. Slavery is destroyed as a system: the Proclamation broke it in the rebellious states as a war measure, and the Thirteenth Amendment, passed while the war still ran, abolished it everywhere in the United States. Lincoln's own accounting named American slavery as the offense and the war as its woe, a judgment on both sections, and closed by asking the country to bind up its wounds. What the war left open is the rest of freedom's content. The Proclamation and the amendment ended the legal condition of slavery and said nothing about the freed people's land, their safety in the states that had held them, or their place at the ballot box, and Annie Davis of Maryland, writing the president in August 1864 to ask whether she was free, measured the settlement at its edge, in a loyal state the Proclamation had not named. Those questions stood exactly where April 1865 left them, and answering them is the next unit's work.

Teacher Answer Key

Part 1: Key Terms

Lesson 1: The Compromise of 1850 and the Fugitive Slave Act

Compromise of 1850the package of five measures enacted in September 1850 that admitted California as a free state, organized Utah and New Mexico without a slavery restriction, settled the Texas boundary and assumed its debt, ended the slave trade in the District of Columbia, and enacted a far stricter Fugitive Slave Act, passed bill by bill under Douglas after Clay's single omnibus failed
Fugitive Slave Actthe law of September 18, 1850, that required the return of people accused of escaping slavery on an owner's affidavit, denied the accused a jury trial and the right to testify, paid federal commissioners ten dollars for remanding a prisoner and five for freeing one, and made aiding a fugitive a federal crime
Henry Claythe Kentucky senator who had built the compromises of 1820 and 1833 and returned to the Senate in 1849, at seventy two, to frame the Compromise of 1850, the last settlement of his long career as the Union's peacemaker, dying two years after its passage
Daniel Websterthe Massachusetts senator and orator whose Seventh of March speech in 1850 pleaded for the Compromise in the Union's name, breaking with his antislavery supporters, who mourned him in Whittier's poem "Ichabod" as a fallen champion of freedom
personal liberty lawsthe statutes northern states passed to obstruct the Fugitive Slave Act, forbidding state officers to help in renditions, denying the use of state jails, and granting accused fugitives the jury trials and counsel that federal law denied them
Underground Railroadthe informal network of routes, safe houses, and guides, Black and white, by which enslaved people escaped the South for the free states and Canada, carrying thousands north across the middle decades of the century despite the federal law against aiding them
Harriet Tubmanthe Maryland woman born enslaved as Araminta Ross, who escaped in 1849 and returned south again and again to guide some seventy enslaved people to freedom, served the Union army as a scout, spy, and nurse, and became the most famous conductor on the Underground Railroad

Lesson 2: The Antislavery Argument at Full Voice

Uncle Tom's CabinHarriet Beecher Stowe's antislavery novel, serialized in 1851 and published as a book in March 1852, the best selling American novel of its century, which carried the slave narratives' evidence into northern parlors and made slavery's cruelties a household argument
Harriet Beecher Stowethe Cincinnati teacher and writer, daughter of Lyman Beecher and sister of Henry Ward Beecher, who wrote Uncle Tom's Cabin from the slave narratives and from Josiah Henson's account in particular, and later published a documentary key defending her novel's facts
Sojourner Truththe New York woman born enslaved as Isabella Baumfree, freed under that state's gradual emancipation, who renamed herself in 1843 and traveled as an itinerant preacher for abolition and women's rights, speaking at Akron in 1851
Harriet Jacobsthe North Carolina woman who escaped enslavement after seven years hiding in a crawl space above her grandmother's storeroom, and published Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl in 1861 under the name Linda Brent, the first slave narrative by an American woman published in the United States
slave narrativethe first person account of slavery by a formerly enslaved person, published with sponsors' authenticating documents, the genre that supplied the antislavery movement with its evidence and its witnesses from the 1840s onward
higher lawthe doctrine Senator William H. Seward named in 1850, that a law of God and nature above the Constitution condemned slavery and the Fugitive Slave Act, the argument northern resisters used to justify disobeying a law they held no legislature could rightfully make
Anthony Burnsthe Virginia fugitive arrested in Boston in May 1854 and remanded to slavery after a rendition that filled the city with federal troops and mourning crowds, whose case became the Fugitive Slave Act's most famous northern spectacle

Lesson 3: The Kansas-Nebraska Act and Bleeding Kansas

Kansas-Nebraska Actthe law of May 30, 1854, written by Senator Stephen A. Douglas, which organized Kansas and Nebraska as territories, left slavery in them to popular sovereignty, and repealed the Missouri Compromise line of 1820 that had barred slavery from the northern Louisiana Purchase lands
popular sovereigntyDouglas's doctrine that the people of a territory should decide for themselves whether to permit slavery there, offered as a democratic alternative to congressional rule over the territories and tested to destruction in Kansas
Bleeding Kansasthe name the country's newspapers gave the Kansas Territory's guerrilla war of 1855 and 1856, when proslavery and free state settlers, armed by sponsors in Missouri and New England, fought over two rival governments and left some two hundred dead
border ruffiansthe name free state settlers gave the Missourians who crossed the border in organized bands to vote in Kansas elections, intimidate free state voters, and sack the town of Lawrence in May 1856
Lecompton Constitutionthe proslavery constitution drafted at Lecompton in 1857 and offered to Kansas voters in a referendum arranged so that even rejecting it protected slavery already in the territory, whose fraud split President Buchanan from Douglas and delayed Kansas statehood until 1861
John Brownthe Connecticut born abolitionist who, convinced slavery would end only in blood, led the Pottawatomie killings in Kansas in 1856 and the raid on the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry in 1859, and was hanged by Virginia on December 2, 1859
Charles Sumnerthe Massachusetts senator whose two day speech "The Crime Against Kansas" in May 1856 denounced slavery and its southern defenders in personal terms, for which Congressman Preston Brooks beat him nearly to death at his Senate desk

Lesson 4: The Republican Party and Dred Scott

Republican Partythe party organized in 1854 from the wreckage of the Kansas-Nebraska fight, joining ex-Whigs, Free Soilers, and anti-Nebraska Democrats on the single plank that Congress must bar slavery from the federal territories, the first successful wholly northern party in the country's history
free laborthe northern creed that labor was honorable, that the laborer had a right to rise by his work to property and independence, and that slavery degraded work and monopolized the common lands, the economic faith under the Republican Party's promise of free soil
John C. Frémontthe explorer and army officer called the Pathfinder, the Republican Party's first presidential nominee in 1856, who carried eleven of the sixteen free states and showed both sections that a purely northern party could nearly win the presidency
Dred Scott v. Sandfordthe Supreme Court decision of March 6, 1857, in which Chief Justice Taney held that Black people could not be citizens of the United States, that residence on free soil had not freed Dred Scott, and that Congress had never had power to bar slavery from the territories
Roger B. Taneythe Maryland chief justice who succeeded John Marshall in 1836 and wrote the Dred Scott opinion, attempting to settle the slavery question by constitutional decree and instead voiding the Republican platform and deepening the crisis he meant to end
Abraham Lincolnthe Illinois lawyer and former one term congressman who rose on the Kansas-Nebraska fight to lead his state's Republicans, debated Douglas across Illinois in 1858, and was elected the sixteenth president in 1860 on the platform of barring slavery from the territories
House Divided speechLincoln's address of June 16, 1858, accepting the Republican nomination for the Senate, which argued that the government could not endure permanently half slave and half free and charged that the Nebraska repeal and the Dred Scott decision were parts of one design to make slavery national

Lesson 5: The Debates, John Brown, the Election of 1860, and Secession

Lincoln-Douglas debatesthe seven open air debates between Abraham Lincoln and Stephen A. Douglas across Illinois in the Senate campaign of 1858, three hours each before crowds in the tens of thousands, which made Lincoln a national figure and fixed the terms of the slavery argument
Freeport DoctrineDouglas's answer at Freeport in August 1858, that a territory could keep slavery out despite the Dred Scott decision by refusing to pass the local police laws slavery needed to survive, the answer that saved his Senate seat and forfeited the South
Harpers Ferrythe Virginia town at the meeting of the Potomac and Shenandoah rivers, site of the federal arsenal John Brown seized on October 16, 1859, hoping to arm an enslaved rising, and of the engine house where marines under Robert E. Lee captured him two days later
secessionthe doctrine and act by which eleven slaveholding states, beginning with South Carolina on December 20, 1860, declared their union with the United States dissolved and withdrew, holding that the Union was a compact from which a state could withdraw as it had entered
Crittenden CompromiseSenator John J. Crittenden's last settlement proposal of December 1860, which would have written the Missouri line into the Constitution as an unamendable amendment protecting slavery south of it forever, rejected by the Republicans on Lincoln's instruction and failing in January 1861
Confederate States of Americathe government organized at Montgomery, Alabama, in February 1861 by the first seven seceded states, later eleven, with a constitution modeled on the federal one that named slavery's protection in its text where the original had not
Jefferson Davisthe Mississippi senator and former secretary of war chosen president of the Confederate States in February 1861, who directed the Confederacy's war for four years and was captured in Georgia in May 1865 after Richmond fell

Lesson 6: The War Begins: Sumter to Antietam

Fort Sumterthe federal fort in Charleston harbor whose provisioning became the crisis of Lincoln's first month in office, bombarded by Confederate batteries on April 12, 1861, and surrendered on April 14, the act that opened the Civil War
First Bull Runthe war's first great battle, fought near Manassas, Virginia, on July 21, 1861, before excursion crowds from Washington, whose Confederate victory and Union rout ended the hope of a short war and taught both governments to build real armies
Anaconda PlanGeneral Winfield Scott's strategy of 1861, to strangle the Confederacy by naval blockade of its coast and a drive down the Mississippi that would split it in two, derided as slow by a public demanding a quick blow and adopted in substance as the war became long
blockadethe naval closure of an enemy's ports to trade, proclaimed by Lincoln against the Confederacy in April 1861 and enforced by a navy built from almost nothing to some six hundred vessels, the instrument that strangled the cotton trade on which Confederate finance rested
habeas corpusthe constitutional writ by which a prisoner may demand to be brought before a judge and shown the cause of detention, which Lincoln suspended along the war's military lines from 1861 and Congress authorized in 1863, so that military arrests could proceed without civil trial
George B. McClellanthe organizer who built the Army of the Potomac and commanded it through the Peninsula campaign and Antietam, a brilliant administrator whose caution before the enemy Lincoln finally exhausted, removing him in November 1862
Antietamthe battle of September 17, 1862, near Sharpsburg, Maryland, where McClellan, holding a copy of Lee's lost orders, fought the Army of Northern Virginia to a standstill in the single bloodiest day in American history, ending the first invasion of the North and giving Lincoln ground to issue the Emancipation Proclamation

Lesson 7: Emancipation and the War Transformed, 1863

contrabandthe name General Benjamin Butler gave at Fort Monroe in May 1861 to enslaved people who escaped to Union lines, held as property seized from the enemy rather than returned, the legal fiction under which hundreds of thousands reached freedom before any proclamation freed them
Emancipation ProclamationLincoln's executive order, issued in preliminary form on September 22, 1862, and in final form on January 1, 1863, which declared free the enslaved people in the states and parts of states then in rebellion, left slavery untouched in the loyal border states, and opened the Union army and navy to Black enlistment
United States Colored Troopsthe regiments of Black soldiers raised in the Union army from 1863, some 180,000 men by the war's end, who fought at Port Hudson, Milliken's Bend, and Fort Wagner and campaigned for and won equal pay in June 1864
54th Massachusettsthe first Black regiment raised in the North, recruited in 1863 under Governor John Andrew and Colonel Robert Gould Shaw, whose assault on Fort Wagner that July, at the cost of Shaw and nearly half the regiment, answered the claim that Black men would not fight
Gettysburgthe battle of July 1 to 3, 1863, in Pennsylvania, where the Army of the Potomac under Meade stopped Lee's second invasion of the North in the war's greatest battle, costing the two armies some fifty thousand casualties and ending the Confederacy's hope of winning on northern soil
Vicksburgthe Mississippi River fortress whose surrender to Grant on July 4, 1863, after a six week siege, gave the Union the whole Mississippi, split the Confederacy in two, and, falling the day after Gettysburg ended, made that Independence Day the war's turning point
Gettysburg AddressLincoln's two minute dedication of the soldiers' cemetery at Gettysburg on November 19, 1863, which restated the war as a test of the proposition that all men are created equal and rededicated the living to the dead men's unfinished work

Lesson 8: Total War and Appomattox

Ulysses S. Grantthe Ohio born general who won at Fort Donelson, Shiloh, Vicksburg, and Chattanooga, was made general in chief in March 1864, and directed the coordinated campaigns that wore the Confederacy down, accepting Lee's surrender at Appomattox and later serving two terms as president
total warthe strategy of making war on the enemy's resources and will as well as its armies, destroying railroads, crops, mills, and stores so that the society sustaining the armies felt the war in its households, carried to its fullest by Sherman in Georgia and the Carolinas
William Tecumseh Shermanthe Ohio general who took Atlanta in September 1864, cut loose from his supply lines to march to the sea, and carried total war through Georgia and the Carolinas, writing to Atlanta's mayor that war is cruelty and cannot be refined
March to the SeaSherman's campaign of November and December 1864, in which some sixty two thousand Union soldiers marched from Atlanta to Savannah living off the country and destroying the railroads, mills, and stores in a corridor sixty miles wide, presenting Savannah to Lincoln as a Christmas gift
Thirteenth Amendmentthe constitutional amendment, passed by Congress on January 31, 1865, and ratified that December, that abolished slavery and involuntary servitude everywhere in the United States, except as punishment for a crime, the first of the three Reconstruction amendments and the only one adopted while the war still ran
Appomattox Court Housethe Virginia village where, on April 9, 1865, Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to Grant on terms that paroled the soldiers home with their horses for the spring planting, the surrender that effectively ended the Civil War
Second Inaugural AddressLincoln's address of March 4, 1865, which named American slavery as the offense and the war as its woe, read the conflict as judgment on both sections, and closed by calling the country to bind up its wounds, delivered five weeks before his assassination

Part 2: Review Questions

1. What question did California's application for statehood put to Congress in 1849, and what did Henry Clay propose in answer? California, peopled overnight by the gold rush, applied for statehood as a free state, which would break the Senate's balance of fifteen free states and fifteen slave states and leave the South a permanent minority in one house while its share of the other shrank. Zachary Taylor, a Louisiana slaveholder elected as a war hero, proposed admitting California and New Mexico at once and letting the territories skip the question. Henry Clay, seventy two and returned to the Senate in 1849, laid out his resolutions in January 1850, a package built to give each section a victory to carry home. The omnibus collapsed in July, Clay went home exhausted, and Stephen A. Douglas took the measures up one at a time and passed each with a different majority, Taylor's death that July putting Millard Fillmore, who would sign, in the presidency.

2. State the five terms of the Compromise of 1850 as they were finally enacted. California was admitted as a free state. Utah and New Mexico were organized as territories without a slavery restriction, their statehood constitutions to decide slavery when they came. The Texas boundary was settled and its debt assumed. The slave trade, not slavery itself, was ended in the District of Columbia. And a far stricter Fugitive Slave Act was enacted, the settlement's price and its poison. Both sections' newspapers called the package a final settlement of the slavery question, and northern statehouses began at once to pass the personal liberty laws that would make its enforcement a fight between jurisdictions.

3. How did the Fugitive Slave Act of September 18, 1850, conduct a rendition, and how did northern states answer it? A person accused of escaping slavery could be seized on an owner's affidavit and was heard before federal commissioners rather than judges. The accused was denied a jury trial and the right to testify. A commissioner was paid ten dollars when he remanded a prisoner and five when he freed one, every bystander was liable to be summoned into a posse, and aiding a fugitive, by feeding, hiding, or rescuing, was made a federal crime carrying heavy fines and prison. The northern states answered with personal liberty laws, statutes forbidding state officers to help in renditions, denying the use of state jails, and granting accused fugitives the jury trials and counsel that federal law denied them.

4. What happened to Anthony Burns in Boston in 1854, and what did the rendition show about enforcement in the North? Anthony Burns, a Virginia fugitive, was arrested in Boston in May 1854 and tried before a federal commissioner under the Fugitive Slave Act. He was remanded to slavery and marched to the ship that carried him back between lines of federal troops, while church bells tolled and buildings stood draped in mourning. The rendition was the Act's most famous northern spectacle. It showed that enforcing the law in a hostile city now required the army of the United States on its streets, and that every rendition staged the republic against itself before crowds who had come to watch slavery's officers do their work.

5. From what evidence did Harriet Beecher Stowe build Uncle Tom's Cabin, at what readers was its argument aimed, and how did the South answer the book? Stowe, who had lived eighteen years in Cincinnati across the river from Kentucky, built the novel from the slave narratives and from Josiah Henson's account in particular. Serialized in 1851 and published as a book in March 1852, it carried the narratives' evidence into northern parlors and aimed its argument at the Christian mother's conscience, showing slavery corrupting the enslaver's household as surely as it destroyed the enslaved one, with families parted at the auction block as its recurring wound. It became the best selling American novel of its century. Across the South it was banned, and southern writers answered it with a shelf of anti-Tom novels defending slavery, while translations carried it everywhere abroad.

6. Two texts survive of Sojourner Truth's speech at Akron in May 1851. Who wrote each one down, and when, and what does the difference teach about quoting the speech? Marius Robinson reported the speech for the Anti-Slavery Bugle, which printed his transcription in June 1851, weeks after Truth spoke. Frances Dana Gage, who had presided at the convention, published her version twelve years later, in 1863, and her version gave the speech its famous refrain and its dialect. The two texts are therefore not equal evidence. Robinson wrote at the time, Gage from memory in another decade and for another purpose. The lesson the unit draws is the historian's first question about any famous quotation: who wrote it down, and when. The 1851 text is the one that may be quoted with confidence as what Truth said at Akron.

7. Why did Stephen A. Douglas need Kansas and Nebraska organized in 1854, and what price did the South name for the bill? Douglas, chairman of the Senate's committee on territories, needed the two territories organized so that a transcontinental railroad could run west from Chicago. The South named its price: the Missouri Compromise line of 1820, thirty four years standing, which had barred slavery from the northern Louisiana Purchase lands, had to be repealed in so many words, so that the territories could decide slavery for themselves under Douglas's doctrine of popular sovereignty. The act of May 30, 1854, paid the price. The northern answer came in mass meetings and sermons, and the Whig Party, already strained, was dead within two years, while an anti-Nebraska coalition began gathering everyone the repeal had angered.

8. State what happened in Kansas in 1855 and 1856 that gave Bleeding Kansas its name. Free state settlers sent by New England emigrant aid societies and proslavery settlers backed from Missouri contested the territory from its opening. The territorial election of March 1855 was carried by Missourians who rode in, voted, and rode home, the border ruffians of free state naming, and the proslavery legislature they seated at Lecompton expelled its free state members. The free state settlers answered with a rival government at Topeka. In May 1856 a proslavery posse sacked Lawrence, destroying its presses and hotel, and two days later Congressman Preston Brooks beat Senator Charles Sumner nearly to death at his Senate desk for a speech against Kansas. Three nights after the sack, John Brown and his party killed five proslavery men at Pottawatomie Creek. Guerrilla war followed through 1856 and left some two hundred dead.

9. How was the Lecompton Constitution of 1857 offered to Kansas voters, and what did its fraud do to President Buchanan and to Douglas? The proslavery convention at Lecompton drafted a constitution protecting slavery and offered it in a referendum arranged so that even rejecting it protected the slavery already in the territory, so Kansas voters could not vote slavery out by voting the constitution down. President Buchanan endorsed the constitution. Douglas broke with his own president over it, because the fraud betrayed popular sovereignty itself, the doctrine on which his career stood: the people had not been left free to decide. Congress refused the constitution, the fight split the Democratic Party by section, and Kansas entered the Union at last as a free state in January 1861.

10. On what single plank was the Republican Party organized in 1854, and what did the plank leave untouched? The anti-Nebraska meetings of 1854 named themselves Republican and gathered ex-Whigs, Free Soilers, and anti-Nebraska Democrats on one plank every part of the coalition could hold: Congress must bar slavery from the federal territories. The plank left slavery untouched where state law protected it, in the fifteen slave states, and promised instead to put slavery, in the phrase Lincoln would make famous, in the course of ultimate extinction by fencing it in. The creed beneath the plank was free labor, the northern faith that labor was honorable, that the laborer had a right to rise by his work to property and independence, and that slavery degraded work and monopolized the common lands.

11. State the three holdings of the Dred Scott decision of March 6, 1857. Dred Scott had sued for his family's freedom on the ground that his owner's army postings had carried him to Illinois and to Fort Snelling, free soil both. Chief Justice Roger B. Taney, for a majority of seven, held first that Black people, enslaved or free, were not and could not be citizens of the United States. He held second that Scott's residence on free soil had not freed him. He held third that the Missouri Compromise had been unconstitutional from the start, because Congress had never had power to bar slavery from the territories. That third holding voided the Republican platform itself, the promise to do by law the very thing the Court now said no law could do.

12. What did Lincoln argue in the House Divided speech of June 16, 1858, and how did John C. Frémont's campaign of 1856 stand behind the argument? Accepting the Republican nomination for the Senate at Springfield, Lincoln argued that the government could not endure permanently half slave and half free, and charged that the Kansas-Nebraska repeal, the Dred Scott decision, and the administration's policy were parts of one design tending to make slavery national and perpetual. Frémont's campaign stood behind the warning as arithmetic. In 1856, the party's first national run, the Pathfinder had carried eleven of the sixteen free states while Buchanan won on Pennsylvania and the South. Both sections had read the same returns: a purely northern party could now come within one state of the presidency, and it would not stay short forever.

13. What question did Lincoln press on Douglas at Freeport in August 1858, and what did Douglas's answer cost him? Lincoln pressed one question through the seven debates: if the Supreme Court had held that neither Congress nor a territory could bar slavery, could a territory still keep slavery out? At Freeport, Douglas answered that it could, because slavery needed local police laws to survive, and a territory could defeat it by refusing to pass them. The Freeport Doctrine saved Douglas's Senate seat, since Illinois voters heard a territory kept free. It forfeited the South, whose leaders heard a senator teaching territories to nullify a Supreme Court decision, and who now demanded what his doctrine denied, a federal slave code for the territories, the demand on which the Democratic Party split in 1860.

14. What did John Brown do at Harpers Ferry on October 16, 1859, how did the raid end, and how did the two sections read Brown afterward? Brown and twenty one men, five of them Black, seized the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, hoping to arm an enslaved rising. The rising did not come, the town's first casualty was a free Black railroad porter, Heyward Shepherd, shot by the raiders, and marines under Robert E. Lee stormed the engine house and captured Brown two days later. Virginia tried him for treason against the state and hanged him on December 2, 1859. His courtroom speech and his last written note, that the crimes of this guilty land would never be purged away but with blood, made him a martyr in northern church bells, and in southern minds the proof that abolition meant invasion, and militia companies formed across the South that winter.

15. How was Lincoln elected in 1860, and what did South Carolina's Declaration of the Immediate Causes say the break was about? Four parties contested the election. The Democrats split at Charleston and Baltimore over the southern demand for a federal slave code, nominating Douglas in the North and Breckinridge in the South, while Bell ran on the Union alone. Lincoln, nominated at Chicago on the platform of no extension, was elected with 39.8 percent of the popular vote and a majority of the electoral college, with not one electoral vote from the South and, in ten states, not even a place on the ballot. South Carolina seceded on December 20, 1860, and its declaration charged that the northern states had broken the constitutional compact by refusing their fugitive slave obligations, by nullifying the acts of Congress through their personal liberty laws, and by electing a president whose opinions and purposes were hostile to slavery. Six more states followed by February 1, the Crittenden Compromise failed, and Alexander Stephens stated the new government's cornerstone at Savannah that March: racial inequality, and slavery as the Black man's natural condition.

16. What opened the war at Fort Sumter in April 1861, and what did Lincoln's call for volunteers do to the Upper South? The federal fort in Charleston harbor was running out of provisions, and on April 12, 1861, Confederate batteries opened fire on it. The garrison surrendered on April 14. The next day Lincoln called for seventy five thousand volunteers to suppress the rebellion. The call forced the undecided slave states to choose, and Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina seceded rather than supply troops against the South, the Union's second secession. Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri stayed, held by occupation, political maneuver, and, in Kentucky, a neutrality both sides courted until it broke. The war the call assembled pitted the Union's twenty two million people, factories, railroads, and navy against the Confederacy's nine million, more than a third of them enslaved, and its cotton.

17. State the Anaconda Plan and the Confederacy's countering hope, and state what First Bull Run taught both governments. General Winfield Scott proposed to strangle the Confederacy: a naval blockade of its coast, proclaimed by Lincoln in April 1861, and a drive down the Mississippi that would split the Confederacy in two. Critics derided the plan as slow, but it was adopted in substance as the war became long. The Confederacy chose the defensive, hoping to outlast northern will and win European recognition on the strength of King Cotton, whose leverage collapsed as Britain turned to Indian and Egyptian cotton. On July 21, 1861, at First Bull Run near Manassas, excursion crowds from Washington watched a Confederate victory and a Union rout. The battle ended the hope of a ninety day war and taught both governments to build real armies.

18. What did Antietam settle and leave unsettled on September 17, 1862, and what did Lincoln do in the week that followed? Lee had invaded Maryland, and McClellan, holding a copy of Lee's lost orders, met him near Sharpsburg. The fighting of September 17 was the single bloodiest day in American history, some 22,700 casualties by the usual reckoning. Tactically the armies fought to a standstill, but Lee withdrew, so the first invasion of the North had ended, a strategic Union victory. It gave Lincoln the field he had waited for, and on September 22 he issued the Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation, warning that on January 1 the enslaved people in the rebellious states would be declared free. Two days later his proclamation of September 24 suspended habeas corpus across the country, the war's cost to civil liberty stated in the same week as its new purpose.

19. How did enslaved people reach freedom before any proclamation freed them, and what legal fiction carried them? From the war's first spring, enslaved people freed themselves by walking to the Union lines. At Fort Monroe in May 1861, General Benjamin Butler refused to return three men who had escaped to his lines, holding them as contraband of war, property seized from the enemy rather than returned to him. The fiction let the Union army absorb the labor of the people who came while the government still insisted the war was only for the Union. Under it, hundreds of thousands reached freedom before any proclamation freed them, with the First Confiscation Act and the District of Columbia's compensated emancipation of April 1862 following behind the fact the fugitives had made.

20. State what the Emancipation Proclamation of January 1, 1863, did, what it left untouched, and what it changed in the war. The Proclamation, issued in preliminary form on September 22, 1862, declared free the enslaved people in the states and parts of states then in rebellion. It left slavery untouched in the loyal border states, Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri, and in the occupied areas it exempted. Critics charged that it freed people where the Union could not yet enforce freedom and stood aside where it could. Its supporters answered from the record: it struck at the rebellion's labor system, since every person who reached Union lines was now free by law and not by fiction, it ended any European sympathy for the South, and it opened the Union army and navy to Black enlistment, under which some 180,000 Black soldiers served by the war's end.

21. What happened at Gettysburg and Vicksburg in the first days of July 1863, and what did the 54th Massachusetts prove that same month? Lee invaded the North a second time, and the Army of the Potomac under Meade stopped him at Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, on July 1 to 3, in the war's greatest battle, at a cost of some fifty thousand casualties to the two armies. The day after the battle ended, on July 4, Vicksburg, the Mississippi River fortress, surrendered to Grant after a six week siege, giving the Union the whole river and splitting the Confederacy in two. That Independence Day stands as the war's turning point. Later in July the 54th Massachusetts, the first Black regiment raised in the North, assaulted Fort Wagner at the cost of Colonel Robert Gould Shaw and nearly half the regiment, and answered in action the claim that Black men would not fight.

22. How did Grant direct the war from March 1864, and what did Sherman's March to the Sea do? Grant, made general in chief in March 1864, directed coordinated campaigns across every theater at once, holding that the Confederacy's armies, not its cities, were the objective, and he paid the Overland campaign's ledger through the Wilderness, Spotsylvania, and Cold Harbor to the siege of Petersburg. Sherman took Atlanta in September 1864, a fall that arrived in time to save Lincoln's reelection, then cut loose from his supply lines. From November into December 1864 some sixty two thousand Union soldiers marched from Atlanta to Savannah living off the country, destroying the railroads, mills, and stores in a corridor sixty miles wide, total war carried against the resources and will that sustained the Confederate armies, and Sherman presented Savannah to Lincoln as a Christmas gift.

23. State the terms Grant gave Lee at Appomattox on April 9, 1865, and what the Thirteenth Amendment had already done that winter. Richmond was evacuated and burned on April 2 and 3, Lee's retreat was cut off at Appomattox Court House, and on April 9, 1865, Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia. Grant's terms, generous by Lincoln's standing instruction, paroled the soldiers home, let officers keep their sidearms, and let the men take their horses home for the spring planting, and Grant ordered no rejoicing over a fallen foe. The surrender effectively ended the Civil War. That winter, on January 31, 1865, Congress had passed the Thirteenth Amendment, which the states ratified in December: slavery and involuntary servitude were abolished everywhere in the United States, except as punishment for a crime, the only one of the three Reconstruction amendments adopted while the war still ran.

24. What does the Second Inaugural Address of March 4, 1865, name as the war's cause and its meaning, and what does it ask the country to do at the end, and what stood unanswered when Lincoln was killed? Lincoln named American slavery as the offense, one eighth of the whole population held as slaves and making war to strengthen and perpetuate their interest, and read the war itself as the woe due to those by whom the offense came, a judgment falling on both sections, North and South alike. He closed by asking the country to bind up its wounds and to care for the soldier, his widow, and his orphan. Five weeks later, on April 14, Booth shot him at Ford's Theatre, and he died on April 15. The war had settled that the Union was indivisible and that slavery was destroyed as a system. It left standing, exactly where April 1865 left them, the freed people's claims to land, to safety, and to the vote, which the next unit takes up.
