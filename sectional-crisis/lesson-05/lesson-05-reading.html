The Debates, John Brown, the Election of 1860, and Secession

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Seven Meetings on the Prairie

In the summer and fall of 1858, Abraham Lincoln challenged Senator Stephen A. Douglas for his seat, and the two men met seven times in open air debates across Illinois, at Ottawa, Freeport, Jonesboro, Charleston, Galesburg, Quincy, and Alton. Each meeting lasted three hours. Crowds in the tens of thousands stood in fields and town squares to listen, and newspapers across the country printed every word. Lincoln was a former one term congressman leading a party only four years old. Douglas was the leading Democrat in the Senate and the author of the Kansas-Nebraska Act. The debates made Lincoln a national figure before the legislature chose between them.

B. Freeport and the Doctrine

Lincoln pressed one question hardest at Freeport, the second meeting, on August 27. The Supreme Court had held in the Dred Scott decision that Congress could not bar slavery from a territory. Could the people of a territory still keep slavery out, Lincoln asked, against a slaveholder who chose to settle there? Douglas answered yes. A territory could exclude slavery, he said, by refusing to pass the local police laws that slavery needed to survive. That answer became known as the Freeport Doctrine. It satisfied Illinois voters, and Douglas kept his seat. In the South it read as defiance of the Court. Southern leaders began demanding what the doctrine denied them, a federal slave code for the territories.

C. The Racial Ground of the Debates

The debates were argued on racial ground that this lesson states without softening. At Charleston, Lincoln said he had no purpose to bring about social and political equality between the races, that a physical difference would forever forbid the two races from living together as equals, and that he favored assigning the superior position to the white race. Douglas built his campaign on the charge that Lincoln preached Black equality, and said the same things more bluntly. Yet Lincoln held beside those statements the position he had argued at Peoria in 1854. Slavery, he held, was a moral, social, and political wrong. The nation had no right to spread it. His party would stop its spread and put it on the road toward its final end. Both statements are his, from the same campaign.

D. The Raid on Harpers Ferry

On the night of October 16, 1859, John Brown and twenty one men, five of them Black, crossed into Harpers Ferry, Virginia, and seized the federal arsenal there. Brown meant to arm enslaved people and begin a rising that would end slavery. The rising did not come. The first man the raiders killed was Heyward Shepherd, a free Black railroad porter shot on the bridge. Townspeople and militia trapped the party, and on October 18 United States Marines under Colonel Robert E. Lee stormed the arsenal engine house, where Brown made his last stand. Ten raiders died, two of them Brown's sons. Brown was wounded and captured alive.

E. The Trial, the Gallows, and the Note

Virginia tried Brown at Charles Town for treason against the state, conspiracy, and murder. On November 2, 1859, asked whether he had anything to say before sentence, Brown rose from his cot and spoke. He denied intending murder or rebellion, admitted one design only, to free enslaved people, and told the court that the same act, done for the rich and powerful, would have been called worthy of reward. He was hanged on December 2. That morning he wrote that the crimes of this guilty land would never be purged away but with blood. In the North he was mourned as a martyr. In the South his raid was read as proof that abolition meant invasion, and militia companies formed across the region that winter.

F. Four Parties and an Election

The argument of 1858 decided the election of 1860 before it began. The Democratic Party, meeting first at Charleston and then at Baltimore, split over the southern demand for a federal slave code. The northern wing nominated Douglas on popular sovereignty. The southern wing nominated John C. Breckinridge on the slave code platform. John Bell ran for a fourth party on the Union alone. The Republicans, meeting at Chicago, nominated Lincoln on the platform of no extension of slavery. Lincoln won with 39.8 percent of the popular vote and a clear majority of the electoral college. He carried no southern state, and in ten slave states his name did not appear on the ballot at all.

G. Secession Answers in Its Own Words

South Carolina answered first. On December 20, 1860, its convention voted the state out of the Union and published a Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession. The declaration charged that the northern states had broken the compact by refusing to return people escaping slavery, and that a president hostile to slavery had been elected. Six more states followed by February 1, 1861. In the Senate, John J. Crittenden of Kentucky offered a last compromise, extending the Missouri line by an amendment no later amendment could change. Lincoln instructed Republicans to refuse compromise on extension, and the plan failed in January. The seven seceded states organized the Confederate States of America at Montgomery, Alabama, on February 4, and chose Jefferson Davis as president. In March, Vice President Alexander H. Stephens said at Savannah that the government's cornerstone was the claim that Black people were not equal to white people, and that slavery was their natural condition. On March 4 Lincoln took the oath in Washington, pledged not to interfere with slavery in the states, and held the Union perpetual, while Fort Sumter's provisions ran out in Charleston harbor.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the terms of the seven debates. Seven open air meetings across Illinois in 1858, at Ottawa, Freeport, Jonesboro, Charleston, Galesburg, Quincy, and Alton, each lasting three hours, between Abraham Lincoln, a former one term congressman leading a four year old party, and Senator Stephen A. Douglas, the Kansas-Nebraska Act's author. Crowds in the tens of thousands stood in fields and town squares, and newspapers nationwide printed every word. The debates made Lincoln a national figure before the legislature decided between them.

2. State the Freeport question, the answer, and its double effect. At Freeport on August 27, Lincoln asked whether the people of a territory could still keep slavery out, against a slaveholder who settled there, after the Supreme Court had held in Dred Scott that Congress could not bar slavery from a territory. Douglas answered yes: a territory could exclude slavery by refusing to pass the local police laws slavery needed to survive. That answer, the Freeport Doctrine, satisfied Illinois voters and Douglas kept his seat. In the South it read as defiance of the Court, and southern leaders began demanding a federal slave code for the territories, the demand that split their party in 1860.

3. Carry both of Lincoln's positions together. At Charleston, Lincoln said he had no purpose to bring about social and political equality between the races, that a physical difference would forever forbid the two races from living together as equals, and that he favored assigning the superior position to the white race. Beside that he held the position argued at Peoria in 1854: slavery was a moral, social, and political wrong, the nation had no right to spread it, and his party would stop its spread and put it on the road toward its final end. Douglas built his campaign on the charge that Lincoln preached Black equality, saying the same denials more bluntly. An answer that keeps only one set of statements has halved the record.

4. Narrate the raid. On the night of October 16, 1859, John Brown and twenty one men, five of them Black, seized the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, meaning to arm enslaved people and begin a rising that would end slavery. The rising did not come. The first man the raiders killed was Heyward Shepherd, a free Black railroad porter shot on the bridge. United States Marines under Colonel Robert E. Lee stormed the engine house on October 18. Ten raiders died, two of them Brown's sons. Brown was wounded and captured alive, which is why the nation heard him again.

5. Answer the lesson question. Strong answers run the chain in order. The Freeport Doctrine saved Douglas in Illinois and forfeited the South, whose slave code demand split the Democratic Party at Charleston and Baltimore in 1860, so Lincoln won the presidency with 39.8 percent of the popular vote, a majority of the electoral college, no southern state, and no place on the ballot in ten slave states. Brown's raid had taught the South to read a Republican victory as invasion. South Carolina seceded on December 20, 1860, six more states followed by February 1, and the Crittenden Compromise failed in January on Lincoln's instruction to refuse compromise on extension. The seceded states then stated their grounds themselves: South Carolina's declaration charged the North with breaking the compact over fugitive returns and electing a president hostile to slavery, and Stephens named racial inequality and slavery as the new government's cornerstone. The argument became the break when each side's stated settlement, containment, a slave code, the sword, and departure, had closed every road the others could take.
