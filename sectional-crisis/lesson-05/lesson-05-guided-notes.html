Guided Notes: The Debates, John Brown, and Secession

Lesson question: How did the argument of 1858 become the break of 1860 and 1861?

Unit question: How did compromise over slavery finally fail, and what did the war it caused settle about slavery and the Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Lincoln-Douglas debates: the seven open air debates between Abraham Lincoln and Stephen A. Douglas across Illinois in the Senate campaign of 1858, three hours each before crowds in the tens of thousands, which made Lincoln a national figure and fixed the terms of the slavery argument

Freeport Doctrine: Douglas's answer at Freeport in August 1858, that a territory could keep slavery out despite the Dred Scott decision by refusing to pass the local police laws slavery needed to survive, the answer that saved his Senate seat and forfeited the South

Harpers Ferry: the Virginia town at the meeting of the Potomac and Shenandoah rivers, site of the federal arsenal John Brown seized on October 16, 1859, hoping to arm an enslaved rising, and of the engine house where marines under Robert E. Lee captured him two days later

secession: the doctrine and act by which eleven slaveholding states, beginning with South Carolina on December 20, 1860, declared their union with the United States dissolved and withdrew, holding that the Union was a compact from which a state could withdraw as it had entered

Crittenden Compromise: Senator John J. Crittenden's last settlement proposal of December 1860, which would have written the Missouri line into the Constitution as an unamendable amendment protecting slavery south of it forever, rejected by the Republicans on Lincoln's instruction and failing in January 1861

Confederate States of America: the government organized at Montgomery, Alabama, in February 1861 by the first seven seceded states, later eleven, with a constitution modeled on the federal one that named slavery's protection in its text where the original had not

Jefferson Davis: the Mississippi senator and former secretary of war chosen president of the Confederate States in February 1861, who directed the Confederacy's war for four years and was captured in Georgia in May 1865 after Richmond fell

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher

Teach this lesson fifth in the unit, after the Republican Party and Dred Scott in Lesson 4 and before the war's opening in Lesson 6; the debates take up the platform and the decision Lesson 4 left standing, and the lesson ends on Fort Sumter, where Lesson 6 begins. It pairs with the lesson reading, the slide deck, and the Break File activity, whose stations quote four audited bank records, the two Ottawa speeches of August 21, 1858, South Carolina's Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession, and the Cornerstone Speech, plus John Brown's address to the court of November 2, 1859, from the named transcription stated in the activity. Students need Lesson 4's Dred Scott decision and Republican platform, and Lesson 3's John Brown in Kansas; no other prior knowledge is assumed. The lesson matches the Common Core literacy standards for history and social studies, reading and citing informational text and writing evidence based arguments, and the C3 Framework, Dimension 2, History indicators on cause and consequence and on evaluating sources. Students will learn how the Freeport Doctrine split the Democratic Party, how Brown's raid changed what each section believed about the other, and how the seceding states stated their own grounds for leaving. Timing: one class period of about fifty minutes for the reading and notes, with the activity in a second period. Materials: the reading, these notes, the activity, and the deck or its printed teacher notes.

Summary table. The debates, 1858: seven open air meetings across Illinois, three hours each before crowds in the tens of thousands, which made Lincoln a national figure and fixed the terms of the slavery argument. Freeport: Douglas answered that a territory could keep slavery out despite Dred Scott by refusing the local police laws slavery needed, an answer that saved his Senate seat and forfeited the South, whose leaders demanded a federal slave code. The racial ground: Lincoln at Charleston disavowed social and political equality and favored the superior position for the white race, and held beside it his Peoria position that slavery was a moral, social, and political wrong whose spread must be stopped. Harpers Ferry: Brown and twenty one men, five of them Black, seized the arsenal on October 16, 1859, killed Heyward Shepherd first, and were stormed in the engine house by marines under Lee on October 18; Brown's speech and hanging made him a northern martyr and southern proof of invasion. Election and secession: the Democracy split over the slave code, Lincoln won with 39.8 percent and no southern state, South Carolina seceded on December 20, 1860, the Crittenden Compromise failed, and the Confederate States organized at Montgomery under Jefferson Davis, with Stephens naming slavery the cornerstone.

Exit ticket 1. The Freeport Doctrine is Douglas's answer at Freeport in August 1858, that a territory could keep slavery out despite the Dred Scott decision by refusing to pass the local police laws slavery needed to survive. The decision it answered is Dred Scott v. Sandford of March 6, 1857.

Exit ticket 2. At Charleston, Lincoln said he had no purpose to bring about social and political equality between the races, that a physical difference would forever forbid the two races from living together as equals, and that he favored assigning the superior position to the white race. Beside it he held his Peoria position of 1854: slavery was a moral, social, and political wrong, the nation had no right to spread it, and his party would stop its spread. Credit requires both halves.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that runs the chain: the Freeport Doctrine split the Democracy over the slave code demand, Lincoln won in 1860 with 39.8 percent and no southern electoral vote, and the seceding states answered in their own words, South Carolina charging the North with breaking the compact over fugitive returns, or Stephens naming racial inequality and slavery as the Confederacy's cornerstone, while the Crittenden Compromise failed and compromise closed.
