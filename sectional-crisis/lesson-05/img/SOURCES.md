# Image and Source Record: Lesson 5, The Debates, John Brown, the Election of 1860, and Secession

All images are public domain as stated by the holding institution or by Wikimedia Commons on the file page cited. Deck crops live in slides-build/img/ and were cropped from the downloads listed here. Document copies live in assets/ and img/.

## Abraham Lincoln, ambrotype portrait, August 1858 (lincoln-1858.jpg; deck crop lincoln-port.jpg; 16 by 9 master title-169.jpg)

- Title: Abraham Lincoln, three quarter length portrait, seated, in ambrotype case (the 1858 ambrotype of Lincoln, taken days after the first debate at Ottawa)
- Creator: Attributed to the ambrotype portrait sitting of August 1858 (the file page presents the Library of Congress ambrotype; a copy of W. H. Pearson's Macomb, Illinois ambrotype of August 26, 1858 is recorded by the Wisconsin Historical Society as the portrait taken five days after the Ottawa debate)
- Date: August 1858
- Holder: Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division (ambrotype in its case, as downloaded)
- Source: Downloaded during this build from Wikimedia Commons, file "Abraham Lincoln, 1858.jpg," by way of the Commons Special:FilePath service (600 by 758 pixels at width 1200 request), opened and verified visually as the beardless Lincoln of the debate year in the ambrotype's ornate gilt case.
- Public domain basis: A photograph created in the United States in 1858. United States works of that date are in the public domain, and the Commons file page marks the work public domain.
- Use rule for this lesson: the portrait appears on the deck title slide (portrait crop), at Station 1 of the activity, and on the notebook page, captioned as the August 1858 ambrotype. It is Lincoln as the debaters' audiences saw him: beardless, and before the presidency.

- Title: Hon. Stephen A. Douglas, Ill.
- Creator: Mathew Brady studio (photograph credited to Mathew Brady)
- Date: Between 1855 and 1861 (Brady studio portrait; the National Archives record is undated within Douglas's Senate years)
- Holder: National Archives, record 528297 (Brady-Handy collection negative)
- Source: Downloaded during this build from Wikimedia Commons, file "Hon. Stephen A. Douglas, Ill - NARA - 528297.jpg," by way of the Commons Special:FilePath service (1280 by 1504 pixels), opened and verified visually as the full length Brady portrait of Douglas standing at a draped table.
- Public domain basis: A United States federal government archival photograph of a work created before 1861; in the public domain, as the Commons file page marks it.
- Use rule for this lesson: the portrait appears on the deck's Section B divider (portrait crop), the Freeport section, since the doctrine taught there is Douglas's answer.

## John Brown, daguerreotype portrait attributed to Augustus Washington (brown.jpg; deck crop brown-port.jpg)

- Title: John Brown (abolitionist)
- Creator: Attributed to Augustus Washington, daguerreotypist
- Date: About 1846 to 1847 (the daguerreotype likeness of Brown circulated in his lifetime)
- Holder: National Portrait Gallery, Smithsonian Institution (the Washington daguerreotype of Brown)
- Source: Downloaded during this build from Wikimedia Commons, file "John Brown (abolitionist).jpg," by way of the Commons Special:FilePath service (966 by 900 pixels), opened and verified visually as the clean shaven, high foreheaded Brown of the early daguerreotype likeness.
- Public domain basis: A daguerreotype created in the United States about 1846. Copyright has long expired, the work is in the public domain in the United States, and the Commons file page marks it public domain.
- Use rule for this lesson: the portrait appears on the deck's Section D divider (portrait crop) and at Station 3 of the activity. Captions carry the attribution and the about 1846 to 1847 date, because this is Brown a dozen years before Harpers Ferry, not a portrait of 1859. No photograph of the bearded Brown of 1859 whose file page could be verified was located during this build; see Images searched for and not used.

## Texts quoted in this lesson, with their editions

- Lincoln's reply at the Ottawa debate, August 21, 1858: quoted verbatim and whole, as the record carries it with its cuts marked, in the activity (Station 1) and in shortened form with cuts marked in the quick check, from the audited TPSP record lincoln-ottawa-debate-reply-1858 in sectional-civil-war.json; the edition named in that record is Abraham Lincoln, 'Reply at the Ottawa Debate,' 1858 (Political Debates, 1860 first edition; punctuation normalized).
- Douglas's speech at the Ottawa debate, August 21, 1858: quoted verbatim and whole, as the record carries it with its cuts marked, in the activity (Station 2), from the audited TPSP record douglas-ottawa-debate-1858 in sectional-civil-war.json; the edition named in that record is Stephen A. Douglas, 'Speech at the Ottawa Debate,' 1858 (Political Debates, 1860 first edition).
- John Brown's address to the court, November 2, 1859: quoted verbatim in excerpt in the activity (Station 3), from the transcription in The World's Famous Orations, Volume 9 (1906), as carried by Wikisource, fetched and verified during this build (text compared against the WGBH Africans in America transcription of the same address, which agrees in wording at every passage quoted). Cuts are marked with ellipses in the activity. The double hyphens in the quoted text are the transcription's own punctuation and are reproduced as printed there.
- South Carolina's Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession, December 24, 1860: quoted verbatim and whole, as the record carries it with its cuts marked, in the activity (Station 4), from the audited TPSP record south-carolina-secession-declaration-1860 in sectional-civil-war.json; the edition named in that record is South Carolina Secession Convention, 'Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession,' 1860 (Avalon Project, Yale Law School).
- Alexander H. Stephens's Cornerstone Speech, March 21, 1861: quoted verbatim in excerpt in the activity (Station 5), with the cut marked, and in one sentence in the quick check, from the audited TPSP record stephens-cornerstone-speech-1861 in sectional-civil-war.json; the edition named in that record is Alexander H. Stephens, 'Cornerstone Speech,' 1861 (American Rhetoric; after Henry Cleveland, Alexander H. Stephens in Public and Private, 1886).
- Lincoln's Charleston statements and Peoria position: carried in the reading, the guided notes, and the deck as stated positions in prose, without quotation marks, following the skeleton's direction that both be carried plainly together. No verbatim Charleston or Peoria text from a named edition was verified during this build, so none is quoted anywhere in the lesson.
- Brown's last written note of December 2, 1859: carried in the reading as reported prose (that the crimes of this guilty land would never be purged away but with blood), following the skeleton's own statement of it, and not presented as a verified verbatim quotation from a named edition.

## Images searched for and not used

- Jefferson Davis portrait (davis.jpg, downloaded to img/ as a master only): the Commons file "Jefferson Davis portrait.jpg" is a painting of Davis in old age, white bearded, made after the war. It was downloaded and verified as an image during this build and is recorded here for traceability. It is not placed in any delivered document or in the deck, because it shows the Davis of the 1880s, not the president chosen in February 1861, and this lesson does not present a later likeness as a period one.
- A photograph or period print of the Harpers Ferry engine house, and a photograph of the bearded John Brown of 1859, were searched for during this build. Period prints of the storming of the engine house exist in the Library of Congress (Frank Leslie's Illustrated Newspaper, October 29, 1859), but no downloadable file whose Commons file page could be verified was located in this build's searches, and the 1859 Brown portrait file names tried on Commons returned no file. The raid is therefore taught in the deck with the verified Brown daguerreotype likeness and in words and a stat slide, and no catalog number is claimed for any image not downloaded.
- No image of violence is used anywhere in this lesson, following the unit's image discipline. The storming of the engine house is narrated, not pictured.
