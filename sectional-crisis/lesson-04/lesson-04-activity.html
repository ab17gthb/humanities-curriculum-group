The Dred Scott File

Directions: The five stations below are the record of the slavery question as a party and a court tried to settle it, from the Republican platform of 1856 to Lincoln's answer of 1858. Study each station and answer its questions. At every station, state what the record claims the Constitution says about slavery and citizenship. In Part 2, judge whether the Dred Scott decision was the Court misreading the Constitution or reading its proslavery logic honestly to its end, and defend your judgment.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: The Dred Scott Majority Opinion, March 6, 1857

Source: Chief Justice Roger B. Taney, Dred Scott v. Sandford majority opinion, 1857, as carried in the audited TPSP bank record (Justia, 60 U.S. 393, as the record's source line names). Cuts are marked with ellipses.

"The question before us is whether the class of persons described in the plea in abatement compose a portion of this people, and are constituent members of this sovereignty? We think they are not, and that they are not included, and were not intended to be included, under the word "citizens" in the Constitution, and can therefore claim none of the rights and privileges which that instrument provides for and secures to citizens of the United States. On the contrary, they were at that time considered as a subordinate and inferior class of beings who had been subjugated by the dominant race, and, whether emancipated or not, yet remained subject to their authority, and had no rights or privileges but such as those who held the power and the Government might choose to grant them."

"[...] They had for more than a century before been regarded as beings of an inferior order, and altogether unfit to associate with the white race either in social or political relations, and so far inferior that they had no rights which the white man was bound to respect, and that the negro might justly and lawfully be reduced to slavery for his benefit. [...] He was bought and sold, and treated as an ordinary article of merchandise and traffic whenever a profit could be made by it."

State the opinion's answer to its opening question, in its own terms: who is not included under the word citizens, and what rights follow from that exclusion. Then state the history the opinion offers as its evidence, naming the length of time it claims and the two things it says a Black person could lawfully be treated as. This is an opinion written to end a political argument. What does its choice to begin with citizenship, rather than with Scott's residence in Illinois, allow it to settle before the facts of his case are reached?

Station 2: Lincoln's House Divided Speech, June 16, 1858

Source: Abraham Lincoln, the House Divided speech, June 16, 1858, as carried in the audited TPSP bank record (Gilder Lehrman Institute, as the record's source line names). The record's spelling and punctuation are preserved, including "can not," "lawfull," and "previleges." Cuts are marked with ellipses.

"[...] Why, Kansas is neither the whole, nor a tithe of the real question. "A house divided against itself can not stand" I believe this government can not endure permanently, half slave, and half free. I expressed this belief a year ago; and subsequent developments have but confirmed me. I do not expect the Union to be dissolved. I do not expect the house to fall; but I do expect it will cease to be divided. It will become all one thing, or all the other. Either the opponents of slavery will arrest the further spread of it, and put it in course of ultimate extinction; or its advocates will push it forward till it shall become alike lawfull in all the states, old, as well as new. Do you doubt it? Study the Dred Scott decision, and then see, how little, even now, remains to be done. That decision may be reduced to three points. The first is, that a negro can not be a citizen. That point is made in order to deprive the negro in every possible event, of the benefit of that provision of the U. S Constitution which declares that: "The citizens of each State shall be entitled to all previleges and immunities of citizens in the several States." The second point is, that the U. S constitution protects slavery, as property, in all the U. S. territories, and that neither congress, nor the people of the territories, nor any other power, can prohibit it, at any time prior to the formation of State constitutions. This point is made, in order that the territories may safely be filled up with slaves, before the formation of State constitutions, and thereby to embarrass the free states. [...]"

State Lincoln's prediction in one sentence, naming the two ends he says the government will reach. Then state the two points into which he reduces the Dred Scott decision, and the purpose he assigns to each. Lincoln delivered this speech accepting a Senate nomination, speaking to his own party. What does his reduction of the decision let his listeners do with a long court opinion, and what does the word "design," implied by his assigning purposes, charge that a mere summary would not?

Station 3: Douglass's Letter to Hugh Auld, October 4, 1857

Source: Frederick Douglass, letter to Hugh Auld, Rochester, October 4, 1857, as carried in the audited TPSP bank record (Gilder Lehrman Institute, as the record's source line names). This was a private letter, written not for publication, as the letter itself states. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

"[...] It is twenty years since I ran away from you, or rather not from you but from Slavery, and since then I have often felt a strong desire to hold a little correspondence with you and to learn something of the position and prospects of your dear children. They were dear to me - and are still - indeed I feel nothing but kindness for you all - I love you, but hate Slavery [...]"

"[...] I could have lived with you during life in freedom though I ran away from you so unceremoniously, I did not know how soon I might be sold."

State what Douglass asks Auld for, and the distinction he draws in the sentence about the twenty years: from whom, he says, did he not run, and from what did he run. This letter makes no constitutional argument at all. Set it beside Station 1, written seven months earlier. What does the letter show about the man the opinion placed outside the Constitution's people, and why might a private letter show it in a way a public speech could not?

Station 4: The Republican Platform of 1856

Source: Republican Party Platform of 1856, adopted June 1856, as transcribed by the American Presidency Project (Peters and Woolley), fetched in full during this lesson's preparation. Spelling follows the transcription, including its double hyphen in the last resolution quoted. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

"[...] who are opposed to the repeal of the Missouri Compromise; to the policy of the present Administration; to the extension of Slavery into Free Territory; in favor of the admission of Kansas as a Free State [...] do resolve as follows: [...]"

"[...] That we deny the authority of Congress, of a Territorial Legislation, of any individual, or association of individuals, to give legal existence to Slavery in any Territory of the United States, while the present Constitution shall be maintained."

"Resolved: That the Constitution confers upon Congress sovereign powers over the Territories of the United States for their government; and that in the exercise of this power, it is both the right and the imperative duty of Congress to prohibit in the Territories those twin relics of barbarism--Polygamy, and Slavery."

State the platform's two constitutional claims: what no authority may do, in the second passage, and what Congress not only may but must do, in the third. The platform denies that anyone can give slavery legal existence in a territory, and in the same document calls prohibiting it a duty. Read beside Station 1, decided nine months later: which of these two claims did Taney's third holding destroy, and what was left of the platform after it?

Station 5: Justice Curtis's Dissent in Dred Scott, 1857

Source: Mr. Justice Curtis, dissenting, in Dred Scott v. Sandford, 60 U.S. 393, as transcribed by Teaching American History from the United States Reports text, fetched during this lesson's preparation. Spelling follows the transcription. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

"It has been often asserted that the Constitution was made exclusively by and for the white race. It has already been shown that, in five of the thirteen original states, colored persons then possessed the elective franchise, and were among those by whom the Constitution was ordained and established. If so, it is not true, in point of fact, that the Constitution was made exclusively by the white race. And that it was made exclusively for the white race is, in my opinion, not only an assumption not warranted by anything in the Constitution, but contradicted by its opening declaration that it was ordained and established by the people of the United States, for themselves and their posterity. [...]"

"I dissent, therefore, from that part of the opinion of the majority of the court in which it is held that a person of African descent cannot be a citizen of the United States, and I regret I must go further and dissent both from what I deem their assumption of authority to examine the constitutionality of the act of Congress commonly called the Missouri Compromise Act and the grounds and conclusions announced in their opinion. [...]"

State Curtis's factual answer to Taney: in how many of the original states did colored persons hold the elective franchise at ratification, and what follows, in his argument, about who made the Constitution. Then state his second objection, about the Court's authority to reach the Missouri Compromise at all. Curtis and Taney both claim to be reading the founding generation's record. Name the piece of that record Curtis cites that Taney's opinion does not answer, and state why a vote is harder evidence than an opinion about opinion.

Part 2: The Closing Verdict

The five stations, with the reading, are the evidence for your judgment of what the Court did in Dred Scott. Sort first, then judge.

The sorting. Enter each station number under what it claims, in one line each, with one quoted phrase or named fact from the station as the evidence: the Constitution excludes Black people from citizenship, the Constitution protects slavery in the territories as property, the Constitution gives Congress power and duty over the territories, the founding record includes Black citizens, and the excluded man writing his own history. Station 3 makes no constitutional claim; sort it by what it shows instead.

Your verdict. Judge whether the Dred Scott decision was the Court misreading the Constitution or reading its proslavery logic honestly to its end. Defend the judgment in a paragraph. Your verdict must use at least three stations' evidence, cited by number, and it must account for Station 5, where a sitting justice answered the majority from the ratification record, and for Station 4, whose central promise the decision voided.

The strongest counterclaim. A classmate argues for the verdict you did not choose, and adds that the decision settled the question as courts are meant to settle questions. Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: The Dred Scott Majority Opinion

The exclusion and its evidence. The opinion answers that the class described in the plea, people of African descent whose ancestors were imported and sold as slaves, are not and were not intended to be included under the word citizens in the Constitution, and can claim none of the rights and privileges that instrument secures to citizens. Its evidence is a claim about opinion held, in its words, for more than a century before: that this class had been regarded as beings of an inferior order with no rights which the white man was bound to respect, and that a Black person might justly and lawfully be reduced to slavery for his benefit and be bought and sold as an ordinary article of merchandise and traffic. Beginning with citizenship lets the Court dispose of the case before jurisdiction exists: if Scott is not a citizen, no federal court may hear him, and everything said afterward about free soil and Congress is said by a court that had already decided it need not hear the plaintiff at all. Students should see the sequence as the opinion's architecture, not an accident of order.

Station 2: Lincoln's House Divided Speech

The prediction and the two points. Lincoln predicts that the government cannot endure permanently half slave and half free and will become all one thing or all the other, either slavery arrested and placed in course of ultimate extinction, or slavery pushed forward until lawful in all the states. He reduces the decision to two points: first, that a Black person cannot be a citizen, made, he says, to strip Black people of the privileges and immunities clause's protection in every event; second, that the Constitution protects slavery as property in all the territories and no power can prohibit it before statehood, made so the territories may be filled with slaves before their constitutions form. The reduction turns a long opinion into two sentences a crowd can carry, and the assigned purposes convert a ruling into a design, a set of moves made in order to reach a result, which is the charge Lincoln presses through the whole speech. A summary reports what the Court held. Lincoln's version tells his party what the Court was doing, and the difference is the speech's political work.

Station 3: Douglass's Letter to Hugh Auld

The request and the distinction. Douglass asks Auld to write and tell him the year he came to live with the Aulds on Aliceanna Street, the year Mr. Beacham built the frigate, and asks for news of Auld's children. The distinction is exact: it is twenty years since he ran away, not from Auld, but from slavery, and he writes that he feels nothing but kindness for the family, that he loves Auld and hates slavery. Against Station 1, the letter shows a man the opinion described as merchandise conducting his own affairs, recovering his own childhood from the household that owned it, choosing courtesy where the opinion allowed him no standing at all. The privacy is the point. A speech performs for an audience. This letter was written, as it says, not for publication, so its kindness and its precision are not strategy for a crowd; they are the record of a mind the Court's categories could not describe, preserved because Douglass wrote it for one reader.

Station 4: The Republican Platform of 1856

The two claims and the holding that met them. The platform claims, first, a negative: no authority, Congress, a territorial legislature, or any individual or association, may give legal existence to slavery in any territory while the Constitution stands. It claims, second, a duty: the Constitution gives Congress sovereign power over the territories, and exercising it to prohibit slavery and polygamy, the twin relics of barbarism, is both Congress's right and its imperative duty. Taney's third holding destroyed the second claim and the platform with it, by holding that Congress had never had power to bar slavery from the territories at all, so the party's one promised act was unconstitutional before it could be attempted. What was left of the platform was its grievances and its coalition, a party whose program a court had voided, which is why Republicans answered that the decision itself had to be reversed by politics, the argument Lincoln builds at Station 2.

Station 5: Justice Curtis's Dissent

The franchise, the posterity, and the reached question. Curtis answers that in five of the thirteen original states, colored persons possessed the elective franchise at ratification and were among those by whom the Constitution was ordained and established, so the Constitution was not made exclusively by the white race, and its opening declaration, ordained by the people of the United States for themselves and their posterity, contradicts the claim that it was made only for that race, since free colored citizens of those states were in every sense part of that people. His second objection is jurisdictional: he dissents from the majority's assumption of authority even to examine the Missouri Compromise Act's constitutionality, the reaching beyond the citizenship holding to void an act of Congress. The record Curtis cites is the franchise itself, Black men voting in five states under the Confederation and at ratification. A vote is an act recorded in law and returns. Taney's evidence is a claim about what the founding generation felt, and feelings attributed at seventy years' distance cannot be cross examined the way a franchise statute can.

Part 2: The Closing Verdict (Grading Guide)

Full credit should be awarded if the verdict:

  • Sorts every station with one quoted phrase or named fact for each, and treats Station 3 as evidence of a life, not as a constitutional argument it never makes.
  • Chooses one verdict, misreading or honest reading of a proslavery logic, and defends it from at least three stations, cited by number, rather than from the decision's reputation in general.
  • Accounts for Station 5: Curtis's franchise evidence is the strongest single answer in the file to the citizenship holding, and a misreading verdict rests on it, while an honest logic verdict must explain what Curtis's five states do to the claim that the founding settlement was uniformly proslavery.
  • Accounts for Station 4: the decision voided a national party's central promise nine months after the party adopted it, so the verdict must say what a court may legitimately do to a platform, whichever way the student judges the reading.
  • States the strongest rival claim at full strength before answering it. For a misreading verdict: the Constitution's text protected slave property in the fugitive clause, tolerated the trade to 1808, and counted the enslaved in representation, and Taney could present each holding as drawn from those provisions. For an honest logic verdict: Curtis showed the majority had to ignore recorded Black citizenship to reach its first holding, and a reading that needs the record trimmed is a choice, not a discovery.
  • Answers the rival by naming what it cannot hold, most often the settlement's fate: within three years the voided party elected a president on the voided platform, secession followed, and the citizenship holding was reversed by constitutional amendment after a war, which is not how settled questions behave.

Teacher note on the verdicts. The most common failure is a verdict argued from Station 1 alone, in which Taney's claims about founding opinion are repeated as findings. Station 5 exists to test those claims against the ratification record, and Station 3 exists to test the opinion's description of Black people against one Black man's own page. The second failure is treating Douglass's letter as an argument about the Constitution. It is evidence about citizenship's human meaning, and strong answers say exactly that rather than inflating it into a legal brief.
