Guided Notes: The Republican Party and Dred Scott

Lesson question: What did the Republican Party promise, and what did the Supreme Court try to settle in Dred Scott?

Unit question: How did compromise over slavery finally fail, and what did the war it caused settle about slavery and the Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Republican Party: the party organized in 1854 from the wreckage of the Kansas-Nebraska fight, joining ex-Whigs, Free Soilers, and anti-Nebraska Democrats on the single plank that Congress must bar slavery from the federal territories, the first successful wholly northern party in the country's history

free labor: the northern creed that labor was honorable, that the laborer had a right to rise by his work to property and independence, and that slavery degraded work and monopolized the common lands, the economic faith under the Republican Party's promise of free soil

John C. Frémont: the explorer and army officer called the Pathfinder, the Republican Party's first presidential nominee in 1856, who carried eleven of the sixteen free states and showed both sections that a purely northern party could nearly win the presidency

Dred Scott v. Sandford: the Supreme Court decision of March 6, 1857, in which Chief Justice Taney held that Black people could not be citizens of the United States, that residence on free soil had not freed Dred Scott, and that Congress had never had power to bar slavery from the territories

Roger B. Taney: the Maryland chief justice who succeeded John Marshall in 1836 and wrote the Dred Scott opinion, attempting to settle the slavery question by constitutional decree and instead voiding the Republican platform and deepening the crisis he meant to end

Abraham Lincoln: the Illinois lawyer and former one term congressman who rose on the Kansas-Nebraska fight to lead his state's Republicans, debated Douglas across Illinois in 1858, and was elected the sixteenth president in 1860 on the platform of barring slavery from the territories

House Divided speech: Lincoln's address of June 16, 1858, accepting the Republican nomination for the Senate, which argued that the government could not endure permanently half slave and half free and charged that the Nebraska repeal and the Dred Scott decision were parts of one design to make slavery national

E. Citizenship and residence: on March 6, 1857, Chief Justice Roger B. Taney held for a majority of seven that Black people, enslaved or free, were not and could not become citizens of the United States, and that Scott's residence on free soil had not freed him, Missouri law deciding his status on his return. Congress and the Compromise: the Missouri Compromise had been unconstitutional from the beginning, because Congress had never held power to bar slavery from the territories, enslaved people being property the Constitution protected.

F. Curtis's evidence: at ratification, free Black men had been citizens and voters in five of the original states, New Hampshire, Massachusetts, New York, New Jersey, and North Carolina, voting on equal terms with other citizens, so people who helped ordain the Constitution could not have been excluded from its citizenship. Douglass's letter: Douglass wrote courteously to his former master Hugh Auld in 1857 seeking the facts of his own childhood, a private page that shows the decision's world from inside a life the Court had placed outside the Constitution's people.

G. Lincoln's argument: accepting the Senate nomination in June 1858, Lincoln argued that a house divided against itself cannot stand, that the government could not endure permanently half slave and half free, and that the repeal, the decision, and the administration's policy were parts of one design to make slavery national and perpetual. The settlement's fate: within three years the party whose platform the decision voided elected a president on it, and the Union the decision was written to preserve had begun to dissolve.

The Party and the Decision at a Glance. A party and its creed, 1854 to 1856: the Republican Party organized on the plank that Congress must bar slavery from the territories, resting on the free labor creed and carrying Black exclusion inside it. The first campaign and the case, 1856 to 1857: Frémont carried eleven of the sixteen free states, and Dred Scott's suit, begun in 1846 on his residence in Illinois and at Fort Snelling, reached the Court. The decision, March 6, 1857: Taney held that Black people could not be citizens, that free soil residence freed no one, and that Congress had never had power to bar slavery from the territories. The answers, 1857 to 1858: Curtis dissented from the record of free Black voters in five states, Douglass answered without despair, and Lincoln's House Divided speech charged one design.

Exit ticket 2. Dred Scott v. Sandford, decided March 6, 1857, with Chief Justice Taney writing, held that Black people could not be citizens of the United States and that residence on free soil had not freed Dred Scott. It also held that Congress had never had power to bar slavery from the territories, so the Missouri Compromise had been unconstitutional from the start.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that carries the promise, the attempted settlement, and the failure. Example: The Republican Party promised that Congress would bar slavery from the federal territories. The Court tried to settle the question by decree, denying Black citizenship and voiding that promise as unconstitutional. The settlement failed because Curtis showed the record contradicted it and because the party it voided won the presidency on the voided platform in 1860.
