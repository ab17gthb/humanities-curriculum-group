## Sources quoted in this lesson, with editions

- Dred Scott majority opinion, activity Station 1 and (in the decision's holdings, paraphrased) the reading: the audited TPSP bank record dred-scott-decision-1857 in sectional-civil-war.json, whose sourceNote names Justia, 60 U.S. 393. Passages are quoted verbatim from the bank record's text field, extracted programmatically during this build so no transcription drift is possible; the record's own cut markers are preserved. The second Dred Scott bank record (dred-scott-v-sandford-1857, in jackson-reform.json) was not used, as the skeleton directs.
- House Divided speech, activity Station 2: the audited TPSP bank record house-divided-speech-1858 in jackson-reform.json, whose sourceNote names the Gilder Lehrman Institute. Quoted verbatim from the bank record's text field, extracted programmatically, with the record's spelling ("can not," "lawfull," "previleges") preserved and disclosed in the station's Source line.
- Douglass's letter to Hugh Auld, activity Station 3: the audited TPSP bank record douglass-to-hugh-auld-1857 in jackson-reform.json, whose sourceNote names the Gilder Lehrman Institute. Quoted verbatim from the bank record's text field, extracted programmatically; the two excerpts are joined from separated passages in the letter and the join is marked with ellipses on the station's face. The bank text's hyphen in "They were dear to me - and are still" falls outside both excerpts and so does not appear in the lesson.
- Republican platform of 1856, activity Station 4 and quick check: the American Presidency Project transcription (Republican Party Platform of 1856, Peters and Woolley, presidency.ucsb.edu), fetched in full during this build on October 22, 2026, and quoted from that transcription, including its double hyphen in the "twin relics of barbarism--Polygamy, and Slavery" resolution. Verbatim status: verified against the fetched transcription during this build.
- Curtis's dissent in Dred Scott, activity Station 5 and quick check: Dred Scott v. Sandford, 60 U.S. 393, Mr. Justice Curtis, dissenting, as transcribed by Teaching American History (teachingamericanhistory.org) from the United States Reports text, fetched during this build on October 22, 2026, and quoted from that transcription. The quick check passage combines the franchise passage with the "free colored persons were then citizens of at least five states" sentence from the same Curtis passage in that transcription; the join is marked with ellipses on the document's face. Verbatim status: verified against the fetched transcription during this build.

## Figures stated as counts

- Sixteen free states in 1856, of which Fremont carried eleven, as stated in the Frémont definition in the unit spec and carried verbatim in the guided notes.
- Scott's suit: filed 1846, decided March 6, 1857, eleven years, as stated in the bank records' headnotes and the reading.
- The decision's majority: seven justices, with McLean and Curtis dissenting, as stated in the bank record's headnote.
- Curtis's franchise states: five (New Hampshire, Massachusetts, New York, New Jersey, and North Carolina), as stated in Curtis's dissent as transcribed and in the abolishslavery.us case summary consulted during this build; the five are named from the dissent's state list as carried in the standard summaries of the opinion.

## Gaps recorded honestly

- Dred Scott's portrait (image 4) is the file the Commons catalog carries under the simple title DredScott.jpg at 600 by 850; a larger verified rendition was not located during this build, and the softness of its deck enlargement is recorded above rather than concealed.
- Harriet Scott: no separate verified public domain portrait of Harriet Scott was used in this lesson; she is carried in the reading's and the deck's prose (the marriage at Fort Snelling, the daughters born on free soil, the family as the suit's subject) rather than pictured.
- No 1856 campaign banner photograph or Fremont and Dayton broadside beyond the Library of Congress poster (image 1) was verified during this build; the poster carries the campaign visually on its own.
