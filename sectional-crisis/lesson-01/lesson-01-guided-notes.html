Guided Notes: The Compromise of 1850 and the Fugitive Slave Act

Lesson question: What did the Compromise of 1850 settle, and what did its Fugitive Slave Act ask the North to do?

Unit question: How did compromise over slavery finally fail, and what did the war it caused settle about slavery and the Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Compromise of 1850: the package of five measures enacted in September 1850 that admitted California as a free state, organized Utah and New Mexico without a slavery restriction, settled the Texas boundary and assumed its debt, ended the slave trade in the District of Columbia, and enacted a far stricter Fugitive Slave Act, passed bill by bill under Douglas after Clay's single omnibus failed

Fugitive Slave Act: the law of September 18, 1850, that required the return of people accused of escaping slavery on an owner's affidavit, denied the accused a jury trial and the right to testify, paid federal commissioners ten dollars for remanding a prisoner and five for freeing one, and made aiding a fugitive a federal crime

Henry Clay: the Kentucky senator who had built the compromises of 1820 and 1833 and returned to the Senate in 1849, at seventy two, to frame the Compromise of 1850, the last settlement of his long career as the Union's peacemaker, dying two years after its passage

Daniel Webster: the Massachusetts senator and orator whose Seventh of March speech in 1850 pleaded for the Compromise in the Union's name, breaking with his antislavery supporters, who mourned him in Whittier's poem "Ichabod" as a fallen champion of freedom

personal liberty laws: the statutes northern states passed to obstruct the Fugitive Slave Act, forbidding state officers to help in renditions, denying the use of state jails, and granting accused fugitives the jury trials and counsel that federal law denied them

Underground Railroad: the informal network of routes, safe houses, and guides, Black and white, by which enslaved people escaped the South for the free states and Canada, carrying thousands north across the middle decades of the century despite the federal law against aiding them

Harriet Tubman: the Maryland woman born enslaved as Araminta Ross, who escaped in 1849 and returned south again and again to guide some seventy enslaved people to freedom, served the Union army as a scout, spy, and nurse, and became the most famous conductor on the Underground Railroad

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher

Teach this lesson first in the Sectional Crisis and Civil War unit. It sets the settlement of 1850 and the enforcement machinery that Lessons 2 and 3 test against the fugitive rescues, Stowe, and Kansas. It pairs with the lesson reading and with the activity, The Compromise File, whose five stations carry Clay's resolutions, the Seventh of March speech, Calhoun's last speech, the Fugitive Slave Act, and the Kaufmann lithograph. Prior knowledge: students need the Early Republic unit's ground, the Missouri Compromise and the Wilmot Proviso named there, the Senate balance idea, and the Mexican war's territorial gain. Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies (cite textual evidence, determine central ideas, and analyze a sequence of events) and the C3 Framework, Dimension 2, History (change and continuity, and the uses of evidence), stated state neutrally for teachers to map to their own frameworks. Students will learn what the five measures settled, how a package that failed as an omnibus passed bill by bill, and what the Fugitive Slave Act required of accused people, commissioners, marshals, bystanders, and neighbors in the free states. Timing: one period for the reading and these notes, a second period for the activity and writing task. Materials: the reading, these notes, the activity file, and the notebook page.

Summary table. California and the balance: the gold rush state applied free under a constitution prohibiting slavery, breaking the fifteen to fifteen Senate balance that had given the South its veto. The bargain and its passage: Clay's omnibus of January 1850 collapsed in July, Douglas passed the five measures one at a time with different majorities, and Fillmore signed them in September after Taylor's death. The five terms: California free, Utah and New Mexico organized without a restriction, the Texas boundary settled and its debt assumed, the slave trade ended in the District of Columbia, and the stricter Fugitive Slave Act. The Act and the answer: rendition on an affidavit before a commissioner, no testimony and no jury for the accused, ten dollars against five in the fee schedule, posses and penalties for aiding, answered by the personal liberty laws and the Underground Railroad working on.

Exit ticket 1. The Senate stood at fifteen free states and fifteen slave states, and admitting California as a free state made the count sixteen to fifteen and left the South a permanent minority in that chamber.

Exit ticket 2. After Clay's single omnibus failed in July 1850, Stephen A. Douglas took the measures up one at a time and passed each with a different majority. President Millard Fillmore, who had succeeded Taylor on his death in July, signed the five measures in September 1850.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer naming two settlements from the five terms, for example California free and the Texas boundary and debt settled, and two requirements of the Act, drawn from rendition on an affidavit without jury or testimony, the commissioner fee schedule, the duty of marshals and bystanders, and the fines and prison for aiding a fugitive.
