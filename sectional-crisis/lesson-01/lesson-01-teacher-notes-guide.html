The Compromise of 1850 and the Fugitive Slave Act

Sectional Crisis and Civil War · Lesson 1 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Sectional Crisis and Civil War, Lesson 1 of 8 — the unit opener. It picks up the Jackson-era story where the previous unit left it: Calhoun and Webster are both back on screen, now old men in a Senate whose balance California is about to break. The lesson's discipline is its question's two halves — what the Compromise settled, and what its Fugitive Slave Act asked the North to do — because the whole unit runs on the enforcement and the resistance it called out, which begins in this lesson's final section.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Jackson and Reform Lesson 3 (Calhoun's doctrine and Webster's answer, thirty years earlier — the same two voices closing their careers here), Lesson 7 of that unit (the petition machinery the liberty laws now defend), and this unit's Lesson 2 (Uncle Tom's Cabin, the answer in print to the Act at work). The activity, The Compromise File, pairs Clay's resolutions and Webster's plea against Calhoun's warning and the Act itself, with Kaufmann's lithograph picturing the law for the North.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: The Mexican cession and the Wilmot Proviso fight at the level of the earlier course (the lesson re-teaches the adjournment in section A); the Missouri Compromise's pairing habit, which the 15-to-15 stat assumes students can be reminded of in one line; and the Senate's two-per-state arithmetic. No knowledge of the 1850 debates is assumed — Clay, Calhoun, and Webster are introduced as the winter's three alternatives: bargain, threaten, plead.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.9 (the settlement explained as bargain and sequence, not inevitability), D2.His.13 (speeches and statute read as documents), D2.Civ.10 and D2.Civ.13 (federal enforcement power conscripting citizens; state obstruction as intergovernmental conflict), D2.His.2 (the Senate balance as a thirty-year continuity breaking); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.8, WHST.9-10.1 (settlement or armistice, argued).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain why California's free application broke the Senate's thirty-year balance; to state the winter's three roads — Taylor's, Clay's, and the warnings of Calhoun and Webster; to sequence the passage (omnibus failed, Taylor died, Douglas passed the pieces, Fillmore signed); to state the five terms flatly and classify each section's gain; and to walk a rendition through the Fugitive Slave Act's machinery — affidavit, commissioner, fee, posse, penalty — and name the North's two answers, the liberty laws and the railroad.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; California and the balance (slides 4–6) 7 minutes; the two roads and the two speeches (slides 7–12) 14 minutes; passage in pieces (slides 13–14) 5 minutes; the terms (slides 15–16) 4 minutes; the Act at work and the answer (slides 17–20) 9 minutes; synthesis, vocabulary, and exit (slides 21–24) 6 minutes. Give the $10/$5 stat its full slide. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes, and The Compromise File (five stations). The notebook page rebuilds six pieces of the story, classified and evidenced; the quick check is "Ten Dollars and Five"; the exit ticket has three parts — the balance stated, the passage in pieces explained, and the lesson question answered with two settlements and two requirements of the Act.

Key vocabulary

Compromise of 1850 · Fugitive Slave Act · Henry Clay · Daniel Webster · personal liberty laws · Underground Railroad · Harriet Tubman

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title; the standfirst — "A last bargain, its price, and the North commanded to enforce it, 1849 to 1850."

  • Picture: Mathew Brady's photograph of Henry Clay, taken about 1849, when Clay returned to the Senate at seventy-two for the last settlement of his career.
  • This lesson opens the unit, and it opens with a bargain.
  • California, peopled overnight by the gold rush, applied for statehood as a free state against a Senate balanced at fifteen free and fifteen slave states; Clay framed a package meant to give each section a victory to carry home.
  • Calhoun warned that the cords of Union were snapping; Webster pleaded for the Union at the cost of his antislavery standing.
  • The package failed as one bill and passed in pieces, and its enforcement clause — the Fugitive Slave Act — put rendition on northern streets.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the rule; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "What did the Compromise of 1850 settle, and what did its Fugitive Slave Act ask the North to do?"
  • Read the question slowly, because its two halves organize everything that follows.
  • The first half asks what the Compromise settled: California's statehood, the territorial organization of Utah and New Mexico, the Texas boundary and debt, and the slave trade in the District of Columbia.
  • The second half asks what its Fugitive Slave Act asked the North to do: surrender accused people on an affidavit, before commissioners rather than juries, with bystanders commanded to help and neighbors punished for aiding.
  • Keep both halves in view all hour — a student who can answer only the first half knows the terms and misses the lesson, because the unit runs on the enforcement, and the resistance it called out begins in this lesson's final section.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "The last settlement and its enforcement clause, in seven stretches"; the seven section rows, A–G.

  • The map follows the guided notes section by section, so students can use the letters on screen to find their place in their notes.
  • Section A gives California knocking at the door and the Senate balance it threatened; section B gives the two roads, Taylor's direct admission and Clay's omnibus bargain.
  • Section C gives Calhoun's last speech and the snapping cords; section D gives Webster's Seventh of March plea and the answer in Whittier's poem.
  • Section E gives the omnibus failing and Douglas passing the pieces; section F states the five terms flatly.

Slide 4 · Section A: California Knocks at the Door (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "The gold rush state applied free, and the Senate balance broke."

  • Begin with the adjournment, because students must feel how long the question had been postponed before California forced it.
  • The Wilmot Proviso had proposed barring slavery from the lands taken from Mexico, and it had passed the House and died in the Senate again and again, which taught Congress to leave the subject alone.
  • California ended that practice by acting on its own: gold drew tens of thousands west in a single year, a constitutional convention met, slavery was prohibited in the document the settlers wrote, and the application arrived in Washington with senators already chosen.
  • The question was no longer whether Congress would raise slavery in the territories — a state was at the door, and the Senate had to vote.

Slide 5 · A free constitution, written in a year

On the slide: four bullets — gold peopling California overnight; settlers prohibiting slavery in their constitution; statehood applied for as a free state; the Wilmot fight adjourned, the question forced.

  • Work the balance as arithmetic, because arithmetic is what the argument ran on.
  • Each state sends two senators, so fifteen free states and fifteen slave states made thirty votes against thirty, and the South could block any measure in that chamber whatever the House did.
  • California free made it thirty-two against thirty, permanently, because no new slave state stood ready to restore the tie and the free states were growing faster.
  • Teach students to notice what the South was defending: not a majority, which it had already lost in the House and was losing in population, but a veto.
  • The whole crisis of 1850 is the sound of that veto being taken away, and the Compromise was the price paid for taking it peaceably.

Slide 6 · 15 to 15

On the slide: the big figure "15 to 15"; the label — "free states and slave states in the Senate of 1850"; the italic note — two senators each; the balance was the South's veto; California free makes it 16 to 15.

  • Let the two numbers carry the section.
  • Fifteen and fifteen was not a slogan but a working arrangement, renewed each time a free state and a slave state entered in pairs — Maine with Missouri, Michigan with Arkansas, Iowa and Wisconsin against Texas and Florida.
  • Students should see the pairing habit in the count itself: for thirty years admission had been managed so the Senate never tipped.
  • California broke the pattern because gold would not wait for a southern partner to be carved out of Texas or the territories.
  • Ask what a government does when its balancing device fails: it can bargain, which is section B; it can threaten, which is section C; or it can plead, which is section D. The winter of 1850 tried all three in eight weeks.

Slide 7 · Section B: Two Roads to a Settlement (divider)

On the slide: the Clay photograph at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Mathew Brady photograph of Henry Clay, taken about 1849, late in his life."

  • Picture: Brady's Clay of about 1849, the face of a man who had been settling sectional crises since 1820 and knew this was the last one he would frame.
  • Section B contrasts two roads: Taylor's road treated the question as an obstacle to route around — admit the far western states now, let their constitutions answer, and give Congress nothing to fight over.
  • Clay's road treated the question as a market: price every grievance — California for the North, the fugitive law for the South, Texas paid, the District trade ended — and close the whole account in a single vote.
  • Ask students which road a modern legislature would recognize: Clay's is the familiar one, and its logic, that no part passes unless every part passes, is exactly what section E watches fail.
  • His definition belongs to this section's teaching.
  • Say: "Henry Clay: the Kentucky senator who had built the compromises of 1820 and 1833 and returned to the Senate in 1849, at seventy two, to frame the Compromise of 1850, the last settlement of his long career as the Union's peacemaker, dying two years after its passage."

Slide 8 · Taylor's road and Clay's bargain

On the slide: the Clay photograph at right; five bullets — Taylor admitting California and New Mexico at once; their constitutions settling slavery, Congress aside; Clay pricing every question inside one package; each section carrying home a victory; each section swallowing a defeat in the same vote.

  • Teach the omnibus as a machine with five moving parts, because students will meet the five terms again in section F and must see them first as offers.
  • California free was the North's headline gain; the territories without a restriction were the South's doctrinal gain, Congress standing aside.
  • Texas surrendered land and received money, a transaction both sections could price; the District clause gave each side half a loaf, the trade ended and slavery protected.
  • The fugitive provision was the South's enforcement gain and, as the lesson will show, the package's poison.
  • Clay's wager was that members who hated one part would swallow it for the sake of another — that wager held the package together through the spring debates and then broke apart in July, when members learned they could vote for parts and against the whole.

Slide 9 · Section C: Calhoun's Last Word (divider)

On the slide: the Calhoun photograph at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Mathew Brady photograph of John C. Calhoun, taken about 1849, in his last year."

  • Picture: Mathew Brady's photograph of John C. Calhoun, taken about 1849, in the last year of his life.
  • Section C is a deathbed speech in form as well as fact: Calhoun could not project his voice, sat wrapped against the cold of the Senate chamber, and heard his own argument read aloud by James Mason of Virginia while he followed the printed text.
  • Teach the setting before the argument, because the setting is part of the evidence: the South's greatest theorist was delivering his final account of the Union, and everyone in the chamber knew it.
  • He died on March 31, four weeks later, with the Compromise still undecided and his warning standing as his section's last word in the debate.

Slide 10 · The cords snapping, one by one

On the slide: the Calhoun photograph at right; five bullets — dying, his speech read aloud by Mason; March 4, 1850, equality in the territories; faithful return of fugitives demanded; agitation snapping the cords successively; at the end, nothing left but force.

  • Work the cords passage as a mechanism, not a metaphor alone.
  • Calhoun argues that disunion is a process, not an event: the bonds are many — churches, parties, commerce, affection — and agitation of the slavery question snaps them successively, each break weakening the rest.
  • His demands follow from the mechanism: if agitation is the force doing the snapping, then preserving the Union requires ending the agitation, restoring the South's equality in the common territories, and enforcing the return of fugitives faithfully.
  • Notice what he does not offer: any southern concession. In his account the South has nothing to surrender, because the Constitution as he reads it is already the settlement.
  • That is why Clay's market logic could not reach him, and why the speech reads as a warning addressed past the Senate to history.

Slide 11 · Section D: Webster Speaks for the Union (divider)

On the slide: the Webster daguerreotype at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Daniel Webster, daguerreotype by Southworth and Hawes. Metropolitan Museum."

  • Picture: the daguerreotype of Daniel Webster by Southworth and Hawes, held by the Metropolitan Museum of Art — the orator in the last years of his fame.
  • Section D answers section C in three days: where Calhoun spoke for a section's equality, Webster announced that he would speak as an American only, and he carried the whole package, including the fugitive law he knew would damage him at home.
  • Teach the pairing deliberately: the dying Carolinian and the Massachusetts orator were the two most famous voices in the chamber, and between them they stated the winter's alternatives — equality demanded or Union pleaded for.
  • Clay's bargain needed Webster's voice to move northern votes, and Webster gave it at a price the next slide counts.
  • His definition belongs to this section's teaching.
  • Say: "Daniel Webster: the Massachusetts senator and orator whose Seventh of March speech in 1850 pleaded for the Compromise in the Union's name, breaking with his antislavery supporters, who mourned him in Whittier's poem "Ichabod" as a fallen champion of freedom."

Slide 12 · The Seventh of March, and its price

On the slide: the Webster daguerreotype at right; five bullets — not as a Massachusetts man, but as an American; the whole package, the fugitive law included; peaceable secession an impossibility; Whittier answering in "Ichabod"; the glory departed, his readers judged.

  • Take the speech and its answer together.
  • Webster held that peaceable secession was an impossibility, that the Constitution imposed the return of fugitives, and that concession now was cheaper than arms later — and for three hours the Senate heard its most celebrated speaker spend his authority on the Compromise.
  • Then teach the reception, because the reception is the historical fact students remember: Massachusetts antislavery readers, who had counted Webster their champion, read the speech as surrender.
  • Whittier's poem "Ichabod" mourned the glory departed, and the single word became the verdict that followed Webster to his death in 1852.
  • Ask: Should a speech be judged by its argument, or by what it cost and caused? The lesson's documents let students argue both ways from evidence.

Slide 13 · Section E: The Package Fails, the Pieces Pass (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "The omnibus collapsed in July. Douglas passed the measures one at a time."

  • Section E is the lesson's lesson in how laws actually pass, and it deserves plain teaching.
  • The omnibus was a single bill, and a single bill gives every opponent a different reason to vote no while giving supporters only one motion to defend.
  • Through the spring the package shed support clause by clause, and in July the Senate struck out nearly everything but one territorial provision, which was the formal collapse.
  • Clay, ill and exhausted, left Washington for Kentucky believing the settlement dead.
  • Teach students to mark the distinction the rest of the lesson runs on: the Compromise that history names was never voted on as a compromise — it is the collective name for five separate laws, and the difference explains both its passage and its weakness.

Slide 14 · Five majorities do what one package could not

On the slide: five bullets — the omnibus picked apart in July 1850; Clay, exhausted, going home to Kentucky; Douglas passing each measure separately; Taylor dying, Fillmore favoring the settlement; September 1850, five laws, one name.

  • Douglas's method was Clay's machine run in reverse.
  • The Little Giant of Illinois, chairman of the territories committee and young where Clay was old, took each measure separately and built a different majority for each: western and northern votes admitted California, southern votes carried the fugitive bill, and members could support the parts their constituents wanted and oppose the rest on the record.
  • Taylor's death in July removed the president who had opposed the package and threatened a veto fight, and Millard Fillmore signed each measure as it arrived; by late September all five were law.
  • Have students state the sequence in one breath — omnibus failed, pieces passed, president changed — because the unit test will ask for the order and the order is the explanation.
  • The package's own definition belongs to this passage.
  • Say: "Compromise of 1850: the package of five measures enacted in September 1850 that admitted California as a free state, organized Utah and New Mexico without a slavery restriction, settled the Texas boundary and assumed its debt, ended the slave trade in the District of Columbia, and enacted a far stricter Fugitive Slave Act, passed bill by bill under Douglas after Clay's single omnibus failed."

Slide 15 · Section F: The Terms, Stated Flatly (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Read the settlement the way a clerk would read it."

  • Section F slows the lesson down on purpose: after the speeches and the maneuvering, students meet the settlement as a list.
  • The list should be read flatly, the way a clerk would read it, because flat statement is how a class checks whether the bargain balanced.
  • California free is a permanent change in the Senate; the territories clause is a postponement, not a decision, and students should say so when they reach it.
  • Texas is a transaction, land for money, fully closed; the District clause is two half measures in one city.
  • Only the fifth term, the Fugitive Slave Act, reaches into the daily life of people who had never owned slaves or been enslaved — and section G opens that term up to its working parts.

Slide 16 · Five terms, each section paid

On the slide: five bullets — California free; Utah and New Mexico with no slavery restriction; the Texas boundary settled and its debt assumed; the slave trade ended in the District of Columbia; a far stricter Fugitive Slave Act.

  • Walk the five terms in order and have students classify each as the North's gain, the South's gain, or a postponement shared between them.
  • California: the North's. Utah and New Mexico without a restriction: formally neutral, practically a southern doctrinal gain and a question deferred to statehood conventions.
  • Texas boundary and the ten million dollar debt assumption: a settlement both sections priced and closed.
  • The District trade ban: the North's symbolic gain, with slavery itself untouched in the capital.
  • The Fugitive Slave Act: the South's enforcement gain, and the only term that required continuing action by northern officials and citizens for as long as it stood.
  • That last feature is the hinge of the lesson: four terms executed themselves once passed — the fifth had to be executed every week, in public, in the North.

Slide 17 · Section G: The Act at Work, and the Answer (divider)

On the slide: the Tubman photograph at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Harriet Tubman, photographed by Horatio G. Squyer, about 1885. National Portrait Gallery."

  • Picture: Harriet Tubman, photographed by Horatio G. Squyer about 1885, in the copy held by the National Portrait Gallery — it shows her in old age, decades after the journeys this section teaches, and students should be told so plainly.
  • Section G turns from the Senate to the street, because the Fugitive Slave Act was not a statement but a procedure, and procedures are learned at the level of the person standing in the hearing room.
  • The accused in that room could not speak; the commissioner across the desk was paid by the outcome; the marshal in the corridor could summon the crowd outside into a posse.
  • Students should leave this section able to walk a rendition through its steps in order, which is what the activity's fourth station requires of them.
  • The Act's own definition belongs to this section's teaching.
  • Say: "Fugitive Slave Act: the law of September 18, 1850, that required the return of people accused of escaping slavery on an owner's affidavit, denied the accused a jury trial and the right to testify, paid federal commissioners ten dollars for remanding a prisoner and five for freeing one, and made aiding a fugitive a federal crime."

Slide 18 · Rendition at street level

On the slide: five bullets — seized on an owner's affidavit; a commissioner, not a judge and jury; the accused unable to testify, and without a jury; bystanders summoned, all citizens commanded; aiding punished, $1,000 and six months.

  • Teach the machinery in the statute's own order.
  • It begins with paper: an owner's affidavit, sworn in the home state, is satisfactory proof on which a certificate issues.
  • It proceeds without the ordinary safeguards: no jury, and the alleged fugitive's own testimony barred in so many words.
  • It prices the decision: ten dollars to the commissioner who remands, five to the one who frees — a schedule northern newspapers reprinted as the law's confession.
  • It conscripts the public: marshals fined for refusal, bystanders summonable, all good citizens commanded to aid.
  • And it punishes mercy: feeding, hiding, or rescuing a fugitive cost up to a thousand dollars, six months in prison, and civil damages on top.
  • Each feature answers a southern complaint about the feeble law of 1793, and each feature manufactured a northern grievance of its own.

Slide 19 · The North answers in law and in flight

On the slide: the Kaufmann print at right; five bullets — personal liberty laws in the statehouses; state officers forbidden, state jails denied; jury trials granted that federal law withheld; the Underground Railroad working on; Tubman returning south, again and again.

  • The answer came from statehouses and safe houses at once, and students should keep the two answers distinct.
  • The personal liberty laws were statutes, passed in the open by northern legislatures: state officers forbidden to help renditions, state jails closed to federal prisoners, and the jury trials and counsel granted that federal law withheld — they turned enforcement into a contest between governments.
  • Say: "personal liberty laws: the statutes northern states passed to obstruct the Fugitive Slave Act, forbidding state officers to help in renditions, denying the use of state jails, and granting accused fugitives the jury trials and counsel that federal law denied them."
  • The Underground Railroad was the private answer — an informal network of routes, safe houses, and guides, Black and white, that worked on under the heavier penalties.
  • Say: "Underground Railroad: the informal network of routes, safe houses, and guides, Black and white, by which enslaved people escaped the South for the free states and Canada, carrying thousands north across the middle decades of the century despite the federal law against aiding them."
  • Harriet Tubman is its measure: escaped from Maryland in 1849, she returned south again and again and guided some seventy people out.
  • Say: "Harriet Tubman: the Maryland woman born enslaved as Araminta Ross, who escaped in 1849 and returned south again and again to guide some seventy enslaved people to freedom, served the Union army as a scout, spy, and nurse, and became the most famous conductor on the Underground Railroad."
  • The law had been written to end escape; its first decade is a record of escape continuing, defended increasingly by law itself.

Slide 20 · $10 / $5

On the slide: the big figure "$10 / $5"; the label — "the commissioner paid ten dollars to remand, five to free"; the italic note — the fee schedule of section 8, quoted by the Act's critics in the statute's own numbers.

  • Let the fee schedule stand alone for a full slide, because contemporaries did.
  • Ten dollars when the commissioner delivered the certificate and sent the prisoner south; five dollars when the proof failed and the person went free.
  • Defenders answered that the larger fee paid for the larger paperwork of a completed rendition, and that answer is in the record and students should hear it fairly.
  • Then ask the question the northern press asked: whatever the bookkeeping reason, what does a schedule teach the officer who reads it about which outcome his government values?
  • The Act's critics did not need to invent the figure — they quoted the statute. In a lesson built on documents, this slide models the strongest kind of evidence: the opponent's case stated in the law's own numbers.

Slide 21 · Settled, and asked

On the slide: five bullets — settled: statehood, territories, Texas, the District trade; passed in pieces, signed by Fillmore; asked: rendition without jury or testimony; asked: the bystander conscripted, the aider punished; answered: liberty laws and the railroad.

  • Build the synthesis as the answer to the lesson question, in the question's own two halves.
  • Settled: California's statehood, the Senate's new arithmetic, the organization of Utah and New Mexico, the Texas boundary and debt, and the slave trade in the District of Columbia — all of it law by September 1850 through Douglas's separate majorities and Fillmore's signature.
  • Asked of the North: rendition without jury or testimony, commissioners paid ten against five, marshals and bystanders conscripted, aid punished as a federal crime.
  • Then state the lesson's judgment and let students contest it from the documents: the settlement answered every question pending in 1850 and supplied the next decade with its grievance, because the fifth term had to be enforced in northern streets every week it stood.
