The Compromise of 1850 and the Fugitive Slave Act

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. California Knocks at the Door

The war with Mexico ended in 1848 with the United States holding new territory, and the fight over slavery there had adjourned without an answer. The Wilmot Proviso, which would have barred slavery from the conquered lands, had passed the House and died in the Senate repeatedly. In 1849 California ended the adjournment. The gold rush peopled it overnight, its settlers wrote a constitution prohibiting slavery, and it applied for statehood as a free state.

California threatened the arrangement the Senate rested on. Fifteen free states and fifteen slave states sat there, two senators each, and the balance gave the South a veto over the growing North. Admit California free and the count became sixteen to fifteen, leaving the South a minority as its House share shrank each census. Southern members spoke of leaving the Union, and the winter of 1850 opened with the question no longer postponable.

B. Two Roads to a Settlement

President Zachary Taylor proposed to skip the question. A Louisiana slaveholder elected as the war's hero, he would admit California and New Mexico as states at once, their constitutions settling slavery, with no territorial stage for Congress to fight over. The South was asked to accept two likely free states, the North that Congress had stood aside.

Henry Clay offered the opposite road, a bargain. The Kentucky senator who had built the compromises of 1820 and 1833 returned to the Senate in 1849 at seventy two and in January 1850 laid out his resolutions as one package, an omnibus. California would come in free, the rest of the Mexican lands organized without a slavery restriction, Texas would surrender its claim to New Mexico and have its debt paid, the slave trade would end in the District of Columbia, and a stricter fugitive slave law would give the South its demanded enforcement. Each section would carry home a victory and swallow a defeat in one vote.

C. Calhoun's Last Word

John C. Calhoun of South Carolina answered on March 4, 1850. Dying and too weak to project his voice, he sat wrapped against the cold while Senator James Mason read his speech aloud. Calhoun demanded the South's equality in the common territories and faithful return of fugitives, and warned that the cords binding the Union, its churches and parties, had snapped one by one under agitation of the slavery question. If agitation went on, every cord would snap, and nothing would hold the states together except force.

D. Webster Speaks for the Union

Three days later Daniel Webster of Massachusetts rose to answer. His Seventh of March speech pleaded for the Compromise in the Union's name. He spoke, he said, not as a Massachusetts man nor as a Northern man but as an American, and supported the package whole, including the stricter fugitive slave law, holding fugitive return a constitutional duty. Peaceable secession, he argued, was impossible, and the Union had to be preserved by concession now rather than tested by arms later.

Antislavery readers in New England received the speech as a fall. Their champion had lent his name to a law for hunting fugitives. John Greenleaf Whittier answered in the poem “Ichabod,” mourning the glory departed from a fallen leader, and the judgment followed Webster for life.

E. The Package Fails, the Pieces Pass

Clay's omnibus collapsed in July 1850, picked apart by members who would vote for single measures but not the bundle, and Clay went home to Kentucky exhausted. Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois took the measures up one at a time and passed each with a different majority, northern votes carrying California and southern votes the fugitive bill, no member required to swallow the whole settlement at once.

Taylor died suddenly in July, and Vice President Millard Fillmore, who favored the settlement, signed the measures as they reached him. In September 1850 the five measures became law one by one, received as the Compromise of 1850, although Congress had never voted for it in that form.

F. The Terms, Stated Flatly

The terms were these. California entered as a free state. Utah and New Mexico were organized as territories without a slavery restriction, their state constitutions to decide the question at statehood. The Texas boundary was settled at its present line, and the United States assumed ten million dollars of Texas debt. In the District of Columbia the slave trade was abolished, while slavery itself remained lawful there. And the Fugitive Slave Act rebuilt federal enforcement from the ground up.

G. The Act at Work, and the Answer

The Act worked at street level. A person accused of escaping slavery could be seized on an owner's affidavit and was tried not before a judge and jury but before a federal commissioner in a summary hearing. The accused could not testify and had no jury. The commissioner received ten dollars when he remanded the prisoner and five when he freed the person. Marshals who refused faced heavy fines, bystanders could be summoned into a posse, and anyone who fed, hid, or aided a fugitive faced a thousand dollar fine and six months in prison.

Both sections' newspapers called the settlement final, and resistance began at once. Northern legislatures passed personal liberty laws forbidding state officers to help renditions, denying state jails, and granting accused fugitives the jury trials federal law withheld. The Underground Railroad worked on under heavier penalties, its conductors including Harriet Tubman, who had escaped from Maryland in 1849 and returned south repeatedly to guide others out. The Compromise had settled the statehood question of 1850. Its enforcement clause opened a new one on northern streets.
