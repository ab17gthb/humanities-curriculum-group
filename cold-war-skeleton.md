# The Cold War Unit: Assignment Skeleton

_Eighth built unit for the Knoweth curriculum and planner site, sitting in course order after World War II and before Civil Rights. Modeled on the Progressive Era pilot, the Gilded Age, Imperialism and World War I, the Twenties, the Depression and New Deal, and the World War II units. Every lesson has the same working plate so teachers can pick pieces without losing the line of the unit._

## How to read this

Status marks:

- **HAVE**: an audited source or image already exists in the TPSP bank
- **BUILD**: create for this unit
- **LINK**: use the TPSP bank rather than rebuilding it

## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- How did the struggle between the United States and the Soviet Union reshape the world and American life, and why did it end the way it did?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's core plan is 18 instructional days. The unit runs from the breakup of the wartime alliance in 1945 and 1946 to the dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991. It opens where the World War II unit closes: that unit owns the Yalta and Potsdam conferences and the atomic decision as its last material, and names the division between the wartime allies as the frame the next unit takes up. This unit opens with the alliance breaking and the American answer being formulated: the Long Telegram, the Iron Curtain speech, the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan as policy, the Berlin Airlift, and NATO. The Marshall Plan appears here rather than in the World War II unit because it was a Cold War policy, proposed in 1947 to hold Western Europe against Communist pressure, not a part of the war's settlement. The unit runs the full arc: containment formulated, the Cold War turned hot in Asia, the Red Scare at home, the nuclear shadow and brinkmanship, Cuba and the missile crisis, Vietnam, détente and its collapse, the renewed confrontation of the 1980s, and the end in 1989 to 1991. The boundary with the Civil Rights unit, which follows in course order, is kept clean: that unit owns the domestic movement arc from Brown to the legislation of the 1960s and beyond. This unit owns McCarthyism and the Second Red Scare as Cold War home-front material, and touches civil rights only where the Cold War itself drove events, as when foreign opinion pressed Washington during the Little Rock crisis. The final unit, Modern America, takes post-1991 ground; this unit closes with the dissolution itself and the questions it left open.

**The plate in this build**

1. **Reading**: 850 to 900 words with five questions and a teacher key
2. **Guided notes**: keyed to the reading and lesson, with the vocabulary set verbatim and a teacher key
3. **Activity**: stations, jigsaw, map work, simulation, debate, or structured chart work in the locked print design
4. **Notebook page**: a single organizer students keep
5. **Quick check**: a short formative tied to the blueprint
6. **Writing task**: a paragraph or short essay with a scaffold and a teacher key or rubric

TPSP source analyses and photo sets are linked where the audited bank already carries them. They are not rebuilt merely to fill a folder.

**Six state-neutral era topics**

1. Origins and containment: the strategy takes shape
2. Asia and the wars of containment
3. Fear at home: anticommunism and American freedom
4. The nuclear arms race and the shadow it cast
5. Challenge and response: Cuba, Vietnam, détente, and the renewed confrontation
6. The Cold War ends: reform, collapse, and legacy

## Lesson 1: Allies to Adversaries: Containment Takes Shape

**Lesson question:** How did wartime allies become rivals, and what strategy did the United States adopt to meet the Soviet Union?

**Reading scope:** The alliance's terms first: the United States and the Soviet Union had been partners of necessity against Hitler, divided by ideology long before 1945, and the war's end left Soviet armies standing in Eastern Europe and American and British armies in the West. The unraveling in sequence: Poland and the broken Yalta pledges, Soviet pressure on Iran and Turkey, and two documents that named the new reality in February and March 1946, George Kennan's Long Telegram from Moscow, arguing that Soviet expansion was driven by ideology and insecurity and could be contained by firm resistance, and Winston Churchill's Iron Curtain speech at Fulton, Missouri, with President Truman on the platform. The strategy taking shape: the Truman Doctrine of March 1947, aid to Greece and Turkey framed as a worldwide duty to support free peoples against subjugation, and the Marshall Plan proposed that June, American money to rebuild Western Europe so that hunger and chaos could not deliver it to Communist parties. The test of the strategy: the Berlin Blockade of 1948 and 1949, the Soviet closure of the land routes to West Berlin, and the Allied airlift that supplied a city of two million by plane for eleven months until the blockade was lifted. The settlement of the decade: NATO in 1949, the first peacetime alliance in American history, the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic as two German states, and the Soviet atomic bomb of August 1949 ending the American monopoly. The reading closes with the question the strategy left open: containment read Kennan's way was political and economic; events were about to make it military and global.

**Activity concept:** The strategy desk. Students work through five documents, a Long Telegram passage, the Iron Curtain speech passage, the Truman Doctrine passage, Marshall's Harvard address passage, and a Berlin Airlift record, and build the containment briefing from them: what the threat is said to be, what tools the strategy uses, where it draws its line, and what each document adds, then judge which document did the most to fix the policy in place.

**Vocabulary:** superpower, Iron Curtain, containment, Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, Berlin Airlift, NATO

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Long Telegram, Truman Doctrine address, and Marshall Plan Harvard address, all audited records in the Cold War era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 2: The Cold War in Asia: China and Korea

**Lesson question:** How did the Cold War turn into a shooting war in Asia, and why did Korea end without victory?

**Reading scope:** The shocks of 1949 first: Mao Zedong's Communists winning the Chinese civil war and proclaiming the People's Republic of China on October 1, 1949, while the Nationalists fled to Taiwan, and the Soviet atomic bomb a month earlier, two blows that American politics read as losses and that set up the question of who was to blame. The answer on paper: NSC-68 in April 1950, the secret planning paper that described the Cold War as a global struggle between freedom and slavery and called for a tripling of defense spending, shelved as too costly until war made it policy. The war itself: North Korea's invasion of South Korea on June 25, 1950, Truman's decision to intervene under the United Nations flag without a declaration of war, the retreat to the Pusan Perimeter, MacArthur's landing at Inchon in September, and the drive to the Yalu River that brought China into the war in November with hundreds of thousands of troops. The price of ambition: the retreat from the Chosin Reservoir, Seoul changing hands, and the war settling near the 38th parallel into two years of stalemate in trenches while negotiators argued. The constitutional and political fight at home: MacArthur's demand for a wider war, his public defiance of the president's policy, and Truman's dismissal of him in April 1951, defended as the price of civilian control and of keeping a limited war limited. The armistice of July 1953: the line roughly where the war began, more than 36,000 American dead, Korean dead in the millions, and no peace treaty to this day. The reading closes with the template Korea set: an undeclared war, fought for limited aims, against an enemy the United States would not follow into total war.

**Activity concept:** The limited war file. Students work five records in sequence, an NSC-68 passage, a record of the Inchon landing and the drive north, a Chinese intervention account, Truman's statement dismissing MacArthur, and the armistice terms, and at each decision point record what the decision makers knew, what they chose, and what the alternative would have cost, then judge where the war was won, lost, or settled.

**Vocabulary:** NSC-68, People's Republic of China, Korean War, 38th parallel, limited war, stalemate, armistice

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP NSC-68 excerpt and MacArthur's farewell address to Congress, audited records. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 3: The Red Scare at Home

**Lesson question:** How did the fear of communism change American life at home, and what ended the scare?

**Reading scope:** The fear's fuel stated first, because the scare was not invented from nothing: the Soviet atomic bomb of 1949, the Communist victory in China the same year, the Korean War from 1950, and real Soviet espionage, including the atomic spies who passed secrets to Moscow. The machinery built to answer it: Truman's federal loyalty program of 1947, checking millions of government employees against lists of suspect organizations; the House Un-American Activities Committee and its 1947 Hollywood hearings, where friendly witnesses named names and the Hollywood Ten refused, cited contempt, and went to prison; the blacklist that followed, barring hundreds from film and broadcasting work on suspicion and association. The spy cases that kept the fear hot: Alger Hiss, convicted of perjury in 1950, and Julius and Ethel Rosenberg, executed in 1953 for passing atomic secrets, cases Americans argued over then and argue over still. The scare's political entrepreneur: Senator Joseph McCarthy of Wisconsin, whose Wheeling speech of February 1950 claimed a list of Communists inside the State Department, a number that changed as he repeated it, and whose method, accusation as evidence, gave the era its name. The turn: the Army-McCarthy hearings of 1954, thirty six days on television in which McCarthy bullied witnesses and an Army lawyer asked him whether he had no sense of decency, followed by the Senate's censure vote that December. The reading closes on the reckoning: thousands of jobs lost, careers ended by association, and a Supreme Court that narrowed the scare's legal tools only after the scare itself had burned down.

**Activity concept:** The investigation file. Students work from five documents, the Wheeling speech passage, a Hollywood Ten contempt record, a loyalty oath, a Rosenberg case summary drawn from the trial record, and an Army-McCarthy hearing passage, and for each one separate the claim made from the evidence shown, then judge which document shows the scare at its most defensible and which at its least.

**Vocabulary:** Second Red Scare, House Un-American Activities Committee, loyalty oath, Hollywood Ten, blacklist, McCarthyism, Army-McCarthy hearings

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP McCarthy Wheeling speech excerpt, audited record. **LINK** / **HAVE**. The first Red Scare of 1919 and 1920 belongs to the Imperialism and World War I unit; this lesson names the recurrence without reteaching it.

## Lesson 4: Brinkmanship and the Nuclear Shadow

**Lesson question:** How did nuclear weapons change the rules of the rivalry, and how close did brinkmanship bring the world to war?

**Reading scope:** The arsenal's growth first: the hydrogen bomb of 1952, hundreds of times more powerful than the bombs of 1945, the Soviet answer in 1953, and bombers, then missiles, built to deliver them, until each side held the power to destroy the other's society in an afternoon. The doctrine built on the arsenal: Secretary of State John Foster Dulles and massive retaliation, the declared intention to answer aggression with nuclear force at times and places of American choosing, and brinkmanship as he named it, the willingness to walk to the edge of war as a way of making the other side step back. The shadow at home: civil defense drills and the "Duck and Cover" film in schoolrooms, backyard fallout shelters, and a generation that grew up practicing for the end of the world as a school exercise. The competition beyond Europe: covert action as the cheap middle weapon, the CIA's overthrows of elected governments in Iran in 1953 and Guatemala in 1954, justified as containment and answered by decades of consequences; the Warsaw Pact of 1955 answering NATO; and the contests for the newly independent nations of Asia and Africa, where the superpowers armed clients and read every local struggle as a square on the board. The shock of October 1957: Sputnik, the first satellite, a Soviet beeping sphere that Americans heard as proof their country had fallen behind, answered by NASA, new science money for schools, and a missile program pressed into overdrive. The reading closes with Eisenhower, the general who had commanded the largest army in history, warning on his way out of office in January 1961 against the military-industrial complex his own buildup had created, a warning from inside the machine.

**Activity concept:** The brink file. Students work five stations, a hydrogen bomb test record, Dulles's massive retaliation passage, the Iran and Guatemala covert action records, a Sputnik front page record, and the Eisenhower farewell passage, with the "Duck and Cover" film record as the home-front station between them, and for each one state what new power or fear it added to the rivalry and what it cost, then judge which station changed the contest most.

**Vocabulary:** arms race, hydrogen bomb, massive retaliation, brinkmanship, Warsaw Pact, covert action, Sputnik

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP "Duck and Cover" film record and Eisenhower farewell address, audited records. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 5: Cuba: Revolution and the Missile Crisis

**Lesson question:** Why did the Soviet Union put missiles in Cuba, and how did thirteen days in October 1962 change the Cold War?

**Reading scope:** The island first: Cuba ninety miles from Florida, its sugar economy tied to the United States and its Batista government kept by an American-backed strongman, until Fidel Castro's revolution took Havana on January 1, 1959 and turned, through land reform, seized American property, and a trade deal with Moscow, toward the Soviet camp. The American answer and its failure: the Bay of Pigs invasion of April 1961, Cuban exiles landed with American training and supplies and defeated in three days, which taught Castro to seek a stronger guarantee and taught Khrushchev to read Kennedy as young and pushable. The discovery: a U-2 photograph of October 14, 1962 showing Soviet medium-range missile sites under construction, and the week of secret deliberation that followed, the Executive Committee weighing air strike, invasion, and blockade while the public knew nothing. The public crisis: Kennedy's address of October 22 announcing the naval quarantine and demanding the missiles out, Soviet ships turning back at the line, and the closest day of all, October 27, when an American U-2 was shot down over Cuba and another strayed over Siberia. The settlement, in its two parts: public, Soviet missiles withdrawn for an American promise not to invade Cuba; and secret, American Jupiter missiles quietly removed from Turkey months later, a trade both governments denied at the time. The reading closes with what thirteen days built: the hotline between Washington and Moscow, the Limited Test Ban Treaty of 1963, and a shared rule the crisis had taught both sides, that the two arsenals made direct confrontation a game neither could afford to lose control of.

**Activity concept:** The thirteen days file. Students work the chronology from five records, the U-2 photograph with its caption record, Kennedy's October 22 address passage, Khrushchev's letter of October 26, his harder letter of October 27, and the Dobrynin cable on the Robert Kennedy meeting, and keep a day-by-day ledger of what each side knew, offered, and risked, then find the settlement's two parts in the documents and judge which letter mattered more.

**Vocabulary:** Cuban Revolution, Bay of Pigs invasion, deterrence, Cuban Missile Crisis, naval quarantine, hotline, Limited Test Ban Treaty

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP U-2 reconnaissance photograph, Kennedy's missile crisis address, the Kennedy and Khrushchev letters, and the Dobrynin cable, all audited records in the Cold War era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 6: Vietnam: The War Containment Built

**Lesson question:** How did containment lead the United States into Vietnam, and why did the war fail on its own terms?

**Reading scope:** The road in first: Vietnam divided at Geneva in 1954 after the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu, Ho Chi Minh's Communists in the north and an American-backed government in the south, elections to reunify the country promised and never held, and the domino theory as the stated reason the United States could not let the south fall. The commitment deepening by steps: advisers under Kennedy, then the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution of August 1964, passed on reports of attacks on American destroyers, the second of which almost certainly never happened, giving the president a blank authority Congress spent years regretting. The escalation: Rolling Thunder's bombing from 1965, ground troops growing past half a million by 1968, search and destroy in the villages, body counts as the measure of progress, and a draft that fell hardest on the poor and the young, all while official statements promised light at the end of the tunnel. The turn: the Tet Offensive of January 1968, coordinated attacks across South Vietnam including the American embassy grounds in Saigon, a military defeat for the attackers and a strategic defeat for the administration, because it broke the public's belief that the war was being won. The way out that was not out: Nixon's Vietnamization, American troops coming home while the bombing widened into Cambodia and Laos, the antiwar movement at its height, Kent State in 1970, and the Paris Peace Accords of January 1973, after which the last American troops left and the war went on without them. The end: Saigon's fall on April 30, 1975, more than 58,000 American dead, Vietnamese dead in the millions, and a containment war that had consumed the strategy that built it. The reading closes with the war's domestic wreckage: inflation, a broken presidency in Johnson's, distrust of government that outlasted the war, and the veterans who came home to a country that wanted to forget.

**Activity concept:** The escalation ledger. Students keep a ledger across five records, the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, a Bundy memorandum passage on the war's aims, a Tet Offensive record, a Vietnamization speech passage, and the draft lottery record, entering for each step the commitment added, the reason given, and the cost, then judge from the ledger at which step the war could still have been ended on better terms and defend the judgment from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** domino theory, Viet Cong, Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, escalation, Tet Offensive, Vietnamization, draft lottery

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, Bundy memorandum, the Johnson and Bundy telephone record, and Robert Kennedy's 1967 letter on Vietnam, audited records. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 7: Détente, Human Rights, and the Evil Empire

**Lesson question:** Why did the rivals try to ease the Cold War in the 1970s, and why did the confrontation return in the 1980s?

**Reading scope:** The easing and its reasons first: by 1969 both sides had cause to step back, the Soviet Union near nuclear parity and split with China, the United States bleeding in Vietnam, and Nixon and Kissinger building détente as a structure of agreements that would give Moscow a stake in restraint. The architecture: the opening to China in 1972, using one Communist power against the other; SALT I the same year, capping missile launchers; trade in grain and technology; and the Helsinki Accords of 1975, in which the West accepted Europe's postwar borders and the Soviet bloc, signing for advantage, accepted human rights language its own dissidents then quoted back to it, a slow fuse inside the settlement. The counterattack at home: critics on the right who charged that détente let the Soviets build while America slept, and a human rights politics on the other side, Carter making the treatment of dissidents a test of relations and embarrassing allies and adversaries alike. The collapse: the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979, ending the decade's bargain, bringing a grain embargo, an Olympic boycott, the death of SALT II in the Senate, and a Soviet army sinking into its own Vietnam. The return of confrontation: Reagan elected on the charge that America had grown weak, the defense buildup, aid to anti-Communist insurgencies under the Reagan Doctrine, the "evil empire" speech of March 1983 naming the Soviet system itself as the enemy, and the Strategic Defense Initiative announced the same month, a promised shield in space that Soviet leaders read as an attempt to make their arsenal useless. The reading closes on the danger the renewed contest carried: 1983, the year of the Korean airliner shootdown and the Able Archer exercise that some in Moscow read as cover for a real attack, may have been the most dangerous year since 1962, run by leaders who were not speaking to each other.

**Activity concept:** The summit file. Students work five records, the SALT I agreement summary, a Helsinki Accords passage, the record of the Afghanistan invasion and the American answer, the "evil empire" speech passage, and the Strategic Defense Initiative announcement passage, and sort each by its direction, easing the contest or raising its pressure, with the evidence for the sorting, then judge whether détente failed because it was tried or because it was abandoned.

**Vocabulary:** détente, arms control, SALT, Helsinki Accords, Reagan Doctrine, Strategic Defense Initiative, Evil Empire

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP "evil empire" speech excerpt, audited record; the SALT and Helsinki texts are public documents of the United States and the Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 8: The Wall Falls: The Cold War Ends

**Lesson question:** Why did the Cold War end between 1989 and 1991, and what did its ending settle and leave unsettled?

**Reading scope:** The reformer first: Mikhail Gorbachev taking power in 1985 over a Soviet Union whose economy had stagnated for a decade, whose shops stood empty while its army held Afghanistan and its arsenal matched America's, and who concluded the system had to change to survive. His two instruments: glasnost, openness, releasing political prisoners, freeing the press, and letting the Soviet past itself be argued about in public; and perestroika, restructuring, meant to make the economy work and loosening, in the attempt, the party's grip on everything it touched. The diplomacy of the second chance: Reykjavik in 1986, where Reagan and Gorbachev came within reach of abolishing nuclear weapons and walked away, and the INF Treaty of 1987, the first agreement to destroy an entire class of nuclear weapons, signed a year after Reagan had stood at the Brandenburg Gate and told Gorbachev to tear down the wall. The year it broke: 1989, Poland voting Solidarity into government, Hungary opening its border, crowds in Leipzig growing by the week, and on November 9 East Germany's spokesman garbling a travel announcement and Berliners climbing the wall with hammers while the guards stood aside, Gorbachev refusing, all that autumn, to use the Soviet army to hold the empire. The settlement: German reunification inside NATO in October 1990, the Warsaw Pact dissolved, the Baltic states free, and then the center itself giving way, the failed hard-line coup of August 1991, Yeltsin on the tank, Ukraine voting to leave, and on December 25, 1991 Gorbachev resigning an office whose country no longer existed, the Soviet flag coming down over the Kremlin. The reading closes with the reckoning: the Cold War ended without the direct war both sides had armed against for forty five years, an ending no one in 1949 or 1962 or 1983 had predicted, and it left the United States the world's only superpower over a map of new states, old weapons, and unsettled questions the next unit takes up.

**Activity concept:** The end in sequence. Students build the ending from five records, the Brandenburg Gate speech passage, an INF Treaty passage, a November 1989 Berlin record with the photograph of crowds at the gate, the record of the August 1991 coup's failure, and the dissolution record of December 1991, ordering the sequence, marking at each step whose choice moved it, Gorbachev's, Reagan's, the crowds', or the system's own weight, then argue in a closing verdict which cause carried the most weight and answer the strongest counterclaim from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** glasnost, perestroika, Solidarity, INF Treaty, Berlin Wall, German reunification, dissolution of the Soviet Union

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Brandenburg Gate speech and the December 1989 Brandenburg Gate photograph, audited records. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Changes from the working map

- The unit opens with containment's formulation rather than with Korea, because the strategy is the unit's through line and every later lesson tests it: Korea as its first military application, Vietnam as the war it built, détente as its partial suspension, and 1989 as its vindication or its verdict, a judgment the last lesson leaves to the evidence.
- The Marshall Plan is taught here and not in the World War II unit, on the unit frame's reasoning: proposed in 1947 as an answer to Communist pressure in a ruined Europe, it is containment's economic instrument, not war settlement. The World War II unit's final lesson names the division at Potsdam without teaching the policy that answered it, and this unit picks the thread up at the Long Telegram.
- The Second Red Scare is this unit's home-front lesson rather than the Civil Rights unit's, because its machinery, the loyalty program, HUAC, the blacklist, and McCarthy, ran on Cold War fuel: the Soviet bomb, the fall of China, Korea, and real espionage. The Civil Rights unit owns the movement itself; this unit owns the fear the movement's opponents later borrowed against it, without teaching the movement's arc.
- Vietnam stands as its own lesson rather than a section of a wider Asia lesson, because the war is containment's largest test and its clearest failure on its own terms, and because its documents, the Tonkin Resolution, the Bundy memorandum, Tet, and the lottery, reward a full lesson's work.
- The unit closes in December 1991 with the dissolution stated as record, and its last questions left open on purpose. What the unipolar moment became belongs to Modern America.
