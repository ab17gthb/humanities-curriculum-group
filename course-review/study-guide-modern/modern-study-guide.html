The Arguments

Each unit's case in one paragraph, in the order the units were taught. Read the paragraph, close the guide, and restate it; where your restatement thins out is where your studying goes next.

The Gilded Age

Industrial capitalism reorganized American life in a generation: the corporation and the national market, railroads binding a continent, steel, oil, and electricity remaking production, and cities doubling on immigration's new sources. The gains and the costs were distributed by the same machine. Labor organized to answer, the Knights' broad crusade and the AFL's craft discipline, and met the injunction, the blacklist, and the troops; farmers organized into the Alliance and then Populism, the era's most serious third-party challenge. At the bottom of the order, the South wrote Jim Crow into law and disfranchisement into constitutions, Plessy blessed the fiction of separate equality, and lynching enforced what statutes could not say.

Progressive Era

Progressivism was the country's organized answer to the Gilded Age's dislocations, run through three channels at once: the democracy repaired (primaries, initiatives, the direct election of senators, and finally the Nineteenth Amendment), the corporation regulated (railroad rates, antitrust revived, pure food and meat laws, the Federal Reserve, conservation of the public lands), and the city and workplace made survivable (settlement houses, labor law, the muckrakers' exposure that powered all of it). Its presidents, Roosevelt, Taft, and Wilson, disagreed on means and shared the premise that the federal government was now the economy's referee. The movement's standing failure was the color line, which most of its white leadership accepted and its federal government, under Wilson, extended.

Imperialism and World War I

At the century's turn the United States took an empire and argued about it in the open: markets, naval strategy, and mission carried the flag to the Philippines and Puerto Rico, and the anti-imperialists answered in the Declaration's own terms. The Caribbean became an American lake policed by the Roosevelt Corollary, and the Panama Canal made the two-ocean navy possible. In 1914 neutrality was policy and profit at once; by 1917 unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann telegram had closed the argument. Mobilization built the modern war state and policed its home front, the Espionage and Sedition Acts, Schenck's clear and present danger, and the Fourteen Points won the war's diplomacy and lost the peace's in the Senate.

The Twenties

The Twenties' prosperity was real and narrow. Mass production and consumer credit remade daily life, the automobile, radio, and advertising building a national culture, while the era's politics argued over the culture itself: Prohibition's experiment and its repeal movement, the revived Klan, immigration restriction written into law, Scopes, and the Harlem Renaissance's counter-statement. The weak spots stood in plain sight: agriculture depressed all decade, income concentrating while wages lagged production, credit and margin speculation inflating the market the crash then repriced. The unit's verdict is that 1929 revealed the prosperity's structure more than it caused the Depression that followed, which is why the next unit begins in the spiral, not the crash.

Depression and New Deal

The Depression was deflation's machine: falling prices making debts heavier, bank failures destroying credit and savings together, a quarter of the labor force idle by 1933. Hoover answered actively and stopped at direct relief; Roosevelt crossed that line in his first week and experimented for eight years, the Hundred Days' alphabet, the Second New Deal's structures, Social Security, the Wagner Act, the WPA, until the coalition he built held national politics for a generation. The unit's ledger is double: the modern safety net, labor's legal standing, insured deposits, and a federal government answerable for the economy's health on one side; full employment unachieved, the era's exclusions written into its programs, and the 1937 recession proving how much of the recovery was the spending itself, on the other.

World War II

The war did what the New Deal had not, by spending at ten times the scale and drafting the surplus into uniform: the arsenal of democracy made the United States the Grand Alliance's supplier, a two-ocean belligerent whose production decided both wars, on an Eastern Front where the Soviet Union broke the German army and across a Pacific taken island by island to the atomic end. At home the war state rationed, priced, and propagandized; it opened factory gates to women and, under pressure, to Black workers behind the Double V's demand, and it imprisoned 120,000 Japanese Americans on ancestry, a judgment the Supreme Court sustained. The war's reckonings, the Holocaust exposed, Nuremberg's new law of individual responsibility, and the GI Bill's returning generation, are the next era's starting conditions.

The Cold War

Containment began as a premise, that Soviet expansion would crack under patient pressure, and acquired a house by 1950: the Truman Doctrine's pledge, the Marshall Plan's billions, NATO's alliance, and NSC-68's global military posture. Asia tested it first, China read as a loss, Korea fixing limited war as the American way of fighting one. At home the same fear ran the Second Red Scare's machinery until television broke McCarthy, while the nuclear shadow deepened from monopoly to hydrogen brinkmanship to deterrence's mutual hostage. Cuba taught both capitals the game's price; Vietnam applied the doctrine where its premises fit worst; détente managed the rivalry; and the end came from inside, when Gorbachev's reforms released everything they were meant to save.

Civil Rights

The movement stood on decades of construction, the NAACP's litigation ladder, the Double V generation's claims, Truman's committee and desegregation order, the Black church's networks, before Brown declared segregation's law void and massive resistance answered. Its engine was nonviolent direct action: disciplined protesters manufacturing a crisis in a place chosen for its opponent's reliability, national television carrying it into the federal government's in tray, at Montgomery, Greensboro, Birmingham, and Selma. The legal harvest, the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, remade the South's public order in five years. What the statutes could not reach, housing, wealth, the northern economy, is the unfinished agenda the movement itself named at Memphis and the Kerner Commission documented.

Modern America

Modern America opens with the postwar settlement's collapse, stagflation and the tax revolt, and the conservative turn that replaced it: lower taxes, loosened regulation, labor broken at PATCO, deficits accepted as the price. The Cold War's end was cashed as globalization, production integrated across borders, the Rust Belt emptying into the service economy, while the digital transformation rebuilt commerce, news, and politics in sequence. The population remade alongside, the 1965 immigration act's long fuse, the Sun Belt's rise, cities sorted by flight and return. Then the emergencies set the terms: September 11's security state and open-ended authorization, Iraq's failed intelligence case, the Great Recession's bank-first rescue, and populisms on both flanks, all inside the polarization the unit closes on.

Teacher Answer Key

Key Terms, With the Guided-Notes Definitions

The Gilded Age

Lesson 1 : The Engines of Industry

transcontinental railroad: a railroad line connecting the eastern rail network with the Pacific coast
Bessemer process: a method for making steel quickly and cheaply by blowing air through molten iron
refinery: a plant where crude oil is separated into useful products such as kerosene
corporation: a business owned by shareholders and treated by law as distinct from its owners
capital: money or property used to start or expand a business
telegraph: a system for sending coded messages over wires
standard time: a system of uniform time zones that replaced local solar time

Lesson 2 : Big Business and Its Critics

trust: a combination of corporations that limits competition within an industry
monopoly: control of an industry by one company or combination, with no effective competition
vertical integration: control by one company of every stage in making and selling a product
horizontal integration: control by one company of many firms at the same stage of an industry
laissez-faire: the argument that government should not regulate business or the economy
Social Darwinism: the argument that wealth and power show natural fitness and that government aid distorts competition
Sherman Antitrust Act: the 1890 federal law that banned combinations in restraint of trade

Lesson 3 : The Workers' World

union: an organization of workers formed to bargain with employers as a group
strike: a work stoppage meant to pressure an employer to meet workers' demands
collective bargaining: negotiation between an employer and workers organized as a group
injunction: a court order that requires or forbids a specific action
Knights of Labor: a national labor organization of the 1870s and 1880s that sought broad reform and organized many kinds of workers
American Federation of Labor: a federation of craft unions founded in 1886 that focused on wages, hours, and working conditions
child labor: paid work by children, often in factories, mines, mills, and street trades

Lesson 4 : The New Immigration

immigrant: a person who comes to live permanently in a new country
emigrant: a person who leaves a country to live elsewhere
nativism: a policy or attitude favoring native-born residents over immigrants
Ellis Island: the federal immigration station in New York Harbor that processed most European arrivals after 1892
Angel Island: the immigration station in San Francisco Bay that processed and often detained many Asian arrivals
Chinese Exclusion Act: the 1882 federal law that suspended Chinese labor immigration and barred Chinese residents from naturalized citizenship
assimilation: the process by which a person or group adopts the customs and language of another culture

Lesson 5 : The City

tenement: a crowded, often unsafe multi-family apartment building in a city
urbanization: the growth of cities as people move to them for work and services
political machine: an organization that controls a city or state government through favors, jobs, and votes
boss: the leader of a political machine who directs its workers and rewards
graft: the illegal use of political power for personal profit, such as kickbacks and bribes
settlement house: a community center in a poor neighborhood offering classes, childcare, and help to residents
sanitation: public measures that keep water, waste, and streets clean enough to protect health

Lesson 6 : The West

homestead: a farm claim of up to 160 acres of public land made under the Homestead Act
land grant: public land given by the government to a railroad or other company to support construction
cattle drive: the movement of herds on foot from Texas ranches to railheads and markets
mining boom: a rapid rush of people and capital to a place where gold, silver, or another mineral was found
barbed wire: fencing wire with sharp barbs that enclosed open range cheaply
dry farming: methods of growing crops in dry regions by conserving soil moisture
reservation: land set aside by treaty or federal order for a Native nation

Lesson 7 : Native Americans and the Closing Frontier

assimilation: the process by which a person or group is made to adopt the customs and language of another culture
allotment: the division of tribal land into individual parcels under federal policy
Dawes Act: the 1887 federal law that divided reservation land into individual allotments and sold surplus land to non-Native buyers
reservation system: federal policy that confined Native nations to defined areas of land under federal supervision
boarding school: a school that removed Native children from their families to suppress Native languages and cultures
Ghost Dance: a religious movement that promised renewal for Native peoples and spread across western reservations in 1889 and 1890
Wounded Knee: the 1890 killing of Lakota men, women, and children by United States troops in South Dakota

Lesson 8 : Race, Politics, and Protest

Jim Crow: the system of state and local laws and customs that enforced racial segregation in the South
segregation: the separation of people by race in public facilities, schools, and transportation
disfranchisement: the denial of the right to vote through law or practice
Plessy v. Ferguson: the 1896 Supreme Court decision that upheld segregation under the doctrine of separate but equal
Atlanta Compromise: Booker T. Washington's 1895 speech proposing that Black southerners accept segregation for the present and build economic strength first
Populist Party: the People's Party, founded in 1892 to represent farmers and laborers against banks, railroads, and corporations
free silver: the proposal to coin silver without limit at a ratio of 16 to 1 with gold, expanding the money supply

Progressive Era

Lesson 1 : The Problems Progressives Faced

laissez-faire: the idea that government should keep its hands off business and the economy
tenement: a crowded, often unsafe multi-family apartment building in a city
political machine: an organization that controls a city or state government through favors, jobs, and votes
graft: the illegal use of political power for personal profit, such as kickbacks and bribes
trust: a combination of companies formed to control an industry and limit competition
Social Gospel: a movement teaching that Christian faith required improving social conditions
settlement house: a community center in a poor neighborhood offering classes, childcare, and help to residents

Lesson 2 : The Muckrakers

muckraker: a journalist who investigated and exposed corruption, dangerous conditions, and corporate wrongdoing
exposé: a published report that reveals wrongdoing the public did not know about
mass-circulation magazine: a magazine sold cheaply to hundreds of thousands of readers and funded largely by advertising
serialized: published in installments over many issues
The Jungle (1906): Upton Sinclair's novel of the Chicago meatpacking industry, whose descriptions of filth in the food pushed Congress to act
Meat Inspection Act (1906): law requiring federal inspection of meat crossing state lines
public opinion: the views of the general public, which elected officials ignore at their risk

Lesson 3 : Reforming Government

initiative: citizens propose a law by petition and place it directly before the voters
referendum: a law is sent to the voters for final approval or rejection
recall: voters remove an elected official before the term expires, by petition and special vote
direct primary: an election in which a party's voters, not its bosses, choose the party's candidates
Wisconsin Idea: governing through trained experts, with university professors drafting bills and staffing state commissions
commission government: a city run by a small elected commission, each member heading one department
city manager: a professional administrator hired by an elected council to run a city's daily operations

Lesson 4 : Roosevelt and the Square Deal

Square Deal: Roosevelt's program of corporate control, consumer protection, and conservation
Sherman Antitrust Act: the federal law against business combinations in restraint of trade, used to dissolve the Northern Securities holding company in 1904
arbitration: settling a dispute through a neutral third party whose decision both sides accept
Hepburn Act (1906): law giving the Interstate Commerce Commission power to set maximum railroad rates
Pure Food and Drug Act (1906): law banning impure and falsely labeled food and drugs
conservation: the managed, expert-supervised use of natural resources so they last
Antiquities Act (1906): law letting the president protect landmarks as national monuments without Congress

Lesson 5 : Taft, the Split, and the Election of 1912

Mann-Elkins Act (1910): law extending federal regulation of the railroads, passed under President Taft
Payne-Aldrich Tariff (1909): tariff law, promised as reform, that lowered some rates and raised others
Ballinger-Pinchot affair: the 1910 fight in which Taft fired forester Gifford Pinchot after Pinchot accused the Interior secretary of favoring corporate land claims
Progressive Party: the party Roosevelt's supporters formed in 1912, nicknamed Bull Moose
Eugene V. Debs: the Socialist candidate for president in 1912, who argued for public ownership of railroads and major industries
New Nationalism: Roosevelt's 1912 program of regulating large corporations through a strong federal government
New Freedom: Wilson's 1912 program of breaking up monopoly and restoring competition

Lesson 6 : Wilson and the New Freedom

triple wall of privilege: Wilson's name for the three targets of his program, the tariff, the banking system, and the trusts
Underwood Tariff (1913): law cutting average import duties from about 40 percent to about 25 percent
Sixteenth Amendment (1913): amendment authorizing a federal income tax, overturning the Pollock decision
Federal Reserve Act (1913): law creating twelve regional Reserve banks under a federal board in Washington
Clayton Antitrust Act (1914): law naming and banning specific monopoly practices and protecting unions from conspiracy charges
Federal Trade Commission (1914): permanent expert commission empowered to stop unfair methods of competition
Adamson Act (1916): law setting an eight-hour day for railroad workers

Lesson 7 : The Long Road to the Nineteenth Amendment

suffrage: the right to vote
Seneca Falls (1848): the convention that opened the women's rights movement, issuing a Declaration of Sentiments modeled on the Declaration of Independence
NAWSA: the National American Woman Suffrage Association, formed in 1890 by the reunited wings of the movement
Silent Sentinels: Alice Paul's pickets who stood at the White House gates from January 1917
Night of Terror (1917): the November night when guards at the Occoquan Workhouse beat the imprisoned suffrage pickets
Winning Plan: Carrie Chapman Catt's strategy of coordinating state campaigns with a federal amendment drive
Nineteenth Amendment (1920): amendment barring denial of the vote on account of sex

Lesson 8 : The Limits of Progressivism

Jim Crow: the system of southern state laws enforcing segregation and stripping Black citizens of the vote
poll tax: a fee charged before a person could vote, used to keep Black and poor citizens from the polls
grandfather clause: a rule exempting voters whose grandfathers could vote, used to disfranchise Black voters while sparing illiterate whites
NAACP (1909): the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, founded to fight segregation and disfranchisement through the courts
Immigration Act of 1917: law imposing a literacy test on immigrants, passed over Wilson's veto
Eighteenth Amendment (1919): amendment banning the manufacture, sale, and transportation of alcohol
Volstead Act (1919): the federal enforcement law for Prohibition

Imperialism and World War I

Lesson 1 : The Turn Overseas

imperialism: a policy of extending a nation's power by taking territory or control over other peoples and lands
navalism: the policy of making a powerful navy the foundation of national strength and overseas influence
coaling station: a port where steamships stop to load coal, water, and supplies on a long voyage
annexation: the formal act by which a country adds territory to itself
jingoism: boastful and aggressive patriotism that urges war or expansion against other nations
frontier thesis: Frederick Jackson Turner's argument that free land and a moving frontier explained American development and shaped the national character
protectorate: a territory controlled and protected by a stronger nation

Lesson 2 : The Spanish-American War

yellow journalism: sensational newspaper reporting that stretched or invented facts to sell papers and stir public feeling
USS Maine: the American battleship that exploded and sank in Havana harbor on February 15, 1898, killing 266 officers and men and becoming the rallying cause of the war
Teller Amendment: the 1898 congressional provision promising that the United States would not annex Cuba and would leave its government to the Cuban people
Rough Riders: the First United States Volunteer Cavalry, the regiment led by Theodore Roosevelt that won fame in the fighting around Santiago, Cuba
Treaty of Paris: the December 1898 treaty that ended the war and transferred Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines from Spain to the United States
cession: the formal transfer of territory from one country to another by treaty
Platt Amendment: the 1901 congressional provision, written into Cuba's constitution, that gave the United States the right to intervene in Cuba and to hold naval stations there

Lesson 3 : An Empire in the Philippines

Philippine-American War: the war fought from 1899 to 1902 between the United States and the Philippine Republic over American annexation of the Philippines
anti-imperialism: opposition to acquiring and ruling overseas colonies, especially on the ground that governing a people without its consent violates republican principles
Emilio Aguinaldo: the Filipino revolutionary leader who proclaimed Philippine independence in 1898 and served as president of the Philippine Republic
guerrilla warfare: fighting by small, mobile bands that ambush, raid, and disperse instead of meeting an enemy army in open battle
Benevolent Assimilation: the name President McKinley gave his Philippine policy in his proclamation of December 21, 1898, claiming that American rule would substitute just government for arbitrary rule
Insular Cases: the Supreme Court decisions beginning in 1901 that held the new island territories were not fully part of the United States and that the Constitution did not apply there in full
civil government: the rule of a territory by civilian officials acting under law, in place of direct military rule

Lesson 4 : Open Doors and the Big Stick

Open Door: the 1899 and 1900 United States policy calling for equal trading rights for all nations in China and for China to remain one country
sphere of influence: a region in which one outside power holds dominant trading and investment privileges over the others
Boxer Rebellion: the 1900 uprising in China by the Boxers, a society opposed to foreign influence, put down by an eight-nation force that included United States troops
Roosevelt Corollary: Theodore Roosevelt's 1904 addition to the Monroe Doctrine claiming a United States right to intervene in Latin American countries guilty of chronic wrongdoing or unable to pay their debts
dollar diplomacy: President Taft's policy of using American loans and investment, backed by government pressure, to extend United States influence abroad
Panama Canal: the ship canal across the Isthmus of Panama, built by the United States between 1904 and 1914, linking the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans
Gentlemen's Agreement: the 1907 and 1908 understanding in which Japan limited the emigration of its laborers to the United States and the United States ended the segregation of Japanese students in San Francisco schools

Lesson 5 : Neutrality Tested

neutrality: a nation's policy of taking no side in a war between other countries
Lusitania: a British passenger liner sunk by a German submarine off Ireland on May 7, 1915, killing nearly 1,200 people, including 128 Americans
unrestricted submarine warfare: a naval policy of sinking ships in a declared war zone without warning, including neutral and passenger ships
Sussex Pledge: Germany's promise of May 4, 1916, after the Sussex attack, that its submarines would not sink merchant vessels without warning and without saving human lives
Zimmermann Telegram: a secret January 1917 message from Germany's foreign minister proposing that Mexico ally with Germany against the United States and recover Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona
contraband: goods, such as weapons and military supplies, that a belligerent claims the right to seize from neutral ships bound for its enemy
preparedness: the movement and program in the United States before 1917 to strengthen the army and navy in case the country was drawn into the war

Lesson 6 : Americans in the War

conscription: compulsory enrollment for military service, also called the draft
American Expeditionary Forces: the United States forces sent to fight in Europe under General John J. Pershing
convoy system: the grouping of troopships and supply ships to sail together under naval escort against submarines
trench warfare: fighting from lines of dug trenches facing each other across open ground called no man's land
armistice: an agreement to stop fighting, signed on November 11, 1918, that ended the fighting in the First World War
Doughboys: a nickname for American infantry soldiers in the First World War
influenza pandemic: the worldwide outbreak of influenza in 1918 and 1919 that killed millions of people, including an estimated 675,000 in the United States

Lesson 7 : The Home Front

Liberty Bonds: government bonds sold to the public to raise money for the war, repaid with interest
War Industries Board: the federal agency that coordinated war production and set priorities for industry during the war
Committee on Public Information: the federal agency that promoted support for the war through posters, pamphlets, films, and speakers
Espionage Act: the 1917 federal law that punished spying and acts intended to interfere with the military or obstruct recruiting
Sedition Act: the 1918 federal law that punished disloyal or abusive speech about the government, the Constitution, the flag, and the armed forces
Great Migration: the movement of hundreds of thousands of Black southerners to northern cities for jobs and greater rights during and after the war years
Schenck v. United States: the 1919 Supreme Court case that upheld an Espionage Act conviction and stated the clear and present danger test for wartime speech

Lesson 8 : Wilson's Peace and the Turn Inward

Fourteen Points: President Woodrow Wilson's program for a just peace, presented to Congress on January 8, 1918, in fourteen numbered principles that ended with a general association of nations
League of Nations: the general association of nations created by the Treaty of Versailles in 1919, whose members promised to preserve peace and protect one another against external aggression
Treaty of Versailles: the treaty signed on June 28, 1919, between Germany and the Allied and Associated Powers, which created the League of Nations and imposed territorial losses, disarmament, and reparations on Germany
reparations: payments imposed on a defeated nation for the loss and damage its aggression caused during a war
Lodge reservations: the fourteen conditions Senator Henry Cabot Lodge and the Senate Foreign Relations Committee attached to ratification of the Treaty of Versailles, chiefly reserving to Congress the decision on any United States obligation under Article 10
Red Scare: the fear in 1919 and 1920 that Communists and other radicals were plotting revolution in the United States, and the government campaign against suspected radicals that the fear produced
normalcy: Warren G. Harding's word in the 1920 campaign for a return to ordinary life after war, with less government activism at home and less involvement abroad

The Twenties

Lesson 1 : The Business of America

assembly line: a factory arrangement in which a product moves past workers who each add one part or perform one task until the product is finished
mass production: the making of goods in very large quantities using standardized parts and machinery, so that each item costs less to produce
Model T: the low priced automobile built by the Ford Motor Company from 1908 to 1927 and sold to millions of ordinary buyers
productivity: the amount of goods or work produced for each hour of labor or for each unit of input
installment buying: purchasing a good by paying part of the price at the time of purchase and the rest in regular payments over time, usually with interest or fees added
advertising: paid public messages that present a product to potential buyers and seek to create a desire to buy it
chain store: one of a group of retail stores owned by a single company, carrying the same name, goods, and prices across many locations

Lesson 2 : Mass Media and the Celebrity Age

mass media: forms of communication that reach very large audiences at the same time, including radio, film, and newspapers sold across the country
broadcast: to send a program by radio waves to many listeners at once from a single station
radio network: a group of stations linked by wire that carry the same programs at the same time under one company
talkie: a motion picture with recorded dialogue and sound, which replaced silent film after 1927
tabloid: a newspaper printed in a smaller format that used large photographs and bold headlines to report crime, scandal, and celebrity news
celebrity: a person who is widely known and followed by the public because the media cover his or her life and work
fad: a craze that spreads quickly through the public and fades within months, such as a dance, a game, or a stunt

Lesson 3 : America on Wheels

suburb: a residential area on the edge of a city, outside the center, where people live and travel into the city for work
commuter: a person who travels regularly between home in one place and work in another, usually each day
paved highway: a main road between places with a hard, surfaced roadway of concrete, asphalt, or brick that can be used in wet weather
filling station: a roadside business that sells gasoline and oil and provides air, water, and basic repairs for automobiles
tourism: travel for pleasure, and the businesses that serve travelers with food, fuel, lodging, and entertainment
motel: a lodging built for motorists, from motor and hotel, in which guests park their cars beside or near their rooms
dating: a form of courtship in which a young man and a young woman go out together to public amusements, away from the family home and its supervision

Lesson 4 : The Politics of Prosperity

Teapot Dome: the scandal in which Secretary of the Interior Albert B. Fall secretly leased federal naval oil reserves in Wyoming and California to private oil companies and accepted money from the oilmen in return; Fall was convicted and imprisoned
tax cut: a reduction in the rates at which a tax is charged, here the cuts in federal income tax rates during the 1920s
tariff: a tax charged on goods brought into a country from abroad
isolationism: a policy of avoiding political commitments and alliances with other countries while keeping a country free to act on its own
Washington Conference: the conference held in Washington, D.C., from November 1921 to February 1922, where the major naval powers agreed to limit the size of their battle fleets
war debt: money one government owes another for wartime loans, here the debts the Allied governments owed the United States from the First World War
Kellogg-Briand Pact: the agreement signed in Paris in 1928 in which the signing nations renounced war as an instrument of national policy

Lesson 5 : The Noble Experiment

Prohibition: the nationwide ban on the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquor for beverage purposes, in force under the Eighteenth Amendment from 1920 to 1933
Volstead Act: the National Prohibition Act of 1919, the federal law that enforced the Eighteenth Amendment and defined intoxicating liquor as any beverage containing 0.5 percent or more alcohol by volume
bootlegger: a person who illegally made, transported, or sold liquor during Prohibition
speakeasy: an illegal bar or club that sold liquor during Prohibition, often hidden behind a locked door or a legitimate business
organized crime: criminal activity carried out by a structured group that operates like a business, with leaders, ranks, territory, and regular sources of income
racketeering: the use of threats, violence, or a front business to extract money from businesses or workers, including forcing them to pay for protection or services they did not choose
repeal: the cancellation of a law or constitutional amendment; the Twenty-first Amendment repealed the Eighteenth in 1933

Lesson 6 : The Culture Wars

quota system: a method of limiting immigration by setting a fixed maximum number of immigrants that may be admitted from each country in a year
National Origins Act: the federal immigration law of 1924 that set each country quota at 2 percent of its people counted in the census of 1890 and barred nearly all immigration from Asia
eugenics: a movement that claimed ability and character were inherited and could be ranked by ancestry, and that argued government should favor the groups it ranked high and restrict the groups it ranked low
Ku Klux Klan: the organization revived in 1915 that claimed to defend white Protestant America and used intimidation and violence against Black Americans, Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and others it declared un-American
fundamentalism: a Protestant movement that defended a literal reading of the Bible and opposed public school teaching that conflicted with it
evolution: the scientific theory that species change over generations and that present species, including human beings, developed from earlier forms of life
anarchism: a political belief that government and all ruling authority should be abolished and society organized without a state

Lesson 7 : The Great Migration and the Harlem Renaissance

Great Migration: the movement of hundreds of thousands of Black southerners to northern cities for jobs and greater rights during and after the war years
New Negro: a Black American of the 1920s who claimed dignity and equal rights, took pride in Black history and culture, and refused a second class place
Harlem Renaissance: the flowering of Black writing, music, and art in the 1920s, centered in Harlem in New York City
jazz: music formed in Black communities of New Orleans from ragtime, blues, and brass band traditions, built on syncopation and improvisation
blues: a Black musical form built on a repeating pattern of chords, with verses that name hardship, work, and endurance in plain language
UNIA: the Universal Negro Improvement Association, Marcus Garvey's mass organization, founded to unite Black people worldwide and to build Black owned businesses and political power
Chicago Defender: the Black newspaper founded in Chicago in 1905 by Robert S. Abbott, which urged Black southerners to move north and printed the news of lynching and discrimination that white papers ignored

Lesson 8 : The Weak Spots and the Crash

overproduction: production of more of a good than buyers will purchase at prices that cover the cost of producing it
open shop: a workplace in which workers are not required to join a union in order to hold their jobs
welfare capitalism: an employer's policy of providing benefits such as pensions, paid vacations, and recreation in order to keep workers satisfied and to keep unions out
company union: an organization for discussing workers' grievances that is set up and financed by the employer rather than by an independent union
speculation: buying an asset in the hope of selling it soon at a higher price, rather than for the income the asset produces
buying on margin: buying stock by paying only part of the price and borrowing the rest from a broker, with the stock pledged as security for the loan
Black Tuesday: October 29, 1929, the day of the heaviest selling in the stock market crash of 1929, when about 16.4 million shares were traded

Depression and New Deal

Lesson 1 : The Human Cost of the Depression

Great Depression: the severe economic collapse that began in the United States in 1929 and lasted through the 1930s, marked by bank failures, mass unemployment, and falling prices
deflation: a general fall in prices across the economy, which raises the real burden of debts and rewards waiting to spend
bank run: a rush of depositors to withdraw their money from a bank at the same time, driven by fear that the bank will fail
unemployment: the condition of people who are able to work and seeking work but cannot find a job
breadline: a line of people waiting for free food given out by a charity, a church, or a city agency
Hooverville: a shantytown built by homeless people from scrap wood, tin, and cardboard during the Depression, named in bitter reference to President Hoover
Dust Bowl: the region of the southern Great Plains where drought and poor farming practices turned topsoil to dust in the 1930s, driving many farm families from their land

Lesson 2 : Hoover and the Limits of Voluntarism

voluntarism: Hoover's policy of meeting the Depression through voluntary cooperation by business, charities, and local government rather than through federal relief programs
rugged individualism: Hoover's belief that Americans should succeed through their own effort and that direct federal relief would weaken character and self reliance
Reconstruction Finance Corporation: the federal agency created in 1932 that lent public money to banks, railroads, and insurance companies in the hope that stability at the top would restore jobs below
public works: government funded construction projects such as dams, roads, and public buildings, built to provide jobs as well as lasting structures
Hawley-Smoot Tariff: the 1930 law that raised United States tariffs to the highest level in the country's history, and drew retaliation that cut world trade
Bonus Army: the veterans of the First World War who camped in Washington in the summer of 1932 demanding early payment of their promised service bonus, and were driven out by federal troops
interregnum: the period between Roosevelt's election in November 1932 and his inauguration in March 1933, when the outgoing and incoming presidents shared responsibility for a deepening crisis and neither acted decisively

Lesson 3 : Roosevelt and the First Hundred Days

bank holiday: the closing of all the nation's banks by presidential proclamation on March 6, 1933, so that examiners could sort sound banks from failed ones before reopening
Emergency Banking Act: the law passed by Congress on March 9, 1933, that gave the president broad power over banking and allowed sound banks to reopen under federal license
fireside chat: one of President Roosevelt's radio addresses to the nation, delivered in plain conversational language from the White House
Hundred Days: the first phase of Roosevelt's presidency, from March 9 to June 16, 1933, in which Congress passed an exceptional volume of New Deal legislation
Glass-Steagall Act: the 1933 law that separated commercial banking from investment banking and created federal insurance of bank deposits
Agricultural Adjustment Act: the 1933 law that paid farmers to reduce production of basic crops and livestock in order to raise farm prices toward parity
Tennessee Valley Authority: the federal corporation created in 1933 to build dams, control flooding, and bring electricity and development to the Tennessee River valley

Lesson 4 : Relief, Recovery, and the Alphabet Agencies at Work

direct relief: money, food, or goods given directly to people in need without work required in return
work relief: government paid jobs on public projects, given to unemployed people instead of direct relief payments
Civilian Conservation Corps: the 1933 program that employed young men in camps to plant trees, fight fires, and build park roads and trails, paying thirty dollars a month, most of it sent home
Federal Emergency Relief Administration: the 1933 agency, led by Harry Hopkins, that sent federal relief money to the states to pass on to people in need
Civil Works Administration: the emergency program that hired about four million unemployed people for the winter of 1933 to 1934 on small public projects
Public Works Administration: the 1933 agency, led by Harold Ickes, that funded large construction projects such as dams, bridges, schools, and courthouses, built by private contractors
National Recovery Administration: the agency created in 1933 to write and enforce codes of fair competition for each industry, setting prices, wages, and hours and allowing workers to organize

Lesson 5 : Too Little, Too Much

Share Our Wealth: Huey Long's program and movement, begun in 1934, which promised to cap large fortunes and guarantee every family a homestead and a minimum income
Townsend Plan: Dr. Francis Townsend's proposal that the federal government pay two hundred dollars a month to every citizen over sixty, on condition the money be spent within the month
old-age pension: a regular payment made by a government to people who have reached a set age and left regular work
demagogue: a leader who gains power by arousing popular fears, prejudices, and hopes with simple promises, rather than by answering arguments
American Liberty League: the organization founded in 1934 by wealthy businessmen and conservative Democrats to oppose the New Deal as a threat to property and the Constitution
Schechter v. United States: the 1935 Supreme Court decision that struck down the National Industrial Recovery Act, holding that Congress had given away its lawmaking power and reached business beyond its authority
United States v. Butler: the 1936 Supreme Court decision that struck down the Agricultural Adjustment Act's processing tax as an unconstitutional use of federal power over farm production

Lesson 6 : The Second New Deal

Works Progress Administration: the agency created in 1935, led by Harry Hopkins, that employed about eight and a half million people over its life building roads, schools, and airports and funding arts and writing projects
Social Security Act: the law of August 1935 that created old-age insurance, unemployment insurance with the states, and aid to dependent children and other vulnerable groups
old-age insurance: the part of Social Security that pays a monthly benefit to retired workers, funded by payroll taxes on workers and their employers
unemployment insurance: the joint federal and state system, created in 1935, that pays temporary benefits to workers who lose their jobs
payroll tax: a tax taken from workers' wages and matched by employers, used to fund Social Security
National Labor Relations Act: the 1935 law, called the Wagner Act, that guaranteed workers the right to organize unions and bargain collectively and created a board to enforce those rights
collective bargaining: negotiation between an employer and a union chosen by the workers over wages, hours, and working conditions

Lesson 7 : Labor Rising and the Recession of 1937

sit-down strike: a strike in which workers stop work and remain inside the factory, holding the plant so that replacement workers cannot be brought in
Congress of Industrial Organizations: the federation formed in 1935 to organize workers by whole industry, including the unskilled, rather than by craft
United Auto Workers: the union of automobile workers, recognized by General Motors in February 1937 after the Flint sit-down strike
collective bargaining: negotiation between an employer and a union chosen by the workers over wages, hours, and working conditions
court packing: the name opponents gave Roosevelt's 1937 proposal to add a new justice to the Supreme Court for each sitting justice over seventy, which would have let him appoint up to six
Roosevelt recession: the sharp economic downturn of 1937 to 1938, caused in part by federal spending cuts and new payroll taxes pulling money out of a weak economy
deficit spending: government spending beyond the revenue it collects, financed by borrowing, used to put money into an economy in a slump

Lesson 8 : What the New Deal Changed and What It Did Not

New Deal coalition: the alliance of white southerners, northern city voters, union members, farmers, and increasingly Black voters that kept the Democratic Party in national power for a generation after 1932
Fair Labor Standards Act: the 1938 law that set a federal minimum wage, required overtime pay past forty hours a week, and restricted child labor
safety net: the set of government programs that catch people who fall into poverty through old age, unemployment, disability, or the loss of a family earner
redlining: the practice by which federal housing officials and lenders marked minority neighborhoods as risky on maps and denied loans there, keeping Black families from buying homes on equal terms
repatriation: the removal of people of Mexican descent, including many United States citizens, to Mexico during the Depression, carried out by local and federal authorities under economic pressure
Securities and Exchange Commission: the federal agency created in 1934 to regulate the stock markets and require honest public reporting by companies selling stock
Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation: the federal agency created in 1933 that insures bank deposits, so that depositors are repaid by the government if their bank fails

World War II

Lesson 1 : The Road to War: Neutrality and Its Critics

isolationism: a policy of avoiding political commitments and alliances with other countries while keeping a country free to act on its own
Neutrality Acts: the laws passed by Congress in the 1930s that barred arms sales and loans to nations at war, written to keep the United States out of another world war
fireside chat: one of President Roosevelt's radio addresses to the nation, delivered in plain conversational language from the White House
cash-and-carry: the policy adopted in 1939 that let nations at war buy American arms and goods if they paid cash and carried them away in their own ships
Lend-Lease: the law of March 1941 that let the president lend or lease arms and supplies to any country whose defense he judged vital to the defense of the United States
Four Freedoms: the four freedoms Roosevelt named in his annual message of January 6, 1941 as the foundations of a just world: freedom of speech, freedom of worship, freedom from want, and freedom from fear
Atlantic Charter: the joint statement of aims issued by Roosevelt and Churchill in August 1941, pledging self-determination for peoples, freer trade, and freedom of the seas

Lesson 2 : Pearl Harbor and the Arsenal of Democracy

Pearl Harbor: the United States naval base in Hawaii attacked by Japanese carrier aircraft on December 7, 1941, the attack that brought the United States into the Second World War
Axis powers: the alliance of Germany, Italy, and Japan that fought the Allied nations in the Second World War
Allies: the nations allied against the Axis in the Second World War, led by the United States, Great Britain, and the Soviet Union
Grand Alliance: the formal alliance of the United States, Great Britain, and the Soviet Union against the Axis, joined by many other nations in the Declaration by United Nations of January 1, 1942
Germany first: the Allied strategy of concentrating the main effort on defeating Germany before Japan, agreed by the American and British staffs and kept through the war
second front: the Allied invasion of German-held western Europe that the Soviet Union demanded from 1942 onward to draw German strength away from the Eastern Front
theater: a large geographic area of military operations, such as the European theater or the Pacific theater

Lesson 3 : Mobilizing the Home Front

mobilization: the process of preparing a nation's armed forces, industry, and economy for war
Selective Training and Service Act: the law of 1940 that created the first peacetime draft in American history, requiring men of set ages to register for military service as called
War Production Board: the federal agency created in 1942 to direct the conversion of industry to war production and to allocate steel, rubber, and other scarce materials
war bonds: government bonds sold to the public to raise money for the war, repaid with interest
rationing: a system limiting how much of a scarce good each person may buy, enforced with coupons or ration stamps
Office of Price Administration: the federal agency that set price ceilings and ran the rationing system during the war
Rosie the Riveter: the popular name and image for the American women who took factory and shipyard jobs during the war, used in songs and posters to recruit women to war work

Lesson 4 : The War in Europe and North Africa

amphibious landing: an attack from the sea onto a defended shore by forces carried in ships and landing craft
Eastern Front: the battlefront between Germany and the Soviet Union after June 1941, where the largest land fighting of the war took place
Stalingrad: the battle of August 1942 to February 1943 in which the Soviet army surrounded and destroyed a German army, the turning point of the war on the Eastern Front
strategic bombing: the use of heavy bombers against an enemy's industry, transportation, and cities, aiming to break its capacity and will to make war
D-Day: June 6, 1944, the day Allied forces landed in Normandy, France, in the largest seaborne invasion in history
Battle of the Bulge: the last major German offensive in the west, launched through the Ardennes in December 1944 and beaten back by February 1945
VE Day: May 8, 1945, the day victory in Europe was announced after Germany's unconditional surrender

Lesson 5 : The War in the Pacific

Guadalcanal: the Solomon Islands campaign of August 1942 to February 1943, the first major Allied offensive of the Pacific war, fought on land, at sea, and in the air around one island and its airfield
Battle of Midway: the naval battle of June 1942 in which American carrier aircraft sank four Japanese carriers and ended Japan's ability to take the offensive in the Pacific
island hopping: the Allied Pacific strategy of seizing selected islands for airfields while bypassing strong Japanese garrisons and cutting them off from supply
naval blockade: the use of warships, submarines, and aircraft to cut off an enemy's sea trade and supplies
firebombing: the bombing of cities with incendiary weapons designed to start mass fires
kamikaze: a Japanese pilot who made a deliberate crash attack on an enemy ship; also the attack itself
unconditional surrender: the demand that an enemy give up without negotiated conditions, announced as Allied policy for Germany and Japan

Lesson 6 : A War for Democracy? Exclusion, Internment, and Service

Executive Order 8802: President Roosevelt's order of June 1941 barring racial discrimination in defense industries and federal employment, issued under the threat of a protest march on Washington
Fair Employment Practices Committee: the federal committee created in 1941 to investigate complaints of job discrimination in war industries
Double V campaign: the campaign led by Black newspapers from 1942 for victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home
Executive Order 9066: President Roosevelt's order of February 19, 1942 authorizing the military to exclude people from areas of the West Coast, the order under which Japanese Americans were removed from their homes
internment: the forced confinement of a group of people; in this unit, the removal of about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, two thirds of them American citizens, to guarded camps for much of the war
Korematsu v. United States: the 1944 Supreme Court decision that upheld the exclusion of Japanese Americans from the West Coast as a military necessity
442nd Regimental Combat Team: the segregated Japanese American army unit that fought in Europe and became one of the most decorated units of its size and length of service in American history

Lesson 7 : The Holocaust and the War's Crimes

antisemitism: prejudice against, hatred of, or discrimination against Jews as a religious or ethnic group
Nuremberg Laws: the German laws of 1935 that stripped Jews of citizenship and forbade marriage between Jews and non-Jewish Germans
Holocaust: the state-sponsored persecution and murder of six million European Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators, together with the murder of millions of others, including Roma, people with disabilities, and Soviet prisoners of war
ghetto: a section of a city where Jews were forced to live in crowded and starved conditions under German occupation
Final Solution: the Nazi program to murder every Jew in Europe, carried out by shooting units, deportations, and killing centers from 1941 to 1945
death camp: one of the camps built by Nazi Germany in occupied Poland where Jews and others were murdered, most in gas chambers on arrival
war crimes: violations of the laws of war, such as the murder of prisoners and civilians, for which individuals were tried after the war

Lesson 8 : Victory and the Atomic Reckoning

Yalta Conference: the meeting of Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin in February 1945 that planned the postwar settlement, including the United Nations and Soviet entry into the war against Japan
Potsdam Conference: the meeting of Truman, Churchill, and Stalin in July 1945 in occupied Germany, where the leaders set occupation policy and argued over Europe's future
Potsdam Declaration: the Allied ultimatum of July 26, 1945 demanding Japan's unconditional surrender and warning of prompt and utter destruction if Japan refused
Manhattan Project: the secret American program that built the first atomic bombs, employing more than 100,000 people at sites across the country
atomic bomb: a bomb whose explosive power comes from splitting atoms, releasing energy on a scale no ordinary explosive can match
demobilization: the process of bringing armed forces back to civilian life and reducing a wartime military to peacetime size
GI Bill: the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, which gave veterans unemployment pay, tuition for college and training, and government-backed home and business loans

The Cold War

Lesson 1 : Allies to Adversaries: Containment Takes Shape

superpower: a nation with power and influence far beyond that of other states, able to project military and political strength across the world
Iron Curtain: the boundary Winston Churchill named in 1946 between the Soviet-dominated states of Eastern Europe and the free states of the West, and the division of Europe it described
containment: the American strategy of blocking the further expansion of Soviet power and communism, first argued by George Kennan and adopted as the guiding policy of the early Cold War
Truman Doctrine: the policy President Truman declared in 1947, that the United States would support free peoples resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or outside pressures
Marshall Plan: the American program of 1948 to 1952 that gave about thirteen billion dollars in aid to rebuild the economies of Western Europe after the Second World War
Berlin Airlift: the American and British air supply of West Berlin in 1948 and 1949, flown around the Soviet blockade of the city's land routes
NATO: the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, the alliance formed in 1949 in which the United States, Canada, and Western European nations pledged to treat an attack on one member as an attack on all

Lesson 2 : The Cold War in Asia: China and Korea

NSC-68: the secret American planning paper of 1950 that described the Cold War as a global struggle and called for a large buildup of American military strength
People's Republic of China: the Communist state Mao Zedong proclaimed in 1949 after his forces won the Chinese civil war, recognized by the Soviet Union and not by the United States
Korean War: the war of 1950 to 1953 in which the United States and United Nations forces fought North Korea and China to preserve a non-Communist South Korea
38th parallel: the line of latitude chosen in 1945 to divide Soviet and American occupation zones in Korea, which became the border between North and South Korea
limited war: a war fought for limited aims with some weapons and targets deliberately left unused, rather than for the enemy's total defeat
stalemate: a condition in fighting in which neither side can gain ground or force a decision, however long the fighting continues
armistice: an agreement to stop fighting; the Korean armistice of 1953 ended the shooting without a peace treaty or a political settlement

Lesson 3 : The Red Scare at Home

Second Red Scare: the fear from the late 1940s through the 1950s that Communists had infiltrated American government and institutions, and the investigations, loyalty tests, and blacklists the fear produced
House Un-American Activities Committee: the House of Representatives committee that investigated alleged Communist influence in American life, most famously in its hearings on the film industry in 1947
loyalty oath: a sworn statement of loyalty to the government and denial of Communist membership, required of federal employees and many other workers during the Second Red Scare
Hollywood Ten: the ten screenwriters and directors who refused to answer the House Un-American Activities Committee's questions in 1947, were cited for contempt of Congress, and went to prison
blacklist: a list of people barred from employment in an industry because of suspected Communist ties or refusal to cooperate with investigators, circulated in film and broadcasting during the Second Red Scare
McCarthyism: the practice of making public accusations of Communist disloyalty without firm evidence, named for Senator Joseph McCarthy, who made his charges the center of a national campaign from 1950 to 1954
Army-McCarthy hearings: the televised Senate hearings of 1954 in which the Army and Senator McCarthy accused each other of improper conduct, watched by millions, after which the Senate censured McCarthy

Lesson 4 : Brinkmanship and the Nuclear Shadow

arms race: the competition between the United States and the Soviet Union to build more and more powerful weapons, each side answering the other's advances
hydrogen bomb: a thermonuclear weapon far more powerful than the atomic bombs of 1945, first tested by the United States in 1952 and by the Soviet Union in 1953
massive retaliation: the Eisenhower administration's declared policy of answering Communist aggression with the threat of a full nuclear strike, rather than matching force at the point of attack
brinkmanship: the practice of pushing a dangerous confrontation to the edge of war in order to force the other side to back down, described by Secretary of State John Foster Dulles
Warsaw Pact: the military alliance formed in 1955 joining the Soviet Union and the Communist states of Eastern Europe, created in answer to NATO and West Germany's place in it
covert action: a secret government operation meant to influence events abroad while hiding the sponsor's hand, carried out in the Cold War chiefly by the Central Intelligence Agency
Sputnik: the first artificial satellite, launched by the Soviet Union in October 1957, whose success shocked the United States and opened the space race

Lesson 5 : Cuba: Revolution and the Missile Crisis

Cuban Revolution: the revolution that brought Fidel Castro to power in Cuba in 1959, overthrowing the government of Fulgencio Batista and turning toward alliance with the Soviet Union
Bay of Pigs invasion: the failed invasion of Cuba in April 1961 by Cuban exiles trained and supplied by the United States, defeated on the beach within three days
deterrence: the policy of preventing an enemy attack by holding a threat of retaliation so destructive that the attack would cost more than it could gain
Cuban Missile Crisis: the confrontation of October 1962 in which the United States discovered Soviet nuclear missiles being installed in Cuba and forced their withdrawal short of war
naval quarantine: President Kennedy's name for the naval blockade of Cuba in 1962, chosen because a blockade is legally an act of war and quarantine was not
hotline: the direct communications link between Washington and Moscow established in 1963 so the two leaders could reach each other at once in a crisis
Limited Test Ban Treaty: the treaty of 1963 in which the United States, the Soviet Union, and Britain banned nuclear weapons tests in the atmosphere, in space, and under water

Lesson 6 : Vietnam: The War Containment Built

domino theory: the belief, stated publicly by President Eisenhower in 1954, that if one country fell to communism its neighbors would fall one after another like a row of dominoes
Viet Cong: the Communist guerrilla forces and political organization in South Vietnam, supplied and directed from North Vietnam, that fought the South Vietnamese government and its American allies
Gulf of Tonkin Resolution: the resolution Congress passed in August 1964 giving the president authority to take all necessary measures to repel attacks on American forces and prevent further aggression in Southeast Asia
escalation: the steady enlargement of a military commitment step by step, as the American ground force in Vietnam grew from advisers to more than half a million troops
Tet Offensive: the coordinated attacks by North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces across South Vietnam in January 1968, a military defeat for the attackers that shook American confidence in official claims of progress
Vietnamization: President Nixon's policy of withdrawing American troops while building up South Vietnam's forces to carry the fighting themselves
draft lottery: the system begun in 1969 that chose the order in which young men were called for the draft by a random drawing of birth dates

Lesson 7 : Détente, Human Rights, and the Evil Empire

détente: the easing of tension between the United States and the Soviet Union pursued in the 1970s, built on arms agreements, trade, and regular summit meetings
arms control: negotiated limits on the weapons the superpowers could build or deploy, agreed in treaties rather than won in competition
SALT: the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks between the United States and the Soviet Union, and the agreements they produced in 1972 and 1979 limiting nuclear delivery vehicles
Helsinki Accords: the 1975 agreement in which Western nations recognized Europe's postwar borders and the Soviet Union and its allies pledged respect for human rights, a pledge dissidents used against their own governments
Reagan Doctrine: the policy of the 1980s under which the United States aided anti-Communist insurgencies fighting Soviet-backed governments, in Afghanistan, Nicaragua, and elsewhere
Strategic Defense Initiative: President Reagan's 1983 proposal to build a defense in space against nuclear missiles, called Star Wars by its critics, which promised protection deterrence could not
Evil Empire: President Reagan's name for the Soviet Union in a speech of March 1983, declaring it the focus of evil in the modern world and rejecting the idea that the two systems were morally equal

Lesson 8 : The Wall Falls: The Cold War Ends

glasnost: Mikhail Gorbachev's policy of openness in the Soviet Union from 1985, allowing public discussion, criticism of officials, and a freer press
perestroika: Gorbachev's program of restructuring the Soviet economy and political system, meant to make a failing system work and ending by loosening the party's control of both
Solidarity: the Polish labor movement founded in 1980 at the Gdansk shipyard, the first independent union in the Communist bloc, which survived martial law to lead Poland's turn from Communist rule in 1989
INF Treaty: the treaty of 1987 in which the United States and the Soviet Union agreed to destroy all their land-based missiles with ranges between about 300 and 3,400 miles, the first agreement to eliminate a class of nuclear weapons
Berlin Wall: the wall the East German government built in 1961 to stop its citizens fleeing to the West, which became the central symbol of the divided Europe, and which crowds breached on November 9, 1989
German reunification: the joining of East and West Germany into one state in October 1990, ending the division imposed after the Second World War
dissolution of the Soviet Union: the ending of the Soviet Union in December 1991, when its republics declared independence and the state that had stood since 1922 ceased to exist

Civil Rights

Lesson 1 : Foundations: The Movement Before the Movement

Jim Crow: the system of state and local laws and customs that enforced racial segregation in the South
segregation: the separation of people by race in public facilities, schools, and transportation
disfranchisement: the denial of the right to vote through law or practice
NAACP: the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, founded in 1909, which fought segregation through the courts, through lobbying, and through its magazine The Crisis
Double V campaign: the campaign led by Black newspapers from 1942 for victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home
To Secure These Rights: the 1947 report of President Truman's Committee on Civil Rights, which called for federal laws against lynching and disfranchisement and for the end of segregation in American life
Executive Order 9981: President Truman's order of July 26, 1948 directing equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed forces, the order that desegregated the United States military

Lesson 2 : Brown and the Massive Resistance

Plessy v. Ferguson: the 1896 Supreme Court decision that upheld segregation under the doctrine of separate but equal [carried from the Gilded Age unit]
separate but equal: the doctrine set by the Supreme Court in Plessy v. Ferguson in 1896, holding that segregation was constitutional as long as the facilities provided for each race were equal
Brown v. Board of Education: the Supreme Court decision of May 17, 1954 that declared segregation in public schools unconstitutional, ruling that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal
Brown II: the Supreme Court's 1955 decision ordering school desegregation to proceed with all deliberate speed, a phrase southern officials used to justify years of delay
massive resistance: the campaign by southern state governments after Brown to prevent school desegregation, using new laws, defiance of federal courts, and in some places the closing of public schools
Southern Manifesto: the declaration of 1956 signed by 101 southern members of Congress denouncing Brown v. Board of Education as an abuse of judicial power and pledging to resist it
Cooper v. Aaron: the Supreme Court decision of 1958, arising from Little Rock, holding that state officials are bound by the Court's interpretation of the Constitution and cannot nullify its rulings

Lesson 3 : Montgomery and Little Rock

boycott: a refusal to buy goods or use services as a form of protest, meant to pressure an institution by withdrawing the money and cooperation it depends on
Montgomery bus boycott: the protest of 1955 and 1956 in which Black residents of Montgomery, Alabama refused to ride the city's buses for 381 days, ending when the Supreme Court ordered the buses desegregated
nonviolent direct action: protest that confronts an unjust law or practice openly and without violence, accepting arrest and punishment in order to expose the injustice and force negotiation
Southern Christian Leadership Conference: the organization founded in 1957 by Martin Luther King Jr. and other Black ministers to coordinate nonviolent protest campaigns across the South
Emmett Till: the fourteen-year-old Chicago boy murdered in Mississippi in August 1955 after being accused of offending a white woman, whose open-casket funeral and published photographs showed the nation what southern racial violence looked like
Little Rock Nine: the nine Black students who desegregated Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas in 1957 under the protection of federal troops after the state's governor tried to bar their entry
Executive Order 10730: President Eisenhower's order of September 24, 1957 federalizing the Arkansas National Guard and directing federal troops to enforce school desegregation at Little Rock

Lesson 4 : Sit-Ins and Freedom Rides

sit-in: a protest in which demonstrators seat themselves at a segregated facility and refuse to leave until they are served on equal terms or arrested
Greensboro sit-in: the protest of February 1, 1960 in which four Black college students sat at a segregated Woolworth lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina and refused to leave, sparking sit-ins in cities across the South
Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee: the organization founded by student activists in April 1960, known as SNCC, which organized sit-ins, Freedom Rides, and voter registration drives in the deepest parts of the South
Congress of Racial Equality: the civil rights organization founded in 1942, known as CORE, which pioneered nonviolent direct action and organized the Freedom Rides of 1961
Freedom Rides: the bus journeys of 1961 in which interracial groups of activists rode through the South to test court orders desegregating interstate bus travel and bus terminals
Albany Movement: the campaign of 1961 and 1962 in Albany, Georgia, where a police chief's polite mass arrests denied the movement a televised crisis and taught it the limits of protest that moved no federal power
de jure segregation: segregation imposed and enforced by law, as under the southern statutes the courts struck down one by one

Lesson 5 : Birmingham and the March on Washington

Birmingham campaign: the protest campaign of 1963 in Birmingham, Alabama, led by the Southern Christian Leadership Conference and the local movement, whose televised repression pushed President Kennedy to propose a national civil rights bill
Letter from Birmingham Jail: Martin Luther King Jr.'s letter of April 1963, written from a Birmingham jail cell, defending nonviolent direct action against white clergymen who had called his protests unwise and untimely
Children's Crusade: the marches of May 1963 in Birmingham in which thousands of Black schoolchildren left their classes to demonstrate and were met with fire hoses, police dogs, and jail, producing the images that turned national opinion
Kennedy's civil rights address: President Kennedy's televised address of June 11, 1963, after the Birmingham crisis, calling civil rights a moral issue and announcing that he would send Congress a comprehensive civil rights bill
March on Washington: the demonstration of August 28, 1963 that brought about 250,000 people to the Lincoln Memorial to demand jobs and freedom and to press Congress to pass the civil rights bill
I Have a Dream: Martin Luther King Jr.'s address closing the March on Washington on August 28, 1963, casting the movement's demands as a claim on the promises of the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution
16th Street Baptist Church bombing: the bombing of a Birmingham church by Ku Klux Klan members on September 15, 1963, which killed four Black girls, Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley, Carole Robertson, and Carol Denise McNair, as they prepared for Sunday services

Lesson 6 : The Laws Change: 1964 and 1965

Civil Rights Act of 1964: the federal law that banned discrimination in public accommodations and employment and allowed the government to cut off funds to segregated programs, the movement's first great legislative victory
filibuster: the Senate practice of extending debate to block a bill from coming to a vote, broken on the civil rights bill of 1964 when the Senate voted for cloture after more than two months of debate
Title VII: the section of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 that banned discrimination in employment on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, or national origin
Freedom Summer: the campaign of 1964 that brought about a thousand mostly northern student volunteers to Mississippi to register Black voters and run Freedom Schools
Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party: the integrated political party organized in Mississippi in 1964 that challenged the seating of the state's all-white regular delegation at the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City
Bloody Sunday: the attack of March 7, 1965 by Alabama state troopers and a sheriff's posse on voting rights marchers at the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, televised to the nation that evening
Voting Rights Act of 1965: the federal law that suspended literacy tests, sent federal examiners to register voters in covered states, and required those states to clear any change in voting rules with the federal government first

Lesson 7 : The Movement Turns North and Inward

de facto segregation: segregation in fact rather than in law, produced by housing patterns, economics, and custom, the form segregation took in the cities of the North
Chicago Freedom Movement: Martin Luther King Jr.'s campaign of 1966 against housing and school segregation in Chicago, met by violent mobs on its open-housing marches and ended by an agreement the city did not enforce
Watts uprising: the six days of violence in the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles in August 1965, beginning five days after the Voting Rights Act was signed, in which thirty four people died
Black Power: the call raised by Stokely Carmichael in Mississippi in June 1966 for Black Americans to build their own political and economic power and to define freedom for themselves rather than rely on white goodwill
Black Panther Party: the organization founded in Oakland, California in 1966 by Huey Newton and Bobby Seale, which patrolled the police, carried arms in self-defense, and ran free breakfast and health programs in Black neighborhoods
Poor People's Campaign: Martin Luther King Jr.'s planned campaign of 1968 to bring an interracial army of the poor to Washington to demand federal action on poverty, carried on after his assassination as Resurrection City
Memphis sanitation strike: the strike of 1968 by Black sanitation workers in Memphis, Tennessee, whose signs read I Am a Man, and which brought Martin Luther King Jr. to the city where he was assassinated

Lesson 8 : Victory, Backlash, and the Unfinished Agenda

Kerner Commission: the federal commission appointed by President Johnson whose 1968 report concluded that the nation was moving toward two societies, one black, one white, separate and unequal, and charged white racism with building the conditions of the urban uprisings
Fair Housing Act: the Civil Rights Act of 1968, passed in the week after Martin Luther King Jr.'s assassination, banning discrimination in the sale and rental of most housing
white backlash: the political reaction among white voters against the pace and reach of civil rights change, visible in the Wallace campaigns of the 1960s and the law and order politics that followed
southern strategy: the Republican electoral strategy of the Nixon years that sought white southern voters by opposing federal civil rights enforcement, busing, and the pace of change the movement had set
busing: the court-ordered transportation of students across neighborhood lines to desegregate schools, upheld by the Supreme Court in Swann in 1971 and resisted in cities across the country
affirmative action: government policies that take race into account in hiring, contracting, and college admissions in order to remedy the effects of past discrimination, ordered for federal contractors in the revised Philadelphia Plan of 1969
Bakke decision: the Supreme Court decision of 1978 in Regents of the University of California v. Bakke, which struck down a set-aside of seats by race in admissions and allowed race to be used as one factor among others

Modern America

Lesson 1 : The Conservative Turn: Stagflation and the Tax Revolt

stagflation: the combination of stagnant economic growth, high unemployment, and high inflation that afflicted the American economy in the 1970s, defying the usual rule that inflation and unemployment moved in opposite directions
energy crisis: the shortages and sharp price rises in oil and gasoline that struck the United States in 1973 and again in 1979, when oil-producing states cut exports and revolution in Iran halted production
tax revolt: the movement of the late 1970s in which voters demanded lower taxes and limits on government spending, beginning with California's Proposition 13 in 1978 and spreading across the country
Proposition 13: the California ballot measure of 1978 that cut property taxes sharply and capped their future growth, the opening victory of the national tax revolt
New Right: the coalition of conservative activists, organizations, and voters that rose in the 1970s around lower taxes, a stronger military, and resistance to the social changes of the 1960s
Moral Majority: the political organization founded by the Reverend Jerry Falwell in 1979 to mobilize evangelical Christian voters behind conservative candidates and causes
southern strategy: the Republican electoral strategy of the Nixon years that sought white southern voters by opposing federal civil rights enforcement, busing, and the pace of change the movement had set

Lesson 2 : The Reagan Revolution

supply-side economics: the economic theory behind the Reagan program, holding that cutting tax rates and reducing regulation would free investment and production and so grow the whole economy
deregulation: the removal or loosening of government rules on an industry, pursued from the late 1970s onward in airlines, trucking, banking, and communications
Reagan Democrat: a traditionally Democratic voter, often a white working class or union voter, who voted for Ronald Reagan in 1980 and 1984 while often remaining a Democrat
PATCO strike: the strike of August 1981 in which President Reagan fired more than eleven thousand federal air traffic controllers who had struck illegally, a blow unions took as a signal of the era's terms
federal deficit: the amount by which a year's federal spending exceeds the year's revenue, added each year to the national debt
Iran-Contra affair: the scandal of 1985 to 1987 in which officials of the Reagan administration secretly sold arms to Iran and diverted the proceeds to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua, against stated policy and a congressional ban
War on Drugs: the campaign of intensified drug enforcement and mandatory sentences pursued by the federal government from the 1970s onward and greatly expanded in the 1980s, which raised prison populations across the country

Lesson 3 : After the Cold War

peace dividend: the money and attention Americans expected to redirect from military spending to needs at home once the Cold War ended in 1991, debated through the 1990s
Persian Gulf War: the war of January and February 1991 in which an American-led coalition, acting with United Nations authorization, drove Iraq's army out of Kuwait in six weeks
globalization: the deepening connection of the world's economies through trade, investment, and production spread across borders, which accelerated after the Cold War
North American Free Trade Agreement: the agreement among the United States, Canada, and Mexico that took effect in 1994 and removed most tariffs and trade barriers among the three countries
outsourcing: the practice of moving work, especially factory production and later office and service jobs, to lower-cost workers in other countries
Rust Belt: the name given to the industrial region of the Northeast and Midwest where factory closings from the 1970s onward emptied mills and cost whole cities much of their population
service economy: an economy in which most jobs produce services, such as health care, retail, finance, and food, rather than goods, the form the American economy took as factory employment shrank

Lesson 4 : The Digital Transformation

personal computer: a computer built for one person's desk and home, sold in large numbers from the late 1970s onward, which carried computing out of institutions and into daily life
Internet: the network of computer networks that grew out of government and university systems into a public means of communication, commerce, and publication in the 1990s
e-commerce: the buying and selling of goods and services over the Internet, which grew from almost nothing in the middle 1990s into a central part of American retail
dot-com bubble: the speculative boom in Internet company stocks in the late 1990s, ending in a crash in 2000 and 2001 that erased trillions of dollars in paper wealth
cable news: television news carried by cable channels around the clock, beginning with CNN in 1980, which replaced the nightly broadcast as many Americans' main source of news
social media: the Internet platforms, growing from the middle 2000s, on which users publish their own words, photographs, and video and build networks of followers and friends
digital divide: the gap between Americans with ready access to computers and the Internet and those without, which followed lines of income, education, age, and place

Lesson 5 : A Changing America

Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965: the law that ended the national origins quota system in place since the 1920s and opened American immigration chiefly to family reunification and needed skills, transforming the sources of immigration
Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986: the law that granted legal status to about three million unauthorized immigrants already in the country and made it illegal for employers knowingly to hire unauthorized workers
amnesty: in immigration law, a grant of legal status to people who entered or remained in the country without authorization, as the 1986 act gave to those who had arrived before 1982
Sun Belt: the fast-growing region of the South and Southwest, whose population, jobs, and political weight rose from the 1970s onward on air conditioning, defense and technology work, and lower costs
deindustrialization: the long decline of manufacturing employment in the United States from the 1970s onward, as factories closed, automated, or moved abroad
gentrification: the movement of wealthier residents and new investment into poorer city neighborhoods, raising prices and rents and often pushing out the people who lived there
white flight: the movement of white families from cities to suburbs in the decades after the Second World War, accelerated by school desegregation orders and changing neighborhoods, which shrank city tax bases

Lesson 6 : September 11 and the War on Terror

September 11 attacks: the terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001, in which hijackers of the al Qaeda network flew passenger planes into the World Trade Center and the Pentagon and crashed a fourth plane in Pennsylvania, killing 2,977 people
al Qaeda: the terrorist network led by Osama bin Laden that carried out the September 11 attacks, built from veterans of the war against the Soviet Union in Afghanistan
War on Terror: the worldwide campaign of military action, intelligence work, and security measures the United States declared after September 11, 2001, against al Qaeda and the groups and states said to shelter it
Authorization for Use of Military Force: the law Congress passed on September 18, 2001 authorizing the president to use all necessary and appropriate force against those responsible for the September 11 attacks and those who harbored them
USA PATRIOT Act: the law of October 2001 that widened the government's powers of surveillance, search, and detention in terrorism investigations, passed by overwhelming margins in the weeks after September 11
Department of Homeland Security: the cabinet department created in 2002 that merged twenty two federal agencies and offices charged with protecting the country against terrorist attack and disaster
weapons of mass destruction: chemical, biological, and nuclear weapons; the claim that Iraq possessed stockpiles of them was the stated ground for the American invasion of Iraq in 2003, and no such stockpiles were found

Lesson 7 : The Great Recession and Its Long Aftermath

subprime mortgage: a home loan made to a borrower with weak credit at a higher interest rate, issued in large numbers during the housing boom of the 2000s and central to the financial crisis when house prices fell
Great Recession: the economic collapse of 2007 to 2009, the deepest since the Great Depression, in which the financial system nearly failed, unemployment reached ten percent, and millions of families lost their homes
Troubled Asset Relief Program: the program of October 2008, known as TARP, in which the federal government was authorized to spend up to seven hundred billion dollars to steady the banking system by buying troubled assets and investing in banks
stimulus: government spending and tax cuts meant to restart a stalled economy by putting money in consumers' hands, as in the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009, at about eight hundred billion dollars
Affordable Care Act: the health care law of 2010 that required most Americans to carry health insurance, barred insurers from denying coverage for prior illness, and expanded Medicaid, the largest change to American health policy since the 1960s
Tea Party movement: the conservative protest movement that rose in 2009 against the bank bailouts, the stimulus, and the health care law, demanding smaller government and lower taxes, and that reshaped Republican politics
Occupy Wall Street: the protest movement that began in New York in September 2011 and spread to cities across the country, camping in financial districts to protest economic inequality in the name of the ninety nine percent against the one percent

Lesson 8 : A Polarized Nation

political polarization: the sorting of Americans into two hostile political camps with fewer voters in the middle, visible from the 1990s onward in voting records, media habits, and measures of party identity
Bush v. Gore: the Supreme Court decision of December 2000 that stopped the recount of Florida's presidential ballots and settled the election of 2000 for George W. Bush, decided by five votes to four
Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission: the Supreme Court decision of 2010 holding that the government may not limit independent political spending by corporations and unions, which opened the way to super PACs and large outside spending in elections
District of Columbia v. Heller: the Supreme Court decision of 2008 holding that the Second Amendment protects an individual's right to keep a handgun in the home for self-defense
Obergefell v. Hodges: the Supreme Court decision of 2015 holding that the Fourteenth Amendment requires every state to license marriages between two people of the same sex and to recognize such marriages licensed in other states
affirmative action: government policies that take race into account in hiring, contracting, and college admissions in order to remedy the effects of past discrimination, ordered for federal contractors in the revised Philadelphia Plan of 1969
COVID-19 pandemic: the worldwide spread of the coronavirus disease first identified in 2019, which reached the United States in 2020, killed more than a million Americans, and closed schools, businesses, and public life for months

Review Questions

Band 4: Industry and Reform

1. The Knights of Labor organized broadly, all producers in one movement, skilled and unskilled, Black and white, women and men, and aimed past wages at the wage system itself, toward a cooperative commonwealth. The AFL under Gompers organized narrowly and durably, craft by craft, for wages, hours, and conditions inside capitalism, pure and simple unionism. The legal weapon that defeated strikes most reliably was the injunction: a federal court order against a strike or boycott, issued on equity grounds, enforced by contempt proceedings without a jury and by troops when needed, with Pullman in 1894 the exhibit and the Sherman Act, written against trusts, applied to the union as a combination in restraint of trade.

2. Democracy repaired: the Australian secret ballot adopted state by state, the direct primary, initiative, referendum, and recall, and the Seventeenth Amendment's direct election of senators. The corporation regulated: the Interstate Commerce Commission given rate power by the Hepburn Act, the Sherman Act revived in Northern Securities and extended by the Clayton Act, the Federal Reserve System of 1913, and the Pure Food and Drug and Meat Inspection Acts of 1906. The electorate widened: the state suffrage campaigns and then the Nineteenth Amendment (1920), and, in the same channel, the era's school and franchise reforms that made the electorate a mass public the parties had to address in print.

3. The Gilded Age built the color line as a legal system: southern states disfranchised Black voters by constitution and statute through the 1890s, segregation was codified across public life, Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) supplied the fiction of separate but equal, and lynching enforced what the statutes left unsaid, thousands murdered with near-total impunity. Progressivism, with honorable exceptions, did its work on the line's white side and often deepened the system: the Wilson administration segregated the federal civil service in 1913, southern progressives made white-only primaries and at-large schemes into reforms, and the national movement priced the South's Black citizens out of its coalition.

Band 5: Power Abroad, Turmoil at Home

1. Markets and materials: an industrial economy outproducing its home demand needed outlets and inputs abroad, and the depression of the 1890s made the need feel urgent. Sea power: Mahan's argument that national greatness ran on a great navy, coaling stations, and an isthmian canal, which made distant bases a strategic necessity rather than a luxury. Mission: the claim, racial in its terms, that Anglo-Saxon peoples were fitted and obliged to carry their civilization and government to peoples deemed unready for it. The anti-imperialists' answer, in their own platform's logic, was one sentence long: a republic founded on the consent of the governed cannot rule subjects abroad without becoming the empire it was born against.

2. Structure first: from 1914 the United States traded and lent overwhelmingly with the Allies, built a preparedness program in 1915 and 1916, and organized a National Defense establishment, so neutrality's economics and machinery leaned one way while its law leaned straight. Incidents second: the Lusitania (1915) and the crises after it were managed by diplomacy into pledges, the Arabic and Sussex pledges narrowing Germany's submarine practice without war. The closers came in early 1917: Germany's announcement of unrestricted submarine warfare from February 1, a decision to accept American belligerency as the price of starving Britain, and the Zimmermann telegram's publication in March, proposing Mexico an alliance against the United States, which made neutrality untenable in public as the sinkings were making it untenable in policy.

3. Agriculture had been depressed all decade: overproduction against shrinking export markets, collapsing prices, and farm debt carried at wartime values. Incomes were concentrated while wages lagged productivity, so mass purchasing power could not buy what mass production could make, and installment credit papered the gap. The market itself was leveraged, stocks bought on margin against borrowed money, with the Florida land boom's collapse in 1926 as the rehearsal nobody heeded. The crash revealed rather than caused these because the demand failure was already built: when credit and confidence vanished in October 1929, what stood exposed was an economy whose production had outrun its customers' incomes for years.

Band 6: Depression, Recovery, and Total War

1. Falling prices made every debt heavier in real terms, so farmers and firms defaulted as their incomes shrank; the defaults and the runs together killed the banks, some nine thousand of them, and with the banks went the savings and the credit of whole regions; firms that were sound on any normal accounting failed for want of loans, cut production and payrolls, and a quarter of the labor force stood idle, collapsing demand and pulling prices down again. Hoover stopped at indirect action: the Reconstruction Finance Corporation lending to banks and railroads, public works, pressure on employers and charities, and no direct federal relief and no abandonment of the gold standard and the balanced budget. Roosevelt began by crossing the line in his first week, the bank holiday and federal inspection of every reopened bank, and within the Hundred Days came federal relief itself.

2. Scale: the war state spent at multiples of the New Deal's peak and sustained the spending until the economy ran past full capacity, so the demand the New Deal had rationed by politics was supplied without limit. The draft: conscription pulled some eleven million workers directly out of the labor market into uniform, so war spending did not bid against a surplus, it met empty benches, and unemployment fell under two percent.

3. Victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home, the Pittsburgh Courier's pairing. Abroad, advanced by the cause's own stated terms, the Four Freedoms, and by Black service in every theater; betrayed abroad by the segregated army that did the fighting and the blood policy that separated plasma by race. At home, advanced by Executive Order 8802 and the FEPC, won by Randolph's threatened march, and by the defense jobs themselves; betrayed at home by internment, 120,000 people imprisoned on ancestry in the same years, and by the zoot suit attacks on the communities of Mexican American servicemen.

Band 7: Cold War Abroad, Rights at Home

1. Containment's premise, Kennan's, was that Soviet power, expansionist from insecurity and ideology alike, bore the seeds of its own decay and would crack or mellow under firm, patient counter-pressure at shifting points, applied politically and economically before militarily. By 1950 it was housed in three instruments: money, the Marshall Plan's recovery billions that rebuilt Western Europe against despair's politics; alliance, NATO (1949), the first peacetime alliance in American history, garrisoning the doctrine; and military posture, NSC-68's call for a permanent global buildup, which the Korean War's outbreak let Washington fund at triple the defense budget.

2. It manufactured crisis: disciplined, trained protesters violated an unjust local law openly, accepted the assault and the arrest without answering either, and did it where the local opponent, a Bull Connor, could be relied on to supply the brutality on schedule. Television carried it: the national networks and the wire photographs moved the crisis out of the town and into the country's living rooms and, decisively, into the federal government's in tray, converting a city ordinance into a national emergency within a news cycle. It forced the federal executive's hand, repeatedly and by design: Eisenhower's at Little Rock, Kennedy's at Birmingham, Johnson's at Selma, each intervention the movement's strategy collecting its debt.

3. The Cold War ended from inside the Soviet system: a reformer, Gorbachev, opened it to save it, glasnost letting the grievances speak and perestroika loosening the command economy without replacing it, until Eastern Europe's peoples walked out in 1989 and the Union dissolved itself in December 1991, the war containment's planners had armed against for forty-five years never fought. The movement's unfinished agenda, when its legal war was won in 1964, 1965, and 1968, was the structure underneath the statutes: segregated housing and the wealth homeownership builds, joblessness and an urban economy abandoning its workers, schools sorted by the address the housing market assigned, the agenda King had carried to Memphis and the Kerner Commission named as two societies forming.

Band 8: Modern America and the Long Arc

1. Taxes: the 1981 act cut the top marginal rate from 70 percent to 50, and the 1986 reform brought it to 28, making lower marginal rates and a broader, flatter code the default settlement both parties governed inside for a generation. Regulation: the era's posture treated rules as drag, continuing deregulation across administrations and loosening finance's in particular, a line that runs to the Glass-Steagall repeal of 1999. Labor: the PATCO firing of 1981 announced from the presidency itself that a strike against the settlement would be broken from the top; union density declined through the era, and the strike as labor's central weapon fell out of American practice.

2. Any two threads, run in three stops, earn the points; the model here runs America in the world and federal power. America in the world: the Farewell's restraint (1796), trade with all and alliance with none, gives way to 1898 and 1917, empire and intervention debated and taken, and then to 1945's settlement, a permanent architecture of alliances, bases, and guaranteed trade, inside which the AUMF's open-ended war (2001) is the present tense. Federal power: Philadelphia's frame, strong enough to act and limited enough to trust, becomes the New Deal settlement, the federal government as the economy's referee and the citizen's insurer, and arrives at the modern doubling, a regulatory and security state of New Deal scale beside a tax politics and a Court committed to suspicion of it.

3. The attacks of September 11, 2001 broke trust in the government's competence to protect: the security establishment had the hijackers' fragments and missed the plot, as the 9/11 Commission's record established, and the Iraq case that followed, stockpiles that were not there, broke trust in its candor on the same account. The crisis of 2008 broke trust in candor and fairness together: a financial system the government had deregulated and then rescued, at public expense and with no account rendered by those who ran it, while the households whose mortgages were the crisis's fuel were left to the foreclosure process.
