Band 3 Teacher Guide: Slavery, War, and the Unfinished Settlement

When to teach it: On the third day of the Course Review unit, in the review days before the course final or state test. The unit runs its eight bands in course order; this band lands mid-review, after the founding and early republic bands and before industry and reform. If your review is compressed to the core minimum, this is one of the six bands that stay.

What it pairs with: The two units it reviews, Sectional Crisis and Civil War and Reconstruction, whose own decks and documents are the reteach stock when the practice set exposes a gap. It also pairs with the unit's combined Rycal deck: students who set their test date when review began have been cycling this band's 112 terms in short sessions, and the sprint below is the paper check on that work.

Prior knowledge students need: Both units taught in full. The packet assumes the Missouri Compromise and the gag rule era from the earlier units by name only, as the Sectional Crisis unit itself does. No new content is introduced anywhere in the day; every fact in the packet, the sprint, and the practice set was taught in the two units.

Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies, grades 9 to 10: RH.9-10.1 (cite specific textual evidence), RH.9-10.2 (determine central ideas), RH.9-10.3 (analyze a series of events in sequence), and WHST.9-10.1 (write arguments supported by evidence). C3 Framework: D2.His.1.9-12 (how historical context shapes interpretation), D2.His.13.9-12 (analyze causes and effects), D2.His.16.9-12 (how people and institutions worked for social change), and D2.Civ.3.9-12 (the ideals and principles in the founding documents as amended).

What students will review: The failure of compromise from 1850 to 1861, the war's transformation from restoration to emancipation, the constitutional settlement of 1865 to 1870, and the settlement's collapse by 1877. The day's skill is causation: separating the causes people chose from the conditions nobody chose.

Timing and materials: One class day. Packet reading and timeline, about 15 minutes; term sprint, about 15 minutes; practice set, about 20 minutes; skills task organizer in class with the verdict essay finished as homework, or both in class on a block schedule. Print the packet, sprint, practice set, and skills task for every student. Nothing else is required.

How the Day Runs

Students read the packet with one instruction: mark any timeline row they cannot explain aloud. The sprint follows closed-book, then students self-check against the packet (the definitions are the guided-notes wording verbatim, so the check is exact). The practice set runs closed-packet. Read the results by lesson, not by student: each item names its lesson, so a weak item is a reteach assignment, usually one reading or one deck, before the practice test.

Practice Set Key

Multiple choice: 1. B. 2. B. 3. C. 4. A. 5. D. 6. B. 7. C. 8. D. 9. B. 10. B. 11. D. 12. C.

13. (SC, Lesson 2) The higher law argument held that a moral law above the Constitution's compromises forbade returning a fugitive to slavery, whatever the statute required. Credit any of: the argument in the form the unit's speakers and writers made it, the personal liberty laws as the argument in statutory form, the rescues (Jerry, Burns) as the argument in action, or the antislavery writers' appeal to conscience. A miss usually means the student has the slogan without a bearer; reteach from Lesson 2's stations.

14. (SC, Lessons 6 and 7) Militarily, Antietam checked Lee's first invasion of the North and ended in a draw the Union could claim. Politically, that standing let Lincoln issue the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation five days later without its reading as an act of desperation. A miss on the second half usually means emancipation is being taught as a 1863 surprise; the bridge is Lesson 6's close and Lesson 7's open.

15. (SC, Lesson 8) Lincoln names American slavery, held by some, as the offense and the war as its woe, and frames the war's whole cost, measured against two hundred and fifty years of unrequited toil, as a judgment he calls true and righteous altogether. Accept close paraphrase of the address on both points. The point to credit is that he assigns the cause to slavery and the meaning to judgment, not to accident or to northern virtue.

16. (RC, Lessons 2 and 3) A statute can be repealed by a later Congress and its constitutional basis attacked, as the Black Codes' defenders attacked Congress's power to define citizenship at all. The Fourteenth Amendment put citizenship and equal protection into the Constitution itself, beyond repeal and beyond the reach of Dred Scott's citizenship holding. A miss usually collapses the two into one event; the sequence is the content.

17. (RC, Lesson 6) The Enforcement Acts made interference with voting, officeholding, and jury service a federal crime, and the Klan Act let the president suspend habeas corpus where terror amounted to rebellion. Using them required a working federal apparatus in the former Confederacy: marshals, prosecutors, federal courts, troops in support, and sustained political will in Washington. A miss usually names the laws without the apparatus; the apparatus is the lesson, since its withdrawal is how the story ends.

18. (RC, Lesson 8) Settled: the presidency resolved and the last federal troops withdrawn, with the southern states under Redeemer home rule; and, in law, abolition, citizenship, and the protected vote, which were never repealed. Left open (accept any one): the land question, equal protection in daily practice, the vote in fact as the Redeemer constitutions and practices narrowed it, or enforcement as a permanent federal duty. Credit answers that hold both halves at once; the unit's essential question is built to make students do exactly that.

Skills Task Guidance

The verdict essay rewards separation of choice from condition. Strong work names at least one decision that could have gone the other way (Douglas's repeal price in 1854, the restoration of the Sea Island lands in 1865, the Amnesty Act's timing, the choice not to sustain prosecutions after 1872) and at least one condition no single actor controlled (the territorial question's return with every acquisition, the northern electorate's exhaustion, the cotton economy's demand for bound labor). Weak work retells the narrative, or calls everything inevitable, or everything chosen. The counterpoint requirement is the discriminator: a student who argues 1877 was the more avoidable failure must answer the enforcement-cost argument, and a student who argues 1861 must answer the point that compromise had been losing ground for a decade before Douglas.

A fair score: claim clear in the first lines (2), three packet facts accurately used for each settlement (6), counterpoint answered, not summarized and dropped (2). Ten points, and it grades quickly because the organizers travel with the essay and show the evidence base before a word of prose is read.
