Band 3 Review Packet: Slavery, War, and the Unfinished Settlement

Directions: This packet compresses two full units, Sectional Crisis and Civil War and Reconstruction, into one review. Read the two arguments, study the timeline, and keep the packet beside you for the term sprint and the practice set. Both of those name the lesson to reopen when an answer will not come.

The Band in Brief

From 1850 to 1861 the United States tried to settle the slavery question by bargain, and every bargain made the next one harder. The Compromise of 1850 held for four years. The Kansas-Nebraska Act repealed an older settlement and set settlers fighting over a territory. The Supreme Court tried to settle the question by law and settled nothing. A new party rose on the single promise to stop slavery's spread, won the presidency in 1860 without a single southern electoral vote, and eleven states left the Union rather than accept the result. The war that followed began as a war for the Union and became, by 1863, a war that destroyed slavery as it restored the Union.

From 1865 to 1877 the country tried to settle what the war had decided. Three amendments rewrote the Constitution: slavery abolished, citizenship and equal protection guaranteed, the vote protected against racial denial. For a few years the reconstructed South governed as an interracial democracy, and freedpeople built schools, churches, and political organizations at speed. The settlement rested on federal enforcement, and enforcement is what failed. Terror, court decisions, economic panic, and northern exhaustion withdrew the protection piece by piece, and in 1877 the last troops left. The amendments stayed in the Constitution as text. The settlement they described was postponed for generations.

Sectional Crisis and Civil War asked: How did compromise over slavery finally fail, and what did the war it caused settle about slavery and the Union, and what did it leave open?

Reconstruction asked: What did Reconstruction settle about freedom, citizenship, and the federal Union, and what did it leave open?

The Argument of Sectional Crisis and Civil War

The last settlement, and the law that undid it in the North

The Compromise of 1850 was built to be swallowed whole: California free, the territories uncommitted, Texas paid, the District's slave trade ended, and a fugitive slave law with real teeth. The teeth are what the North felt. The Act of September 18, 1850 required marshals and ordinary citizens to join the hunt, denied the accused a jury trial and the right to testify, and paid the commissioner more for a return than a release. Northern states answered with personal liberty laws, crowds answered with rescues, and writers answered with the decade's most powerful political literature. Harriet Beecher Stowe built Uncle Tom's Cabin out of the fugitive question, Douglass and Truth and Jacobs spoke from experience no novelist could claim, and the antislavery argument reached full voice claiming a higher law than the Constitution's compromises.

Kansas, the broken parties, and the Court

Stephen A. Douglas reopened the settled map in 1854 to organize Kansas and Nebraska and build a railroad, offering popular sovereignty: the settlers would decide. The price was repeal of the Missouri Compromise's line, and the repeal killed the Whig Party, split the Democrats by section, and called the Republican Party into being on one plank, no extension of slavery. In Kansas the settlers' decision arrived as two governments, a sacked town, a senator beaten nearly to death on the Senate floor, and John Brown's killings at Pottawatomie. In 1857 the Supreme Court tried to remove the question from politics altogether. Dred Scott held that Scott remained enslaved, that Black Americans could not be citizens, and that Congress had no power to bar slavery from the territories, which made the Missouri line unconstitutional after the fact and the Republican plank unlawful in principle.

The break

The debates of 1858 made Lincoln a national figure arguing that a house divided against itself cannot stand, and forced Douglas at Freeport to keep his doctrine alive by hollowing out the Court's ruling. Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry in October 1859, and the northern grief at his hanging, convinced much of the South that the North endorsed servile war. The Democracy split in 1860, Lincoln won a clear electoral majority with no southern electoral votes at all, and South Carolina seceded in December, saying plainly in its declaration that slavery and the broken fugitive clause were the causes. Six more states followed, the Confederacy organized under Jefferson Davis, and last-minute settlements, Crittenden's among them, failed on the one point neither side would move: extension.

The war, remade and finished

The war opened at Fort Sumter in April 1861 with the Union's aim stated as restoration, nothing more. Winfield Scott's Anaconda Plan proposed the slow squeeze of blockade and river. The fighting instead ran through McClellan's caution, the stalemate in the East, and the suspension of habeas corpus, to Antietam in September 1862, costly enough to check Lee and useful enough to let Lincoln announce emancipation as a measure of war. From January 1863 the Union war carried a second aim. Enslaved people had already voted with their feet to the Union lines, and the Army now enlisted them: about 180,000 served, the 54th Massachusetts among them, and their service made a postwar return to slavery untenable. Gettysburg and Vicksburg in July 1863 turned the war; the Address at Gettysburg restated its purpose. Grant then waged total war, Sherman through Georgia and the Carolinas, until Appomattox in April 1865. Lincoln's Second Inaugural refused triumph and named slavery as the war's cause and its offense. The Thirteenth Amendment, passed by the House in January 1865, carried abolition into the Constitution, ratified that December.

The Argument of Reconstruction

Restoration, and the answer it provoked

Johnson's presidential Reconstruction restored the seceded states quickly, on pardons, repudiation of secession and of the Confederate debt, and ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment, and asked nothing about the rights of the freedpeople. The restored governments answered with the Black Codes, Mississippi's Vagrant Law the model, which bound freedpeople to annual contracts, punished quitting as vagrancy, and rebuilt by statute much of what emancipation had ended in fact. The Freedmen's Bureau stood against the codes where it could: rations, contracts reviewed, schools begun, and its agents a first federal presence in southern daily life. Freedpeople answered for themselves, in conventions that demanded the vote and equal law, in petitions like the Edisto Island freedmen's, and in letters like Jourdon Anderson's to the man who had owned him.

Freedom written into law, then into the Constitution

Congress refused to seat the restored delegations and took the settlement into its own hands. Trumbull's Civil Rights Act of 1866 declared all persons born in the United States citizens and equal in the rights it listed, and Congress passed it over Johnson's veto. Because a statute can be repealed and its constitutional footing doubted, Congress then proposed the Fourteenth Amendment: citizenship by birth, and no state to abridge the privileges or immunities of citizens, to deprive any person of life, liberty, or property without due process, or to deny equal protection of the laws. The ten unreconstructed states refused it, the violence at Memphis and New Orleans in 1866 showed the settlement's enemies at work, and the Reconstruction Acts of 1867 divided the South into five military districts and required new constitutions with Black male suffrage and ratification of the Fourteenth. The collision with Johnson over Stanton and the Tenure of Office Act ended in impeachment and acquittal by one vote in 1868, the year the Fourteenth was ratified.

The vote, and freedom on the ground

What followed was the era's genuine revolution. Union Leagues organized the new electorate, freedmen and their allies wrote constitutions, and Black men held office at every level: Revels and Bruce in the United States Senate, Pinchback briefly as Louisiana's governor, and Black officeholders besides at every level of state and local government. The Fifteenth Amendment in 1870 barred denial of the vote for race. On the ground, the settlement ran into its hardest question, land. Sherman's Special Field Orders No. 15 had settled freed families on coastal land, and Johnson's restoration gave it back. Without land, freedom was worked out in labor contracts, then in sharecropping under the crop lien, which fed a family through the year and frequently took the whole crop at settlement. What freedpeople built without land was still a great deal: schools they taxed themselves to fund, the independent churches of the AME tradition, and a savings bank of their own, until the Panic of 1873 brought the bank down with much else.

The counterrevolution, and the retreat

The Ku Klux Klan and its successor organizations made a politics of terror: night riding, whippings, and murder aimed at voters, officeholders, teachers, and the schools themselves. Abram Colby's testimony, beaten for refusing to sell his vote, is the era's record of its method. Grant answered with the Enforcement Acts and the Ku Klux Klan Act, federal marshals and prosecutors, and habeas corpus suspended in the worst South Carolina counties, and the first Klan was broken by 1872. The protection then ebbed. The Slaughter-House Cases in 1873 emptied the Fourteenth Amendment's privileges or immunities clause. The Panic of the same year and the long depression turned northern voters toward their own troubles, the Amnesty Act restored the political rights of most former Confederates, and the White League and rifle clubs took by open paramilitary force what the Klan had taken by night. The disputed election of 1876, Tilden against Hayes, was settled by the bargain of 1877: Hayes took the presidency, the last federal troops left South Carolina and Louisiana, and the Redeemers' home rule was complete. What the era had settled in law, citizenship, abolition, and the protected vote, remained on the page. What it left open, land, enforcement, and the daily substance of equal citizenship, was the country's unfinished business.

People of the Band

Henry Clay built the Compromise of 1850 and could not pass it whole. Stephen A. Douglas passed it in pieces, then staked his career on popular sovereignty and lost the South at Freeport. Harriet Beecher Stowe wrote the fugitive question into the decade's bestselling novel. John Brown killed at Pottawatomie and raided Harpers Ferry, and his hanging split the country's reading of itself. Abraham Lincoln rose on the debates, won in 1860, and remade the war's purpose by 1863. Jefferson Davis led the Confederacy from Montgomery to collapse. Ulysses S. Grant finished the war in the East and, as president, enforced the settlement until his party tired of it. Andrew Johnson restored the seceded states on his own terms and fought Congress for three years over whose settlement would stand. Thaddeus Stevens pressed Congress toward confiscation and equal citizenship, and got half of each. Hiram Rhodes Revels and Blanche K. Bruce sat in the United States Senate from Mississippi inside the decade. Jourdon Anderson answered the man who had owned him with a letter that priced his own stolen labor. Abram Colby testified to what the Klan did to a legislator who would not sell his vote.
