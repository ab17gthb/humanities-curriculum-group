Band 8 Review Packet: Modern America and the Long Arc

Directions: This packet closes the review. Its first half compresses the course's final unit, Modern America. Its second half runs the course's six threads from their first units to the present, because the practice test will ask about the whole course and not only its last forty years. Read the unit argument, study the timeline, then read the threads with the earlier bands' packets closed: the threads are the course stated as six continuing arguments.

The Band in Brief

Modern America opens in the wreckage of the postwar settlement: stagflation that the era's economics could not explain, an energy crisis that queued the nation at the gas pump, and a tax revolt that began in California and nationalized. The conservative turn that followed, the New Right's coalition of market economics, evangelical organization, and the southern strategy's harvest, remade the federal government's posture for a generation: taxes down, regulation loosened, unions broken at PATCO, deficits accepted as the price, and a drug war and an Iran-Contra scandal as the era's shadows. Then the Cold War ended and the peace dividend was spent on globalization: NAFTA and the integrated world economy, the Rust Belt's long emptying against the service economy's growth, and a digital transformation, personal computer to internet to social media, that rebuilt commerce, news, and eventually politics itself. The population remade alongside: the 1965 immigration act's long fuse burned down to a new demographic America, the Sun Belt took the electoral votes, and the cities sorted by gentrification and flight.

The century's emergencies then set the terms. September 11 built a security state on a three-day-old authorization that outlasted the administrations that wielded it, and the Iraq war's missing weapons of mass destruction cost the government a generation's trust. The Great Recession repeated the pattern in finance: subprime credit, a frozen system, a rescue that saved the banks first and a recovery that arrived unevenly enough to power populisms on both flanks, the Tea Party and Occupy, and a health-care settlement that survived its own repeal campaigns. The unit closes on polarization, the condition underneath all of it: two coalitions sorted by geography, education, and media ecosystem, a Supreme Court deciding the era's terms in Bush v. Gore, Citizens United, Heller, and Obergefell, and a pandemic that stress-tested every institution at once. What the remaking settled, and what it left open, is the unit's question, and after the threads, it is the course's.

Modern America asked: How did the United States remake itself after 1980, in its economy, its population, and its place in the world, and what did the remaking settle and what did it leave open?

The Argument of Modern America

The conservative turn and the Reagan settlement

Stagflation was an intellectual event before it was a political one: prices and unemployment rising together refuted the postwar toolkit in public, and the energy crisis gave the refutation a queue at the pump. The tax revolt supplied the mass politics, Proposition 13's two-thirds vote announcing that homeowners would cap the tax state themselves. The New Right assembled the coalition, market conservatives, the Moral Majority's evangelical organization, defense hawks, and the voters the southern strategy had been courting since 1968, and the Reagan settlement followed: supply-side tax cuts wagered on growth, deregulation across finance and transport, PATCO's lesson to labor, a military buildup, and deficits that tripled the national debt while the president denounced it. Iran-Contra, arms sold to Iran in defiance of stated policy and the proceeds diverted to the Contras against Congress's prohibition, was the settlement's constitutional shadow, and the War on Drugs its domestic one.

Globalization and the digital economy

The Cold War's end was cashed as integration. The peace dividend argument, spend the surplus on domestic renewal or bank it against the debt, was overtaken by an economy globalizing on its own: NAFTA's continental market, China's entry into the trading system, production chains that made the factory a network and the Rust Belt a region, with the service economy's jobs, lower-paid and less secure, as the replacement offer. The digital transformation compounded it: the personal computer put the office on the desk, the internet put the market in the home, the dot-com bubble repriced the promise without repealing it, and cable news, then social media, rebuilt the information environment the course's final lesson finds polarized. E-commerce and the digital divide are the same fact at two incomes.

A changing population

The era's demography was legislated a generation early. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, written to end the national-origins quotas with no expectation of transforming the flow, replaced Europe with Asia and Latin America as immigration's sources once family reunification compounded. The 1986 act was the era's attempt at a settlement, amnesty for the settled millions, sanctions for the employers of the future flow, and its enforcement half was the one that lapsed. The Sun Belt's rise, air conditioning, defense spending, right-to-work laws, and cheap land pulling people and electoral votes south and west, redrew the political map the parties competed on, while the older cities sorted themselves by deindustrialization's losses, white flight's earlier departures, and gentrification's returns, the same streets legible as abandonment or revival depending on the decade and the viewer.

The long emergencies and the polarized settlement

September 11 reorganized the state around security: a department amalgamated, a surveillance architecture authorized, and an Authorization for Use of Military Force, passed three days after the attacks, under which presidents of both parties waged war for twenty years. Iraq, invaded on intelligence that failed, was the era's trust-breaking event, as Vietnam had been for its generation. The Great Recession reprised the pattern in the economy: the subprime machine, the freeze, TARP's rescue of the institutions before the households, a stimulus sized to the politics rather than the hole, and a recovery whose gains pooled at the top. The responses defined the politics after: the Affordable Care Act's regulated-market settlement, the Tea Party's constitutional populism, Occupy's distributive one. Underneath, the sorting completed itself, by geography, education, and information source, and the Supreme Court became the era's legislature of last resort: an election decided, speech and money equated, gun rights individualized, marriage equalized, affirmative action narrowed toward its end. COVID-19 closed the unit as the stress test of everything at once, federalism, expertise, supply chains, and the two publics' separate facts.

The Long Arc: The Course in Six Threads

Federal power and the Constitution

The course's longest argument is over what the federal government is for. Philadelphia built a frame strong enough to act and limited enough to trust, and every era since has re-litigated the dosage: Hamilton's bank and Jefferson's doubts, Jackson's veto and the nullifiers, the Civil War amendments' enforcement powers, the New Deal settlement that made the federal government the economy's referee and the citizen's insurer, and the modern era's doubled answer, a regulatory and security state of New Deal scale beside a Court and a tax politics committed to its suspicion. Heller, Citizens United, and Obergefell are the current terms of the argument, not its end.

Expansion and its costs

Americans have expanded in every generation and argued about the bill in every one: Louisiana bought and the continent taken by treaty, war, and removal; the Philippines annexed and the conscience indicted at home; a world ordered after 1945 by American alliance and credit; globalization as expansion's economic form, which sent the jobs along the same roads the goods traveled; and Iraq as the older form's return. The thread's constant is that the costs are always real, always itemized late, and always distributed to people with the least say in the decision: Native nations, Filipino and Iraqi civilians, the Rust Belt's towns.

Slavery, race, and rights

The course's moral spine runs from the hereditary statutes of the 1660s through the Declaration's borrowed promise, the compromises that protected slavery and the war that ended it, the amendments that rewrote citizenship and the century that betrayed them in law's forms, the movement that collected on the amendments in 1964 and 1965, and the unfinished agenda the movement itself named: housing, wealth, schools, and the vote's administration. Modern America's chapters, affirmative action's narrowing, Obergefell's extension of the Fourteenth Amendment to a new class, a Black presidency and the arguments it did not settle, are this thread's present tense.

Industrial capitalism and labor

The corporation organized in the 1870s set the thread: combination answered by combination, the trust by the union, and the federal government choosing, era by era, which combination to fear. The Sherman Act slept and woke; the Wagner Act armed labor and the postwar settlement housed it; PATCO and deindustrialization disarmed it again; and the service economy's workforce, harder to organize and easier to schedule by algorithm, met 2008 with the weakest labor institutions since before the New Deal. The thread explains why the same country keeps rediscovering the question of who bears risk, the shareholder or the household.

America in the world

Washington's Farewell named the pole of restraint, and 1898 named the other, and the course is the pendulum's record: empire debated and taken, a world war joined late and a league refused, a second war that made the architecture, alliances, bases, an open trading system under American guarantee, containment's forty years, the unipolar moment's demonstrations and overreaches, and a War on Terror whose authorization had no terminus written into it. The thread's question is the Farewell's and the anti-imperialists', updated: what does the exercise of power abroad do to the republic at home, in its treasury, its liberties, and its habits of consent.

Reform movements

Reform is the course's recurring weather: born in revivals, organized by the aggrieved, aimed at the machine, the trust, the bottle, the ballot's exclusions. Its two great constructions, progressivism and the New Deal, rebuilt the state's capacities; its rights revolutions collected the Constitution's outstanding notes; and its modern form is double, the New Right and then the Tea Party reforming from the right against the state's growth, Occupy and its heirs from the left against the settlement's distribution. The thread's lesson is that reform movements are judged by their institutions: what they built that outlasted the march, a statute, an amendment, an agency, a court doctrine.

People of the Band

Ronald Reagan made the conservative turn a governing settlement and a sunny rhetoric, cut the taxes, broke the strike, and left the deficits and the debt as the era's fine print. Bill Clinton governed the globalization decade, signing NAFTA and presiding over the boom the bubble ended. George W. Bush led the country through September 11 into two wars, one on intelligence that failed, and out of a financial collapse. Barack Obama, the first Black president, governed the recession's aftermath, built the health-care settlement, and embodied, in his election and in the reaction to it, the era's argument about race's place. Bill Gates and Steve Jobs, the personal computer's rival evangelists, made the machine a household appliance and then a pocket one, and built the platforms the era's politics would run on. Ruth Bader Ginsburg and Antonin Scalia, from opposite poles of the era's Court, made constitutional interpretation itself a public argument the country followed by name.
