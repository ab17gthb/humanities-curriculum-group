Band 8 Teacher Guide: Modern America and the Long Arc

When to teach it: On the eighth and last band day of the Course Review unit (the fifth band of the Modern Course Review). The day differs from the other seven: it reviews one unit and then the whole course, and its practice set and skills task should be given the day's second half entire. The course study guide's flex day follows it, and the practice test closes the unit.

What it pairs with: The Modern America unit, and behind it the full course: the packet's six threads are written to be taught against the earlier bands' packets, which students should have in the same folder. The skills task's four documents were each taught, in their home units, earlier in the course.

Prior knowledge students need: Modern America taught in full, and the course behind it. No new content is introduced anywhere in the day; every fact in the packet, the sprint, and the practice set was taught in the units. The thread sections restate earlier units' arguments in compressed form, which is the review's purpose, not new material.

Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies, grades 9 to 10: RH.9-10.1 (cite specific textual evidence), RH.9-10.2 (determine central ideas), RH.9-10.6 (compare the point of view of two or more authors), RH.9-10.9 (compare and contrast treatments of the same topic), and WHST.9-10.1 (write arguments supported by evidence). C3 Framework: D2.His.5.9-12 (how a source's audience and purpose affect its message), D2.His.13.9-12 (analyze causes and effects), D2.His.16.9-12 (how choices in the past shape the present), and D2.Civ.8.9-12 (how citizens and institutions apply the Constitution over time).

What students will review: Modern America's conservative turn and Reagan settlement, globalization and the digital economy, the changing population, and the long emergencies inside a polarized settlement; then the course as six threads, federal power, expansion, race and rights, capital and labor, America in the world, and reform, each run from its first unit to the present. The day's skill is work with documents, the course's first skill, used here at course length.

Timing and materials: One class day, fuller than the other band days. Unit packet and timeline, about 12 minutes; term sprint, about 12 minutes; the thread sections read against the practice set's needs, about 15 minutes; practice set, about 20 minutes; skills task begun in class and its Part 3 finished as homework. Print the packet, sprint, practice set, and skills task for every student. Nothing else is required.

How the Day Runs

Students read the unit half of the packet with the usual instruction: mark any timeline row they cannot explain aloud. The sprint follows closed-book, then students self-check against the packet (the definitions are the guided-notes wording verbatim, so the check is exact). Read the thread sections next, one question in hand: which of the six could you not yet narrate unaided? The practice set runs closed-packet; its last two short answers are the day's real examination, and they are scored on the thread's accuracy, not on Modern America alone. Read the results by lesson and by thread before the practice test.

Practice Set Key

Multiple choice: 1. B. 2. D. 3. A. 4. C. 5. B. 6. D. 7. A. 8. C. 9. B. 10. D. 11. A. 12. C.

13. (MA, Lesson 2) The transactions: American arms were sold to Iran, officially embargoed and designated a sponsor of terror, in hopes of freeing hostages in Lebanon; and the proceeds of those sales were diverted to fund Nicaragua's Contra rebels. The violated principle: Congress's power of the purse, exercised in the Boland Amendments' prohibition on Contra funding, was circumvented by an executive running a private foreign policy off the books. Answers naming only the hostages miss the second transaction, which is the constitutional half.

14. (MA, Lessons 3 and 5) What left: unionized mass-production manufacturing, steel, autos, textiles, electronics, drawn by cheaper labor abroad and in the right-to-work South, and by the integrated supply chains globalization built. What replaced it: service employment, retail, health care, logistics, food service, and the professions at the top. The difference: the replacement jobs paid less on average, carried weaker benefits and pensions, and were far harder to unionize, so the same metropolitan regions held more jobs and less security at once.

15. (MA, Lesson 5) Causes, any two: air conditioning making the region's summers workable at scale; federal spending, military bases and defense and space industry sited in the South and West; right-to-work laws and cheaper land and labor drawing the factories; interstate highways and air travel knitting the region to the national economy; retirees and migrants following the jobs. Political consequence, any one: reapportionment and the Electoral College's center moved south and west, decade by decade; the Republican coalition's geographic base was built there, the southern strategy made geography; and national politics' agenda, taxes, growth, and defense, was set increasingly by the region's growth model.

16. (MA, Lesson 6) Powers, any two: roving and expanded wiretap and surveillance authority; delayed-notice ("sneak and peek") search warrants; access to business, library, and financial records under gag orders; information sharing across intelligence and law-enforcement agencies that the pre-2001 wall had separated; material-support definitions broadened. The objection, in the earlier war's terms: the same one Schenck's generation pressed and lost, that emergency powers over speech, privacy, and detention, granted in the crisis's first weeks, outlast the crisis and normalize themselves; the Espionage Act's prosecutions and the internment that Band 6 reviewed are the precedents the critics cite.

17. (Long Arc, America in the World) The Farewell (1796) holds that the republic should trade with all nations and bind itself politically to none, permanent alliances being the road to foreign influence and domestic faction. The AUMF (2001) holds that the president may make war, without geographic or temporal limit stated in the text, on any nation, organization, or person he determines was connected to a single day's attacks. The distance: from a presumption that foreign entanglement is the republic's chief danger, distrusted even in gratitude's case, to an authorization so open that two decades of war were waged inside its one sentence; students should note that the earlier document feared exactly the habit the later one embodies, the making of war on executive determination.

18. (Long Arc, Slavery, Race, and Rights) The betrayal: the Plessy era, in which the amendment's equal protection was read to permit state-mandated segregation as "separate but equal," and its citizenship guarantees were administered into disfranchisement for three generations. The revival: Brown and the civil rights decade, in which the movement and the Warren Court made the amendment the weapon it was written to be, against schools, accommodations, and the franchise's machinery, with the 1964 and 1965 statutes built on its enforcement clauses' logic. The extension: Obergefell (2015), in which the amendment's liberty and equality were held to reach a class and a right, same-sex marriage, its framers never named, the amendment's generality doing the work its authors left it able to do.

Skills Task Guidance

Part 1 key: Document A: 1796, George Washington's Farewell Address; thread, America in the World; claim, commerce with all, political connection and permanent alliance with none. Document B: 1848, the Declaration of Sentiments, Seneca Falls; thread, Slavery, Race, and Rights (accept Reform Movements, with the note that the document is the rights thread's founding text); claim, the equality the Declaration of Independence announced extends to women, with the rights that follow. Document C: 1945, Franklin D. Roosevelt's Fourth Inaugural Address, January 20, 1945; thread, America in the World; claim, isolation is finished as a possibility: American well-being depends on the world's, and Americans are citizens of the world community whether they choose it or not. Document D: 2001, the Authorization for Use of Military Force, enacted September 18, 2001; thread, America in the World (accept Federal Power, since its delegation is the document's mechanism); claim, the president is authorized to use all necessary and appropriate force against those he determines were connected to the September 11 attacks.

Part 2: Between A and C stand the two world wars, the failure of the interwar settlement the Senate's refusal helped shape, air power and then atomic weapons abolishing the oceans' protection, and an economy whose markets and supply lines were the world's. In 2001 the country was following C in its reach, a global posture exercised on the day of the attacks, and D's instrument, open-ended executive war under one authorization, is what that posture looks like as law. Strong answers notice that D's text never mentions a terminus, which is where A re-enters the argument. For Document B: the borrowing argues that the claim is not new rights but old ones misapplied, that the nation's own founding text, read as written, already contains the conclusion, so that the burden of argument shifts to anyone who would read "all men" as excluding half the signers' species. That is the same move the civil rights century made with the Fourteenth Amendment, and students who say so have understood the course's method point.

Part 3: Score the defense on accuracy of the chosen document's claim, the reality of the present argument named, and the objection answered rather than waved at. All four choices are defensible: A against the permanent-alliance system's latest war, C against the newest isolationism, D against the authorization's third decade of use, B against any present reading of the founding texts that narrows their subjects. Paragraphs that choose by fame ("Washington is the most important") without the claim and the present argument earn nothing beyond Part 1's facts.
