Part 3: Sources

A1. (3 points) Government is instituted by the people to secure rights they already hold by nature, and it derives its just powers only from their consent. When a government becomes destructive of those ends, the people retain the right to alter or abolish it and institute a new one. All three elements are required: pre-existing rights, consent as the sole source of just power, and the reserved right of revolution.

A2. (2 points) John Locke, whose Second Treatise argued government by consent and a right of resistance to tyranny. Accept any one of: Paine's Common Sense, which carried the argument to a mass readership; the state constitutions and declarations of rights written in 1776 and after; the Declaration's own grievance list, which is the theory applied as an indictment; or the ratification debates, where consent of the governed was the ground both sides claimed.

B1. (3 points) Inequality that arises from natural differences of talent, industry, and fortune is, in Jackson's argument, unavoidable under any just government and no ground of complaint. Inequality created by law, the artificial distinctions of charters, exclusive privileges, and gratuities granted to those with the access to obtain them, is the kind government must never manufacture. The Bank, in his telling, is the second kind in corporate form.

B2. (2 points) The Cherokee and the other southeastern nations, removed west on the Trail of Tears under his Indian Removal Act, or enslaved people, whose labor his own household used and whose freedom his politics defended slavery against. The point the answer must make: the veto's producing public was white. Its equality argument ran inside a racial boundary his administration enforced by removal and by the defense of slavery, which is the unit's verdict on Jacksonian democracy's shape.

C1. (3 points) The war tests whether a nation conceived in liberty and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal can long endure, meaning whether self-government on an egalitarian premise is viable at all. Dating the founding to 1776 makes the Declaration, with its equality proposition, the nation's founding act and standard, rather than the Constitution of 1787 with its compromises protecting slavery. Emancipation then follows as fidelity to the founding, not as a departure from it.

C2. (2 points) It promises that the war's outcome will extend the founding proposition itself: the nation will emerge with freedom enlarged, in context the destruction of slavery and the beginning of its promise's application to the enslaved. Restoration alone, the Union as it was, is explicitly not the address's goal; the dead are to be honored by a nation reborn, not merely reassembled.
