Band 5 Review Packet: Power Abroad, Turmoil at Home

Directions: This packet compresses two full units, Imperialism and World War I and The Twenties, into one review. Read the two arguments, study the timeline, and keep the packet beside you for the term sprint and the practice set. Both of those name the lesson to reopen when an answer will not come.

The Band in Brief

In the 1890s the United States went looking for the standing its economy had already earned: a battle fleet and the coaling stations to feed it, markets in Asia kept open by diplomacy, and, after the Maine, an empire taken from Spain in a single summer. The republic then had to argue with itself about what it had done. Cuba was supervised, the Philippines were conquered in a second, uglier war, and the anti-imperialists said the founding documents had been left behind in Manila. A decade later the country policed the Caribbean with the Roosevelt Corollary, and when Europe went to war in 1914 it tried neutrality, sold and lent to one side until neutrality was a legal word only, and entered in 1917 after the U-boats and the Zimmermann Telegram left it no posture to keep. The war it fought was short and total: conscription, a managed economy, propaganda and prosecutions at home, and a peace program, the Fourteen Points and a League, that its own Senate refused to ratify.

The Twenties were the peace's domestic argument. The business civilization reached ordinary households: the Model T and the installment plan, radio and the talkies, suburbs and the celebrity age, all built on productivity's climb. The same decade fought itself over what the prosperity was for: prohibition's underworld of speakeasies and organized crime, a mass Ku Klux Klan marching in daylight, immigration closed by national origins, fundamentalism and evolution tried in a Tennessee courtroom. And underneath the noise the structure was weak all decade long: farms in depression from 1921, factories overproducing into saturated markets, unions beaten back by the open shop, and a stock market levitating on margin. Black Tuesday did not cause the weakness. It announced it.

Imperialism and World War I asked: Why did the United States seek power overseas, and how did the First World War change the country that fought it?

The Twenties asked: How did the prosperity of the 1920s change American life, and why was that prosperity weaker than it looked?

The Argument of Imperialism and World War I

The turn overseas

The arguments for expansion were made before the occasions arrived. Navalism said sea power was national power and a fleet needed coaling stations, canals, and colonies to stand on. The frontier thesis said the safety valve and the school of democracy had closed in 1890, and expansionists drew the moral that American energy needed a new frontier abroad. Manufacturers wanted markets no tariff wall could close; jingoism wanted the flag respected; and a depression decade wanted outlets for goods, capital, and discontent. Hawaii was annexed, the Venezuela boundary was brandished at Britain, and Cuba's revolt against Spain, retailed nightly by the yellow journals, gave the arguments their war.

An empire, and the argument against it

The war with Spain was brief and lopsided, and its settlement made the difference: Puerto Rico and Guam taken as spoils, the Philippines purchased and then conquered, because Aguinaldo's republic expected the independence the Americans had encouraged against Spain. The Philippine-American War, guerrilla war, reconcentration, and atrocity on both sides in the record, cost more Filipino lives than the Spanish war cost anyone. Cuba, barred from annexation by the Teller Amendment, was made a protectorate by the Platt Amendment instead. The Anti-Imperialist League's case was constitutional and moral at once: a government resting on consent cannot rule subjects, and the empire would import the republic's corruption wholesale, militarism, executive war-making, and a colonial class. The Insular Cases answered in law that the Constitution did not follow the flag in full, which both sides understood as the argument continuing.

Policeman of the Caribbean, neutral in a world war

The Roosevelt Corollary reasoned from the Monroe Doctrine: if European creditors collected debts in the Americas by force, the United States would be drawn in, so the United States would intervene first, as the hemisphere's policeman. Dollar diplomacy under Taft put bankers where Marines had been, and the Panama Canal, dug under American control after Panama's convenient revolution, joined the oceans on American terms. In 1914 the same country proclaimed neutrality, and neutrality eroded by structure rather than by decree: the Allies' blockade made American trade one-sided, American loans followed American goods, and Germany's only counter-weapon, the submarine, could not observe the cruiser rules without surfacing to be sunk. The Lusitania, the Sussex Pledge, and then, in February 1917, Berlin's decision to resume unrestricted sinkings and Zimmermann's proposal to Mexico, and the neutral era closed.

Total war at home, and the peace that failed

The war state conscripted men, directed industry through the War Industries Board, sold Liberty Bonds to the crowd, and propagandized through the Committee on Public Information, while the Espionage and Sedition Acts and their prosecutions, upheld in Schenck's clear and present danger, marked the era's cost to the First Amendment. Black southerners voted with their feet in the Great Migration, and influenza killed more Americans than the trenches did. The AEF arrived in time to make 1918's offensives possible and the armistice Germany's request. Then the peace failed at home: the Fourteen Points had promised a League, Versailles built one and attached it to a treaty of reparations and guilt, Lodge offered reservations on Article X, Wilson forbade his own senators to accept them, wrecked his health on a national tour, and the Senate refused the treaty in every form. The country chose normalcy, and the choice framed the next unit's decade.

The Argument of The Twenties

The business civilization

The decade's engine was productivity: scientific management, electrification, and Ford's moving line cut the cost of the durable goods, cars, radios, appliances, that the decade sold as a standard of living. Installment buying put the goods in the home before they were paid for, advertising taught households to want them on schedule, and the chain store retailed them at prices the independent merchant could not meet. The politics matched: Coolidge's dictum that the business of America is business, tax cuts at the top, high tariffs, and a federal government that left the economy's steering to its owners. Union membership fell under the open shop drive and welfare capitalism's company unions and pension plans, offered precisely so that workers would not build institutions of their own.

Mass culture and the automobile

Radio networks and then the talkies synchronized the nation's amusements; the tabloids and the newsreels manufactured celebrity at industrial scale, with Lindbergh's flight the decade's purest product. The automobile was the deeper revolution. It built the suburb and the commuter, the paved highway and the motel, moved dating out of the parlor into public space beyond the chaperone's reach, and handed young people, women prominently among them, a freedom their parents had supervised indoors. The decade's cultural argument was, at bottom, about who controlled the new freedoms, and the car had already decided much of it.

The culture wars

Prohibition was the old America's statute against the new: meant to close the saloon and sober the immigrant city, it built instead the speakeasy, the bootlegger's supply chain, and organized crime's national syndicates, and taught a generation of respectable citizens to break the law at dinner. The second Ku Klux Klan sold itself as Protestant America's defense against Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and bootleggers alike, and marched in the tens of thousands before scandal and its own corruption shrunk it. The National Origins Act wrote the same anxieties into immigration law with eugenics' vocabulary on the record. At Dayton, the Scopes trial put fundamentalism's case against evolution, and modern education itself, before a national audience; the verdict convicted the teacher and advertised the town, and both sides claimed the century.

The weak spots, and the Crash

The prosperity was narrow where it counted. Farmers had been in depression since the wartime demand collapsed in 1921, overproducing into falling prices with wartime debts fixed at wartime levels. Industry by the decade's end was producing more cars, radios, and houses than the wage structure could buy, overproduction being the polite term for a distribution problem. The financial structure rested on credit at every level: installment debt in the household, corporate debt pyramids in the utilities, and a stock market where buying on margin let a ten percent stake carry a hundred percent position, with the banks' call loans underneath. October 1929 called those loans. The unit's discipline is to keep the order straight: the crash did not manufacture the weakness, in the farms, the factories, or the banks. It revealed it, and the next band opens on what the revelation became.

People of the Band

Alfred Thayer Mahan wrote the strategy of sea power that a generation of statesmen, Roosevelt first among them, memorized. Emilio Aguinaldo declared a Philippine republic, fought Spain beside the Americans, and then fought the Americans for three years. Mark Twain lent the Anti-Imperialist League its most famous voice and its bitterest essays. Woodrow Wilson took the country into a war to make the world safe for democracy and lost the peace in his own Senate. Henry Cabot Lodge offered the treaty a set of reservations its author would not take, and the League an American grave. Henry Ford made the car cheap, the line fast, and the five-dollar day famous, and fought his own workers' unions to the end of the next decade. Al Capone industrialized the liquor law's black market in Chicago and became prohibition's most honest product. Langston Hughes and the Harlem Renaissance's writers made the Great Migration's culture the decade's lasting art. Charles Lindbergh flew alone to Paris and woke up the most famous man alive, a celebrity manufactured at the speed of the new media.
