Band 5 Teacher Guide: Power Abroad, Turmoil at Home

When to teach it: On the fifth day of the Course Review unit (the second band of the Modern Course Review). The band's comparison task is the review's most argumentative; schedule it when the class can give the skills task a full half period.

What it pairs with: The two units it reviews, Imperialism and World War I and The Twenties. It pairs forward with Band 6: the Twenties' weak spots, reviewed at the end of this day, are the next band's opening conditions, and students should leave today able to name them without prompting.

Prior knowledge students need: Both units taught in full. No new content is introduced anywhere in the day; every fact in the packet, the sprint, and the practice set was taught in the two units. One term, the Great Migration, is defined in both units; the sprint prints it once, from The Twenties, where the movement's cultural consequences are taught.

Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies, grades 9 to 10: RH.9-10.1 (cite specific textual evidence), RH.9-10.2 (determine central ideas), RH.9-10.9 (compare and contrast treatments of the same topic), and WHST.9-10.1 (write arguments supported by evidence). C3 Framework: D2.His.2.9-12 (classify historical sources and arguments), D2.His.13.9-12 (analyze causes and effects), D2.Civ.14.9-12 (how the United States relates to the world), and D2.His.16.9-12 (how historical choices shape the present).

What students will review: The expansion arguments of the 1890s, the Spanish-American War's settlement and the Philippine war, Caribbean policing, neutrality's erosion and the road to 1917, total war's home front and the failed peace; then the Twenties' business civilization, mass culture and the automobile, the culture wars over prohibition, immigration, and religion, and the weak structure under the prosperity. The day's skill is comparison: two national debates with the same skeleton.

Timing and materials: One class day. Packet reading and timeline, about 15 minutes; term sprint, about 15 minutes; practice set, about 20 minutes; skills task in class, with the Part 3 paragraph finished as homework if the period runs short. Print the packet, sprint, practice set, and skills task for every student. Nothing else is required.

How the Day Runs

Students read the packet with one instruction: mark any timeline row they cannot explain aloud. The sprint follows closed-book, then students self-check against the packet (the definitions are the guided-notes wording verbatim, so the check is exact). The practice set runs closed-packet. Read the results by lesson, not by student: each item names its lesson, so a weak item is a reteach assignment before the practice test.

Practice Set Key

Multiple choice: 1. C. 2. C. 3. A. 4. D. 5. A. 6. D. 7. C. 8. B. 9. D. 10. A. 11. C. 12. B.

13. (WWI, Lessons 2 and 3) Cuba was made nominally independent, as the Teller Amendment had promised no annexation, and then supervised through the Platt Amendment's intervention right and the Guantánamo base. The Philippines were annexed outright as a cession and held by conquest when Aguinaldo's republic resisted. The separating principle: the Teller promise covered Cuba alone, and behind it the era's judgment, taught plainly in the unit, that Filipinos were held unready for self-government in a way Cubans, at the threshold of supervision, were not. Credit the settlement facts and the Teller Amendment; the readiness judgment is the deeper half of the answer.

14. (WWI, Lesson 7) Any two reaches: the draft itself; the War Industries Board's direction of production and prices; the Committee on Public Information's propaganda in every medium; Liberty Bond drives that made patriotism a purchase; the Espionage and Sedition Acts' surveillance of speech, mail, and meeting. The cost, from any of them: roughly two thousand prosecutions for speech, Debs in prison for an antiwar address, Schenck's doctrine that wartime speech could be jailed on a danger standard, newspapers losing mailing rights, and a habit of equating dissent with disloyalty that ran on into the Red Scare.

15. (WWI, Lesson 8) The provision was Article X of the League Covenant, the members' undertaking to preserve one another's territory and independence, which Lodge read as an obligation that could send American soldiers to war on a League vote, without Congress. Lodge's position: ratify with reservations, his own, that made Article X binding only as Congress directed in each case. Wilson's position: the reservations nullified the treaty, his party would vote no on the Lodge version, and he took the question to the country instead. The treaty therefore failed twice, with and without reservations, each coalition preferring defeat to the other's version.

16. (TW, Lesson 5) The mechanism: the law abolished the legal supply while leaving the demand standing, so the trade's profits, now untaxed and unregulated, flowed to whoever would supply it illegally and defend the territory for it. Bootleggers and speakeasies needed supply chains, protection, and distribution, which organized crime, Capone's Chicago the type case, built as national syndicates, buying police and officials with the trade's margins. The reform thus converted a taxed, regulated, local vice into an untaxed, violent, national industry, and funded it with the drinker's money at higher prices.

17. (TW, Lesson 7) Push, any one: Jim Crow's daily violence and humiliation, lynching unpunished, disfranchisement, the collapse of cotton tenancy's economics. Pull, any one: northern war-industry wages, recruited by labor agents and by the Chicago Defender's pages arriving in southern towns, with earlier migrants' letters home as the network. Consequence, any one: the Harlem Renaissance and the New Negro's assertive culture; the Black metropolitan electorate that northern politicians would have to court; the Defender itself as a national Black press. A miss usually gives jobs alone, without the push or the culture.

18. (TW, Lesson 8) Any three: agriculture in depression from 1921, overproducing against collapsed wartime demand with debts fixed; industrial overproduction by the decade's end, durable goods outrunning the wage structure that had to buy them; a labor movement beaten back by the open shop, unable to widen the consumer base; installment debt at the household level; speculation on margin and the banks' call loans beneath it; a fragile banking system of small unit banks failing by the hundreds in farm country through the decade. The full explanation should follow one thread to the household or the firm, not list terms.

Skills Task Guidance

Part 1: Sound structure: for 1898, the case for runs on markets, sea power, and national standing (Mahan, the expansionist press), and the case against on consent and corruption at home (the League, Twain); the fears underneath are decline and irrelevance on one side, and a militarized republic with colonial subjects on the other. For 1919, the case for runs on preventing the next war through collective security (Wilson), and the case against on Article X and the surrender of Congress's war power (Lodge, the irreconcilables); the fears are another 1917 on one side and a superstate's draft on American blood on the other. The comparison's payoff, which strong answers state: in both debates the losing side feared the republic would be changed by the commitment, and in both the winning arrangement arrived anyway in part, the empire in 1898 and, a generation later, the alliance system the League debaters had refused.

Part 2: Defensible marks: 1. E (a negotiated arms settlement among rivals is diplomacy, on American terms). 2. E in form, R in substance: credit either with a reason (a universal pledge to renounce war, with no machinery, is engagement's vocabulary doing retreat's work). 3. E or R, argued: the debt policy kept the United States at the center of Europe's finances while refusing Europe's politics, economic engagement as a substitute for political commitment. 4. R (the decade's clearest closing of doors). 5. R (the refusal itself), with the nuance that the same Senate sustained the Washington treaties. Score the defense lines, not the letters, where the two diverge honestly.

Part 3: The strong verdict is qualified: isolationist in commitment, internationalist in commerce, finance, and diplomacy of a limited kind. Require the two facts per side as directed (League refusal and quotas against Washington and Kellogg-Briand, or the debt and loan traffic), and penalize any paragraph that treats the decade as simple withdrawal, since the units' own evidence contradicts it.
