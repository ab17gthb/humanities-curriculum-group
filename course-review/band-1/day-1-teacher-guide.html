Band 1 Teacher Guide: Colonies and the Founding

When to teach it: On the first day of the Course Review unit, in the review days before the course final or state test. For the Early Course Review it is likewise the first of three bands. The day assumes the class begins review here; if your students tested weakly on the colonial units during the year, this is the band to give a second period rather than the later ones.

What it pairs with: The two units it reviews, Colonial America and The Founding, whose decks and documents are the reteach stock. It also pairs with the review deck in Rycal: students who set their test date when review begins cycle these terms in spaced sessions, and the sprint below is the paper check on that work.

Prior knowledge students need: Both units taught in full. No new content is introduced anywhere in the day; every fact in the packet, the sprint, and the practice set was taught in the two units.

Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies, grades 9 to 10: RH.9-10.1 (cite specific textual evidence), RH.9-10.2 (determine central ideas), RH.9-10.3 (analyze a series of events in sequence), and WHST.9-10.1 (write arguments supported by evidence). C3 Framework: D2.His.1.9-12 (how historical context shapes interpretation), D2.His.9.9-12 (how historical events and developments are connected across time), D2.His.13.9-12 (analyze causes and effects), and D2.Civ.3.9-12 (the ideals and principles in the founding documents).

What students will review: The meeting of three worlds and the colonial societies it produced, the Atlantic system of mercantilism and slavery that bound them, and the founding sequence from the revenue crisis of 1763 through the Constitution's compromises to the Bill of Rights. The day's skill is chronology: order as an argument about what made what possible.

Timing and materials: One class day. Packet reading and timeline, about 15 minutes; term sprint, about 15 minutes; practice set, about 20 minutes; skills task in class, with Part 2's second paragraph finished as homework if the period runs short. Print the packet, sprint, practice set, and skills task for every student. Nothing else is required.

How the Day Runs

Students read the packet with one instruction: mark any timeline row they cannot explain aloud. The sprint follows closed-book, then students self-check against the packet (the definitions are the guided-notes wording verbatim, so the check is exact). The practice set runs closed-packet. Read the results by lesson, not by student: each item names its lesson, so a weak item is a reteach assignment before the practice test.

Practice Set Key

Multiple choice: 1. B. 2. A. 3. B. 4. C. 5. C. 6. C. 7. A. 8. D. 9. D. 10. B. 11. A. 12. D.

13. (CA, Lesson 2) Spain organized its empire as conquest and extraction: dense settlement on Native land, and Native labor drafted through the encomienda to work mines and estates. France organized its empire as trade: thin settlements, and Native nations courted as allies and partners in the fur trade, which needed their hunting and diplomacy intact. Credit the contrast in both labor and relationship; answers that make France gentle and Spain cruel without the labor point earn half.

14. (CA, Lesson 7) The Awakening let ordinary people judge religious authority for themselves: itinerant preachers drew crowds away from settled ministers, congregations split and multiplied, and converts learned to choose a church rather than inherit one. That practiced habit of questioning and choosing authority is what transferred to politics a generation later. A miss usually stays inside religion; the transfer is the point.

15. (CA, Lesson 8; FD, Lesson 1) Victory doubled the national debt and left Britain paying for an American military establishment and a vast new territory to administer. London ended salutary neglect and raised the money where the war had been fought, beginning with the Stamp Act. Students who answer "the war was expensive" without the standing costs and the policy change have the fact without the mechanism.

16. (FD, Lesson 5) Lord Dunmore, Virginia's royal governor, proclaimed freedom in November 1775 to enslaved people held by rebels who would bear arms for the Crown. It offered enslaved men a road out of slavery through British service, and it told slaveholders that the empire would weaponize their own labor force against them, which converted many wavering planters to independence. Credit both halves: the offer, and the fear.

17. (FD, Lesson 6) Weaknesses, any two: Congress could not tax, only requisition the states; it could not regulate commerce; the Articles could not be amended without unanimity; there was no executive or federal judiciary worth the name; each state kept sovereignty and one vote. Achievement, any one: the Land Ordinance of 1785's survey system, the Northwest Ordinance's government for the territories with slavery barred, or the peace itself, the Treaty of Paris negotiated under the Articles. A miss usually lists grievances about the Constitution instead.

18. (FD, Lesson 8) The strongest objection was the absence of a bill of rights in a constitution that created a powerful general government (the fear behind it, that a distant consolidated government would swallow the states, earns the point when named with it). It was answered by the Federalists' promise of amendments, kept when the First Congress proposed them and the states ratified ten in 1791.

Skills Task Guidance

Part 1 key: 1. Jamestown, 1607. 2. Mayflower Compact, 1620. 3. Bacon's Rebellion, 1676. 4. The Great Awakening, 1730s (accept 1739, Whitefield's tour). 5. Treaty of Paris, 1763. 6. Proclamation of 1763, 1763. 7. Stamp Act, 1765. 8. Boston Tea Party, 1773. 9. Declaration of Independence, 1776. 10. Saratoga, 1777. 11. Yorktown, 1781. 12. Shays's Rebellion, 1786. 13. Constitution ratified, 1788. 14. Bill of Rights ratified, 1791. The same-year pair at 5 and 6 is the teaching moment: the Proclamation followed the Treaty by months and was a consequence of the victory, not a coincidence of dates.

Part 2: Score each paragraph on the options test: the student must name an option that existed before the year and was gone after it. Strong common choices are 1763 (before it, colonists imagined a future inside the empire; the revenue program closed that future by stages), 1776 (allegiance itself), 1781 to 1783 (independence as fact, which made the Confederation's weaknesses a governing problem rather than a wartime arrangement), and 1787 to 1788 (the Confederation itself). Famous-but-weak choices (1607, 1620) earn credit only when the options argument is genuinely made.

Part 3: A fair chain: war debt and garrisons to pay for (1763) answered by the Stamp Act (1765); boycott and repeal with the Declaratory Act's claim of total authority; Townshend duties answered by renewed boycott and troops in Boston, ending in the Massacre; the Tea Act answered in the harbor (1773); the Intolerable Acts answered by a continental Congress (1774); the march on Lexington answered by a continental army and, within fifteen months, a Declaration (1776). Any five linked pairs in order, each with a British action and a colonial answer, earn full credit.
