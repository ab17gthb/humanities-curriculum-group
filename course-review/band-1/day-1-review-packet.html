Band 1 Review Packet: Colonies and the Founding

Directions: This packet compresses two full units, Colonial America and The Founding, into one review. Read the two arguments, study the timeline, and keep the packet beside you for the term sprint and the practice set. Both of those name the lesson to reopen when an answer will not come.

The Band in Brief

In 1491 the Americas held cities, confederacies, and farming peoples of their own, and Europe was a crowded shore of rival kingdoms fresh from the Reconquista. Contact remade both sides of the ocean: crops, animals, and disease crossed in the Columbian Exchange, Spain built an empire on conquest and the encomienda, France built one on the fur trade, and England planted colonies that grew into three distinct societies, a tobacco Chesapeake, a covenanted New England, and a many-peopled middle. One system bound them together: an Atlantic economy run on mercantilism and, at its center, the slave trade, which made slavery hereditary, racial, and permanent in colonial law.

Britain's victory in the French and Indian War doubled its American empire and its debt, and the attempt to make the colonies pay for both ended the long era of salutary neglect. A decade of taxes, boycotts, and street politics turned a revenue dispute into a constitutional argument, and the argument into a war. The colonies declared independence on a theory of natural rights and consent, survived the war with French help, wrote a confederation that could not hold, and replaced it at Philadelphia with a Constitution of compromises: representation bargained, powers divided, slavery counted and protected, and a Bill of Rights promised to the skeptics who ratified it.

Colonial America asked: How did the meeting of Europe, Africa, and the Americas remake all three worlds, and what kind of society grew up in England's colonies?

The Founding asked: Why did the colonies break with Britain, and what did Americans build in the place of the empire they rejected?

The Argument of Colonial America

Two worlds, and the exchange that remade both

The Americas in 1491 were not empty. Cahokia's mounds, Tenochtitlan's causeways, and the Iroquois Confederacy's diplomacy were the work of societies as organized as Europe's, and Europe in 1491, fresh from finishing the Reconquista, had the ships, the caravel above all, and the motive to cross. What crossed remade both hemispheres. American crops fed population growth around the world, European animals transformed American plains and forests, and epidemic disease, against which Native peoples had no immunity, emptied country on a scale no conquest could have managed. Spain organized the meeting as extraction: conquistadors, then the encomienda, then haciendas and missions, and a society sorted by mixture, the mestizo world. France organized it as trade, thin settlements bound to Native nations by the fur trade's alliances.

The Chesapeake: tobacco, servants, and the turn to slavery

Jamestown nearly starved as a joint-stock speculation and survived as a tobacco colony. Tobacco demanded land and labor without limit. The headright system paid land for imported labor, the House of Burgesses gave the planters a legislature, and indentured servants supplied the hands, English poor working out a term for passage. Bacon's Rebellion in 1676, when those servants' hopes curdled into frontier war and the burning of Jamestown itself, taught the planter class the danger of a labor force that outlived its contracts armed and aggrieved. The turn that followed, toward enslaved Africans held for life under law that made the condition hereditary, was a choice with causes, and the unit teaches it as one.

New England and the middle: two other Americas

New England was settled by covenant. Plymouth's Mayflower Compact and Massachusetts Bay's congregations built towns meant to be watched by the world, governed by church members, and kept pure by discipline that expelled an Anne Hutchinson and a Roger Williams for taking the covenant's logic further than its magistrates allowed. The Salem trials showed the covenant's anxiety at its breaking point. The middle colonies were the opposite experiment: New Netherland's trading posts, Penn's Quaker proprietorship, and peoples of half a dozen nations and churches living under one proprietary roof, the colonies where the Atlantic economy's variety concentrated.

One Atlantic system, and a war that ended it

Mercantilism was the system's theory: colonies existed to enrich the mother country, trade was routed and taxed by the Navigation Acts, and the triangular trade joined European manufactures, African captives, and American plantation produce in one circuit. The Middle Passage was that circuit's human core, and colonial slave codes, the task system's small negotiations notwithstanding, built a legal order of hereditary racial slavery that Stono's rebels answered in arms. Colonists meanwhile governed themselves more than London noticed, under salutary neglect, through assemblies that held the purse, until the Great Awakening and the Enlightenment alike taught them habits of questioning authority. The French and Indian War then settled the continent's ownership and unsettled everything else: Britain victorious under Pitt, France gone, Pontiac's rebels answered by a Proclamation line the colonists had no intention of honoring, and a debt that London intended the victors' colonies to help pay.

The Argument of The Founding

A revenue program becomes a constitutional argument

Britain taxed for reasons any treasury would recognize: a doubled debt and an American military establishment to fund. The Stamp Act taxed paper directly, and the colonies answered with the argument that would carry the decade: no taxation without representation, against Parliament's claim of virtual representation of every British subject. Repeal came with the Declaratory Act's reservation of total authority, the Townshend duties tested whether the colonies would accept the distinction between internal and external taxes (they would not, for long), and the Massacre gave the argument its martyrs. The pattern to hold is the ratchet: each measure narrower than the last, each colonial answer broader.

From protest to war to independence declared

The Tea Act was a rescue of the East India Company that made taxed tea cheaper, and Boston answered it in the harbor because the principle, not the price, was the point. The Intolerable Acts punished Massachusetts as an example and instead made its cause continental: the First Continental Congress coordinated resistance, and by the time regulars marched on Lexington and Concord the colonies had governments, militias, and a war. Independence still needed an argument, and 1776 supplied it twice over, Paine's Common Sense in plain language against kingship itself, and the Declaration in formal theory: natural and unalienable rights, consent of the governed, a people's right to alter or abolish, and a list of grievances as the evidence. The Declaration was a promise made in universal terms by men who held slaves and governed women, and the war's home fronts, Dunmore's Proclamation and the Black soldiers it drew, Native nations choosing sides, camp followers and Deborah Sampson, are the record of everyone who tried to collect on it.

Winning, and the government that could not hold the peace

The war was survived before it was won: Washington's army kept in being through Valley Forge, Saratoga earning the French alliance that brought fleets and money, and Yorktown ending the argument when a British army surrendered on a Virginia peninsula. The Articles of Confederation, the government the Revolution had actually designed, were a league of sovereign states with a Congress that could request money (requisitions) but not compel it, amend nothing without unanimity, and neither tax trade nor settle the West without the states' gifts of land, though it governed the West well once it had it, in the Land Ordinance and the Northwest Ordinance. Inflation, unpaid soldiers, interstate quarrels, and Shays's farmers closing Massachusetts courts in 1786 made the Confederation's failure a felt experience, and Annapolis led to Philadelphia.

Compromise as the Constitution's method

The Constitution is a set of bargains, and the unit teaches it that way. The Virginia Plan against the New Jersey Plan was settled by the Great Compromise, a House by population and a Senate by equality. Representation's arithmetic ran through slavery, settled by counting each enslaved person as three fifths. The powers were federal, divided between nation and states, and separated among three branches so that ambition could check ambition. Ratification was a second campaign, fought in newspapers, Federalist against Anti-Federalist, over what the design omitted, and the skeptics' price, a bill of rights, was paid in 1791. What Americans built in place of empire was a republic strong enough to tax and act, limited on paper by enumeration and amendment, and founded with slavery's protection written into its arithmetic. The whole course after this band is, in one sense, the working out of those two facts.

People of the Band

Anne Hutchinson and Roger Williams were expelled from Massachusetts for following its covenant where its rulers would not. Olaudah Equiano wrote the Middle Passage from inside it. Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield carried the Great Awakening's two poles, the sermon of terror and the itinerant crowd. William Pitt funded the war that won an empire and the debt that undid it. Thomas Paine made independence sound like common sense. George Washington held an army together by retreat and endurance, surrendered his commission, and presided over the Convention that made him the new government's obvious head. Deborah Sampson fought disguised as a man and petitioned for her pension as a woman. Joseph Brant led Mohawk forces for the Crown in a war his confederacy could only lose by choosing either side. James Madison drafted the Virginia Plan, kept the Convention's notes, and argued the design into ratification in the Federalist Papers.
