Band 7 Teacher Guide: Cold War Abroad, Rights at Home

When to teach it: On the seventh day of the Course Review unit (the fourth band of the Modern Course Review). The packet's timeline runs the two units on one clock deliberately; point that out before students read, because several practice items reward seeing the years side by side.

What it pairs with: The two units it reviews, The Cold War and Civil Rights. It pairs forward with Band 8: the southern strategy and the backlash politics in the Civil Rights unit's final lesson are Modern America's opening conditions.

Prior knowledge students need: Both units taught in full. No new content is introduced anywhere in the day; every fact in the packet, the sprint, and the practice set was taught in the two units. The Cold War unit deliberately reserved Executive Order 9981, Little Rock, and Emmett Till to the Civil Rights unit; they appear in this review only on the Civil Rights side, as taught.

Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies, grades 9 to 10: RH.9-10.1 (cite specific textual evidence), RH.9-10.2 (determine central ideas), RH.9-10.9 (compare and contrast treatments of the same topic), and WHST.9-10.1 (write arguments supported by evidence). C3 Framework: D2.His.16.9-12 (how choices in the past shape the present), D2.Civ.13.9-12 (how federalism allocates and contests power), D2.His.13.9-12 (analyze causes and effects), and D2.Civ.14.9-12 (how the United States relates to the world).

What students will review: Containment's design and instruments, the limited war in Korea, the Second Red Scare and McCarthy's fall, the nuclear shadow and the missile crisis, Vietnam and détente, and the Soviet collapse from inside; and the movement's foundations, Brown and massive resistance, the direct-action engine at Montgomery, Greensboro, Birmingham, and Selma, the two great statutes, and the northern turn with its unfinished agenda. The day's skill is continuity and change across twenty shared years.

Timing and materials: One class day. Packet reading and timeline, about 15 minutes; term sprint, about 15 minutes; practice set, about 20 minutes; skills task in class, with Part 2's turn finished as homework if the period runs short. Print the packet, sprint, practice set, and skills task for every student. Nothing else is required.

How the Day Runs

Students read the packet with one instruction: mark any timeline row they cannot explain aloud. The sprint follows closed-book, then students self-check against the packet (the definitions are the guided-notes wording verbatim, so the check is exact). The practice set runs closed-packet. Read the results by lesson, not by student: each item names its lesson, so a weak item is a reteach assignment before the practice test.

Practice Set Key

Multiple choice: 1. A. 2. C. 3. B. 4. D. 5. B. 6. A. 7. C. 8. B. 9. D. 10. A. 11. D. 12. B.

13. (CW, Lesson 2) NSC-68 (1950) redefined the conflict as a global, indefinite struggle between freedom and slavery and called for a vast, permanent military buildup, containment converted from a European policy of economic and political resistance into a worldwide military posture. What funded it was the Korean War: the invasion that June let the Truman administration triple the defense budget through Congress as an emergency measure, and the posture outlasted the emergency.

14. (CW, Lesson 4) Brinkmanship, as Dulles described it, was the art of getting to the verge of war without falling in: the adversary, believing you might accept general war rather than retreat, retreats first, the massive-retaliation threat standing behind every local dispute. Its premise was that American nuclear supremacy, or at least escalation dominance, made the threat credible and the adversary the cautious party. Sputnik, October 1957, shook exactly that premise in the public mind: a Soviet satellite overhead, carried by a Soviet rocket, advertised that American territory was reachable and American technological leadership contestable.

15. (CW, Lesson 7) The United States got arms control with verification's beginnings (SALT I's caps), a managed rivalry with rules, trade and grain sales, and, in the Helsinki Accords, the Soviet signature on human-rights language its dissidents then quoted back. The Soviet Union got recognition as an equal superpower, access to Western grain and technology, and the West's formal acceptance of Europe's postwar borders at Helsinki. What killed it: the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979, with the Senate's refusal to ratify SALT II and the American turn, under Carter's last year and then Reagan, back to confrontation.

16. (CR, Lesson 1) Any two, with contributions: the NAACP's litigation campaign, which built the precedent ladder, graduate schools first, that Brown climbed; the Double V generation, whose wartime service and migration created both the claims and the northern Black electorate to press them; Truman's presidency, To Secure These Rights naming the federal program and Executive Order 9981 desegregating the military, with the Justice Department arguing against segregation at the Court; the Black church and college networks, which supplied the movement's meeting places, money, and organizers when the tactic arrived.

17. (CR, Lesson 4) The established organizations were committed to litigation and negotiation, slow machinery controlled by adults with institutions to protect; the students of 1960 had neither property nor position to lose, had grown up on the movement's promises, and could act the week the idea struck them. The sit-in's advantages: it cost almost nothing to mount and could be replicated in any city with a Woolworth; the moral picture was unmistakable, well-dressed students beaten for ordering coffee; it attacked segregation at the cash register, where downtown merchants were vulnerable; and it forced a choice on everyone watching, including the federal government. SNCC's founding that spring gave the tactic an organization of its own.

18. (CR, Lesson 8) Any two, with facts: housing and the wealth it builds, de facto segregation hardening in the North and suburbs while the Fair Housing Act went weakly enforced, and redlining's legacy in the homeownership gap; economic equality itself, the Poor People's Campaign's unmet program, with Black unemployment and poverty rates stuck at roughly double the white rates the Kerner Commission had indicted; school segregation's return by address, busing ordered, resisted, and narrowed until northern and southern cities alike resegregated by neighborhood; affirmative action, the remedy the era improvised, contested from Bakke forward. The item fails answers that name attitudes ("racism") without an institution and a fact.

Skills Task Guidance

Part 1: Sound verdicts: the federal posture toward segregation is the clean reversal, from a government that tolerated and in its own facilities practiced segregation (9981 being 1948's first breach) to one that outlawed it in accommodations, employment, voting, and housing and sent examiners and troops to enforce the change. Voting expectation follows the same line with a lag: 1948's Mississippi registration under seven percent against 1968's near sixty, the verdict still split by county and by courage. Asia is the continuity trap: 1948's posture was postwar demobilization and a China policy failing; by 1968 the United States had fought two Asian land wars for containment, so the honest verdict is escalation, then check. The nuclear row is "changed in scale, continuous in dread": 1948's small atomic stockpile against 1968's tens of thousands of warheads on hair-trigger systems, while duck-and-cover's children became the test ban's adults. Accept "changed in law, continuous in life" where students spend it on the voting row's local reality or the segregation row's de facto half, with evidence.

Part 2: Scene one should show the courthouse's ordinary apartheid: a white registrar, a literacy test administered as an obstacle course, Black applicants absent or turned away on a pretext, and the poll tax receipt economy around it. Scene two shows the same room under new management: federal examiners or their threat present, tests suspended, Black citizens registering in numbers, local officials administering a law they campaigned against. The machinery to name: the Voting Rights Act, its examiners, its preclearance, and the Justice Department behind them, with the Civil Rights Act and the federal courts as the frame. The turn's unphotographable changes: expectation itself (a generation that now planned to vote), Black officeholding's return beginning, the Democratic Party's southern electorate remade, the legal premise that the federal government could police a county registrar at all. The unphotographable continuity: the wealth and schooling gaps the registration line did not touch, the registrar's neighbors' attitudes, or the economic reprisal that still waited outside the courthouse door. Credit specifics over sentiments in every line.
