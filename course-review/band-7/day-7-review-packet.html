Band 7 Review Packet: Cold War Abroad, Rights at Home

Directions: This packet compresses two full units, The Cold War and Civil Rights, into one review. Read the two arguments, study the timeline, and keep the packet beside you for the term sprint and the practice set. Both of those name the lesson to reopen when an answer will not come. The two units run on the same years, and the timeline reads them together deliberately: watch how often the foreign war and the domestic movement set each other's stage.

The Band in Brief

The alliance that won the Second World War divided its peace within two years. The United States answered Soviet pressure with a doctrine, containment, and built the doctrine a house: Marshall's billions for a recovering Western Europe, NATO's alliance, NSC-68's global military posture. The doctrine was tested first in Asia, where China's revolution was read in Washington as a loss to be answered, and Korea fixed the pattern of the limited war, fought to restore a line rather than to win an empire, at the price of a permanent garrison. At home the same fear governed by suspicion: loyalty oaths, HUAC's subpoenas, blacklists, and McCarthy's season of accusation, until television let the country watch the method and the Senate censured the man. Above everything hung the nuclear shadow, hydrogen bombs, brinkmanship, Sputnik's beep of doubt, until the thirteen days of October 1962 taught both capitals the price of the game's rules and bought the hotline and the test ban. Vietnam was containment's longest application and its refutation in the public mind; détente managed the rivalry for a decade; and the end, when it came, came from inside the Soviet house, from reforms that released everything they were meant to save.

At home those same years held the century's great democratic movement. The civil rights movement was built over decades before it was televised: the NAACP's litigation ladder, the Double V's claim, Truman's committee and his order desegregating the armed forces. Brown declared the law's old answer void, and massive resistance answered the declaration. Then the movement found its engine: nonviolent direct action, organized through the Black church and then by students, designed to manufacture the crisis that would force federal power off the sidelines, at Montgomery, Greensboro, Birmingham, and Selma. It worked in law: the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 remade the South's public order within five years. What it met next, in the North's housing lines and the economy's structure, in Watts and Chicago, and in the backlash politics that followed King to Memphis, is the movement's unfinished agenda, and this course's bridge to its final unit.

The Cold War asked: How did the struggle between the United States and the Soviet Union reshape the world and American life, and why did it end the way it did?

Civil Rights asked: How did the civil rights movement change American law and American life, and what did it leave unfinished?

The Argument of The Cold War

Containment takes shape

The doctrine was an answer to a map: a devastated Europe, communist parties positioned to win its elections, Soviet armies standing in its eastern half, and an American public that wanted its soldiers home. The Truman Doctrine universalized the answer, support for peoples resisting subjugation, on the occasion of aid to Greece and Turkey. The Marshall Plan gave the doctrine an economics: recovery as anti-communism, dollars against despair. NATO gave it an alliance and NSC-68, in 1950, gave it a military budget and a global perimeter, the blueprint Korea's outbreak let Washington fund. The design's premise, Kennan's, was patient pressure until the Soviet system mellowed or broke; its practice, at the hands of frightened electorates, was a line drawn everywhere at once.

Asia, and the limited war

China's revolution was read in Washington not as a Chinese event but as a loss on the containment ledger, and the politics of "who lost China" armed the decade's domestic inquisition. Korea then fixed how the Cold War would be fought: when the North invaded, the United States fought under a UN flag to restore the line; when MacArthur's success at Inchon tempted rollback to the Yalu, China's armies answered, and Truman fired his general rather than widen the war. The armistice of 1953 left Korea divided at the waist and American troops in garrison, and limited war, victory defined as the status quo defended, became the template every later president inherited and resented.

The enemy within, and the shadow overhead

The Second Red Scare had real spies in its background, the Soviet bomb's early arrival and the Fuchs and Rosenberg cases, and a machinery that ran far past them: Truman's loyalty program, HUAC's subpoenas and the Hollywood Ten's prison terms, blacklists kept by private vigilance, state oaths by the shelf. McCarthyism was the machinery's demagogic phase, accusation as a career, and its end came through the medium that had built it: the Army-McCarthy hearings of 1954 let thirty-six days of the country watch the method at work, and the Senate censured him that December. Over the same years the weapons changed the world's terms: hydrogen bombs, massive retaliation's threat to answer any probe with everything, brinkmanship as the stated art of seeming ready, Sputnik's demonstration that Soviet rockets could reach what Soviet armies could not. Deterrence, the peace of mutual hostage, was the era's real constitution abroad.

Cuba, Vietnam, détente, and the end

Cuba brought the doctrine home to the hemisphere: a revolution Washington read as Moscow's beachhead, an invasion that failed at the Bay of Pigs, and then the missile crisis, thirteen days in which the quarantine, the back channel, and the Jupiter trade settled what threats could not, and from which both capitals drew the hotline and the Limited Test Ban as insurance. Vietnam was the doctrine applied where its premises fit worst: the domino theory read a nationalist revolution as a Moscow-Peking maneuver, Tonkin gave the escalation a legal blank check, and Tet in 1968 broke the home front's belief before it broke the army's position. Détente managed the rivalry after Vietnam's lesson: SALT's caps, Helsinki's paper promises that dissidents weaponized, grain and trade, until Afghanistan and Reagan's counter-pressure, the evil empire named, SDI's shield proposed, military spending raised against a Soviet economy already rotting. The end came when a reformer tried to save the system by opening it: glasnost let the grievances speak, perestroika loosened the economy's commands without replacing them, Eastern Europe's peoples walked out in 1989, Solidarity's Poland first among them, and in December 1991 the Soviet Union dissolved itself. Containment's premise, that the system carried the seeds of its own decay, was vindicated by the man who tried to prune them.

The Argument of Civil Rights

Foundations: the movement before the movement

The movement's televised decade stood on fifty years of construction. The NAACP's litigation campaign climbed a ladder of graduate-school and teacher-salary cases toward Plessy itself, building precedent the way masons lay courses. The Double V generation came home from a war against racism abroad with claims the country had armed them to make, and a Black electorate was growing in the northern cities where it could not be disfranchised. Truman's committee wrote the program in To Secure These Rights, and Truman's presidency, needing those northern votes and reading the Cold War's optics, desegregated the armed forces by executive order and sent the Justice Department to argue against segregation at the Supreme Court. The church networks, the colleges, and the union locals of the Black South were the infrastructure waiting for a tactic.

Brown, and the answer it received

Brown held that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal, unanimously, in the language of social fact as much as of law. Brown II then handed enforcement to the South's own district courts at the pace of "all deliberate speed," and the answer was massive resistance: the Southern Manifesto's charge of judicial usurpation, interposition resolutions, private academies, and whole school systems, Prince Edward County's, closed for five years rather than integrated. Little Rock set the federal floor: when Governor Faubus used the National Guard to bar nine children, Eisenhower federalized the Guard and sent the 101st Airborne, and Cooper v. Aaron answered that the Constitution's meaning was the Court's to state, binding on governors who disliked it. The law had changed in 1954. The South's schools, a decade later, had barely begun to, which is why the movement left the courtroom for the street.

The movement's engine: crisis, broadcast, federal power

Nonviolent direct action was a technology with a logic: disciplined protesters, accepting assault without returning it, manufactured a crisis in a place chosen for its opponent's reliability, Bull Connor's Birmingham above all, and national television carried the crisis into the country's living rooms and the federal government's in tray. Montgomery proved the community's stamina, a year of carpool logistics run through the churches until the Court ended bus segregation. The sit-ins proved the tactic replicable by students anywhere, and made SNCC. The Freedom Rides forced the Kennedy administration to enforce its own law at the price of burned buses. Birmingham's Children's Crusade and the Letter from Birmingham Jail, the movement's fullest statement of its method and its impatience, moved Kennedy to the bill he had avoided; the March on Washington showed the coalition at its most disciplined and hopeful; the 16th Street Baptist bombing showed the price list. Selma repeated the design on voting, Bloody Sunday's footage doing in weeks what litigation had not done in years. King's assassination-year America should be read as the engine's inventor understanding its limits: it worked where a Bull Connor could be relied on and a federal government could be shamed, and neither condition held in the North.

The laws change, and the agenda turns

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 outlawed segregation in public accommodations and discrimination in employment, Title VII building the EEOC to enforce it, and survived the Senate's longest filibuster on a bipartisan cloture. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 went further into the federal structure than any statute since Reconstruction: literacy tests suspended, federal examiners registering voters, covered jurisdictions required to preclear any change in election law with Washington. Black registration in Mississippi went from under seven percent to near sixty in three years. Freedom Summer and the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party's challenge at Atlantic City taught the movement the liberal coalition's limits in the same season. Then the agenda turned where the statutes did not reach: de facto segregation in northern housing and schools, joblessness and the urban economy, Watts in 1965 and the Chicago Freedom Movement's marches into brick-throwing suburbs, Black Power's redefinition of the goal from access to power, the Poor People's Campaign, and Memphis, where King died standing with garbage workers whose demand was a union and a wage. The Kerner Commission named the structure, two societies forming, and the politics that followed, the white backlash, the southern strategy, busing's war in Boston and Detroit, Bakke's narrowing of affirmative action, is the unfinished agenda's first draft.

People of the Band

George Kennan wrote containment's first draft in the Long Telegram and spent forty years objecting to its militarization. Joseph McCarthy rode accusation to the Senate's center and was broken by his own hearings' cameras. Thurgood Marshall argued Brown as the NAACP's counsel and later sat on the Court that had decided it. Rosa Parks was a trained organizer, the Montgomery NAACP's secretary, before she was a tired seamstress in the myth. Martin Luther King Jr. gave nonviolence its American theology and its finest prose, and died widening the movement's agenda from rights to economics. Fannie Lou Hamer told the Democratic convention what Mississippi registration cost, on live television, in the plainest English of the century. John Lewis led SNCC's marchers across the Pettus Bridge and carried the movement's conscience into Congress. Lyndon Johnson spent a landslide's legislative capital on the two acts, knowing the second cost his party the South for a generation. Mikhail Gorbachev reformed the Soviet Union to save it and presided, decently and without a war, over its dissolution.
