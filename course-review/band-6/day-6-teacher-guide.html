Band 6 Teacher Guide: Depression, Recovery, and Total War

When to teach it: On the sixth day of the Course Review unit (the third band of the Modern Course Review). The day's two units are the course's most causally argued; students who can rebuild the deflation chain here will handle Band 7's containment arguments without new scaffolding.

What it pairs with: The two units it reviews, Depression and New Deal and World War II. It pairs backward with Band 5: the war that mobilizes this band's economy is the one whose home front Band 5's first unit built, and the Twenties' weak spots are this band's opening conditions.

Prior knowledge students need: Both units taught in full. No new content is introduced anywhere in the day; every fact in the packet, the sprint, and the practice set was taught in the two units. Two terms repeat inside the source units themselves: collective bargaining is printed in both the sixth and seventh lessons of Depression and New Deal, and fireside chat in both Depression and New Deal and World War II; the sprint prints each once.

Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies, grades 9 to 10: RH.9-10.1 (cite specific textual evidence), RH.9-10.2 (determine central ideas), RH.9-10.3 (analyze a series of events in sequence, including causal links), and WHST.9-10.1 (write arguments supported by evidence). C3 Framework: D2.His.13.9-12 (analyze causes and effects), D2.Eco.7.9-12 (how fiscal and monetary policy affect the economy), D2.His.14.9-12 (how actions in the past shape the present), and D2.Civ.12.9-12 (how governments respond to crises).

What students will review: The deflation spiral and Hoover's limited answer; the Hundred Days and the alphabet agencies; the critics, the Court, and the Second New Deal's structural turn; labor's rise, the twin stumbles of 1937, and the New Deal's double ledger; then neutrality's erosion, total mobilization, the two-ocean war, the Double V and internment, the Holocaust and Nuremberg, and the atomic end. The day's skill is causation: chains that can be rebuilt, and cut points that can be defended.

Timing and materials: One class day. Packet reading and timeline, about 15 minutes; term sprint, about 15 minutes; practice set, about 20 minutes; skills task in class, with Part 3 finished as homework if the period runs short. Print the packet, sprint, practice set, and skills task for every student. Nothing else is required.

How the Day Runs

Students read the packet with one instruction: mark any timeline row they cannot explain aloud. The sprint follows closed-book, then students self-check against the packet (the definitions are the guided-notes wording verbatim, so the check is exact). The practice set runs closed-packet. Read the results by lesson, not by student: each item names its lesson, so a weak item is a reteach assignment before the practice test.

Practice Set Key

Multiple choice: 1. A. 2. D. 3. B. 4. A. 5. D. 6. C. 7. A. 8. D. 9. C. 10. B. 11. A. 12. D.

13. (DP, Lesson 2) Actions, any two: the Reconstruction Finance Corporation lending federal money to banks, railroads, and states; expanded federal public works; the Federal Farm Board's price supports; his pressure on employers to hold wages and on charities and cities to carry relief; the 1932 tax increase meant to defend the budget. The refused step: direct federal relief to the unemployed, and with it the abandonment of the balanced-budget and gold-standard discipline. Roosevelt's first weeks brought the bank holiday and federal inspection of every bank, and by May, FERA's federal relief grants, the line crossed in both finance and relief.

14. (DP, Lesson 5) Long promised to cap fortunes and incomes and guarantee every family a homestead and an income, Share Our Wealth's clubs enrolling millions. Townsend promised two hundred dollars a month to every citizen over sixty, on condition it be spent within the month. Coughlin promised monetary reform against the bankers to a radio audience in the tens of millions. Together they told Roosevelt that the New Deal's recovery was too slow and its security too thin for the country's patience, and that the votes for going further, on pensions and on wealth, were already organized. The Second New Deal is unintelligible without them.

15. (DP, Lesson 7) The plan failed because it was read as what it was: an attempt to change the Court's decisions by changing its personnel, dressed as an efficiency reform for aged justices. The Senate Judiciary Committee's report called it a needless, futile, and utterly dangerous abandonment of constitutional principle, and the bill died after Justice Roberts's switch and Van Devanter's retirement had already removed its pretext. The Court changed anyway: Roberts sustained Washington's minimum wage in March 1937, the Wagner Act and Social Security followed, and Roosevelt's subsequent appointments made the New Deal's constitutional settlement permanent. The lesson students should carry: he lost the plan and won the doctrine, at the price of his political capital.

16. (WW2, Lessons 2 and 3) It had to build or convert nearly everything: shipyards and aircraft plants where none stood, synthetic rubber, Liberty ships and bombers at line speed, with Detroit's auto industry retooled entire. The builders, two named groups of: the six million women who took war jobs; the Black southerners of the second Great Migration to the shipyard and aircraft cities; Mexican and Mexican American workers in the Southwest's fields and yards; the drafted and deferred skilled men kept at the benches by the production boards. Answers that say "factories and workers" without naming who changed have missed the unit's point.

17. (WW2, Lesson 6) Victory over fascism and the Axis abroad, and victory over racism and Jim Crow at home, the Pittsburgh Courier's pairing. Advanced it, any one: Executive Order 8802 and the FEPC, won by Randolph's threatened march; Black service in widening roles, the Tuskegee Airmen and the million Black servicemen; the defense jobs themselves. Betrayed it, any one: internment of Japanese Americans on ancestry in the same war; the segregated armed forces and the blood banks' separation; the zoot suit attacks on Mexican American servicemen's communities; the FEPC's death by filibuster as the war ended.

18. (WW2, Lesson 7) The principle: individuals, including heads of state and obedient subordinates, bear personal criminal responsibility for war crimes and for crimes against humanity, and superior orders are no defense. Trials were chosen deliberately over summary execution, which several Allied leaders had contemplated, because a documented public record was the point: evidence tested in open court could not later be dismissed as victors' invention, and Eisenhower's insistence on filming the camps belongs to the same decision.

Skills Task Guidance

Part 1 key: The chain in order: (1) overproduction meets a market that cannot buy; (2) prices fall; (3) debts grow heavier as prices and incomes fall, borrowers default; (4) bank runs and failures destroy savings and credit; (5) lending freezes, sound firms fail beside unsound ones; (6) businesses cut production and payrolls; (7) unemployment reaches a quarter of the labor force and demand collapses further, feeding step 2 again. Accept 5 and 6 in either order if defended as simultaneous. Defensible cut points: at step 3 to 4, deposit insurance or a bank holiday applied in 1930 or 1931 rather than 1933 (the Federal Reserve and the Hoover administration are the agents students should name); at step 2, abandoning the gold standard earlier to reflate prices (Hoover or the Federal Reserve); at step 7, direct federal relief and work relief at 1931 scale rather than 1933 (Congress and Hoover).

Part 2: A sound middle: (2) war orders at full capacity absorb the idle plants and the unemployed, with the draft withdrawing eleven million more from the labor market; (3) wages and farm prices rise inside a controlled economy, rationing and price ceilings hold the inflation the spending would otherwise cause, and war bonds soak up the pay. The comparison's answer: scale (deficits an order of magnitude larger, sustained), compulsion (the draft and the production boards directed labor and materials rather than inviting them), and consent (war legitimized what peacetime politics had capped). Answers naming only "more money" earn half; the institutional differences are the skill.

Part 3: The verdict the evidence supports is the war's, with the New Deal's accomplishments stated as the paragraph requires: the safety net, the labor law, the insured banks, the coalition, and a floor under collapse that kept 1933 to 1937 from being worse. The 1937 recession is the hinge evidence: when federal spending ebbed, the economy relapsed, showing the recovery had been carried by the spending's level, which only the war raised high enough for long enough. Verdicts for the New Deal must answer 1937 and the 1940 unemployment figures directly to earn full credit.
