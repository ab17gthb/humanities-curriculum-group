Band 6 Review Packet: Depression, Recovery, and Total War

Directions: This packet compresses two full units, Depression and New Deal and World War II, into one review. Read the two arguments, study the timeline, and keep the packet beside you for the term sprint and the practice set. Both of those name the lesson to reopen when an answer will not come.

The Band in Brief

The Depression was a machine running in reverse: prices falling, debts growing heavier as they fell, banks dying by the thousand as frightened depositors and failing borrowers killed each other, and a quarter of the labor force idle by 1933. Hoover answered with more federal action than any peacetime president before him and a hard limit he would not cross, no direct federal relief, while the country named its shantytowns for him. Roosevelt crossed the limit in his first week, and the New Deal that followed over eight years rebuilt the federal government's relationship with ordinary life: banks inspected and insured, relief and then work relief by the million, Social Security and the Wagner Act as permanent law, a coalition that held the Democracy together for a generation. What it did not do was end the Depression. Unemployment stood in double digits when the decade closed, and the recession of 1937, made by the administration's own turn toward economy, showed how much of the recovery had been the spending itself.

The war did what the New Deal had not, by spending at ten times the scale and drafting the surplus workers into uniform. The arsenal of democracy made the United States the supplier of a Grand Alliance, the employer of last resort at home, and a two-ocean belligerent whose production and manpower decided both wars: on an Eastern Front where the Soviet Union broke the German army, and across a Pacific taken island by island under firebombing and, at the end, atomic bombs. The war also held the republic's mirror up. It opened factory gates and a Fair Employment Practices Committee to Black workers under pressure, celebrated the Double V, and imprisoned 120,000 Japanese Americans, most of them citizens, on a racial judgment the Supreme Court sustained. It liberated the camps of the Holocaust and then tried their architects at Nuremberg on a new principle, that individuals answer for the crimes of states. The country that came home in 1945, with the GI Bill in its pocket and a rival superpower across both oceans, is the subject of the next band.

Depression and New Deal asked: How did the New Deal change the relationship between Americans and their federal government, and what did it leave undone?

World War II asked: How did the Second World War transform the United States, and how far did the country live up to the democracy it claimed to defend?

The Argument of Depression and New Deal

The spiral, and Hoover's limit

Deflation was the Depression's engine: as prices fell, debts fixed in dollars grew heavier, so farmers and firms produced more into falling markets, banks that had lent against yesterday's prices failed when the loans did, and each bank failure destroyed the credit and savings that might have kept the next firm alive. Hoover understood more of this than his reputation allows. He jawboned wages, built public works, created the RFC to lend where private credit had frozen, and signed the Hawley-Smoot Tariff that strangled trade further. His limit was philosophical and absolute to him: direct federal relief would corrupt character and the Constitution alike. The Bonus Army's dispersal in the summer of 1932, veterans gassed by the Army they had served in, fixed the image, and the interregnum's four months of drift let the banking system finish collapsing before Roosevelt took the oath.

The Hundred Days, and the alphabet at work

Roosevelt's first move was confidence engineering: the bank holiday stopped the runs by stopping the banks, the Emergency Banking Act reopened the solvent under federal blessing, and the first fireside chat told depositors their money was safer in a reopened bank than under a mattress. They believed him, and deposits flowed back. The Hundred Days then ran relief, recovery, and reform through Congress together: the CCC's camps, FERA's direct relief and then the CWA's winter jobs, the PWA's dams and schools, the AAA paying farmers to produce less, the NRA's codes writing prices and wages industry by industry, the TVA remaking a river valley as public enterprise, and Glass-Steagall with the FDIC making bank deposits a federal promise. The experiments contradicted each other cheerfully, scarcity at the AAA against abundance at the NRA, and the unit teaches them as experiments, judged by their results.

The critics, the Court, and the Second New Deal

By 1935 the pressure came from the flanks: Huey Long's Share Our Wealth promising to cap fortunes and homestead every family, Townsend's two hundred dollars a month to every senior who would spend it, Coughlin's radio congregation against the bankers, and the Liberty League's moneyed horror from the right. The Supreme Court, meanwhile, voided the NRA in Schechter and the AAA in Butler, and the whole program's legal future hung on the same nine men. Roosevelt's answer, the Second New Deal, was structural where the first had been improvisational: the WPA's work relief at national scale, the Social Security Act's contributory insurance, old-age and unemployment protection funded by the payroll tax so that no future Congress could call it charity, and the National Labor Relations Act, which put the federal government behind the right to organize. Long's assassination in September 1935 removed the most dangerous critic in the middle of the turn.

Labor rising, the reckoning of 1937, and the ledger

The Wagner Act's promise was collected by a new labor movement: the CIO organized the mass-production industries the AFL had disdained, and the sit-down strike, workers occupying the plant so no strikebreakers could run it, won recognition from General Motors and United States Steel in 1937. Then the administration stumbled twice. The court-packing plan, dressed as judicial efficiency, was read by the Senate and the country as an attack on the Court's independence, and it failed even as Justice Roberts's switch made it unnecessary. And the turn toward a balanced budget, spending cut while the recovery was still federal life support, produced the Roosevelt recession of 1937 and 1938. The ledger the unit closes with is double. Changed: the safety net, labor's legal standing, bank deposits insured, Wall Street refereed, the federal government answerable for the economy's health, and a coalition of labor, cities, the South, farmers, and, increasingly, Black voters in the North. Not done: full employment, which waited for the war; the agricultural and domestic workers, largely Black and brown, written out of Social Security's first coverage; the redlined maps that steered the era's credit away from Black neighborhoods; and the Mexican repatriation drives that deported citizens with the rest.

The Argument of World War II

Neutrality's long erosion

The Neutrality Acts of the 1930s were the previous war's lesson codified: no loans, no arms, no travel on belligerent ships, lest the merchants of death drag the country in again. Roosevelt dismantled them in stages as the dictatorships advanced: cash-and-carry in 1939, destroyers for bases in 1940, Lend-Lease in March 1941 making the United States the Allies' arsenal while Germany and America fought an undeclared naval war in the Atlantic, and the Atlantic Charter stating joint war aims with Britain in August. The Four Freedoms had already told the country what the war, when it came, would be said to be for. Pearl Harbor settled the argument in a morning, and Germany's declaration of war four days later settled the second argument, which ocean came first: the Grand Alliance's answer was Germany first, and it held.

Mobilization: the arsenal and the transformed workforce

The war state outbuilt every New Deal agency in a year: the War Production Board allocated steel and rubber by directive, the Office of Price Administration rationed and froze, war bonds sold the debt to the public, and the Selective Service registered the nation's manpower and put sixteen million Americans in uniform. Production answered with the miracle the phrase arsenal of democracy had promised, Liberty ships in weeks, a bomber an hour at Willow Run, unemployment under two percent by 1943. The workforce was remade in the process: six million women in war jobs under Rosie the Riveter's poster, a second Great Migration to the shipyard and aircraft cities of the West and North, and, under the pressure of Randolph's threatened march, Executive Order 8802 and the FEPC barring discrimination in defense work. The war, not the New Deal, restored full employment, and it did it by spending at a scale peacetime politics had never permitted.

Two wars at once

In Europe the weight fell first on the Soviet Union: the Eastern Front consumed the great majority of Germany's army, and Stalingrad, where an entire German army was encircled and destroyed in the winter of 1943, was the war's hinge. The Western Allies fought up Italy, bombed Germany's cities and industry in a strategic campaign whose costs the unit does not hide, and landed in Normandy on June 6, 1944, the largest amphibious operation in history, then survived the Bulge and crossed the Rhine to VE Day. In the Pacific the navy's codebreakers made Midway possible six months after Pearl Harbor, four Japanese carriers sunk in a day, and the island-hopping advance then bypassed the strongholds and strangled the home islands by blockade and firebombing, against kamikaze desperation, toward an invasion every planner dreaded. The demand of unconditional surrender, made at Casablanca, held both coalitions together and lengthened both wars in ways the unit asks students to weigh rather than settle.

Democracy's mirror, and the reckoning

The Double V campaign named the home front's contradiction: victory over fascism abroad and over racism at home, demanded by a Black press while Black soldiers served in a segregated army. The era's gains were real and forced: the FEPC, the defense jobs, the 442nd Regimental Combat Team, Japanese Americans who volunteered from behind barbed wire to become the most decorated unit of its size in the Army. Against them stands internment: Executive Order 9066, the removal of 120,000 people, two-thirds of them citizens, on ancestry alone, sustained by the Supreme Court in Korematsu, and the Mexican American sailors and soldiers' war beside the zoot suit riots at home. What the liberators found in April 1945, at Ohrdruf and Buchenwald and Dachau, gave the war its moral floor: the Holocaust, the Nuremberg Laws' destination, six million Jews and millions of others murdered in a system the Final Solution administered. Nuremberg answered with law, individual responsibility for crimes against humanity, a principle invented for the occasion and kept ever since. And over the settlement hung the Manhattan Project's product, used twice in August 1945 on cities whose names became the argument. The GI Bill's tuition and mortgages, demobilization's twelve million homecomings, and the superpower rivalry already visible at Potsdam are the bridge to the Cold War band.

People of the Band

Herbert Hoover was an organizer of genius and a president trapped by his own philosophy, active against the Depression up to the one line he would not cross. Franklin D. Roosevelt governed by experiment and confidence, built the modern presidency's domestic half, and led the war coalition until three weeks before its European end. Huey Long built Share Our Wealth into a national movement out of Louisiana and was assassinated at the height of his leverage over the Second New Deal. Frances Perkins, the first woman to hold a cabinet office, carried Social Security through Congress as its chief architect. John L. Lewis built the CIO and used the sit-down weapon the Wagner Act had made plausible. A. Philip Randolph won the FEPC with a march he did not have to hold, the threat being the organization itself. Eleanor Roosevelt made the First Ladyship an office of its own, reporting the Depression's human cost to her husband from the road and pressing, against the administration's caution, on civil rights. Douglas MacArthur commanded the Southwest Pacific's island-hopping advance and accepted Japan's surrender on the Missouri's deck. Dwight D. Eisenhower commanded the Normandy invasion and insisted on filming the camps, so that no one could later call the evidence invention. Robert Oppenheimer directed Los Alamos and spent the rest of his life inside the argument the bomb began.

Carry Three Questions Into the Practice Set

1. Can you run the deflation chain from falling prices to failed banks in four steps, and name the point where Hoover's policy stopped and Roosevelt's began?

2. Can you explain, in one sentence each, why the war ended the Depression when the New Deal had not, using scale and the draft?

3. Can you state the Double V's two victories and give, for the war years, one fact that advanced each and one that betrayed it?
