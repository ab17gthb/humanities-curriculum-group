Band 4 Review Packet: Industry and Reform

Directions: This packet compresses two full units, The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, into one review. Read the two arguments, study the timeline, and keep the packet beside you for the term sprint and the practice set. Both of those name the lesson to reopen when an answer will not come. Three terms in this band, trust, laissez-faire, and the Sherman Antitrust Act, are defined a little differently in the two units; the sprint prints the Progressive Era wording, and the difference itself is worth noticing.

The Band in Brief

Between Appomattox and the First World War the United States became an industrial nation: railroads standardized the continent's time and markets, Bessemer steel and Rockefeller's refineries set the scale, and the corporation, pooling capital beyond any partnership's reach, became the economy's ruling form. The new order made fortunes and a working class in the same motion. Its labor force arrived from southern and eastern Europe and from Asia until exclusion closed one door; its cities stacked newcomers into tenements under machine governments; its farmers watched prices fall for twenty years and organized the largest third party of the century; and its conquest of the West finished, at Wounded Knee, the dispossession that allotment continued by paperwork. The era's defenders called the result natural law. Its critics called it a problem to be solved.

Progressivism was the solving generation's name for itself. Its method was exposure first: muckrakers in the mass magazines, settlement workers with survey data, a Social Gospel that made social conditions a religious question. Its program ran in three channels, repairing democracy with direct legislation and honest city government, regulating the corporation through a presidency grown into the nation's chief executive, and extending the vote to women after a seventy-year campaign. Its record by 1920 was real: railroads and food and banks under federal rule, the trusts sued under a revived Sherman Act, the Eighteenth and Nineteenth Amendments both in the Constitution. Its limits were also real, and they are the band's closing subject: the color line stood where the Gilded Age had built it, and much of the reform coalition preferred it there.

The Gilded Age asked: How did industrial growth transform American life, and who gained, who paid, and who resisted?

The Progressive Era asked: How did Americans respond to the problems created by industrialization, urbanization, and political corruption?

The Argument of The Gilded Age

The engines and the corporation

The railroad was the era's first big business and its template: financed by land grants and pooled capital, run on standard time, and managed by salaried professionals in systems no single owner could oversee. The corporation form, limited liability and endless life, let steel, oil, and meatpacking reach the same scale: Carnegie's vertical integration owning the process from ore to finished rail, Rockefeller's horizontal combination gathering refineries into a trust that could set the industry's terms. The system's theory came in two registers. Laissez-faire held that government which governed least let the economy's energies work; Social Darwinism gave the theory a laboratory accent, teaching that wealth was fitness made visible and relief of the poor a tampering with nature's verdict. The Sherman Act of 1890 stood on the books as the counter-argument, and in its first decade it was readier against unions than against the combinations it named.

The workers' world

Industrial work was new in kind: wage labor under a clock, in workplaces owned by strangers, at jobs deskilled by machinery and refilled from an immigration stream that employers could always widen. Organization answered in two philosophies. The Knights of Labor enrolled nearly everyone, skilled and unskilled, women and Black workers alongside white, behind a program of cooperation and reform, and collapsed after Haymarket's bomb made "labor" read as "anarchist" in the public prints. The AFL endured by narrowing: craft unions of skilled men, collective bargaining over wages and hours, no crusades. The record of the era's strikes, 1877, Homestead, Pullman, is a record of government on the other side, militia, troops, and above all the injunction, the court order that made striking a contempt of court. Child labor stood at the system's base, defended as family economics and visible in every mill photograph.

New peoples, new cities

The new immigration, from southern and eastern Europe and from Asia, remade the industrial city's population in a generation. Ellis Island processed the European stream; Angel Island detained the Asian one, and the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, nativism's first federal statute, announced that the labor movement's welcome had a color line. In the cities, the newcomers stacked into tenements whose conditions the settlement house workers, Hull House above all, documented and answered with kindergartens, baths, and labor legislation drafted from fieldwork. City government ran on the political machine, which traded jobs, contracts, naturalization papers, and Christmas turkeys for the votes that kept it in office, and skimmed graft from every contract. The machine was corrupt and it was also the immigrant city's welfare system, which is why reforming it took two generations.

The West and the color line

West of the hundredth meridian the same system ran on land: homesteads and land-grant colleges, cattle drives and mining booms, barbed wire closing the open range and dry farming gambling on rain. For Native peoples the era was the closing: the reservation system compressed into allotment under the Dawes Act, the tribal estate dissolved farm by farm and "surplus" by sale, children taken to boarding schools to be made un-Indian, and the Ghost Dance's hope of restoration answered at Wounded Knee. In the South the settlement of 1877 hardened into Jim Crow: disfranchisement by poll tax, literacy test, and grandfather clause; segregation ratified by Plessy; and Black America's argument with itself, Washington's Atlanta Compromise against the critics who would found the NAACP in the next unit. The farmers' Populism, the Omaha Platform and free silver, was the era's largest attempt to organize the losers of industrialization into a political majority, and 1896 was its funeral.

The Argument of the Progressive Era

Exposure as method

Progressivism began as an information system. The mass-circulation magazine put the muckraker's serialized exposé, Tarbell on Standard Oil, Steffens on the cities, Sinclair on the stockyards, into a national middle-class audience that had bought the industrial system's products without seeing its conditions. The settlement house supplied the same method on foot: statistics gathered tenement by tenement, then carried to legislatures as draft bills. The Social Gospel gave the method a pulpit, teaching that Christianity applied to wages and housing and not only to souls. The Jungle's harvest states the method's power and its limit in one anecdote: Sinclair aimed at the public's heart, at the packers' treatment of men, and hit its stomach; the nation got meat inspection, not labor reform.

Democracy repaired

The reform of government ran on the diagnosis that the machine and the lobby had captured the legislature, so the voters must be handed the legislature's tools: the initiative to propose laws, the referendum to judge them, the recall to remove officials, the direct primary to take nominations from the bosses. Wisconsin under La Follette made the program a system, the Wisconsin Idea, university experts drafting statutes and staffing commissions. The cities got their own versions, Galveston's commission plan and the city manager, government redesigned as administration. What the repair largely did not touch, the South's one-party disfranchisement, is the era's standing exception, and students should be able to say so plainly.

The presidency as engine

Roosevelt made the presidency the reform program's motor: the Square Deal's three parts, control of corporations, consumer protection, and conservation, pursued through a revived Sherman Act (Northern Securities), the Hepburn Act's rate power, the food and drug laws, and a conservation estate of forests, parks, and monuments under the Antiquities Act. Taft, chosen to continue the program, prosecuted more trusts and broke with the insurgents over the Payne-Aldrich Tariff and the Ballinger-Pinchot affair, until Roosevelt challenged him in 1912 and the split elected Wilson. Wilson's New Freedom then attacked the triple wall of privilege: the Underwood Tariff down, the Federal Reserve built, the Clayton Act and the FTC written, and the Adamson Act's eight-hour day for railroad men. In twenty years the federal government acquired the regulatory shape it still has.

The limits

The suffrage campaign is the era's triumph and its measure. NAWSA's Winning Plan worked state by state and Congress at once; the National Woman's Party's Silent Sentinels picketed a wartime president with his own sentences and were jailed and beaten for it, at Occoquan in the Night of Terror; and the Nineteenth Amendment closed the argument Seneca Falls had opened in 1848. But the amendment, like the Fifteenth before it, was read in the South to exclude Black women by the same machinery that excluded Black men, and the federal government of the reform era segregated its own offices. Prohibition, the Eighteenth Amendment, showed the reform method at its most confident and its most coercive. And the Immigration Act of 1917 wrote restriction into the reform record. The band's verdict is the unit's: progressivism rebuilt American government, and drew its circle, as the Gilded Age had, inside the color line.

People of the Band

Andrew Carnegie integrated steel from ore to rail and wrote that the rich man was a trustee of his surplus, a defense his workers at Homestead met with Pinkertons. John D. Rockefeller built Standard Oil into the trust the era argued about. Samuel Gompers kept the AFL narrow, durable, and focused on the contract. Eugene V. Debs went to jail for the Pullman boycott, read socialism there, and polled six percent for president in 1912. William Jennings Bryan carried the farmers' program into the Democratic Party in 1896, lost three presidential elections, and remained the prairie's tribune into the next century. Upton Sinclair wrote The Jungle to convert the nation to socialism and converted it instead to meat inspection, the era's favorite parable about reform's surprises. Jane Addams turned Hull House into the settlement movement's headquarters and its research department. Booker T. Washington offered the Atlanta Compromise, industry and patience in exchange for time, and his critics founded the NAACP against it. Ida Tarbell dismantled Standard Oil in nineteen installments of documented fact. Theodore Roosevelt made the presidency the nation's agenda-setter and conservation its estate. Robert La Follette built the Wisconsin Idea into a working state. Alice Paul took the suffrage fight to the White House fence and paid for the publicity at Occoquan.

3. Can you state the band's verdict on the color line: what the Gilded Age built, what progressivism did with it, and one fact for each half?
