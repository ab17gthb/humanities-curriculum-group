Band 4 Teacher Guide: Industry and Reform

When to teach it: On the fourth day of the Course Review unit, opening the Modern Course Review for classes on the post-1877 shape, for whom it is the first band. The day carries the review's heaviest factual load; if any band earns a second period, it is this one.

What it pairs with: The two units it reviews, The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era. The Progressive Era is the course's free sample unit, so this is also the band whose source unit many visiting teachers will know best. It pairs forward with Band 5: the war that follows in 1917 lands on the regulatory state this band builds.

Prior knowledge students need: Both units taught in full. No new content is introduced anywhere in the day; every fact in the packet, the sprint, and the practice set was taught in the two units.

Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies, grades 9 to 10: RH.9-10.1 (cite specific textual evidence), RH.9-10.2 (determine central ideas), RH.9-10.9 (compare and contrast treatments of the same topic), and WHST.9-10.1 (write arguments supported by evidence). C3 Framework: D2.His.3.9-12 (how perspectives shape interpretations), D2.His.13.9-12 (analyze causes and effects), D2.Eco.1.9-12 (how economic decisions affect individuals and groups), and D2.Civ.5.9-12 (how constitutions and laws are implemented and contested).

What students will review: Industrialization's engines and corporation form, its justifications, its labor wars, its immigrant cities, its Western and Southern settlements; and the Progressive response in its three channels, exposure, democratic repair, and presidential regulation, through the suffrage victory and the era's limits at the color line. The day's skill is continuity and change across forty years.

Timing and materials: One class day. Packet reading and timeline, about 15 minutes; term sprint, about 15 minutes; practice set, about 20 minutes; skills task in class, with Part 2 finished as homework if the period runs short. Print the packet, sprint, practice set, and skills task for every student. Nothing else is required.

How the Day Runs

Students read the packet with one instruction: mark any timeline row they cannot explain aloud. The sprint follows closed-book, then students self-check against the packet (the definitions are the guided-notes wording verbatim, so the check is exact). A note on the sprint: three terms, trust, laissez-faire, and the Sherman Antitrust Act, carry different printed definitions in the two units, and the sprint prints the Progressive Era versions, since that is where the terms do their later work. A student who remembers the Gilded Age sentence has not missed the term; the sense is the same, and the difference makes a good thirty-second conversation. The practice set runs closed-packet. Read the results by lesson, not by student: each item names its lesson, so a weak item is a reteach assignment before the practice test.

Practice Set Key

Multiple choice: 1. B. 2. C. 3. D. 4. D. 5. D. 6. A. 7. B. 8. B. 9. C. 10. B. 11. D. 12. A.

13. (GA, Lesson 2) Social Darwinism held that competition in the economy worked like natural selection: the wealthy were the fit, their fortunes the proof, and interference on behalf of the poor obstructed nature's sorting. Carnegie's argument in "Wealth" made the rich man a trustee, obliged to administer his surplus for the public good in his lifetime, in libraries and institutions rather than wages or charity. Both conclusions converged: government should not redistribute, regulate, or relieve; the order that competition produced was to be left alone, at most improved by the voluntary stewardship of its winners.

14. (GA, Lesson 5) The machine provided the immigrant city's working welfare: jobs on the public payroll and the docks, naturalization help, coal and food in a hard winter, a word with a judge, funerals and picnics, in exchange for the neighborhood's votes, delivered in blocs. Reformers called the exchange corrupt because the machine paid for those services out of the public itself, graft on every contract, franchises sold, inspectors bought, and because the favors were priced in political obedience: help with a landlord's duty to fix the stairs was the machine's gift to bestow, not the tenant's right to claim.

15. (GA, Lesson 8) The diagnosis: deflation. Since the Civil War, prices for farm goods had fallen for a generation while debts stayed fixed in dear dollars, the railroads charged what the traffic would bear, and eastern banks and the gold standard set the terms. Omaha demanded, any two: free and unlimited coinage of silver; a graduated income tax; government ownership of the railroads and the telegraph; a subtreasury or postal savings system; the direct election of senators; the secret ballot and the initiative and referendum. By 1896 silver had swallowed the program because it was the one demand that could carry a national campaign and unite farmers with western miners and urban debtors in a single word, free silver, and the Democratic nomination on a silver platform made the rest of Omaha negotiable.

16. (PE, Lesson 3) Any three: the initiative (citizens propose laws), the referendum (voters judge laws the legislature passed), the recall (voters remove officials before their terms end), the direct primary (voters, not conventions, choose the nominees). The single problem: capture. Legislatures and nominations had been bought or bossed by the machines and the corporate lobbies, so the devices route around the legislature and hand its powers to the electorate directly.

17. (PE, Lessons 4 and 5) The two quarrels: the tariff, Taft signing the Payne-Aldrich bill, lighter than promised, and calling it the best tariff ever, which the insurgents read as surrender to Aldrich's Senate; and conservation, the Ballinger-Pinchot affair, in which Taft's interior secretary reopened Alaskan coal claims and Taft fired Pinchot, Roosevelt's forester, for insubordination. Behind both stood the read of Taft himself as a retreat from the Square Deal. The consequence: Roosevelt's Progressive candidacy split the Republican vote, and Wilson won the presidency with a plurality.

18. (PE, Lesson 8) Any two, with facts: Black Americans, for whom the reform era deepened disfranchisement across the South, left lynching without a federal law, and accepted a segregated federal civil service under Wilson; the Nineteenth Amendment itself was administered in the South through the same machinery that excluded Black men. Immigrants, against whom the era's tolerance thinned to the 1917 literacy test and the restrictionist campaign that followed the war. Workers, whose core demands, union recognition and the end of the injunction, the era's courts and most of its presidents still refused. Prohibition's subjects, governed by the reform coalition's most coercive statute. A miss usually praises the era in general terms; the item demands the named group and the fact.

Skills Task Guidance

Part 1: Sound verdicts: the workplace row changed in ownership's scale (the corporation's salaried hierarchy was already built by 1877; what changed by 1917 is the regulatory and union presence around it, so either verdict scores if argued). The combinations row is the trap and the teaching point: against capital, the posture changed (Sherman revived, Clayton, the FTC); against labor, it largely did not (the injunction still ruled, Clayton's labor clauses notwithstanding), so the strong answer is split. City government changed in form and service (commission and manager plans, professional sanitation and water) while the machine's functions persisted where the immigrants still needed them. Family risk is the continuity row for most classes: no general system of compensation, insurance, or relief stood between a family and a breadwinner's injury in either year, the first state compensation laws of the 1910s being the thin exception a strong answer will name.

Part 2: Credit the paragraph that names the failed agent of change: for labor's legal position, Clayton's promise that labor was not a commodity, undone by the courts' reading; for family risk, the reform era's own preference for regulating conditions over insuring outcomes; for the color line, the reform coalition's deliberate bargain, stated plainly. Paragraphs that answer "nothing changed anywhere" have missed the task's split verdicts and should be sent back to the table.
