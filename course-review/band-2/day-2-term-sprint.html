a. the name given in 1845 to the belief that the United States was destined, by providence and by the superiority of its institutions, to expand across the continent to the Pacific, a belief its holders used to justify annexation and war

b. Henry Clay's program of a national bank, a protective tariff, and federally funded internal improvements, meant to bind the sections into one economy in which western food, southern cotton, and northern manufactures traded with each other

c. the forced removal of the Cherokee in 1838 and 1839, when some sixteen thousand people were rounded up, held in camps, and marched west in detachments through the winter, roughly a quarter of them dying on the road, and the name memory has extended to all the southeastern removals

d. the transformation of the American economy between about 1815 and 1848, when canals, steamboats, railroads, and the telegraph cut the cost and time of moving goods and news, and households that had made most of what they used began producing for distant markets and buying from them

e. the political movement of the 1820s and 1830s that celebrated the common white man, widened the vote, attacked privileged institutions such as the national bank, and held that the president, chosen by the majority, should govern energetically in the people's name

h. the Supreme Court decision of 1803 in which Chief Justice John Marshall held that the Court could not order the delivery of a judicial commission because the law granting it that power was unconstitutional

i. the policy declared by President Monroe in his annual message of December 2, 1823, holding that the American continents were closed to future European colonization and that European interference in the hemisphere would be viewed as dangerous to the United States

n. the law of May 1830 that authorized the president to offer Native nations land west of the Mississippi in exchange for their lands inside the states, the legal instrument under which the southeastern nations were driven out in the 1830s

o. the practice of filling government offices with the winning side's supporters, named by Jackson's opponents for the claim that to the victor belong the spoils, and defended by Jacksonians as rotation that kept office from hardening into property

p. the law of 1798 that made it a crime to publish false, scandalous, or malicious writing against the federal government, its Congress, or its president, under which Republican newspaper editors were tried and jailed

q. the Tariff of 1828, which raised duties on imported goods to protect northern manufacturers and which southerners denounced by that name because it raised the prices the planting states paid and invited foreign retaliation against their cotton

t. the convention held at Seneca Falls, New York, on July 19 and 20, 1848, organized by Lucretia Mott, Elizabeth Cady Stanton, and their associates, the first convention in the United States called to discuss the social, civil, and religious condition and rights of woman

a. the name Jefferson gave his election, the transfer of power in 1801 from the Federalists to the Democratic-Republicans after a bitter campaign and a deadlocked electoral vote, the first time a ruling party surrendered the government to its opponents by election

b. the tax supported public schools open to all children of a district, which reformers of Mann's generation spread across the northern states in the 1830s and 1840s as the republic's instrument for making citizens out of a diverse and mobile people

c. the treaty of 1803 by which the United States bought France's claim to Louisiana for fifteen million dollars, doubling the nation's territory in a single signature and testing what a president's treaty power could do

d. the right to vote, which state after state extended in the 1820s until nearly every white man could vote regardless of property, while women, Native Americans, and most free Black men remained excluded

e. the Supreme Court decision of 1832 in which Chief Justice Marshall held that Georgia's laws had no force within Cherokee country, a judgment Georgia defied and President Jackson declined to enforce against the state

f. the labor system of the mills at Lowell, Massachusetts, where the Boston Associates built factories, boardinghouses, and churches together and recruited young women from New England farms to work the looms under rules meant to guard their morals and their hours

g. the treaty signed at Ghent on December 24, 1814, that ended the War of 1812 by restoring the boundaries and possessions of 1811, settling none of the questions of impressment or neutral rights over which the war had been declared

h. the claim, drawn from the Kentucky Resolutions, that a state may declare a federal law void and of no force inside its borders, a doctrine later generations carried much further than its 1798 authors did in print

i. the Shoshone woman who joined the Corps of Discovery as an interpreter with her husband in 1804, carried her infant son across the continent, and whose presence and kinship opened doors, including her own people's, that the expedition could not have opened alone

j. the financial collapse of May 1837, when banks across the country suspended payment in specie, cotton prices and land values fell, and a depression followed that lasted into the 1840s and broke the presidency of Martin Van Buren

k. the amendment proposed by Representative David Wilmot in 1846, passed repeatedly by the House and blocked in the Senate, that would have barred slavery in any territory taken from Mexico, forcing the slavery question onto the nation's table three years before the gold rush

l. the resolutions of 1798 and 1799, written secretly by Madison for Virginia and Jefferson for Kentucky, which argued that the states, as parties to the Constitution's compact, could judge federal laws and declare them unconstitutional

m. the electromagnetic signaling system Samuel Morse demonstrated to Congress and opened between Washington and Baltimore in 1844, which separated the speed of news from the speed of travel for the first time in human history

n. the declaration adopted at Seneca Falls in 1848, modeled sentence by sentence on the Declaration of Independence, which held that all men and women are created equal and charged man with a history of repeated injuries and usurpations toward woman as its bill of grievances

o. a government ban on trade, the policy of the Embargo Act of 1807, which closed American ports to foreign trade entirely in the hope of forcing Britain and France to respect American neutral rights without a war

p. the settlement of 1820 that admitted Maine as a free state and Missouri as a slave state and drew a line at 36 degrees 30 minutes across the Louisiana Purchase lands, barring slavery north of it and permitting it south

q. the rule by which the House of Representatives from 1836 to 1844 tabled every antislavery petition without printing, reading, or debate, which former president John Quincy Adams, serving in the House, fought year after year as a violation of the right of petition until its repeal

r. the Army expedition of 1804 to 1806, commanded by Meriwether Lewis and William Clark, sent by President Jefferson to explore the Louisiana Purchase, find a water route toward the Pacific, and establish an American presence among the West's peoples

s. the Supreme Court decision of 1819 in which Chief Justice Marshall held that Congress could charter a bank under its implied powers and that Maryland could not tax it, because the power to tax is the power to destroy

t. the party that coalesced in 1834 from Jackson's opponents, National Republicans, Anti-Masons, and disaffected Democrats among them, named for the historic opponents of royal power and united at first chiefly by resistance to what they called executive usurpation
