Band 2 Teacher Guide: A Working Republic and Its Discontents

When to teach it: On the second day of the Course Review unit (the second of three bands in the Early Course Review). The day assumes both units were taught in the fall and winter; its comparison task leans on the Jacksonian unit's essential question, which students should have met twice by now.

What it pairs with: The two units it reviews, Early Republic and Expansion and Jackson and Reform. It pairs forward with Band 3: the nullification doctrine whose career this band traces from 1798 to 1833 is the same theory South Carolina's heirs carried to secession, and students who can tell that story here will read Band 3's first section faster.

Prior knowledge students need: Both units taught in full. No new content is introduced anywhere in the day; every fact in the packet, the sprint, and the practice set was taught in the two units.

Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies, grades 9 to 10: RH.9-10.1 (cite specific textual evidence), RH.9-10.2 (determine central ideas), RH.9-10.9 (compare and contrast treatments of the same topic), and WHST.9-10.1 (write arguments supported by evidence). C3 Framework: D2.His.2.9-12 (classify historical sources and arguments), D2.His.13.9-12 (analyze causes and effects), D2.Civ.2.9-12 (analyze the powers and limits of governments), and D2.Civ.5.9-12 (how constitutions are implemented and contested).

What students will review: The invention of the working federal government between 1789 and 1823, parties, the first transfer of power, the Louisiana Purchase and its paradox, the War of 1812 and the nationalist season, the two settlements of 1820 and 1823; and the Jacksonian widening of white male democracy with its boundaries, removal, the two Union tests, the market revolution, revival and reform, immediatist abolition, and Seneca Falls. The day's skill is comparison across eras.

Timing and materials: One class day. Packet reading and timeline, about 15 minutes; term sprint, about 15 minutes; practice set, about 20 minutes; skills task in class, with the Part 3 paragraph finished as homework if the period runs short. Print the packet, sprint, practice set, and skills task for every student. Nothing else is required.

How the Day Runs

Students read the packet with one instruction: mark any timeline row they cannot explain aloud. The sprint follows closed-book, then students self-check against the packet (the definitions are the guided-notes wording verbatim, so the check is exact). The practice set runs closed-packet. One note on the sprint: the term nullification is defined in both units; the sprint prints the Jackson and Reform version, since the doctrine belongs to that unit's crisis, and the Early Republic version is the same idea stated for 1798. Read the results by lesson, not by student: each item names its lesson, so a weak item is a reteach assignment before the practice test.

Practice Set Key

Multiple choice: 1. B. 2. C. 3. B. 4. A. 5. D. 6. B. 7. C. 8. B. 9. A. 10. C. 11. B. 12. D.

13. (ER, Lesson 1) Party spirit, prompted by the 1790s' organizing of Federalist and Republican camps that fought over Hamilton's system and over France and Britain; and permanent foreign alliances, prompted by the European war that made neutrality a daily decision. Both warnings were read back at every later party fight and alliance debate, which is the reteach point if the item is missed.

14. (ER, Lesson 4) The West was other nations' country: the Corps survived on Native diplomacy, trade, and guidance, with Sacagawea interpreting and provisioning as a Shoshone woman traveling among her own and neighboring peoples. York, enslaved by Clark, hunted, hauled, and voted in the expedition's councils and returned to slavery afterward, so the expedition's work was done in part by people the republic did not free or pay. A miss usually retells the route; the item asks about the labor and the sovereignty.

15. (ER, Lesson 7) Settled: the Monroe Doctrine declared the hemisphere closed to new European colonization, and the Missouri Compromise settled that admission crisis on paired states and a geographic line. Postponed: enforcement of the doctrine, which rested on British sea power and European distraction; and the slavery question itself, which the line merely mapped, and which returned with every new acre, as the Wilmot fight showed.

16. (JR, Lesson 5) Any two, explained rather than named: transport collapsed in cost and time as canals and railroads replaced wagons, so goods, news, and people moved differently; factory time discipline replaced task and season with the clock and the bell, Lowell's boardinghouse system its fullest form; the telegraph separated communication from transportation for the first time; interchangeable parts made repair and mass production ordinary; market production replaced household production for cloth, shoes, and food in town after town. A list of inventions without the change in daily life earns half.

17. (JR, Lesson 7) Immediatism dropped the older antislavery's gradualism and colonization, demanded emancipation now as a matter of sin and right, and organized interracial moral agitation to demand it. Southern answers, any two: the gag rule tabling petitions in the House; the post-Turner closing of ranks, emancipation debate suppressed in legislatures and slave codes tightened; mob violence against abolitionists and their presses; and the shift in proslavery argument from apology (necessary evil) to defense (positive good), which the lesson's closing section traces.

18. (JR, Lesson 8) Training ground: the abolitionist movement, where women organized, petitioned, spoke, and learned the rights argument on slavery first. Model: the Declaration of Independence, whose form the Declaration of Sentiments rewrote charge by charge. Trigger: the 1840 London antislavery convention's refusal to seat the American women delegates, which made the movement's own subordination undeniable to them. The vote was the demand they named first among the resolutions, the point students most often miss.

Skills Task Guidance

Part 1: Score cells on evidence, not phrasing. Sound entries: for suffrage, property tests still common in 1801 against their fall by 1828; for the named enemy, aristocratic and British-connected finance (and Federalist sedition law) against the Bank, the tariff's beneficiaries, and chartered privilege generally; for the economic job, Jefferson's small-government retrenchment against a presidency actively breaking the Bank and distributing offices; for slavery, Jefferson's generation apologizing for it as a dying inheritance against the 1830s' defense closing ranks and slavery's defenders holding the era's commanding heights, with removal and the gag rule as the boundary facts. Cells that answer "democracy grew" for both eras have missed the band's point: the circle widened and hardened at once.

Part 2: The first era's bank was an instrument of national construction: a fiscal arm, a uniform currency's anchor, and the government's creditor made loyal, upheld in McCulloch as implied power. Jackson's bank was the same instrument read as a private monopoly that made the rich richer and bought political influence. Full credit requires the closing turn: the institution's functions were constant, and what moved was the definition of democracy, from a system that builds national capacity to one that guards equal standing against concentrated privilege.

Part 3: The natural verdict is split: Jackson's democracy was wider in membership (the mass white male electorate) and narrower in boundary (removal, the gag rule, slavery's hardening), while Jefferson's was narrower in membership and, in the Purchase and the bank fight's precedents, surprisingly broad in what the federal government could do. Credit any argued split with one fact per era per measure, as directed; unargued verdicts ("Jackson, obviously") earn nothing beyond the facts offered.
