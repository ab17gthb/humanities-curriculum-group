# Imperialism and World War I Unit — Assignment Skeleton

_Third built unit for the Knoweth curriculum and planner site, sitting in course order between the Progressive Era and the Twenties. Modeled on the Progressive Era pilot and the Gilded Age unit. Every lesson has the same working plate so teachers can pick pieces without losing the line of the unit._

## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- Why did the United States seek power overseas, and how did the First World War change the country that fought it?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The unit runs from the expansion debates of the 1890s through the election of 1920. It opens where the Gilded Age unit's politics left off, runs alongside the Progressive Era rather than after it (the war years overlap the reform years), and ends with the turn inward that sets up the Twenties. The planner's core plan is 18 instructional days.

**The plate in this build**

1. **Reading** — 750 to 900 words with five questions and a teacher key
2. **Guided notes** — keyed to the reading and lesson, with the vocabulary set verbatim and a teacher key
3. **Activity** — stations, jigsaw, map work, simulation, debate, or structured chart work in the locked print design
4. **Notebook page** — a single organizer students keep
5. **Quick check** — a short formative tied to the blueprint
6. **Writing task** — a paragraph or short essay with a scaffold and a teacher key or rubric

TPSP source analyses and photo sets are linked where the audited bank already carries them. They are not rebuilt merely to fill a folder.

**Six state-neutral era topics**

1. Imperial expansion and its critics
2. The United States in Asia and the Pacific
3. The United States in the Caribbean and Latin America
4. The First World War: causes, combat, and mobilization
5. The home front: liberty, labor, race, and migration
6. Peace, the League fight, and the turn inward

## Lesson 1 — The Turn Overseas

**Lesson question:** Why did the United States look overseas for territory, markets, and influence in the 1890s?

**Reading scope:** The closing-frontier argument of Frederick Jackson Turner, Alfred Thayer Mahan and the new steel navy, the search for markets for surplus production, missionary and racial ideas about American mission, the Hawaiian annexation drive, and the Cuban rebellion against Spain as the crisis that turned talk into war. The reading treats expansion as an argument Americans made, not a fate, and names the anti-expansion voices from the start.

**Activity concept:** Motive stations. Students work through a Mahan passage on sea power, the Turner frontier thesis, a coaling-station map of the Pacific, a missionary appeal, and an anti-expansion statement, then rank the motives by the evidence and defend the ranking.

**Vocabulary:** imperialism, navalism, coaling station, annexation, jingoism, frontier thesis, protectorate

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP Beveridge "March of the Flag" (1898) — **LINK**.

## Lesson 2 — The Spanish-American War

**Lesson question:** Was the war with Spain fought to free Cuba, to take an empire, or both?

**Reading scope:** The Maine explosion and the yellow press, the Teller Amendment's promise, Dewey at Manila Bay, the fighting in Cuba and the Rough Riders, and the Treaty of Paris terms that handed the United States Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines while Cuba became a protectorate under the Platt Amendment. The reading keeps the war's short length and lopsided cost in view, including the far larger death toll from disease.

**Activity concept:** Headline and evidence lab. Students compare yellow-press front pages with the Maine inquiry, the Teller Amendment, and the Treaty of Paris, then sort each claim about the war into liberation, conquest, or both, with the document that supports the sort.

**Vocabulary:** yellow journalism, USS Maine, Teller Amendment, Rough Riders, Treaty of Paris, cession, Platt Amendment

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP "Remember the Maine" cartoon, Harper's Weekly coverage, and "Our Victorious Fleets" illustration — **LINK**.

## Lesson 3 — An Empire in the Philippines

**Lesson question:** Did taking the Philippines extend American ideals or contradict them?

**Reading scope:** The annexation debate in the Senate, the Anti-Imperialist League and its unlikely coalition, Emilio Aguinaldo and the Philippine Republic, McKinley's Benevolent Assimilation proclamation, the Philippine-American War and its methods and costs, the Insular Cases, and the civil government that followed. The reading gives Filipino resistance and Filipino deaths their full weight rather than treating the islands as a prize handed over in Paris.

**Activity concept:** Annexation debate simulation. Students take the parts of an expansionist senator, an anti-imperialist senator, Aguinaldo, a League organizer, and an American soldier writing home, argue from their documents, and the class votes the question the Senate faced in 1899.

**Vocabulary:** Philippine-American War, anti-imperialism, Emilio Aguinaldo, guerrilla warfare, Benevolent Assimilation, Insular Cases, civil government

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP American Anti-Imperialist League Platform — **LINK**.

## Lesson 4 — Open Doors and the Big Stick

**Lesson question:** How did the United States project power in Asia and Latin America without ruling formal colonies there?

**Reading scope:** Hay's Open Door notes and the Boxer Rebellion in China, the Gentlemen's Agreement with Japan, the Panama revolution and canal, the Roosevelt Corollary and its use in the Dominican Republic and elsewhere, and Taft's dollar diplomacy. The reading compares the instruments, trade notes, loans, warships, and a canal, and asks what each cost the peoples on the receiving end.

**Activity concept:** Instruments of power stations. Students classify an Open Door note, a Boxer Rebellion account, a Panama map, the Roosevelt Corollary passage, and a dollar-diplomacy cartoon by instrument, then judge which instrument changed the most on the ground.

**Vocabulary:** Open Door, sphere of influence, Boxer Rebellion, Roosevelt Corollary, dollar diplomacy, Panama Canal, Gentlemen's Agreement

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP material on China and the canal era where the bank carries it — **LINK**.

## Lesson 5 — Neutrality Tested

**Lesson question:** Why did the United States enter the First World War in 1917 after three years of declared neutrality?

**Reading scope:** The alliance system and the July crisis in brief, Wilson's neutrality and the rights of neutral trade, the Lusitania and the Arabic and Sussex pledges, the preparedness fight and the election of 1916, Germany's return to unrestricted submarine warfare, the Zimmermann Telegram, the Russian Revolution, and Wilson's war message of April 1917. The reading builds the chain link by link so students can argue about which link was decisive.

**Activity concept:** Chain-to-war evidence lab. Students place the Lusitania notes, the Sussex Pledge, the Zimmermann Telegram, a preparedness poster, and the war message on one timeline, then identify the link they judge decisive and defend the choice against the strongest alternative.

**Vocabulary:** neutrality, Lusitania, unrestricted submarine warfare, Sussex Pledge, Zimmermann Telegram, contraband, preparedness

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP Zimmermann Telegram (full text) and Wilson's War Message to Congress — **LINK**.

## Lesson 6 — Americans in the War

**Lesson question:** How did the United States mobilize, and what did its soldiers meet in France?

**Reading scope:** The Selective Service draft and the raising of the American Expeditionary Forces, convoys and the U-boat fight, training and Pershing's insistence on an independent army, trench warfare and the new weapons, St. Mihiel and the Meuse-Argonne, the segregated service of Black soldiers and the labor battalions, the influenza pandemic that moved with the armies, and the armistice of November 11, 1918.

**Activity concept:** Mobilization system stations. Students trace a draftee from registration to the Argonne through a draft record, a convoy diagram, a trench map, a soldier's letter, and a camp influenza report, then chart how the parts depended on one another.

**Vocabulary:** conscription, American Expeditionary Forces, convoy system, trench warfare, armistice, Doughboys, influenza pandemic

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP Wilfred Owen, "Dulce et Decorum Est," and the Indian sepoy letter for the allied trench experience — **LINK**.

## Lesson 7 — The Home Front

**Lesson question:** How did the war change American life at home, and who gained and who lost by the change?

**Reading scope:** The War Industries Board and the management of production, Liberty Bonds and the draft of money, the Committee on Public Information and official persuasion, the Espionage and Sedition Acts and the prosecution of dissent from Schenck to Debs, anti-German hysteria, women's war work and the suffrage argument it strengthened, the Great Migration of Black southerners to northern war industry, and the labor unrest the war years stored up.

**Activity concept:** Liberty and security stations. Students weigh a Committee on Public Information poster, the Espionage Act's key clauses, a Schenck case summary, a Liberty Bond appeal, and a Great Migration account, then render a verdict on where the government crossed a line, with the document that proves it.

**Vocabulary:** Liberty Bonds, War Industries Board, Committee on Public Information, Espionage Act, Sedition Act, Great Migration, Schenck v. United States

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP Flagg "I Want You" poster, Hopps "Destroy This Mad Brute" poster, and Scott, "Negro Migration During the War" (1920) — **LINK**.

## Lesson 8 — Wilson's Peace and the Turn Inward

**Lesson question:** Why did the United States refuse the League of Nations it had proposed, and what did the country turn toward in 1920?

**Reading scope:** The Fourteen Points and what survived of them at Paris, the Treaty of Versailles and its reparations and war-guilt clauses, Lodge and the reservationists against Wilson's all-or-nothing stand, the speaking tour, the stroke, and the Senate votes, the strikes and race violence of 1919 and the Red Scare and Palmer raids, and the election of 1920 and Harding's normalcy as the bridge into the Twenties.

**Activity concept:** Treaty fight hearing. Students staff a Senate hearing with Wilson's Fourteen Points, the Versailles clauses, Lodge's reservations, and an irreconcilable's speech, draft the compromise each side could have taken, and compare it with the votes that actually killed the treaty.

**Vocabulary:** Fourteen Points, League of Nations, Treaty of Versailles, reparations, Lodge reservations, Red Scare, normalcy

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP Fourteen Points and Treaty of Versailles excerpts — **LINK**.

## Changes from the working map

- The unit is dated from the 1890s to 1920 rather than 1898 to 1919 so the Hawaiian and Cuban run-up and the 1920 election both sit inside the frame the lessons actually teach.
- Entry 8 carries a deliberate double mapping in the topic table: mobilization is a war topic for the draft and the AEF and a home-front topic for production and finance, and the planner's entry-to-topic data must take both (entry 8 maps to topics 4 and 5). The row appears twice in this table only to make that reasoning visible; the entry itself is one entry.
- Imperialism is taught as a debate from Lesson 1 onward rather than as a settled good or ill: the expansionists and their critics share every lesson's evidence, and the writing tasks grade the handling of both sides.
