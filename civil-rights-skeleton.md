# The Civil Rights Unit: Assignment Skeleton

_Ninth built unit for the Knoweth curriculum and planner site, sitting in course order after The Cold War and before Modern America. Modeled on the Progressive Era pilot, the Gilded Age, Imperialism and World War I, the Twenties, the Depression and New Deal, the World War II, and the Cold War units. Every lesson has the same working plate so teachers can pick pieces without losing the line of the unit._

## How to read this

Status marks:

- **HAVE**: an audited source or image already exists in the TPSP bank
- **BUILD**: create for this unit
- **LINK**: use the TPSP bank rather than rebuilding it

## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- How did the civil rights movement change American law and American life, and what did it leave unfinished?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's core plan is 18 instructional days. The unit is the African American freedom struggle as its core arc, running from the postwar foundations of the late 1940s to the movement's transformations and the nation's answers through the 1970s. The Cold War unit deliberately reserved this unit's ground: its home-front lesson owns McCarthyism and the Second Red Scare, and it left Executive Order 9981, the Emmett Till record, and the Little Rock records (Eisenhower's address, the Faubus statement, Executive Order 10730) in its era file for this unit, which takes them up here. The World War II unit taught the Double V campaign and Executive Order 8802 as wartime material; this unit opens by inheriting them. The unit closes with the Fair Housing Act, the Kerner verdict, busing, affirmative action, and Bakke, the movement's victories and the unfinished agenda stated together. What came after, the conservative ascendancy and the post-1980 settlement, belongs to Modern America, the final modern unit. The Till material and all movement violence are handled with documentary sobriety throughout: verified sources, exact captions, no sensationalism, and no atrocity image used as decoration. The Jet magazine photographs of Emmett Till are copyrighted magazine photographs; the audited bank record describes them without reproducing them, and this unit teaches them the same way, as a described record, stating plainly in the student materials that the photographs themselves are not reproduced and why Mamie Till-Mobley's decision to publish them changed the country anyway.

**The plate in this build**

1. **Reading**: 850 to 900 words with five questions and a teacher key
2. **Guided notes**: keyed to the reading and lesson, with the vocabulary set verbatim and a teacher key
3. **Activity**: stations, jigsaw, map work, simulation, debate, or structured chart work in the locked print design
4. **Notebook page**: a single organizer students keep
5. **Quick check**: a short formative tied to the blueprint
6. **Writing task**: a paragraph or short essay with a scaffold and a teacher key or rubric

TPSP source analyses and photo sets are linked where the audited bank already carries them. They are not rebuilt merely to fill a folder.

**Six state-neutral era topics**

1. The long groundwork: Jim Crow and the institutions that fought it
2. The courts and the Constitution: law as a movement weapon
3. Mass protest: nonviolent direct action and its makers
4. The federal government: ally, referee, and reluctant enforcer
5. The movement divides and widens: region, class, and visions of freedom
6. Legacy: what changed, what endured, what was left undone

## Lesson 1: Foundations: The Movement Before the Movement

**Lesson question:** What had Black Americans already built, and already won, before the movement's famous decade began?

**Reading scope:** The system first, stated plainly: Jim Crow as law and custom across the South, segregation in schools, transport, and public life, and disfranchisement by poll tax, literacy test, white primary, and violence, the settlement Plessy had blessed in 1896. The institutions the system could not close: the Black church, the Black college, the Black press, and the NAACP, founded in 1909, which had been fighting in the courts for decades and had already won cases against the white primary and restrictive covenants. The war's inheritance: a million Black servicemen, the Double V campaign's demand for victory over fascism abroad and racism at home, Executive Order 8802's fair employment precedent, and the Great Migration's second wave, which moved Black voters into northern cities where their ballots counted in national elections. The first federal steps: Truman's Committee on Civil Rights and its 1947 report To Secure These Rights, which named lynching, disfranchisement, and segregation as federal business; the president's 1948 civil rights message to Congress, which split his party and sent the Dixiecrats out of the convention; and Executive Order 9981 of July 26, 1948, ordering equality of treatment in the armed forces, carried out through the Korean War until the military became the most integrated institution in American life. The reading closes with A. Philip Randolph, who appears twice in this groundwork, threatening a march in 1941 and threatening a draft boycott in 1948, as the standing proof that the movement's central tactic, organized mass pressure, was older than its famous decade.

**Activity concept:** The groundwork file. Students work through five records, a To Secure These Rights passage, the Executive Order 9981 text, a Double V editorial record drawn from the Black press, a record of the NAACP's early court victories, and a Great Migration record, and for each one name what it built, a law, an institution, a constituency, or a tactic, then judge which piece of groundwork the later movement could least have done without.

**Vocabulary:** Jim Crow, segregation, disfranchisement, NAACP, Double V campaign, To Secure These Rights, Executive Order 9981

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP To Secure These Rights excerpt and Executive Order 9981, audited records (the order carried in the Cold War era file). **LINK** / **HAVE**. The Jim Crow, segregation, and disfranchisement definitions are carried verbatim from the Gilded Age unit, and the Double V definition verbatim from the World War II unit, as recorded in this unit's build spec.

## Lesson 2: Brown and the Massive Resistance

**Lesson question:** How did the NAACP turn a Kansas school case into the end of legal segregation, and how did the South answer?

**Reading scope:** The legal strategy first: Charles Hamilton Houston's plan to attack segregation where it was most visibly unequal, graduate and professional schools first, winning Sweatt and McLaurin in 1950, and Thurgood Marshall carrying the campaign to the elementary schools themselves. The case: five cases joined under the Kansas one, the Topeka parents and Linda Brown, argued and reargued on the question whether segregation itself, whatever the facilities, did constitutional harm, and the Warren Court's answer of May 17, 1954, unanimous, that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal and that segregation in public schools violates the Fourteenth Amendment. The delay built into the victory: Brown II in 1955 ordering desegregation with all deliberate speed, a phrase with no deadline in it, which southern officials read as permission to wait. The answer organized: massive resistance, the Byrd machine's program of defiance, the Southern Manifesto of 1956 signed by 101 members of Congress calling the decision an abuse of judicial power, pupil placement laws, private school schemes, and whole districts, Prince Edward County in Virginia first among them, closing their public schools rather than integrating them. The Court's reply: Cooper v. Aaron in 1958, arising from Little Rock, holding that state officials are bound by the Court's reading of the Constitution and that no governor or legislature may nullify it. The reading closes on the gap the decade exposed: ten years after Brown, barely one Black child in a hundred in the deep South attended school with white children, a verdict on what a court order alone could and could not move.

**Activity concept:** The case file. Students work five records, a Brown opinion passage, a Brown II passage, the Southern Manifesto passage, a Cooper v. Aaron passage, and a record of the Prince Edward County school closings, and build the argument chain in order: what the Court claimed, what it declined to order, what the resistance built in the space between, and what the Court answered, then judge whether the delay was the price of unanimity or a separate failure.

**Vocabulary:** Plessy v. Ferguson, separate but equal, Brown v. Board of Education, Brown II, massive resistance, Southern Manifesto, Cooper v. Aaron

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Brown v. Board, Brown II, Southern Manifesto, and Cooper v. Aaron, audited records in the Civil Rights era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**. The Plessy definition is carried verbatim from the Gilded Age unit.

## Lesson 3: Montgomery and Little Rock: The Movement Finds Its Tactics

**Lesson question:** How did a bus boycott and nine schoolchildren teach the movement its methods, and force the federal government to act?

**Reading scope:** Two tactics discovered in sequence. First Montgomery: Jo Ann Robinson's letter of May 1954 warning the mayor that a boycott was coming, Rosa Parks's arrest on December 1, 1955, the Women's Political Council's leaflets overnight, and the Montgomery Improvement Association under a twenty-six-year-old pastor, Martin Luther King Jr., sustaining a boycott of 381 days through carpools, church networks, mass meetings, arrests, and a bombed house, until the Supreme Court in Browder v. Gayle ordered the buses desegregated. The boycott's lessons stated as method: economic pressure, an institution of one's own to run it, a moral claim stated in the language of the Constitution and the church, and cameras watching. Then the summer's wound that stands behind the tactic's urgency: Emmett Till, fourteen, visiting Mississippi from Chicago, murdered in August 1955, his killers acquitted by an all-white jury in an hour and then confessing to a magazine for money; his mother Mamie Till-Mobley holding an open-casket funeral in Chicago and letting Jet publish the photographs, so that tens of thousands filed past the body and the nation saw what the jury had excused. The record is taught here as the bank carries it, described and not reproduced, because the photographs are copyrighted magazine images, and the lesson says so. Then Little Rock: Governor Faubus posting the Arkansas National Guard to bar nine Black students from Central High in September 1957, the mob around Elizabeth Eckford, the students withdrawn, and President Eisenhower, who had never endorsed Brown, deciding that a governor's defiance of a federal court was the one thing he could not let stand; his address to the nation, Executive Order 10730 federalizing the Guard and sending the 101st Airborne, and a school year spent under bayonets. Faubus's own statement, pleading bloodshed and local order, is read beside Eisenhower's. The reading closes with Faubus closing Little Rock's high schools entirely for the 1958 and 1959 year, the Lost Year, as the resistance's answer to the troops.

**Activity concept:** The tactics ledger. Students work five records, the Robinson letter, a Montgomery diary record from the boycott's first weeks, the Till photograph record as the bank describes it, Eisenhower's Little Rock address passage, and the Faubus statement with Executive Order 10730, and enter for each the tactic or power it shows, boycott, witness, court order, troops, defiance, then judge which of the five moved the country most and defend the judgment from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** Montgomery bus boycott, nonviolent direct action, Southern Christian Leadership Conference, Emmett Till, Little Rock Nine, Executive Order 10730, boycott

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Robinson letter, Rustin Montgomery diary, Parks fingerprint card and reflections, Till photograph record, Eisenhower Little Rock address, Faubus statement, and Executive Order 10730, audited records (the Little Rock records carried in the Cold War era file, the Till record likewise). **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 4: Sit-Ins and Freedom Rides: The Students Take the Lead

**Lesson question:** How did students a decade younger than the movement's leaders change its pace, its tactics, and its demands?

**Reading scope:** February 1, 1960 first: four freshmen at North Carolina A&T sitting down at a Greensboro Woolworth lunch counter, refused service, returning with more students each day, and the tactic running through the South in weeks, Nashville's students trained in nonviolence workshops by James Lawson, sitting in, beaten, arrested, and filling the jails rather than posting bail. The organization the moment made: the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, founded at Shaw University in April 1960 under Ella Baker's guidance, committed to local leadership and to staying in the hardest places. The movement's moral arithmetic stated by its practitioners: Diane Nash asking Nashville's mayor on the courthouse steps whether he believed it was wrong to discriminate against a person by the color of his skin, and the mayor's answer opening the counters. Then the buses: CORE's Freedom Rides of 1961, interracial teams riding into the deep South to test Boynton v. Wright's order desegregating interstate terminals, the bus burned at Anniston, the beatings in Birmingham and Montgomery, riders arrested in Jackson and sent to Parchman prison, and the Kennedy administration trading quiet protection for cooling-off periods until the Interstate Commerce Commission ordered the terminals desegregated that fall. The limit learned next: Albany, Georgia, 1961 and 1962, where Police Chief Laurie Pritchett studied the movement's method and answered it with polite mass arrests, no cameras' worth of violence, and jail space borrowed from every county around, so that King, jailed and then released against his will, left without a victory. The reading closes with the lesson the students carried forward: protest worked when it created a crisis the federal government could not ignore, and Albany showed what happened when it did not.

**Activity concept:** The crisis file. Students work five records, a Greensboro sit-in record, a Nashville workshop record drawn from Lawson's teaching as the bank and the public record carry it, a Freedom Ride record, the ICC order record of September 1961, and an Albany record, and for each one state the crisis the protesters tried to create, the answer it drew, and whether the federal government was moved, then judge which record shows the tactic working and which shows it failing, and why.

**Vocabulary:** sit-in, Greensboro sit-in, Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, Freedom Rides, Congress of Racial Equality, Albany Movement, de jure segregation

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Jesús Colón memoir record for the northern student's-eye view where it fits, and the era file's photograph records. **LINK** where the bank carries a record; several of this lesson's records are public documents and contemporaneous public records (the ICC order, the Boynton decision) built for this unit with editions named. **BUILD**.

## Lesson 5: Birmingham and the March on Washington

**Lesson question:** How did Birmingham force the nation's hand, and what did the March on Washington ask the nation to become?

**Reading scope:** The city chosen on purpose: Birmingham, the most segregated city in America, where the SCLC and Fred Shuttlesworth's Alabama movement aimed Project C, for confrontation, at Bull Connor's police in the spring of 1963. The letter: King arrested on Good Friday and answering eight white clergymen from a cell, in the Letter from Birmingham Jail, arguing that injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere, that an unjust law is no law at all, and that the white moderate who preferred order to justice was the greater obstacle, quoted here within the copyright cap the unit keeps for King's words, counted and disclosed. The children: after the adult campaign stalled, James Bevel's Children's Crusade of May 1963 sending thousands of schoolchildren into the streets, where Connor answered with fire hoses and police dogs before the cameras, and Birmingham's businessmen settled within the week. The president moved: Kennedy's address of June 11, 1963, calling civil rights a moral issue as old as the scriptures and as clear as the Constitution, and sending Congress the bill that became the Civil Rights Act; the same night, Medgar Evers murdered in his driveway in Jackson. The march: August 28, 1963, a quarter of a million people at the Lincoln Memorial for jobs and freedom, organized by Rustin and Randolph, the movement's demands read into the record, John Lewis's censored speech, and King's address closing it, casting the movement's claim as a promissory note written in the Declaration and the Constitution, quoted within the cap. The answer from the other side, eighteen days later: the bombing of the 16th Street Baptist Church on a Sunday morning, killing Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley, Carole Robertson, and Carol Denise McNair. The reading closes with the bill still stalled in Congress and the movement counting what the year had cost and what it had not yet bought.

**Activity concept:** The pressure file. Students work five records, a Letter from Birmingham Jail passage within the cap, Kennedy's June 11 address passage, a Children's Crusade record, the March on Washington's official program and demands record, and a 16th Street Baptist Church bombing record drawn from the public record, and map the chain of pressure in order: what each event added, what it cost, and who it moved, then judge whether Birmingham or the march did more to move the federal government, and answer the strongest counterclaim.

**Vocabulary:** Birmingham campaign, Letter from Birmingham Jail, Children's Crusade, Kennedy's civil rights address, March on Washington, I Have a Dream, 16th Street Baptist Church bombing

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Letter from Birmingham Jail and I Have a Dream (both copyrighted; the unit quotes each within a 90-word total cap, counted and disclosed wherever they appear), Kennedy's June 11, 1963 address, the March on Washington program and photograph records, audited records in the Civil Rights era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 6: The Laws Change: 1964 and 1965

**Lesson question:** How did the movement turn protest into law, and what could the two great acts actually reach?

**Reading scope:** The legislative fight first: Kennedy's bill stalled at his death, and Johnson, who knew the Senate as few presidents have, staking his presidency on passing it whole, the House voting in February 1964 and the southern filibuster running sixty working days in the Senate until cloture, two thirds of the Senate voting for the first time in the century to close debate on a civil rights bill, and the Civil Rights Act signed on July 2, 1964, banning discrimination in public accommodations and employment and letting the government cut funds to segregated programs. The summer that tested the country's patience alongside it: Freedom Summer in Mississippi, a thousand northern volunteers, the murders of Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner in June, and the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party's challenge at Atlantic City in August, Fannie Lou Hamer testifying to the credentials committee on national television and the party offered two seats as a compromise it refused. Then the vote itself: Selma, where Sheriff Jim Clark's county had registered almost no Black voters though Black residents were half its population, the campaign through the winter of 1965, Jimmie Lee Jackson killed in Marion, and Bloody Sunday on March 7, John Lewis's skull fractured on the Edmund Pettus Bridge before the television cameras. The president answering in the movement's own words: Johnson's address of March 15, 1965, We Shall Overcome, asking Congress for the voting bill as a matter of the American promise. The march completed under federal protection, and the Voting Rights Act of August 6, 1965, suspending literacy tests, sending federal examiners to register voters, and requiring covered states to clear any voting change with Washington first. The reading closes with the arithmetic of enforcement: a quarter of a million new Black voters registered by the end of 1965, Mississippi's Black registration rising from under seven percent toward sixty in three years, and the two acts standing as the movement's legislative monument, reached by different tools, protest outside the Capitol and mastery inside it.

**Activity concept:** The statute file. Students work five records, a Civil Rights Act passage, a Title VII passage, Johnson's We Shall Overcome passage, a Voting Rights Act passage, and Johnson's Howard University passage on equality as a result, and for each one name the power it creates, who holds it, and what it cannot reach, then judge which single provision did the most work in the decade after, and defend the judgment from the texts.

**Vocabulary:** Civil Rights Act of 1964, filibuster, Title VII, Freedom Summer, Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, Bloody Sunday, Voting Rights Act of 1965

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Civil Rights Act, Title VII, Voting Rights Act, We Shall Overcome address, Howard University address, and the E. Jackson letter, audited records in the Civil Rights era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 7: The Movement Turns North and Inward: Chicago, Black Power, Memphis

**Lesson question:** Why did the movement's methods stop working as it moved north, and how did its own people argue about what freedom required next?

**Reading scope:** Five days first: the Voting Rights Act signed on August 6, 1965, and Watts in Los Angeles burning from August 11, thirty four dead, a warning that the movement's southern legal targets were not the whole of American racism. The northern problem named: de facto segregation, no statute to strike down, built instead from housing covenants, redlining, white flight, and school lines drawn around neighborhoods, so that King took the movement to Chicago in 1966, rented a tenement flat, and marched into Marquette Park to be met by mobs carrying swastikas and bricks, one of which struck him, and came away with a Summit Agreement on open housing that the city did not enforce. The argument inside the movement: in June 1966, on the Meredith March in Mississippi, Stokely Carmichael, jailed for the twenty seventh time, raising the call of Black Power, SNCC and CORE moving toward Black leadership and away from interracial liberal coalition, and King refusing the slogan while understanding its source, a decade of nonviolence answered with bombs and acquittals. Malcolm X stands behind the argument as its earlier voice, assassinated in February 1965 after breaking with the Nation of Islam, his Autobiography and his ballot or the bullet speech carrying a nationalist case, quoted within the cap, that integration into a burning house was no freedom. The Panthers: Oakland, October 1966, Huey Newton and Bobby Seale founding the Black Panther Party, patrolling the police with law books and rifles, then building breakfast programs and health clinics that the FBI worked to destroy. The turn to economics: King's last campaign, the Poor People's Campaign, an interracial army of the poor to camp in Washington and demand an economic bill of rights, planned through the winter of 1967 and 1968. Memphis: the sanitation workers on strike from February 1968 under signs reading I Am a Man, King coming to support them, a march broken by violence, his return determined to prove a peaceful march possible, the Mountaintop speech of April 3, and the shot on the balcony of the Lorraine Motel on April 4, 1968. The reading closes with the uprisings in more than a hundred cities in the week after, and the Kerner Commission's verdict taken up in the next lesson: the nation moving toward two societies.

**Activity concept:** The argument file. Students work five records, a Ballot or the Bullet passage within the cap, a Black Power record from the Meredith March as the public record carries it, a Panthers' Ten-Point Program passage, the I Am a Man poster record from the bank, and a Kerner Commission passage, and sort the records by the vision of freedom each one carries, integration, nationalism, economic transformation, self-defense, then judge which vision the decade's evidence supported best, and answer the strongest counterclaim from the documents. TPSP premium DBQ number 22, on the visions of freedom, is the natural extension of this lesson and is named in the writing task rather than rebuilt.

**Vocabulary:** de facto segregation, Chicago Freedom Movement, Watts uprising, Black Power, Black Panther Party, Poor People's Campaign, Memphis sanitation strike

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Ballot or the Bullet (copyrighted; quoted within a 90-word total cap, counted and disclosed), Autobiography of Malcolm X excerpt, Memphis poster record, and Kerner Commission introduction, audited records in the Civil Rights era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 8: Victory, Backlash, and the Unfinished Agenda

**Lesson question:** What did the movement win, what answered it, and what was still undone when its famous decade ended?

**Reading scope:** The last statute first: in the week after King's assassination, with Washington still smelling of smoke from the uprisings, Congress passed the Fair Housing Act, the Civil Rights Act of 1968, banning discrimination in the sale and rental of most housing, the third great act and the one whose enforcement was written weakest. The diagnosis on the table beside it: the Kerner Commission's report of 1968, appointed by Johnson to explain the uprisings and reporting instead that white racism had built the ghetto and that the nation was moving toward two societies, one black, one white, separate and unequal, a report the president who commissioned it could not accept and whose recommendations were shelved. The movement's institutional harvest stated in numbers: Black elected officials rising from a few hundred in 1965 toward five thousand by 1970 and beyond, Black mayors in Cleveland, Gary, and then Atlanta and Detroit, the Congressional Black Caucus founded in 1971, and a Black middle class enlarged by the opened professions. The backlash organized in parallel: George Wallace's campaigns carrying five states in 1968 on segregation's afterlife and then a national politics of law and order, the southern strategy courting white voters who felt the movement had gone too far, and the courts becoming the contested ground, Swann in 1971 upholding busing to desegregate Charlotte's schools and Milliken in 1974 stopping Detroit's desegregation at the city line, so that schools could resegregate inside metropolitan borders the law would not cross. The widening the movement taught: its tactics, language, and legal tools taken up by Mexican American, Native American, Asian American, women, and gay Americans, the Delano grape strike standing in this unit's bank as the bridge, Cesar Chavez's movement learning openly from King. The argument over remedy: affirmative action, from the Philadelphia Plan of 1969 to Bakke in 1978, where the Court struck down a quota and let race stand as one factor among others, the argument about whether formal equality was enough, first posed in Johnson's Howard University address, still running. The reading closes the unit by returning to its question: the movement destroyed a legal system of racial caste in a decade, the fastest moral transformation in the nation's history, and it left wealth, housing, and schools substantially where law alone could not move them, which is the agenda the next era inherited.

**Activity concept:** The reckoning file. Students work five records, a Fair Housing Act passage, a Kerner Commission passage, a Swann or Milliken record from the public record, a Bakke passage, and the Johnson Howard University passage revisited, and sort each record under won, answered, or undone, with the evidence for the sorting, then write the unit's closing verdict: the strongest single claim the documents support about what the movement changed and what it did not.

**Vocabulary:** Kerner Commission, Fair Housing Act, white backlash, southern strategy, busing, affirmative action, Bakke decision

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Kerner Commission introduction, Howard University address, and the RFK Delano records, audited records in the Civil Rights era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**. The Fair Housing Act text, the Swann and Milliken holdings, and the Bakke opinion are public documents and court records, built for this unit with editions named.

## Changes from the working map

- The unit's core arc is the African American freedom struggle, as briefed, with the movement's own institutions (the church, the college, the Black press, the NAACP) carrying Lesson 1 rather than a presidential frame, because the movement made its leaders more often than presidents made the movement, and the groundwork explains why the famous decade could happen at all.
- Executive Order 9981 sits in Lesson 1 and the Little Rock records sit in Lesson 3, per the boundary the Cold War unit set: that unit reserved them here, and this unit honors the reservation in the same places its frame names them.
- The Emmett Till record stands in Lesson 3 beside Montgomery and Little Rock rather than in a lesson of its own, because its classroom weight is the nation's education in what the tactic of witness was answering, and because the record the bank carries is a described photograph, best taught inside the year that made it impossible to ignore, 1955 to 1957.
- Court-packing style boundary calls, recorded for honesty: the Selma campaign is taught in Lesson 6 with the legislation it produced rather than in Lesson 5 with the protest sequence, because the Voting Rights Act is the lesson's subject and Selma is its cause; Albany stands in Lesson 4 with the Freedom Rides, where the students learned its lesson, rather than with King's campaigns in Lesson 5.
- The unit closes in the 1970s with Bakke rather than in 1968, because the unfinished agenda is the unit's final subject and Bakke is where the argument about remedy, posed first at Howard University in 1965, was still running when the famous decade's tools were spent. What followed belongs to Modern America.
