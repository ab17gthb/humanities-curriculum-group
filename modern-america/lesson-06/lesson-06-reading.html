September 11 and the War on Terror

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Day: September 11, 2001

On the morning of September 11, 2001, nineteen men hijacked four airliners. Two were flown into the World Trade Center's twin towers, and both towers collapsed. A third struck the Pentagon. A fourth crashed in a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, after its passengers fought the hijackers. These were the September 11 attacks, the terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001, in which hijackers of the al Qaeda network flew passenger planes into the World Trade Center and the Pentagon and crashed a fourth plane in Pennsylvania, killing 2,977 people.

The hijackers belonged to al Qaeda, the terrorist network led by Osama bin Laden that carried out the September 11 attacks, built from veterans of the war against the Soviet Union in Afghanistan. The commission Congress created to investigate the attacks opened its account this way: "September 11, 2001, was a day of unprecedented shock and suffering in the history of the United States. The nation was unprepared."

B. The President's Answer

President George W. Bush addressed the nation that night and stated the rule of the American answer: "We will make no distinction between the terrorists who committed these acts and those who harbor them." Nine days later, before a joint session of Congress, he demanded that Afghanistan's Taliban government surrender al Qaeda's leaders and close its camps, and told every nation to choose between the United States and the terrorists.

The campaign that followed took the name the War on Terror, the worldwide campaign of military action, intelligence work, and security measures the United States declared after September 11, 2001, against al Qaeda and the groups and states said to shelter it.

C. The Legal Architecture

The Authorization for Use of Military Force, the law Congress passed on September 18, 2001 authorizing the president to use all necessary and appropriate force against those responsible for the September 11 attacks and those who harbored them, passed the Senate 98 to 0 and the House 420 to 1. Its text reaches whoever the president determines planned or aided the attacks or harbored the attackers. Barbara Lee cast the only opposing vote.

In October, Congress passed the USA PATRIOT Act, the law of October 2001 that widened the government's powers of surveillance, search, and detention in terrorism investigations, passed by overwhelming margins in the weeks after September 11. The Senate approved it 98 to 1; the president signed it on October 26. Its Section 102 condemned discrimination against Arab and Muslim Americans and declared that "the civil rights and civil liberties of all Americans, including Arab Americans, Muslim Americans, and Americans from South Asia, must be protected."

D. Afghanistan: The First War

The Taliban refused to surrender bin Laden, and on October 7, 2001 American and British bombing opened a campaign fought on the ground with the Afghan fighters of the Northern Alliance. Kabul fell in November, and the Taliban regime collapsed within weeks. Bin Laden escaped the fighting at Tora Bora in December and crossed into Pakistan.

The quick victory opened a long war; American forces were fighting there when the financial crisis arrived in 2008. Bin Laden himself was killed by American forces in Pakistan in 2011.

E. Iraq: The Second War

The second war rested on a claim rather than on an attack. The administration said that Iraq possessed stockpiles of weapons of mass destruction, chemical, biological, and nuclear weapons; the claim that Iraq possessed stockpiles of them was the stated ground for the American invasion of Iraq in 2003, and no such stockpiles were found. Secretary of State Colin Powell presented the administration's evidence at the United Nations. The invasion began on March 20, 2003, and Baghdad fell in April.

No stockpiles were found. The Iraq Survey Group, searching the country after the invasion, reported in 2004 that Iraq had destroyed its chemical weapons stockpile in 1991 and that Saddam Hussein had ended the nuclear program the same year. The regime's fall opened an insurgency and a sectarian civil war. The two wars together killed more than seven thousand American service members and cost sums in the trillions.

F. The State Built at Home

In 2002 Congress created the Department of Homeland Security, the cabinet department created in 2002 that merged twenty two federal agencies and offices charged with protecting the country against terrorist attack and disaster. Airport security was federalized, and border, coast guard, immigration, and emergency agencies were gathered under one secretary.

Surveillance authorities widened by the PATRIOT Act ran for years in programs the public did not know, until Edward Snowden's disclosures revealed in 2013 that the government had collected Americans' telephone records in bulk. Officials defended the programs as lawful tools that had helped stop plots. Critics charged that it swept in millions with no connection to terrorism and answered to too little public check.

G. The Commission's Judgment and the Standing Argument

The 9/11 Commission delivered its report in July 2004. It found that no measure adopted from 1998 to 2001 had disturbed or delayed the plot, and it named the failures plainly: "Across the government, there were failures of imagination, policy, capabilities, and management." "The most important failure was one of imagination. We do not believe leaders understood the gravity of the threat."

The argument the attacks opened has never closed. Officials who built the new machinery called the powers necessary, pointing to the absence of a second attack on September 11's scale. Critics answered that Iraq rested on a claim the record did not support and the powers reached further than safety required. The era closed with two wars running, its argument unsettled.
