Writing Task: The Gap Between the Evidence and the Record

The prompt. Answer the question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Judged only from the documents, which response to the attacks, the address and the doctrine, the Authorization for Use of Military Force, the PATRIOT Act, or the decision for war in Iraq, carried the largest gap between the evidence offered at the time and what the record later showed? Build the comparison before you judge it. A strong answer sets out, for the response you choose and for one rival, what evidence was offered when the decision was made and what the later record, the Commission's report of 2004, the Iraq Survey Group's findings of 2004, and the disclosures of 2013, showed instead. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, drawn from both halves of the bank, and explain how each piece supports your judgment.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the Powers File. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. On the night of September 11, 2001, President Bush directed the full resources of the intelligence and law enforcement communities to find those responsible and stated the rule of the response: "We will make no distinction between the terrorists who committed these acts and those who harbor them." The address named no country, presented no proof, and set no limit or time frame. (Audited TPSP record bush-911-address-2001.)

2. The Authorization for Use of Military Force, passed September 18, 2001 by 98 to 0 in the Senate and 420 to 1 in the House, authorized the president to use all necessary and appropriate force against those nations, organizations, or persons he determines planned, authorized, committed, or aided the attacks, or harbored such organizations or persons. (Audited TPSP record authorization-use-military-force-2001.)

3. The USA PATRIOT Act of October 2001 widened the government's powers of surveillance, search, and detention, passing the Senate 98 to 1. Its Section 102 found that Arab Americans, Muslim Americans, and Americans from South Asia are entitled to the full rights of every American, and declared that the civil rights and civil liberties of all Americans must be protected. (Audited TPSP record patriot-act-section-102-2001.)

4. The administration said Iraq possessed stockpiles of weapons of mass destruction, and that claim was the stated ground for the invasion of March 2003. Secretary of State Colin Powell presented the administration's evidence at the United Nations in February 2003. Congress had authorized force against Iraq in October 2002. (Lesson reading.)

5. The 9/11 Commission reported in July 2004 that none of the measures adopted by the government from 1998 to 2001 had disturbed or delayed the al Qaeda plot, and named failures of imagination, policy, capabilities, and management. "The most important failure was one of imagination. We do not believe leaders understood the gravity of the threat." (Audited TPSP record 911-commission-report-exec-summary-2004.)

6. The Iraq Survey Group reported in 2004: "Saddam Husayn ended the nuclear program in 1991 following the Gulf war. ISG found no evidence to suggest concerted efforts to restart the program." And: "ISG judges that Iraq unilaterally destroyed its undeclared chemical weapons stockpile in 1991. There are no credible indications that Baghdad resumed production of chemical munitions thereafter." The same report found that Saddam intended to rebuild his capability once sanctions ended. (Comprehensive Report of the Special Advisor to the DCI on Iraq's WMD, Key Findings, 2004.)

7. The two wars together killed more than seven thousand American service members and cost sums in the trillions. Surveillance programs widened by the PATRIOT Act ran in secret until 2013, when disclosures revealed bulk collection of Americans' telephone records; officials defended the programs as lawful tools that had helped stop plots, and critics charged that they swept in millions with no connection to terrorism. (Lesson reading.)

Plan Before You Write

My judgment (one sentence naming the response with the largest gap, and the two records that measure it):

My evidence picks (bank numbers, at least one from items 1 to 4 and at least one from items 5 to 7) and what each one proves:

The strongest case for a different response, and how I will answer it:

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your judgment, set out the evidence offered at the time for your chosen response, set out what the later record showed, run the same comparison briefly for one rival, answer the strongest case for that rival, and end by restating what the documents show.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment that names one response directly and measures a gap, the distance between the evidence offered at the time and what the later record showed, rather than judging which response the student approves of. Any of the four responses can earn full credit when the gap is measured from the documents. (Blueprint 11 and Blueprint 12)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, with at least one from each half. Bank items 1 through 4 carry the evidence offered at the time: the address's rule with no proof in the text, the AUMF's breadth and its near unanimous votes, the PATRIOT Act's widened powers beside Section 102's declaration, and the Iraq stockpile claim presented at the United Nations. (Blueprint 11) Bank items 5 through 7 carry the later record: the Commission's four failures, the Survey Group's 1991 findings, and the costs and the 2013 disclosures. (Blueprint 12)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the judgment. The strongest essays keep the two measurements separate, what was offered then and what was shown later, and notice the forms the evidence took: a speech with no proof in it, a statute one page long, a claim presented to the United Nations, a search of the country itself.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest case for a rival response, answered from the record rather than dismissed. An essay naming Iraq must answer the report's second half, that Saddam intended to rebuild once sanctions ended, and the fact that the prewar claim was believed well beyond the administration. An essay naming the AUMF or the PATRIOT Act must answer the votes, 518 to 1 across both houses for the authorization, and the absence of a second attack on September 11's scale, which officials cited as the powers' evidence.

Model opening for teacher reference. Judged only from the documents, the largest gap belongs to the decision for war in Iraq, where the evidence offered in 2002 and 2003 was a set of stockpile claims presented to the United Nations, and the record of 2004, written by the government's own inspectors, was that the stockpiles had been destroyed and the nuclear program ended twelve years before the invasion. A full model continues in this pattern, runs the same comparison for the AUMF, whose text promised a tie to the September 11 attacks that its later uses strained, answers the intent finding in the Duelfer report's second half, and closes on what the documents show.

Common weak answers. A judgment with no rival (the gap measured for one response only) has not performed the comparison the prompt requires. A judgment built on which response the student likes or dislikes, rather than on the distance between the evidence then and the record later, earns partial credit. An essay that reports the Duelfer findings without the intent finding, or the PATRIOT Act without Section 102, has used half a document and should be graded as such.
