Guided Notes: September 11 and the War on Terror

Lesson question: How did the attacks of September 11, 2001 change what the American government did abroad and at home, and what did Americans argue about the changes?

Unit question: How did the United States remake itself after 1980, in its economy, its population, and its place in the world, and what did the remaking settle and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

September 11 attacks: the terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001, in which hijackers of the al Qaeda network flew passenger planes into the World Trade Center and the Pentagon and crashed a fourth plane in Pennsylvania, killing 2,977 people

al Qaeda: the terrorist network led by Osama bin Laden that carried out the September 11 attacks, built from veterans of the war against the Soviet Union in Afghanistan

War on Terror: the worldwide campaign of military action, intelligence work, and security measures the United States declared after September 11, 2001, against al Qaeda and the groups and states said to shelter it

Authorization for Use of Military Force: the law Congress passed on September 18, 2001 authorizing the president to use all necessary and appropriate force against those responsible for the September 11 attacks and those who harbored them

USA PATRIOT Act: the law of October 2001 that widened the government's powers of surveillance, search, and detention in terrorism investigations, passed by overwhelming margins in the weeks after September 11

Department of Homeland Security: the cabinet department created in 2002 that merged twenty two federal agencies and offices charged with protecting the country against terrorist attack and disaster

weapons of mass destruction: chemical, biological, and nuclear weapons; the claim that Iraq possessed stockpiles of them was the stated ground for the American invasion of Iraq in 2003, and no such stockpiles were found

Summary table. The day and the doctrine: nineteen hijackers of al Qaeda flew two planes into the World Trade Center, one into the Pentagon, and a fourth into a Pennsylvania field, killing 2,977 people; President Bush answered that night with the rule that no distinction would be made between the terrorists and those who harbored them, and set the full doctrine before Congress on September 20. The laws of the fall of 2001: the AUMF passed 98 to 0 and 420 to 1, reaching whoever the president determines planned or aided the attacks or harbored the attackers; the PATRIOT Act passed the Senate 98 to 1 and was signed October 26, widening surveillance, search, and detention powers while its Section 102 condemned discrimination against Arab and Muslim Americans. The two wars: Afghanistan began October 7, 2001 against the Taliban government that sheltered al Qaeda, and ran for years; Iraq was invaded on March 20, 2003 on the stated ground of weapons stockpiles, and none were found. The state built at home: the Department of Homeland Security merged twenty two agencies in 2002, airport security was federalized, and secret surveillance programs ran until the Snowden disclosures of 2013 revealed bulk telephone record collection. The judgment of 2004: the 9/11 Commission found failures of imagination, policy, capabilities, and management, imagination the most important, and the Iraq Survey Group found the chemical stockpile destroyed and the nuclear program ended, both in 1991.

Exit ticket 1. The AUMF passed the Senate 98 to 0 and the House 420 to 1. Representative Barbara Lee cast the only opposing vote. The law's text reaches the nations, organizations, or persons the president determines planned, authorized, committed, or aided the September 11 attacks, or harbored such organizations or persons, so the president makes the determination.

Exit ticket 2. The Iraq Survey Group reported in 2004 that Iraq had unilaterally destroyed its undeclared chemical weapons stockpile in 1991 and that Saddam Hussein had ended the nuclear program in 1991, with no evidence of concerted efforts to restart it. No stockpiles were found.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer pairing one change abroad with one change at home. The strongest answers run from the doctrine and the two wars abroad to the AUMF, the PATRIOT Act, and the Department of Homeland Security at home, and close on the argument the changes opened, over the Iraq claim and the reach of the security powers.
