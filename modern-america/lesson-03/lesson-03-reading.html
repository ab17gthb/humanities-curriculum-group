After the Cold War: Globalization and the New Economy

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The First War of the Sole Superpower

In August 1990 Iraq invaded Kuwait, and the United States built a coalition of twenty eight countries to reverse the invasion. The diplomacy of the Cold War's end, the summits and the Soviet dissolution of December 1991, belongs to the Cold War unit. This lesson takes those same years at home. On January 16, 1991, President George H. W. Bush told the nation that allied air forces had begun attacks on military targets in Iraq and Kuwait. A ground campaign followed in late February, and in six weeks the coalition drove Iraq's army out of Kuwait. The war was brief, televised live, and fought with United Nations authorization and the consent of Congress. It was the first war the United States fought as the world's sole superpower.

B. The Peace Dividend Debate

Victory raised a question about money. For four decades the rivalry with the Soviet Union had justified large military budgets, bases across the country, and whole industries built on defense contracts. If the rivalry was over, should some of that money now go to the federal deficit, to tax cuts, or to needs at home such as roads, schools, and health care? Americans called the expected saving the peace dividend. Its supporters claimed it for domestic spending and for debt reduction. Defense workers and the towns around bases and shipyards faced the other side of the saving, because a smaller military budget meant lost contracts and lost jobs in their communities.

C. A Rights Settlement in the Same Years

The hinge years also produced a major civil rights law. On July 26, 1990, President Bush signed the Americans with Disabilities Act. In its findings Congress stated that some 43,000,000 Americans had one or more physical or mental disabilities, and that discrimination against them persisted in employment, housing, public accommodations, education, and transportation. In his signing statement the president said it promised to open up all aspects of American life to individuals with disabilities. The act extended the method of the civil rights era, a federal ban on discrimination backed by law, to disability. It required reasonable accommodation at work and access to public accommodations, with cost limits for employers.

D. An Economy Rebuilt Around Trade

The larger change of the decade was economic. Globalization, the deepening connection of the world's economies through trade, investment, and production spread across borders, accelerated after the Cold War. Imports and exports together rose as a share of the American economy, from about one quarter of gross domestic product in 1990 toward three tenths by 2010, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysis. The North American Free Trade Agreement removed most tariffs among the United States, Canada, and Mexico. Its objectives, stated in Article 102, were to eliminate barriers to trade and increase investment opportunities. Congress approved it in 1993 over the opposition of organized labor and Ross Perot, who warned that jobs would move south. It took effect on January 1, 1994. The World Trade Organization followed in 1995, and permanent normal trade relations with China in 2000.

E. The Factory Economy Gives Its Answer

Factory employment kept falling through the boom. Manufacturing employed about 17.3 million Americans in 1990 and about 11.5 million by 2010, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Companies used outsourcing, moving production to lower cost workers abroad, and automation at home. The Rust Belt, the industrial Northeast and Midwest, saw mills close and cities lose population. In place of goods jobs grew the service economy. Most new jobs produced services, in health care, retail, finance, and food, rather than goods. Supporters of the trade agreements pointed to lower consumer prices, export jobs, and rising productivity. Critics pointed to closed plants and slow growth in median wages. Both claims were argued from the same federal figures, and the argument was distributional. It asked not only whether the economy grew, but who gained and who paid.

F. The Boom, the Surplus, and a New Welfare Law

By the measures at the top, the 1990s boom was long and strong. The expansion ran through the decade, unemployment fell to 3.9 percent in 2000, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and the federal budget ran a surplus from 1998 to 2001, the first surpluses in a generation, according to the Congressional Budget Office. In 1996 President Bill Clinton signed the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act, which replaced the Aid to Families with Dependent Children program with Temporary Assistance for Needy Families. The new law set work requirements, generally after two years of benefits, and a five year lifetime limit on benefits paid with federal funds. Clinton signed it over the opposition of many in his own party. Supporters called it a move from welfare to work.

G. Shadows Inside the Boom

The decade opened with a cleanup and carried warnings. The savings and loan failures of the 1980s cost taxpayers well over one hundred billion dollars to resolve in the early 1990s. In February 1993 a truck bomb exploded beneath the World Trade Center in New York, killing six people, a warning of a new kind of enemy that drew limited attention at the time. In 1999 protesters filled the streets of Seattle during a World Trade Organization meeting, charging that globalization served corporations over workers and the environment. The reading therefore closes on the question the numbers frame but do not settle. Were the gains of the new economy a tide that lifted all boats, or a sorting that lifted some Americans while others paid? The documents of this lesson give you the figures and the claims.
