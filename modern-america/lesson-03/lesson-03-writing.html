Writing Task: Was the Peace Dividend Ever Really Paid?

The prompt. Answer in a developed essay of about 300 words: Was the peace dividend ever really paid? When the Cold War ended in 1991, Americans expected money and attention to move from military spending to needs at home. Judge from the documents whether that payment arrived, who received anything you can document, and who paid costs in the same years. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim. Grade rests on the evidence, not on the verdict you reach.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank wording.

Evidence Bank

1. In his address of January 16, 1991, President Bush stated that the Gulf War action was taken in accord with United Nations resolutions and with the consent of Congress, by a coalition of 28 countries. The coalition drove Iraq's army out of Kuwait in six weeks.

2. Congress found in the ADA of 1990 that some 43,000,000 Americans had one or more disabilities and that discrimination against them persisted in employment, housing, public accommodations, education, and transportation. President Bush, signing the act, said it promised to open up all aspects of American life to individuals with disabilities.

3. NAFTA's Article 102 set the objectives of eliminating barriers to trade and increasing substantially investment opportunities among the United States, Canada, and Mexico. The agreement took effect on January 1, 1994, after a 1993 fight in which organized labor and Ross Perot warned that jobs would move south.

4. Manufacturing employment fell from about 17.3 million in 1990 to about 11.5 million by 2010, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, while imports plus exports rose from about one quarter of the economy toward three tenths, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysis.

5. Unemployment fell to 3.9 percent in 2000, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and the federal budget ran a surplus from 1998 to 2001, according to the Congressional Budget Office.

6. The welfare law of 1996 replaced AFDC with TANF, required work after two years of benefits, and set a five year lifetime limit on benefits paid with federal funds. Its stated purposes included providing assistance so children could be cared for in their own homes and ending dependence on government benefits by promoting work.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the prompt directly, whether the verdict is paid, partly paid, or never paid, and that names both a documented receipt and a documented cost. (Blueprint 5 and Blueprint 6)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 supports the setting, a sole superpower war that opened the dividend debate. (Blueprint 5) Bank item 2 supports a domestic settlement paid in rights rather than cash. (Blueprint 5) Bank items 3 and 4 support the trade opening and its factory cost, with figures attributed to the Bureau of Labor Statistics and the Bureau of Economic Analysis. (Blueprint 6) Bank item 5 supports the closest thing to a cash payment in the record, the surplus years and full employment. (Blueprint 6) Bank item 6 supports a cost charged inside the boom to families on assistance. (Blueprint 6) A full credit essay draws on at least two groups.
  • Explanation that connects each piece of evidence to the claim. An essay that lists the surplus and the factory figures side by side without judging what their pairing shows has assembled the ledger without reading it, and should earn partial credit.
  • An ending that states what the verdict leaves open, as the lesson does. The figures frame the distributional question. They do not settle whether lower prices and export gains outweighed closed mills, and a strong essay says so rather than forcing the numbers past what they show.

Model opening for teacher reference. The peace dividend was paid in pieces, and never to everyone at once. The clearest payment in this record is not a check but a set of figures: a federal surplus from 1998 to 2001 and unemployment of 3.9 percent in 2000, reached in the first decade in a generation when the country was not funding a rivalry with another superpower. A second payment arrived as rights, when the ADA of 1990 opened employment and public life to 43,000,000 Americans Congress counted as facing discrimination. A full model continues in this pattern, then charges the other side of the ledger, the 5.8 million manufacturing jobs lost across the same decades and the welfare families who met the 1996 law's limits, before judging the balance.

Common weak answers. A verdict with no costs (the 1990s were a boom, so the dividend was paid) ignores bank items 4 and 6. A verdict with no receipts (nothing improved, so it was never paid) ignores bank items 2 and 5. A verdict that treats the surplus as money mailed to households misreads what a surplus is. Credit requires the ledger held whole: what the Cold War's end freed, where the record shows it going, and who was charged in the same years.
