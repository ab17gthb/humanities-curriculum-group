The Digital Transformation

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Machine on the Desk

In 1975 a computer filled a room, cost more than a house, and belonged to a government agency, a university, or a large company. Within fifteen years a machine sat on an office desk and then on a kitchen table, run by its owner. That machine was the personal computer: a computer built for one person's desk and home, sold in large numbers from the late 1970s onward, which carried computing out of institutions and into daily life.

Software became an industry of its own. The workplace changed task by task. Typing pools gave way to workers drafting their own letters, drafting tables to screens, and filing cabinets to disks.

B. The Network Opens

The computers then learned to talk to each other. The result was the Internet: the network of computer networks that grew out of government and university systems into a public means of communication, commerce, and publication in the 1990s. Its roots ran back to a defense research network begun in 1969 and a university network of the 1980s. In the middle 1990s it opened to the public and to business, and browser software made it usable by anyone.

The Census Bureau counted the arrival. In 1997 about 18 percent of households had Internet access at home, the first year the Bureau collected the figure. By 2000 the share reached about 42 percent, and by 2001 a majority of households had a computer and about half were online.

C. The Law That Framed It

Congress rewrote the rules as the network spread. The Telecommunications Act of 1996, Public Law 104-104, was the first full rewriting of federal communications law since 1934. It loosened ownership rules across broadcasting, cable, and telephone service, letting companies own more stations and enter each other's markets.

One short passage shaped the Internet itself. Section 230, added by the 1996 act, provided that no provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider. A service carrying other people's words was not legally their speaker. Platforms later grew behind that shield, hosting millions of posts without a publisher's responsibility for each one.

D. Commerce, Boom, and Bust

Commerce followed the crowd online, producing e-commerce: the buying and selling of goods and services over the Internet, which grew from almost nothing in the middle 1990s into a central part of American retail. Booksellers, auction sites, and then sellers of nearly everything taught Americans to enter a card number on a screen.

Investment ran ahead in the dot-com bubble: the speculative boom in Internet company stocks in the late 1990s, ending in a crash in 2000 and 2001 that erased trillions of dollars in paper wealth. The NASDAQ composite peaked at 5,048 on March 10, 2000, then fell about 78 percent by October 2002, and firms with no profit failed by the hundreds. Contemporaries compared it to the railroad booms of the nineteenth century. The speculation collapsed, and the network it had paid to build remained in use.

E. The News World Fractures

News changed on a track that began earlier. CNN went on the air on June 1, 1980, and with it came cable news: television news carried by cable channels around the clock, beginning with CNN in 1980, which replaced the nightly broadcast as many Americans' main source of news. A channel filling twenty four hours treated news as a continuous flow, and rival channels followed in the 1990s with distinct audiences.

In 1987 the Federal Communications Commission repealed the Fairness Doctrine, ending the rule requiring broadcasters to present contrasting views on public issues, and national talk radio grew quickly in the space that opened. Newspapers lost ground as advertising drained toward free or cheap online listings. By the 2000s a citizen could spend the day inside a news world of one tendency, where a generation earlier most households drew on the same few broadcasts.

F. Publication in Every Pocket

A second transformation began from the middle 2000s. Its instruments were social media: the Internet platforms, growing from the middle 2000s, on which users publish their own words, photographs, and video and build networks of followers and friends. Publication, once reserved to professionals with presses and transmitters, now belonged to anyone with an account.

The smartphone, sold in large numbers after 2007, joined the network to the person. The computer left the desk for the pocket, and the network became a constant companion rather than a place a person visited. Work followed onto the same screen, and the boundary between office hours and the rest of the day thinned.

G. The Divide and the Country It Made

The transformation did not reach everyone on the same terms. The federal surveys named the gap the digital divide: the gap between Americans with ready access to computers and the Internet and those without, which followed lines of income, education, age, and place. The Falling Through the Net surveys of the National Telecommunications and Information Administration found the highest income households online at several times the rate of the lowest in 2000, college graduates far ahead of adults who had left school early, and rural households behind city households.

By 2010 the country shopped, worked, argued, and read differently than it had a generation before. The machine had moved computing into daily life, the network had joined the machines into a public medium, and its news no longer arrived in one common edition. This unit's last lesson takes up what Americans did with machinery that let every citizen publish and every audience divide.
