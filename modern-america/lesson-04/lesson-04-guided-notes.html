Guided Notes: The Digital Transformation

Lesson question: How did the computer and the Internet remake American work, commerce, and news between 1980 and 2010?

Unit question: How did the United States remake itself after 1980, in its economy, its population, and its place in the world, and what did the remaking settle and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

personal computer: a computer built for one person's desk and home, sold in large numbers from the late 1970s onward, which carried computing out of institutions and into daily life

Internet: the network of computer networks that grew out of government and university systems into a public means of communication, commerce, and publication in the 1990s

e-commerce: the buying and selling of goods and services over the Internet, which grew from almost nothing in the middle 1990s into a central part of American retail

dot-com bubble: the speculative boom in Internet company stocks in the late 1990s, ending in a crash in 2000 and 2001 that erased trillions of dollars in paper wealth

cable news: television news carried by cable channels around the clock, beginning with CNN in 1980, which replaced the nightly broadcast as many Americans' main source of news

social media: the Internet platforms, growing from the middle 2000s, on which users publish their own words, photographs, and video and build networks of followers and friends

digital divide: the gap between Americans with ready access to computers and the Internet and those without, which followed lines of income, education, age, and place

Summary table. The machine: the personal computer carried computing out of institutions and into daily life, and office work changed task by task, typing pools and drafting tables giving way to screens. The network: the Internet opened to the public and to commerce in the middle 1990s, and Census counted household access rising from about 18 percent in 1997 to about 42 percent in 2000. The boom: e-commerce grew from almost nothing into a central part of retail, and the dot-com bubble peaked with the NASDAQ at 5,048 on March 10, 2000, then fell about 78 percent by October 2002. The news: cable news ran around the clock from CNN in 1980, talk radio grew after the Fairness Doctrine's repeal in 1987, newspapers lost advertising online, and social media after the middle 2000s put publication in every pocket. The divide: access followed income, education, age, and place, as the Falling Through the Net surveys measured.

Exit ticket 1. Section 230 provided that no provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider.

Exit ticket 2. Household Internet access was about 18 percent in 1997 and about 42 percent in 2000, per the Census Bureau. The NASDAQ composite peaked at 5,048 on March 10, 2000.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer using one work fact and one news fact, for example the personal computer ending the typing pool and putting drafting on one person's screen, and cable news and social media replacing the common nightly broadcast and the local paper with a continuous, divided news world.
