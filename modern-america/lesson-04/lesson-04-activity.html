The Transformation File: Computers, the Network, and News

Directions: Work through the five stations in order. Each station holds one record from 1980 to 2002. For each station, name the work, commerce, or news practice it changed and the evidence it gives. At the end, judge which change reached furthest into ordinary life by 2010 and defend the judgment from the documents.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: The Telecommunications Act and Section 230, 1996

Source: Telecommunications Act of 1996, Public Law 104-104, Statutes at Large via GovInfo. The act added Section 230 to the Communications Act of 1934, codified at 47 U.S.C. Section 230, text as printed by the U.S. Code, Cornell Legal Information Institute, and GovInfo. The sentence below is quoted in full. The ownership summary that follows it is a labeled summary of the act prepared for this lesson.

"No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider."

The same act was the first full rewriting of federal communications law since 1934. It loosened ownership rules across broadcasting, cable, and telephone service, allowing companies to own more stations and to enter each other's markets.

Name the practice this record changed. A service carrying other people's words holds what legal position under this sentence, and what could grow behind that position?

State the evidence the station gives for the size of the 1996 rewriting. Name the law it replaced as the governing frame and the year of that earlier law.

Station 4: The Cable News Record, 1980 to 1996

Source: Summary prepared for this lesson from the public record. This station is a labeled summary, not a transcription of a document. It states the founding and regulatory facts of cable news and talk radio as the public record carries them.

DateRecord
June 1, 1980CNN goes on the air from Atlanta, the first television channel devoted to news around the clock

1988 onwardNationally syndicated talk radio grows quickly, carried by stations across the country from a single studio

Before 1980 most households drew their television news from a nightly broadcast on one of three networks and their radio news from short reports at the top of the hour. The record above is the machinery by which that common news day came apart, stated as fact: more channels, running all day, aimed at narrower audiences, beside a radio form freed of the obligation to carry the other side.

Name the news practice this record changed. What did a citizen's news day look like before 1980, in the summary's terms, and what replaced it?

State the evidence for the role of law in the change. Name the rule repealed in 1987 and the form of broadcasting that grew after its repeal.

Part 2: The Verdict

Five records stand in the file: a liability shield written into federal law, a Census count of the machine and the network entering the home, an index that rose to 5,048 and fell about 78 percent, a news machinery rebuilt between 1980 and 1996, and a federal survey of who was left behind. Choose the one change you would defend as reaching furthest into ordinary life by 2010: the machine, the network, or the fractured news world. A strong verdict names the change, works from at least two stations, and answers the strongest rival claim among the other two.

Work in the file's own terms. Reach is measured in daily practice: what people did at work, at the store, and with the news, and how many people the change touched, including those it touched by leaving them out. A verdict that names a famous company or a famous year without a practice and a number has not answered the question this file asks.

Write your verdict in a paragraph. Name your change, give the practice it altered and the evidence for its reach from two stations, and answer the strongest rival.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: The Telecommunications Act and Section 230

Practice changed. Publication and legal responsibility online. Under the quoted sentence, a provider or user of an interactive computer service is not treated as the publisher or speaker of information provided by another content provider, so the platforms that hosted the public's words grew behind a liability shield that print and broadcast publishers never had. Social media, comment sections, auction and review sites, and search all rest in part on this sentence.

Size of the rewriting. The Telecommunications Act of 1996, Public Law 104-104, was the first full rewriting of federal communications law since the Communications Act of 1934, a frame sixty two years old, and it loosened ownership rules across broadcasting, cable, and telephone service in the same act.

Station 2: The Census Bureau Record

Practice changed. The home became a connected workplace, store, and reading room. The table measures Internet access entering the household itself, from about 18 percent in 1997 to about half of households in 2001, four years.

The machine before the network. Households with a computer stood at about 8 percent in 1984 and about 51 percent in 2000, while the Bureau did not collect household Internet data until 1997, when access was already about 18 percent. The desk machine arrived across the 1980s; the connection followed across the 1990s.

Station 3: The Dot-Com Peak and Crash Record

Practice changed. Saving, investing, and starting a business. At the peak, investors were buying shares in Internet companies, many with no profit, at prices the index carried to 5,048 on March 10, 2000. E-commerce itself, the buying and selling of goods and services over the Internet, kept growing through the crash, which is the distinction the station is built to teach.

What a crash does and does not destroy. The index fell about 78 percent by October 2002, erasing trillions of dollars in paper wealth, and hundreds of firms failed. The fall did not destroy the network. The cables, servers, and surviving companies remained in use, as the railroad network had remained after the railroad booms collapsed.

Station 4: The Cable News Record

Practice changed. The daily intake of news. Before 1980 most households drew television news from a nightly broadcast on one of three networks. Cable news running around the clock from CNN's founding on June 1, 1980, rival channels with distinct audiences from 1996, and national talk radio replaced the common news day with a continuous flow that a citizen could select by tendency.

The role of law. The Federal Communications Commission repealed the Fairness Doctrine in 1987, ending the requirement that broadcasters present contrasting views on public issues, and nationally syndicated talk radio grew quickly in the space that opened from 1988 onward.

Station 5: The Digital Divide Record

Practice qualified. Every practice in this file arrived unevenly. In the surveys' ratio, households earning $75,000 or more were about twenty times as likely to have Internet access as households at the lowest income level in the 1998 data, and about nine times as likely to have a computer.

Growth and division together. Household Internet access rose from 26.2 percent in December 1998 to 41.5 percent in August 2000, and computer ownership passed half of households, in the same surveys that recorded the twenty to one income ratio and a steep education staircase. Full credit requires both halves held at once.

Part 2: The Verdict (Grading Guide)

Full credit verdicts name one change, the machine, the network, or the fractured news world, support its reach with evidence from at least two stations, and answer the strongest rival. A network verdict rests on the Census table (access from about 18 percent of households in 1997 to about half in 2001) and on Station 1 or 5, and must answer the machine claim, that none of it happens without a computer in the home, and the divide evidence, that reach was uneven. A machine verdict rests on the 1984 to 2000 computer figures and the workplace record of the reading, and must answer the network's speed of adoption. A fractured news verdict rests on Station 4's dates and the reading's advertising record, and must answer the objection that news habits changed for a smaller share of the day than work and shopping did. The side a student takes is not graded; the evidence and the answered rival are.
