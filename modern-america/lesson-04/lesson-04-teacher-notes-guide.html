The Digital Transformation

Modern America · Lesson 4 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Modern America, Lesson 4 of 8. Teach it after Lesson 3, which covered the end of the Cold War at home, globalization, and the economy rebuilt around trade. This lesson takes up the transformation that ran beside those changes: the personal computer carrying computing out of institutions, the Internet opening to the public, the law that framed the network, commerce online and its bubble, the fracturing of the news world, social media and the smartphone, and the digital divide. Lesson 5 takes up immigration, cities, and the Sun Belt.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “The Digital Transformation” and the guided notes, which follow the deck in sections A to G: the machine on the desk, the network opens, the law that framed it, commerce, boom, and bust, the news world fractures, publication in every pocket, and the divide. The activity is “The Transformation File: Computers, the Network, and News.” The notebook page is “Notebook Page: The Digital Transformation.” The quick check is “The Machine, the Network, and the News.” The writing task is “Which Change Reached Furthest?”

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Students need Lesson 3 of this unit for the economy of the 1990s into which the network arrived. No prior knowledge of the personal computer's history, the Internet's origins, the Telecommunications Act of 1996, the dot-com bubble, cable news, or the digital divide surveys is assumed; each is taught in sequence, and every figure names its federal source.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (analyze connections among events: the personal computer, the opening network, the 1996 act, online commerce and its crash, the fractured news world, and the measured divide, in sequence), D2.His.9 (construct arguments using historical evidence: the writing task judging which change reached furthest, from the lesson's figures and the statute), D2.His.16 (assess options for individual and collective action: a liability shield in federal law and household access to a new network weighed as forms of collective action); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from the machine to the network to commerce to news to the divide), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task argued from the evidence bank). The lesson is tagged to blueprint entries 7 and 8.

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to argue the lesson's question from the record, with dates and figures attached: a personal computer in about 51 percent of households by 2000, up from about 8 percent in 1984, on Census Bureau figures; household Internet access rising from about 18 percent in 1997 to about 42 percent in 2000; the Telecommunications Act of 1996 and the liability shield in its Section 230; e-commerce growing steadily while the NASDAQ Composite peaked at 5,048 on March 10, 2000 and fell about 78 percent by October 2002; cable news running from CNN in 1980 through the repeal of the Fairness Doctrine in 1987 to rival channels in 1996; social media and the smartphone after 2007; and a digital divide measured by the NTIA surveys at about twenty to one by income in the 1998 data.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes: opening, question, and map (slides 1 to 3) 5 minutes; the machine on the desk (slides 4 to 6) 6 minutes; the network opens (slides 7 to 9) 6 minutes; the law that framed it (slides 10 to 11) 5 minutes; commerce, boom, and bust (slides 12 to 14) 6 minutes; the news world fractures (slides 15 to 17) 6 minutes; publication in every pocket (slides 18 to 19) 4 minutes; the divide (slides 20 to 22) 6 minutes; glance, synthesis, vocabulary, and the turn to work (slides 23 to 27) 6 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: THE QUESTION FOR TODAY · How did the computer and the Internet remake American work, commerce, and news between 1980 and 2010? · Unit question: How did the United States remake itself after 1980, in its economy, its population, and its place in the world, and what did the remaking settle and what did it leave open?

  • [Read the question slowly and take its three nouns one at a time.]
  • Work: the personal computer rewrote office tasks, and the smartphone later thinned the boundary between office hours and the rest of the day.
  • Commerce: buying and selling moved online from almost nothing in the middle 1990s, while investors bid the new companies to a peak in March 2000 and lost about 78 percent of that value by October 2002.
  • News: cable channels, talk radio, and then social media replaced one common news day with a continuous flow a citizen could select by tendency.
  • Keep the dates in front of the class all hour.
  • The machine belongs chiefly to the 1980s, the network to the 1990s, and the fractured news world runs on a separate track that begins with CNN in 1980 and completes itself in the 2000s.
  • The writing task will ask which of the three changes reached furthest.

Slide 4 · Section A: The machine on the desk (divider)

On the slide: SECTION A · The Machine on the Desk · Operators at a UNIVAC I, U.S. Census Bureau. · Computing begins in institutions like this one. · Photograph: operators at a UNIVAC I at the Census Bureau, in a portrait crop.

  • Picture: Operators at a UNIVAC I computer at the Census Bureau, a Census Bureau photograph in the public domain, shown at the right of the divider. It shows the institutional world this section leaves behind.
  • [Set the opening contrast carefully, because students often assume computers were always personal.]
  • In 1975 a computer filled a room, cost more than a house, and belonged to a government agency, a university, or a large company.
  • A person who needed computing done handed the job to specialists and waited for printouts.
  • The Census Bureau itself is a good example to name: it had used a UNIVAC to count the country since the early 1950s, and no census clerk imagined owning the machine.
  • Section A covers the fifteen years in which a machine moved onto an office desk and then a kitchen table, sold as kits and then as finished products in the late 1970s and standardized for business after 1981.
  • Ask: Watch for the second change inside the first. What is it?
  • Answer: Software became an industry of its own at the same time, and the two changes together rewrote office work.

Slide 5 · Work changed task by task

On the slide: A · THE MACHINE · Work changed task by task · ▪ Software became an industry of its own. ▪ Typing pools gave way to workers drafting their own letters. ▪ Drafting tables gave way to screens. ▪ Filing cabinets gave way to disks. ▪ The skills of whole departments sat in one pair of hands. · Photograph: operators at a UNIVAC I at the Census Bureau.

  • Picture: Operators at a UNIVAC I computer at the Census Bureau, a Census Bureau photograph in the public domain, shown at the right of the slide. Caption it as the institutional computing this section leaves behind, not as a personal computer.
  • [Teach the personal computer through the tasks it absorbed, because the transformation was practical before it was dramatic.]
  • [Carry the definition exactly.]
  • personal computer: a computer built for one person's desk and home, sold in large numbers from the late 1970s onward, which carried computing out of institutions and into daily life
  • Word processing ended the typing pool as a separate department: the person who composed a letter now typed, corrected, and printed it.
  • Spreadsheets ended the long hand calculation of budgets, and a change in one figure recalculated the whole sheet in seconds.
  • Drafting programs moved drawing from the table to the screen.
  • Employers began to expect these skills of nearly every office applicant, which is the quiet measure of the change.
  • A skill that had belonged to a department, with its own staff and its own room, now sat in one person's hands at one desk, and the offices of the country were rebuilt around that fact across the 1980s and early 1990s.

Slide 6 · 51 percent of households had a computer in 2000

On the slide: A · THE MACHINE · 51% · of households had a computer in 2000 · Census Bureau, August 2000 · Up from about 8 percent of households in 1984

  • 51%
  • [Let the two numbers carry the section.]
  • In 1984 about 8 percent of American households had a computer, a hobbyist and professional machine at the edge of ordinary life.
  • By August 2000 the Census Bureau counted 54 million households, about 51 percent, with one or more computers in the home.
  • That is more than a fivefold rise in the share of households in sixteen years, measured by the federal government's own survey, the Current Population Survey.
  • Pair the figure with the workplace record from the last slide.
  • The machine entered the home and the office in the same decades, so a generation learned the same keyboard in both places, and children met at school the machine their parents used at work.
  • Also mark what the figure does not say.
  • A household with a computer in 2000 was not yet necessarily a household online, and the gap between having the machine and having the connection is where the next section begins.
  • The network figures will rise faster than these did, because the machines were already waiting on the desks.

Slide 7 · Section B: The network opens (divider)

On the slide: SECTION B · The Network Opens · The ARPANET logical map, March 1977. · The whole network, drawn on one page. · Photograph: the ARPANET logical map, March 1977, in a portrait crop.

  • Picture: The logical map of the ARPANET in March 1977, marked public domain on its Wikimedia Commons file page, shown at the right of the divider. It shows the network the Internet grew from, small enough to draw whole on one page.
  • Its nodes were government and university machines, joined by a defense research program begun in 1969.
  • A university network of the 1980s, built by the National Science Foundation, carried the design forward.
  • Section B covers what happened when that research system opened to the public and to commerce in the middle 1990s.
  • Browser software is the hinge to stress.
  • Before it, using the network took training in its commands, and its users were researchers.
  • After it, anyone who could point and click could read, write, buy, and publish there.
  • The Census Bureau then counted the arrival household by household, and its figures, 18 percent of households online in 1997 and about 42 percent in 2000, are the fastest adoption figures in this lesson.

Slide 8 · A network of networks, opened to everyone

On the slide: B · THE NETWORK · A network of networks, opened to everyone · ▪ Its roots lay in a defense research network begun in 1969. ▪ A university network carried the design through the 1980s. ▪ The system opened to the public and to business in the middle 1990s. ▪ Browser software made it usable by anyone. ▪ Mail, the Web, and search arrived on one screen. · Photograph: the ARPANET logical map, March 1977.

  • Picture: The ARPANET logical map of March 1977, in the public domain, shown at the right of the slide. Caption it as the research network the Internet grew from, not as the public Internet itself.
  • [State the definition in full, because the test draws on it.]
  • Internet: the network of computer networks that grew out of government and university systems into a public means of communication, commerce, and publication in the 1990s
  • Hold on the phrase network of networks.
  • No single company built or owned the system; separate networks agreed to speak the same technical language, so a message could cross from one to another without asking a central office.
  • That design came from its government and university makers, and it shaped everything that followed, including the difficulty of governing the system later.
  • When the system opened to commerce in the middle 1990s, electronic mail was the first use most households met, then the World Wide Web, then search tools that made the Web's growing mass findable.
  • Each use arrived on the same screen, which is why the network absorbed the post office, the library, the store, and the newspaper stand into one piece of furniture in under a decade.

Slide 9 · 42 percent of households online in 2000

On the slide: B · THE NETWORK · 42% · of households were online in 2000 · Census Bureau · Up from about 18 percent in 1997 · About half of households by 2001

  • 42%
  • [Use this figure to teach speed.]
  • The Census Bureau first collected data on household Internet use in 1997 and found about 18 percent of households online at home.
  • Three years later, in August 2000, it counted 44 million households, about 42 percent, with at least one member using the Internet at home.
  • By 2001 about half of households were online and a majority had a computer.
  • Compare the pace with the last stat slide.
  • The computer took sixteen years to move from about 8 percent of households to about half.
  • The connection took about four years to cover similar ground, because it arrived as a service sold to machines already sitting in the home.
  • Ask: What does a change moving at this speed do to the industries standing in its path?
  • Answer: Hold their answers for section D, where commerce follows the crowd online, and section E, where the advertising that paid for newspapers begins to drain toward the screen.

Slide 10 · Section C: The law that framed it (divider)

On the slide: SECTION C · The Law That Framed It · The Telecommunications Act of 1996, and twenty six words that shielded the platforms.

  • [Teach this section as law, in the statute's words, not as background color.]
  • The Telecommunications Act of 1996, Public Law 104-104, was the first full rewriting of federal communications law since the Communications Act of 1934, a frame sixty two years old.
  • Most of the act concerned ownership: it loosened the rules across broadcasting, cable, and telephone service, letting companies own more stations and enter each other's markets.
  • The passage that shaped the Internet sat inside the act's decency title, as Section 230 of the Communications Act.
  • Its operative sentence is short enough to read aloud, and the next slide does.
  • Tell students the sentence was written when the online services of the day carried discussion boards and early web pages, and that the giant platforms of the next decade grew up behind the shield it created.
  • The activity file opens with this statute, and students will be asked to state the shield in their own words there.

Slide 11 · Section 230: no provider treated as the publisher

On the slide: C · THE LAW · No provider shall be treated as the publisher · ▪ The 1996 act rewrote communications law for the first time since 1934. ▪ Ownership rules loosened across broadcasting, cable, and telephones. ▪ Section 230 said a service is not the speaker of its users' words. ▪ Platforms grew behind the shield in the next decade.

  • [Quote the sentence whole, slowly, because the activity, the quick check, and the exit ticket all return to it.]
  • “No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider.”
  • Then translate it.
  • A newspaper is legally the publisher of what it prints and can be sued for it.
  • An online service carrying other people's words, under this sentence, is not their speaker in law.
  • The practical consequence followed over the next decade.
  • A company could host millions of posts, photographs, reviews, and listings without reviewing each one as a publisher reviews a page, and the social media platforms of section F, the auction and review sites of section D, and the comment and search services in between all grew behind that shield.
  • Students do not need to judge the shield today.
  • They need to be able to state it, date it to 1996, and name what grew behind it.

Slide 12 · Section D: Commerce, boom, and bust (divider)

On the slide: SECTION D · Commerce, Boom, and Bust · Shopping moves online, prices run ahead of it, and the network outlives the crash.

  • [Keep the two stories of this section apart, because the lesson's point is that students often merge them.]
  • The first story is commerce: the buying and selling of goods and services over the Internet, growing from almost nothing in the middle 1990s into a central part of American retail.
  • That growth was steady, year by year, and it never reversed.
  • The second story is speculation: investors bidding the shares of Internet companies, many with no profit, to prices that assumed the commerce of a generation would arrive in a few years.
  • That story rose to a peak on March 10, 2000 and then collapsed, erasing trillions of dollars in paper wealth by 2002.
  • The activity file gives students the index record itself, stripped of any company's advertising, so they can see the shape of the boom in four numbers.
  • Teach the section's closing comparison explicitly: contemporaries reached for the railroad booms of the nineteenth century, when speculation also collapsed and the network it paid to build also remained in use.

Slide 13 · The store and the stock price part company

On the slide: D · COMMERCE · The store and the stock price part company · ▪ E-commerce grew from almost nothing in the middle 1990s. ▪ Books, auctions, and then nearly everything were sold on a screen. ▪ Investors bought companies with a web address and no profit. ▪ The bubble ended in the crash of 2000 and 2001.

  • [Give the two definitions in full, side by side, so students can hold them apart.]
  • e-commerce: the buying and selling of goods and services over the Internet, which grew from almost nothing in the middle 1990s into a central part of American retail
  • dot-com bubble: the speculative boom in Internet company stocks in the late 1990s, ending in a crash in 2000 and 2001 that erased trillions of dollars in paper wealth
  • The teaching difficulty is that the same names appear in both stories.
  • An online bookseller was at once a real store teaching millions of Americans to enter a card number on a screen, and a stock priced as if every store in the country would soon close.
  • The store was real. The price was not, and the crash sorted the two.
  • Hundreds of firms failed, and their workers and investors paid the bill the speculation had run up.
  • The commerce itself, the habit of buying on a screen, kept growing every year through the crash, which is the evidence students should carry into the writing task.

Slide 14 · 5,048: the NASDAQ at its peak

On the slide: D · THE BUST · 5,048 · the NASDAQ Composite at its peak · March 10, 2000 · Down about 78 percent by October 2002, to about 1,114

  • 5,048
  • [Let the number and its fall do the teaching, in the record's own terms.]
  • The NASDAQ Composite, the index heavy with technology and Internet stocks, closed at its peak of 5,048 on March 10, 2000, having first closed above 1,000 less than five years earlier, on July 17, 1995.
  • By October 9, 2002 it closed at about 1,114, a fall of about 78 percent from the peak.
  • Teach what a fall of that size means in a household.
  • A retirement account or a college fund invested at the peak held about twenty two cents of every dollar by the bottom, and the index did not return to its peak for fifteen years.
  • Then make the distinction the last slide built.
  • These are paper prices for shares, not measures of the network's use.
  • In the same thirty months that the index lost about 78 percent, the Census Bureau's figures showed household Internet access still rising, and online shopping still growing.
  • Prices collapsed; the transformation did not.

Slide 15 · Section E: The news world fractures (divider)

On the slide: SECTION E · The News World Fractures · News runs all day from 1980, the other side becomes optional in 1987, and the audience sorts itself.

  • [Note that this section runs on its own track, and that it begins before the public Internet exists.]
  • Cable news starts in 1980, the Fairness Doctrine falls in 1987, and national talk radio grows in the late 1980s, all before a browser sat in an ordinary home.
  • The Internet then deepened a fracture it did not start, draining the advertising that paid for newspaper reporting and giving every tendency its own online edition.
  • Teach the section's factual discipline explicitly, following the reading.
  • The fragmentation is stated as a measured fact of audience data: how many channels existed, when they launched, who listened and watched, and where the advertising went.
  • It is not taught as an accusation against any channel, host, or audience, and students' verdicts in this lesson are graded on evidence, never on which news world they prefer.
  • That discipline is the same one the unit applies to every contested subject, and this is a good hour to say so plainly.

Slide 16 · All day news, and the end of the other side

On the slide: E · THE NEWS · All day news, and the end of the other side · ▪ CNN went on the air on June 1, 1980. ▪ News became a continuous flow, not a nightly report. ▪ The Fairness Doctrine was repealed in 1987. ▪ National talk radio grew in the space that opened. ▪ Rival cable channels launched in 1996.

  • [Give the definition in full first.]
  • cable news: television news carried by cable channels around the clock, beginning with CNN in 1980, which replaced the nightly broadcast as many Americans' main source of news
  • Work through the machinery in order.
  • A channel filling twenty four hours cannot wait for the day's events to settle; it reports the flow itself, returns to the strongest story for hours, and treats every development as urgent.
  • In 1987 the Federal Communications Commission repealed the Fairness Doctrine, the rule that had required broadcasters to present contrasting views on public issues.
  • National talk radio, carried from a single studio to stations across the country, grew quickly from 1988 in the space that opened, and its hosts spoke to their listeners as a side, not as neutral reporters.
  • In 1996 two rival cable channels, MSNBC and Fox News, launched with distinct audiences and outlooks.
  • The common news day, built on three networks and a local paper, now had machinery working against it from several directions at once.

Slide 17 · The advertising leaves, the audience sorts

On the slide: E · THE NEWS · The advertising leaves, the audience sorts · ▪ Classified advertising drained toward free online listings. ▪ Wider advertising followed the audience to the screen. ▪ The reporting that advertising paid for shrank with it. ▪ A citizen could live inside a news world of one tendency.

  • [Teach this slide as an economics lesson before it is a media lesson.]
  • A metropolitan newspaper of the old model was paid for less by its readers than by its advertisers, and its classified pages, jobs, cars, apartments, and personals, were among the most profitable pages ever printed.
  • Free and cheap online listings took the classifieds first, because a searchable listing is simply better for a buyer than a column of small print.
  • The wider advertising then followed the audience to the screen, and the reporting staffs the money had supported shrank, bureau by bureau, across the 2000s.
  • State the result in the audience surveys' terms.
  • Where a generation earlier most households drew on the same few broadcasts and the same local paper, a citizen could now spend the day inside a news world of one tendency, hearing chiefly the accounts that fitted it.
  • Hold the discipline of the last divider: this is a statement about audiences and money, measured in surveys and accounts, and it sets up the political argument of the unit's last lesson.

Slide 18 · Section F: Publication in every pocket (divider)

On the slide: SECTION F · Publication in Every Pocket · From the middle 2000s, anyone with an account is a publisher, and the network joins the person.

  • [Introduce the second transformation, beginning from the middle 2000s, after the crash and after broadband reached the home.]
  • The first transformation had put a connected machine on the desk.
  • The second put publication into the hands of the machine's user and then put the machine into the user's pocket.
  • Stress the word publication, because it is the section's hinge.
  • For five centuries, publishing required a press, a transmitter, or a studio, and the people who owned those tools decided what the public read and heard.
  • The older media in this course, from the pamphlet to the network broadcast, all ran through such gatekeepers.
  • Social media removed the gate: a photograph taken on a street corner and an official statement from a government now traveled the same channels at the same speed, and readers sorted out which to trust.
  • The smartphone, sold in large numbers after 2007, then joined the network to the person, so the transformation stopped being a place a person visited and became a condition of the day.

Slide 19 · Everyone a publisher, everywhere

On the slide: F · THE SECOND TRANSFORMATION · Everyone a publisher, everywhere · ▪ Social media grew from the middle 2000s. ▪ Users published their own words, photographs, and video. ▪ The smartphone after 2007 joined the network to the person. ▪ Work followed onto the same small screen. · Photograph: a UNIVAC 1108 installation at the Census Bureau.

  • Picture: A UNIVAC 1108 installation at the Census Bureau, public domain on its Commons file page, shown at the right of the slide. Its distance from the subject is the point: within one working lifetime, computing went from a room a specialist tended to a screen in every pocket. Caption it for exactly what it shows, not as a smartphone or a social media image.
  • [Give the definition in full.]
  • social media: the Internet platforms, growing from the middle 2000s, on which users publish their own words, photographs, and video and build networks of followers and friends
  • Work through the smartphone's effect on work carefully, because it completes the lesson question's first noun.
  • Mail and documents moved onto the same small screen as news and shopping, so the boundary between office hours and the rest of the day thinned for millions of workers.
  • By 2010 an American could shop, bank, argue, publish, and answer an employer without sitting down at a desk, and the desk machine of section A had become the ancestor of a device carried against the body all day.

Slide 20 · Section G: The divide (divider)

On the slide: SECTION G · The Divide and the Country It Made · The transformation reached everyone at a different speed, and the federal surveys measured the difference.

  • [Close the lesson with the limit on every claim made in it, resting on federal surveys students can name.]
  • The National Telecommunications and Information Administration, in the Department of Commerce, published its Falling Through the Net surveys in 1998, 1999, and 2000, built on Census Bureau data, and gave the gap its working name, the digital divide.
  • Teach the section's shape: the same reports record rapid growth in every group they measured and wide gaps between those groups in the same tables.
  • Both halves are the record, and a student who keeps only one has not read the surveys.
  • The gaps followed the lines the vocabulary names: income, education, age, and place.
  • Access bought at a school or a library, valuable as it was, was not the same as access at home, where homework, job applications, government services, and commerce increasingly assumed a connection.
  • The writing task requires this section's evidence, so teach it as an argument students must answer, not as a footnote to a story of progress.

Slide 21 · Growth everywhere, gaps in the same tables

On the slide: G · THE DIVIDE · Growth everywhere, gaps in the same tables · ▪ Household access rose from 26.2 percent in 1998 to 41.5 percent in 2000. ▪ Top income households were about twenty times as likely to be online. ▪ Access rose steeply with education. ▪ Rural households trailed city households at every income.

  • [Give the definition in full to close the vocabulary.]
  • digital divide: the gap between Americans with ready access to computers and the Internet and those without, which followed lines of income, education, age, and place
  • Then work the figures in pairs.
  • The growth figures: the NTIA surveys counted household Internet access at 26.2 percent in December 1998 and 41.5 percent in August 2000, with computer ownership passing half of households in the same span.
  • The gap figures, from the same survey series: households earning 75,000 dollars or more were about twenty times as likely to have Internet access, and about nine times as likely to have a computer, as households at the lowest income level, and access rose steeply with education while rural households trailed at every income.
  • Ask: State, in one sentence, how both sets of figures can be true at once.
  • Answer: Growth and division together. That sentence is the honest summary of the whole transformation, and it is the sentence the writing task will reward.

Slide 22 · 20 to 1: the income gap in Internet access

On the slide: G · THE DIVIDE · 20 to 1 · the income gap in Internet access, 1998 data · NTIA Falling Through the Net · Households at $75,000 or more against the lowest income households

  • 20 to 1
  • [Use this number to correct the simplest version of the lesson's story, in which a technology arrives and everyone gets it.]
  • The NTIA surveys found that in the 1998 data, households earning 75,000 dollars or more were about twenty times as likely to have Internet access at home as households at the lowest income level, and about nine times as likely to have a computer.
  • Twenty to one is not a lag of a season; at the speed the network was spreading, it meant whole communities meeting the transformation years after others, on shared machines in schools and libraries, while connected households banked, applied, studied, and shopped from home.
  • Cost was the reason the surveys heard most often, which is why the gap follows income so closely.
  • Pair this stat with the growth figure from the last slide before moving on.
  • The divide is not evidence that the transformation was small.
  • It is evidence about its shape: fastest where money and schooling already stood, slowest where they did not, in the same years and the same country.

Slide 24 · Synthesis: what the thirty years changed

On the slide: SYNTHESIS · What the thirty years changed · ▪ Work moved from the desk machine to the pocket screen. ▪ Commerce put the store on a screen and survived its own bubble. ▪ News saw a common edition give way to chosen channels. ▪ Publication moved from gatekeepers to every account holder. ▪ Access followed income, schooling, age, and place.

  • [Draw the lesson together around the question's three nouns, because the exit ticket and the writing task both return to them.]
  • Work: computing began in institutions, moved to the desk with the personal computer, and left the desk for the pocket with the smartphone, thinning the boundary of the working day.
  • Commerce: e-commerce grew steadily from almost nothing into a central part of retail, and it is essential that students separate that steady line from the bubble's prices, which rose to 5,048 and fell about 78 percent while the shopping continued.
  • News: the common edition built on three networks and a local paper gave way to cable channels running all day, talk radio without an obligation to carry the other side, and platforms on which every citizen publishes.
  • Over all three stands the divide, measured by the federal government's own surveys: the transformation's reach was real, rapid, and uneven, and its unevenness is part of its history, not an exception to it.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: the machine, the network, and the boom

On the slide: THE VOCABULARY · Words for the machine, the network, and the boom · personal computer · a computer for one desk and home · Internet · the network of computer networks · e-commerce · buying and selling over the Internet · dot-com bubble · the late 1990s stock boom and its crash

  • These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.
  • personal computer: a computer built for one person's desk and home, sold in large numbers from the late 1970s onward, which carried computing out of institutions and into daily life
  • Internet: the network of computer networks that grew out of government and university systems into a public means of communication, commerce, and publication in the 1990s
  • e-commerce: the buying and selling of goods and services over the Internet, which grew from almost nothing in the middle 1990s into a central part of American retail
  • dot-com bubble: the speculative boom in Internet company stocks in the late 1990s, ending in a crash in 2000 and 2001 that erased trillions of dollars in paper wealth
  • Test the pair students most often merge: e-commerce kept growing through the crash that ended the bubble.

Slide 26 · Vocabulary: the news world and the divide

On the slide: THE VOCABULARY · Words for the news world and the divide · cable news · news by cable, around the clock, from CNN in 1980 · social media · platforms where users publish for themselves · digital divide · the access gap, by income, schooling, age, place

  • Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly.
  • cable news: television news carried by cable channels around the clock, beginning with CNN in 1980, which replaced the nightly broadcast as many Americans' main source of news
  • Students should anchor the term to its date, June 1, 1980, which places the fracture's start before the public Internet.
  • social media: the Internet platforms, growing from the middle 2000s, on which users publish their own words, photographs, and video and build networks of followers and friends
  • digital divide: the gap between Americans with ready access to computers and the Internet and those without, which followed lines of income, education, age, and place
  • Anchor that term to its measuring instruments, the Census Bureau counts and the NTIA Falling Through the Net surveys.
  • With these three terms the full set of seven is complete, in the guided notes, in these slides, and in the review materials for the unit test.
