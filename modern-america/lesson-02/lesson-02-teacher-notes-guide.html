The Reagan Revolution

Modern America · Lesson 2 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Modern America, Lesson 2 of 8. Teach it after Lesson 1, which ended with the postwar economic settlement breaking down, inflation and unemployment high together, a tax revolt organized, and a coalition assembled to answer President Carter's diagnosis with a different one. This lesson takes up the answer in power: a new administration set out to change what the federal government did, in taxes, in regulation, in labor relations, and in the courts, and it governed for eight years. Lesson 3 takes up the Cold War's end and the 1990s that followed.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “The Reagan Revolution” and the guided notes, which follow the deck in sections A to G: the mandate stated, the economic program, PATCO, the coalition held and widened, the limits of the revolution, Iran-Contra, and the other wars and the consoler. The activity is “The Revolution Ledger.” The notebook page is “Notebook Page: The Reagan Revolution.” The quick check is “The Promise and the Ledger.” The writing task is “Changed, and Left Standing.”

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Students need Lesson 1 of this unit: stagflation, the energy crisis, the tax revolt and Proposition 13, the New Right and the Moral Majority, and the coalition assembled for the election of 1980. No prior knowledge of supply-side economics, the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981, the PATCO strike, Iran-Contra, or the Tower Commission is assumed; each is taught in sequence.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (analyze connections among events: the inaugural mandate, the economic program, the PATCO firings, the widened coalition, the limits, the Iran-Contra breach, and the Challenger evening, in sequence), D2.His.9 (construct arguments using historical evidence: the writing task building a ledger of change and survival from the lesson's figures), D2.His.16 (assess options for individual and collective action: a strike by federal workers, a tax cut, and a presidential ultimatum weighed as forms of action); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from mandate to program to limits to breach), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task argued from the evidence bank). The lesson is tagged to blueprint entries 3 and 4.

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to argue both halves of the Reagan record with figures attached: the change column, a tax code rewritten in 1981 with the top rate brought from 70 percent to 50 percent, regulation loosened, inflation broken through a hard recession that put unemployment at 10.8 percent in the autumn of 1982, more than eleven thousand federal air traffic controllers fired in a single week in August 1981, a coalition widened to carry 49 states in 1984, and a judiciary restocked for a generation; and the survival column, Social Security and Medicare untouched, a tax increase accepted in 1982, and a national debt that rose from about 900 billion dollars in 1981 to about 2.9 trillion dollars by 1989, beside the Iran-Contra breach judged by the Tower Commission in February 1987.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes: opening, question, and map (slides 1 to 3) 5 minutes; the mandate stated (slides 4 to 6) 6 minutes; the economic program (slides 7 to 11) 9 minutes; PATCO (slides 12 to 14) 6 minutes; the coalition held and widened (slides 15 to 16) 4 minutes; the limits of the revolution (slides 17 to 19) 6 minutes; Iran-Contra (slides 20 to 21) 4 minutes; the other wars and the consoler (slides 22 to 24) 6 minutes; vocabulary, synthesis, and the turn to work (slides 25 to 29) 4 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: THE QUESTION FOR TODAY · What did the Reagan administration change about what the federal government did, and what did it leave in place? · Unit question: How did the United States remake itself after 1980, in its economy, its population, and its place in the world, and what did the remaking settle and what did it leave open?

  • [Read the lesson question aloud. It is an accounting question, and students will need both columns of the ledger for the exit ticket and the writing task.]

  • The government was not dismantled, and it was not left as it was found.
  • The lesson is the discipline of holding the change and the survival in one view, with the figures for each.

Slide 4 · Section A: The Mandate Stated (divider)

On the slide: SECTION A · The Mandate Stated · An inaugural address that named government itself as the problem.

  • [Set up the inaugural as a governing document, not a ceremony.]
  • Every president promises; this address is worth reading closely because the administration that followed treated its sentences as instructions.
  • Reagan had campaigned on the diagnosis that the federal government had grown beyond the people's control and beyond the economy's capacity to carry it.
  • The inaugural, delivered with the Iranian hostages released the same hour and the economy in stagflation, turned the campaign claim into an official one.
  • Tell students to listen for three things as the lesson works through the speech: the diagnosis, itemized and specific; the conclusion, which is not itemized at all; and the distance between the two, because much of the next eight years was governed inside that distance.

Slide 5 · Government is the problem

On the slide: A · THE MANDATE STATED · Government is the problem · ▪ The address was delivered on January 20, 1981, from the west front of the Capitol. ▪ Inflation was named an affliction of great proportions. ▪ Deficit had been piled upon deficit for decades. ▪ The federal establishment was to be curbed. · Photograph: President Reagan at the podium delivering the address, January 20, 1981.

  • Picture: President Reagan at the podium delivering the address on the west front of the Capitol, in a National Archives photograph. Former President Carter sits at the right of the frame; say so when you caption it.
  • [Read the claim in the speech's own build.]
  • The address first itemizes: an economic affliction of great proportions, the longest and one of the worst sustained inflations in the national history, a tax system that penalizes successful achievement, and public spending that has outrun even that tax burden, piling deficit upon deficit and mortgaging the future.
  • Only after the items does the conclusion arrive, in the sentence students will meet in the reading, the activity, and the quick check: in this present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem.
  • Have students notice what the sentence does not say.
  • It does not name a program, a bureau, or a tax.
  • It names government, whole, and the program that followed had to be built out of that whole cloth.

Slide 6 · What the address promised

On the slide: A · THE MANDATE STATED · What the address promised · ▪ The address promised to curb the size and influence of the federal establishment. ▪ It promised to lighten a tax system called a penalty on achievement. ▪ It promised to end the long trend of deficit upon deficit. ▪ It promised to act today in order to preserve tomorrow.

  • [Walk the promises as a checklist the lesson will grade at the end, because Section E is this slide read backward.]
  • The first promise is institutional: curb the size and influence of the federal establishment, the standing apparatus built since the New Deal.
  • The second is fiscal in the taxpayer's direction: lighten a tax system the speech describes as penalizing successful achievement and keeping the country from full productivity.
  • The third is fiscal in the government's direction: end the long trend of deficit piled upon deficit, which the address calls mortgaging the future for the temporary convenience of the present.
  • The fourth is timing: we must act today in order to preserve tomorrow.
  • Keep the list visible in students' minds.
  • By 1989 the record will show the second promise kept in law, the first kept only in direction and staffing, and the third not kept at all, which is the puzzle the limits section sets out to explain.

Slide 7 · Section B: The Economic Program (divider)

On the slide: SECTION B · The Economic Program · The president at Rancho del Cielo, August 13, 1981, the tax act signed there. · Photograph: President Reagan at Rancho del Cielo, August 13, 1981.

  • Picture: President Reagan laughing at Rancho del Cielo, his ranch in the Santa Ynez Mountains of California, on August 13, 1981, in a White House photograph by Michael Evans: the day he signed the Economic Recovery Tax Act there, in shirtsleeves, the bill stacked on the table beside him.
  • [Use the setting to make a teaching point about the program's self image.]
  • Its authors presented the tax cut not as a favor to the wealthy but as a restoration, returning decisions about money from the government to the people who earned it.
  • They expected the restored incentives to do the work that government programs had failed to do.
  • Whether the program's results matched that account is the argument students will conduct from the figures in this section, and both sides of the argument belong on the record.

Slide 8 · Supply-side economics

On the slide: B · THE ECONOMIC PROGRAM · Supply-side economics · ▪ The program cut tax rates to free work, saving, and investment. ▪ It loosened regulation on industry. ▪ Growth would follow production, not spending. ▪ The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 carried it into law.

  • [Teach the theory as its makers stated it, because the guided notes definition is built from their claim.]
  • The older Keynesian management of the economy worked on demand, putting money in consumers' hands so they would spend it.
  • Supply-side economics worked on the other side of the transaction: it held that high tax rates discouraged the activities the economy needed, work, saving, and investment, and that cutting rates and loosening regulation would free production and so grow the whole economy.
  • The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 carried the theory into law, cutting individual income tax rates by about a quarter over three years: a rate reduction credit against 1981 taxes, then new rate tables for 1982, 1983, and after, each lower than the last.
  • A companion budget act cut domestic programs while military spending rose.
  • Deregulation, begun under President Carter in airlines and trucking, was continued and extended in banking, communications, and energy.
  • The next slide takes the theory's most famous figure.

Slide 9 · 70 to 50: the top income tax rate

On the slide: B · THE ECONOMIC PROGRAM · 70 to 50 · the top income tax rate, percent, under the 1981 act · Rates cut by about a quarter over three years · A 1.25 percent rate reduction credit for 1981, then new tables for 1982 and after · The highest bracket fell from 70 percent to 50 percent

  • 70 to 50
  • [Give students this figure as the program in one pair of numbers.]
  • Before the act, the highest federal income tax rate stood at 70 percent, where it had stood, with brief changes, since the Second World War; a dollar earned at the top margin kept thirty cents.
  • The Economic Recovery Tax Act brought the top rate to 50 percent in its new rate tables, the last line of the 1982 table for joint returns taxing income over $85,600 at 50 percent of the excess.
  • The cut was phased in: a rate reduction credit of 1.25 percent against taxes for 1981, then the new tables for 1982, 1983, and after 1983, each lower than the last.
  • Supply-side theorists argued the rate itself was the policy: at 70 percent, effort and investment fled into shelters; at 50 percent, they would return to productive use.
  • Critics answered at the time that the cut delivered its largest dollar amounts to the highest earners and that the revenue it surrendered would return as deficits.
  • Both arguments are on the record of 1981, and Section E weighs the second one against the debt figures.

Slide 10 · The other half: Volcker's Federal Reserve

On the slide: B · THE ECONOMIC PROGRAM · The other half: Volcker's Federal Reserve · ▪ Interest rates were held above 19 percent. ▪ A recession ran from 1981 to 1982. ▪ Unemployment reached 10.8 percent. ▪ Inflation fell below 4 percent by 1983.

  • [Name the half of the story that was not the president's to command.]
  • The tax cut was only half the economic story of the first term.
  • The Federal Reserve, under Chairman Paul Volcker, an appointee of President Carter whom Reagan kept, set out to break inflation by holding interest rates above 19 percent, a level at which borrowing for houses, cars, and plant nearly stopped.
  • The cost was the deepest recession since the 1930s, running from the summer of 1981 to November 1982, with unemployment reaching 10.8 percent in the autumn of 1982, the highest rate since the Great Depression, on the figures of the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
  • The administration held its course and its chairman through the recession, taking the political damage in the midterm elections of 1982.
  • The reward came in the figures that followed: inflation, above 13 percent in 1980, fell below 4 percent by 1983, and the long recovery began.

Slide 11 · 10.8 percent unemployment, autumn 1982

On the slide: B · THE ECONOMIC PROGRAM · 10.8% · unemployment at the depth of the recession, autumn 1982 · Bureau of Labor Statistics · The highest rate since the Great Depression · Inflation fell from above 13 percent in 1980 to below 4 percent by 1983

  • 10.8 percent
  • [Pause on this figure, because it is the price tag of the era's central economic achievement, and students should be able to say who paid it.]
  • In the autumn of 1982, at the depth of the recession the Federal Reserve's interest rates had induced, 10.8 percent of the American work force was unemployed, about twelve million people, concentrated in the industrial states whose factory towns Lesson 1 described.
  • The rate is the Bureau of Labor Statistics figure, and it was the highest since the Great Depression.
  • The administration's political position fell with the economy in 1982, when its party lost seats in the House.
  • What the high rates bought was the end of the inflation that had defined the 1970s: from above 13 percent in 1980 to below 4 percent by 1983.
  • The recovery that began in November 1982 ran for the rest of the decade, and the coalition of the next section was built on its first two years.
  • Ask: Whose figure is the 10.8 percent unemployment rate, and what had it been the highest rate since?
  • Answer: The Bureau of Labor Statistics figure, and the highest rate since the Great Depression.

Slide 12 · Section C: PATCO (divider)

On the slide: SECTION C · PATCO: Labor's Terms Rewritten · One week in August 1981 that unions and employers both remembered.

  • [Give the section its facts whole, because they are short enough to state in full.]
  • This section covers the single most retold week of the administration's first year.
  • The Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization, the union of the federal controllers who staffed the nation's towers and control centers, had negotiated through the spring of 1981 and rejected the administration's contract offer.
  • Its demands, a ten thousand dollar raise, a shorter work week, and a better retirement package, went far beyond what other federal workers were offered.
  • On the morning of August 3, 1981, about thirteen thousand controllers struck.
  • Federal law forbade the strike, and the president, himself a former union president from his years leading the Screen Actors Guild, answered within hours.
  • What he did, and the terms on which he did it, occupy the next two slides.
  • Note: No federal photograph of the August 1981 strike with a verified public domain basis was used for this section, as recorded in the lesson's image sources, so PATCO is taught in text and a stat slide, and the activity teaches it from the president's own statement.

Slide 13 · 48 hours to report back

On the slide: C · PATCO · 48 hours to report back · ▪ About 13,000 controllers struck on August 3, 1981. ▪ The president read their sworn oath aloud. ▪ Controllers were told to return within 48 hours or forfeit their jobs. ▪ On August 5, more than 11,000 were fired.

  • [Read the ultimatum as a piece of argument, the way the activity asks students to do.]
  • The president's statement to reporters on August 3 built its case in steps.
  • First, the law: Congress had passed a law forbidding strikes by government employees against the public safety.
  • Second, the oath: each controller, on taking the job, had sworn an affidavit that he was not participating in any strike against the Government of the United States or any agency thereof, and would not so participate while an employee.
  • Third, the consequence: those who failed to report for duty were in violation of the law, and if they did not report for work within 48 hours, they had forfeited their jobs and would be terminated.
  • When the deadline passed on August 5, the administration fired the more than 11,000 who had not returned, barred them from federal service, and kept the air system running with supervisors, non-striking controllers, and military controllers at reduced capacity.

Slide 14 · 48 hours: the ultimatum of August 3, 1981

On the slide: C · PATCO · 48 hours · the ultimatum of August 3, 1981 · More than 11,000 controllers fired on August 5 · PATCO decertified that October · The ban on rehiring the fired controllers was lifted in 1993

  • 48 hours
  • [Hold the shortness of the deadline in front of the class.]
  • Forty eight hours converted a labor dispute into a test of whether the president's word was a fact, and both sides understood it in those terms.
  • When the hours ran out, the administration carried the firings through: more than eleven thousand controllers lost their jobs and were barred from federal service, a bar that stood until President Clinton lifted it in 1993, after which only a small number of the fired controllers returned to the towers.
  • In October 1981 the Federal Labor Relations Authority decertified PATCO, and the union that had called the strike ceased to exist.
  • The read of the episode by the people who watched it closest is the point of this section.
  • Unions took the firings as a signal that strike protection had thinned; employers, public and private, took them as permission to plan for permanent replacements, and did so through the decade.

Slide 15 · Section D: The Coalition Held and Widened (divider)

On the slide: SECTION D · The Coalition Held and Widened · Recovery converts the voters of the other party.

  • [Explain the electorate the rest of the unit will keep meeting.]
  • The revolution's political foundation widened in the recovery, and this short section explains it.
  • Reagan's original coalition, assembled in Lesson 1 out of the tax revolt, the New Right's organizations, and the South and West, was joined in office by voters the coalition's builders had not organized: working Democrats in the industrial states, many of them union members, who crossed party lines for the president while often keeping their party registration.
  • Political observers named them Reagan Democrats, and the guided notes definition of the term is one of the seven terms in this lesson.
  • Their crossing is the human fact behind the reelection figures on the next slide.
  • It set the pattern both parties studied for a generation: a presidential candidate could hold a working majority that his party's congressional candidates could not.

Slide 16 · Reagan Democrats, and 49 states

On the slide: D · THE COALITION HELD AND WIDENED · Reagan Democrats, and 49 states · ▪ The recovery ran strong through 1983 and 1984. ▪ Working and union voters crossed party lines. ▪ In 1984, Reagan was reelected carrying 49 states. ▪ Morning in America was the campaign's claim. · Photograph: President Reagan taking the oath of office, January 20, 1981.

  • Picture: President Reagan taking the oath of office on the west front of the Capitol on January 20, 1981, in a National Archives photograph. Nancy Reagan stands beside him in red, Chief Justice Warren Burger administers the oath, and Vice President George H. W. Bush watches from behind the glass.
  • The recovery that began in November 1982 ran strong through 1983 and 1984, with growth high, inflation low, and unemployment falling from the 10.8 percent of the recession's depth.
  • The political harvest was gathered in November 1984.
  • Reagan Democrats, the traditionally Democratic voters, often white working class or union voters, who voted for Reagan in 1980 and 1984 while often remaining Democrats, crossed in numbers large enough to carry states their party had owned.
  • The president was reelected carrying 49 states against former Vice President Walter Mondale, losing only Mondale's Minnesota and the District of Columbia.
  • The campaign's best known advertisement gave the coalition its slogan, the claim that it was morning again in America.

Slide 17 · Section E: The Limits of the Revolution (divider)

On the slide: SECTION E · The Limits of the Revolution · The entitlements stand, the debt triples, and the judges remain.

  • [Grade the revolution against its own program, as Section A set it out.]
  • Every revolution should be graded against its own program, and this section grades this one against the promises of Section A.
  • The administration had promised to curb the federal establishment and to end the piling of deficit upon deficit.
  • What the record shows instead is a government redirected rather than shrunk: taxed less, regulated more loosely, staffed with its appointees, and financed by borrowing on a scale no peacetime administration had attempted.
  • The section's three exhibits are the debt figures, the survival of Social Security and Medicare, and the quiet tax increase of 1982.
  • Its counterweight is the judiciary, the one arena in which the administration's changes were designed from the start to outlast it.
  • Students who can state both the exhibits and the counterweight have the argument of this lesson; students who can state only one side have a slogan.

Slide 18 · What the record shows by 1989

On the slide: E · THE LIMITS OF THE REVOLUTION · What the record shows by 1989 · ▪ The deficit grew year by year. ▪ Social Security and Medicare were left in place. ▪ A 1982 tax increase reclaimed part of the 1981 cut. ▪ Judges were appointed across the federal courts.

  • [State the limits in the record's own terms.]
  • The tax cuts, the military buildup, and the recession together reduced federal revenue, and spending was not cut to match, so the federal deficit grew year by year.
  • The entitlement core of the New Deal and Great Society order, Social Security and Medicare, was left in place; an early proposal to reduce Social Security benefits was withdrawn in the face of congressional opposition, and the administration did not return to it.
  • In 1982 the administration accepted the Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act, a tax increase that quietly reclaimed part of the 1981 cut it had celebrated the year before.
  • What the revolution changed least in the statute books it changed most durably in personnel.
  • Reagan appointed judges across the federal courts, among them Sandra Day O'Connor in 1981, the first woman on the Supreme Court, and the courts became the revolution's longest lasting instrument, still ruling on its premises after its statutes were amended.

Slide 19 · 2.9 trillion dollars: the national debt by 1989

On the slide: E · THE LIMITS OF THE REVOLUTION · $2.9 trillion · the national debt by 1989 · From about $900 billion in 1981 · The debt more than tripled under a president elected against deficits

  • $2.9 trillion
  • [Return to the figure Section A promised to watch.]
  • The national debt stood at about nine hundred billion dollars when Reagan took office, the accumulated borrowing of two centuries, two world wars, a depression, and the Great Society.
  • By 1989, when he left, it stood at about 2.9 trillion dollars, more than tripled in eight years of peace and recovery.
  • The arithmetic behind the figure is the argument of this section in miniature: the 1981 tax act reduced revenue on purpose, the military buildup raised spending on purpose, and the entitlement core that dominated domestic spending was left in place on purpose, after Congress refused the one early attempt to touch Social Security.
  • What remained was the deficit, the amount by which each year's spending exceeded the year's revenue, added each year to the debt.
  • Later arguments about the era begin here, with supporters crediting the borrowing with growth and defense, and critics charging that the revolution financed itself with the future it had promised to unmortgage.

Slide 20 · Section F: Iran-Contra (divider)

On the slide: SECTION F · Iran-Contra: The Breach · A foreign policy run off the books meets its investigators.

  • [State the two public policies and the private contradiction plainly.]
  • The era's largest scandal began as two policies the administration held in public and contradicted in private.
  • Publicly, the United States would make no concessions to terrorists and enforced an arms embargo on Iran; publicly, Congress had barred American military aid to the Contra rebels fighting Nicaragua's government.
  • Between 1985 and 1986, officials of the administration sold arms to Iran in hope of freeing American hostages held in Lebanon, and proceeds of the sales were diverted to the Contras.
  • The affair was exposed in November 1986, first by a Lebanese magazine's account of the arms dealing and then by the Attorney General's disclosure of the diversion.
  • This section teaches the affair through the judgment the president's own investigators delivered, the Tower Commission report of February 1987, which students read in the activity.
  • Note: No copy of the photograph of the president receiving the Tower report with a public domain basis verified on its file page was downloaded for this lesson, as recorded in the lesson's image sources, so the affair is taught from the report's text.

Slide 21 · What the Tower Commission found

On the slide: F · IRAN-CONTRA · What the Tower Commission found · ▪ The affair was a case study in the perils of policy outside orderly process. ▪ Procedures were ignored and reviews were too infrequent. ▪ Congress was never notified. ▪ The Board found no evidence the president knew of the diversion.

  • [Teach the Tower findings in the report's own order, and keep its two judgments distinct, because students habitually merge them.]
  • The first judgment is about the policy: the arms transfers to Iran ran directly counter to the administration's own policies on terrorism, the Iran-Iraq war, and military support to Iran, and the Board called the affair a case study in the perils of policy pursued outside the constraints of orderly process.
  • The second judgment is about the process: established procedures for making national security decisions were ignored, reviews by the National Security Council principals were too infrequent, and in all of this process Congress was never notified.
  • The Board's judgment on the president was double.
  • It found no evidence that he was aware of the diversion of proceeds to the Contras.
  • It held that he should have insured the system did not fail him, that he never insisted on the critical review and accountability the operation required.
  • Ask: On the diversion of proceeds to the Contras, what did the Board find about the president's knowledge?
  • Answer: No evidence that he was aware of the diversion.

Slide 22 · Section G: The Other Wars, and the Consoler (divider)

On the slide: SECTION G · The Other Wars, and the Consoler · Drug sentences and an epidemic at home, and one evening of national mourning.

  • [Name the discipline for contested subjects before either subject is taught.]
  • The lesson closes with the parts of the Reagan record that fit neither the program nor the limits.
  • It teaches them under the discipline this unit applies to every contested subject: claims attributed to their makers, and the record stated at the precision the evidence supports.
  • The War on Drugs expanded through the decade, with mandatory sentences written into the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 as crack cocaine spread, and the prison population rose steeply behind it.
  • The AIDS epidemic, first identified in 1981, killed tens of thousands of Americans in the same years, and the argument over the administration's answer to it is stated on the next slide as an argument, with each side's evidence named.
  • The section then turns to the presidency's other register, the consoler's, on the evening of January 28, 1986.

Slide 23 · Drugs and AIDS: the argued record

On the slide: G · THE OTHER WARS, AND THE CONSOLER · Drugs and AIDS: the argued record · ▪ Mandatory sentences were written into the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986. ▪ The prison population rose steeply. ▪ AIDS was identified in 1981, and tens of thousands were dead by the decade's end. ▪ Koop's 1986 report described the disease plainly.

  • [Take the two subjects in turn and keep the attribution discipline in front of the class.]
  • The War on Drugs is the campaign of intensified drug enforcement and mandatory sentences pursued by the federal government from the 1970s onward and greatly expanded in the 1980s, which raised prison populations across the country.
  • Its legislative peak in this lesson is the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986, passed in the alarm over crack cocaine, which wrote long mandatory minimum sentences into federal law, with penalties for crack set far heavier than for the same weight of powder cocaine.
  • On AIDS, the attributed record runs this way.
  • Activists charged that the administration answered the epidemic slowly, pointing to the president's public silence on the disease in its first years.
  • The administration pointed to rising federal spending on research and treatment and to the 1986 report of Surgeon General C. Everett Koop, which described the disease to the public in plain terms and recommended education rather than quarantine.
  • The lesson records both charges and grades neither.

Slide 24 · January 28, 1986: the consoler

On the slide: G · THE OTHER WARS, AND THE CONSOLER · January 28, 1986: the consoler · ▪ Ronald McNair was a mission specialist, one of the seven. ▪ Christa McAuliffe, a schoolteacher, was among them. ▪ The State of the Union was postponed that evening. ▪ The future belongs to the brave. · Photograph: Ronald McNair, NASA portrait.

  • Picture: The NASA portrait of Ronald McNair, a physicist and mission specialist on the Challenger crew, in his flight suit, with a model of the shuttle behind him. The portrait is used in place of a launch photograph, deliberately.
  • The seven who died on January 28, 1986 were Commander Dick Scobee, Pilot Mike Smith, mission specialists Judy Resnik, Ellison Onizuka, and Ronald McNair, payload specialist Greg Jarvis, and Christa McAuliffe, the New Hampshire schoolteacher chosen to fly as the first teacher in space.
  • The shuttle broke apart shortly after launch, in view of a national audience that included schoolchildren watching for McAuliffe.
  • That evening the president postponed his State of the Union address and spoke as consoler.
  • The address mourned the seven as pioneers, spoke to the schoolchildren who had watched, and committed the program to continue in the sentence students read in the activity: the future doesn't belong to the fainthearted; it belongs to the brave.

Slide 25 · The revolution at a glance

Slide 26 · Vocabulary: words for the program

On the slide: THE VOCABULARY · Words for the program · supply-side economics · cut rates and regulation to grow the economy · deregulation · removing or loosening government rules on an industry · Reagan Democrat · a Democrat who voted for Reagan in 1980 and 1984 · PATCO strike · August 1981: more than 11,000 controllers fired

  • These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.
  • supply-side economics: the economic theory behind the Reagan program, holding that cutting tax rates and reducing regulation would free investment and production and so grow the whole economy
  • deregulation: the removal or loosening of government rules on an industry, pursued from the late 1970s onward in airlines, trucking, banking, and communications
  • Reagan Democrat: a traditionally Democratic voter, often a white working class or union voter, who voted for Ronald Reagan in 1980 and 1984 while often remaining a Democrat
  • PATCO strike: the strike of August 1981 in which President Reagan fired more than eleven thousand federal air traffic controllers who had struck illegally, a blow unions took as a signal of the era's terms
  • Teach the four as the program's working vocabulary: the theory, the method, the voter it created, and the act that announced its terms.

Slide 27 · Vocabulary: words for the limits and the breach

On the slide: THE VOCABULARY · Words for the limits and the breach · federal deficit · a year's spending beyond the year's revenue · Iran-Contra affair · arms to Iran, proceeds to the Contras, 1985 to 1987 · War on Drugs · intensified enforcement and mandatory sentences

  • Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly.
  • federal deficit: the amount by which a year's federal spending exceeds the year's revenue, added each year to the national debt
  • Iran-Contra affair: the scandal of 1985 to 1987 in which officials of the Reagan administration secretly sold arms to Iran and diverted the proceeds to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua, against stated policy and a congressional ban
  • War on Drugs: the campaign of intensified drug enforcement and mandatory sentences pursued by the federal government from the 1970s onward and greatly expanded in the 1980s, which raised prison populations across the country
  • Keep the pairing in view for review: the deficit is the arithmetic of Section E, the affair is the breach of Section F, and the drug war is the domestic campaign of Section G that outlasted the administration that expanded it.

  • Changed: the direction of tax policy, with rates cut by about a quarter and the top rate brought from 70 percent to 50 percent; the regulatory stance of the government; the terms of labor conflict after August 1981; and the federal judiciary, restocked by appointment.
  • Broken: the inflation of the 1970s, ended by the Federal Reserve's rates through a recession that put unemployment at 10.8 percent.

  • Exposed: a foreign policy run in secret against the administration's own stated policies and a congressional ban, judged in February 1987 by the commission the president himself had appointed.
