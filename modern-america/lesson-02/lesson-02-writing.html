Writing Task: Changed, and Left Standing

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: What did the Reagan administration change about what the federal government did, and what did it leave in place? A strong answer works in both directions at once. It explains the changes, through the inaugural claim, the tax act of 1981, deregulation, the breaking of inflation, and the PATCO firings, and it explains the survivals, through the debt figures, the untouched entitlements, and the tax action of 1982, ending with a judgment on which single change ran deepest. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. In his first inaugural address, on January 20, 1981, Ronald Reagan declared that government is not the solution to the problem, government is the problem, and promised to curb the size and influence of the federal establishment.

2. The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 cut individual income tax rates by about a quarter over three years: a rate reduction credit of 1.25 percent against 1981 taxes, then new rate tables for 1982 and after, in which the highest rate fell from 70 percent to 50 percent.

3. Deregulation, begun under President Carter in airlines and trucking, was continued and extended in banking, communications, and energy. Paul Volcker, chairman of the Federal Reserve, broke inflation by holding interest rates above 19 percent; unemployment reached 10.8 percent in the autumn of 1982, and inflation fell from above 13 percent in 1980 to below 4 percent by 1983.

4. On August 3, 1981 about 13,000 air traffic controllers struck, in defiance of federal law and their sworn oath. Reagan gave them 48 hours to return and on August 5 fired the more than 11,000 who had not. PATCO was decertified that fall, and unions and employers read the episode as the era's terms for strikes.

5. The national debt rose from about 900 billion dollars in 1981 to about 2.9 trillion dollars by 1989. Social Security and Medicare were left in place, and in 1982 the administration accepted a tax increase that reclaimed part of the 1981 cut.

6. Reagan appointed judges across the federal courts, among them Sandra Day O'Connor, the first woman on the Supreme Court. In the Iran-Contra affair of 1985 to 1987, administration officials secretly sold arms to Iran and diverted the proceeds to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua, against stated policy and a congressional ban; the Tower Commission reported in February 1987 that established procedures had been ignored and Congress never notified.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the lesson question, what the administration changed about what the federal government did and what it left in place. (Blueprint 3 and Blueprint 4)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank items 1 through 4 support the change half: the inaugural claim of January 20, 1981, the tax act's staged cuts and the top rate's fall from 70 percent to 50 percent, deregulation extended and inflation broken at the cost of 10.8 percent unemployment, and the PATCO firings of August 1981. (Blueprint 3) Bank items 5 and 6 support the survival half and the long instruments: the debt's rise from about 900 billion dollars to about 2.9 trillion, the untouched entitlements and the 1982 tax increase, the judicial appointments, and the Iran-Contra breach. (Blueprint 4) A full credit essay draws on both groups.
  • Figures used as evidence, not decoration. The rates 70 and 50, the 1.25 percent credit of 1981, the 10.8 percent, the 11,000 fired, and the debt figures each mark a step in the argument, and strong essays say what the step is.
  • A closing judgment on the deepest change that follows from the evidence. Any of the candidates earns full credit when argued: the tax act, as the one change written into every taxpayer's standing obligation; PATCO, as the change that traveled furthest beyond the government's own workforce; the judiciary, as the change built to outlast the administration; or the debt itself, as the change no one in the administration had promised. Weak judgments merely repeat the claim.

Model opening for teacher reference. The Reagan administration changed what the federal government did most plainly where the inaugural pointed: it cut the tax rates the government collected, loosened the rules the government enforced, and fired the government's own striking workers when they defied the law and their oath. The tax act of 1981 cut rates by about a quarter over three years and halved nothing so visibly as the top rate, from 70 percent to 50 percent. PATCO set the terms of labor conflict for a generation of employers who never met the president. Yet the government the revolution inherited largely stood. Social Security and Medicare were untouched, part of the tax cut was reclaimed within a year, and the national debt more than tripled on the watch of a president elected against deficits. A full model continues in this pattern and closes by judging which of the changes, tax law, labor terms, courts, or debt, ran deepest, against the strongest rival.

Common weak answers. The dismantling answer (the administration shrank the government) cannot account for bank item 5, the debt figures and the untouched entitlements. The nothing changed answer cannot account for bank items 2 and 4, the rate tables and the fired controllers. The scandal only answer treats Iran-Contra as the whole record of the administration's conduct, which the tax and labor evidence contradicts in scale, though a student may argue it as the deepest change if the argument is made from the Tower findings rather than from the scandal's fame. Grade the use of evidence, never the side a student takes on the revolution's value.
