The Reagan Revolution

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Mandate Stated

Ronald Reagan took the oath of office on January 20, 1981, into the economy the last lesson described: double digit inflation, high unemployment, and a postwar settlement in doubt. His first inaugural address stated his case in one sentence: "In this present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem."

The address promised to curb the size and influence of the federal establishment and to end the long trend of deficit piled upon deficit. It called the tax system a penalty on successful achievement and promised to lighten it.

B. The Economic Program

The program's theory was supply-side economics. Its theorists held that cutting tax rates and loosening regulation would free work, saving, and investment, and so grow the whole economy. The centerpiece was the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981, signed that August. It cut individual income tax rates by about a quarter over three years and brought the top rate down from 70 percent to 50 percent. A companion budget act cut spending on domestic programs while military spending rose.

Deregulation, begun under President Carter in airlines and trucking, was continued and extended in banking, communications, and energy. The other half of the fight belonged to the Federal Reserve. Its chairman, Paul Volcker, broke inflation by holding interest rates above 19 percent. The cost was the deepest recession since the 1930s, ending in November 1982, with unemployment reaching 10.8 percent that autumn (Bureau of Labor Statistics). Inflation, above 13 percent in 1980, fell below 4 percent by 1983.

C. PATCO: Labor's Terms Rewritten

On August 3, 1981, about 13,000 members of the Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization struck for higher pay and shorter hours. Federal law barred strikes by federal employees, and each controller had sworn an oath not to strike. Reagan read that oath to reporters, gave the strikers 48 hours to report back, and on August 5 fired the more than 11,000 who had not returned. The fired controllers were barred from federal service, and the union was decertified that fall.

Unions and employers alike read the episode as a statement of the era's terms. A strike against a determined employer could now cost the strikers their jobs themselves, and private employers grew readier to hire permanent replacements.

D. The Coalition Held and Widened

The recession ended in November 1982, and recovery followed. Growth ran strong through 1983 and 1984, and inflation stayed low. Voters who had been Democrats for generations, many of them white working class and union voters in the industrial states, voted for Reagan and were named Reagan Democrats, and their crossing widened his coalition. In 1984 he was reelected carrying 49 states, under the claim of his campaign's best known advertisement, that it was morning again in America.

E. The Limits of the Revolution

The same record states the limits. Tax cuts, the military buildup, and the recession reduced federal revenue, and spending was not cut to match. The deficit grew, and the national debt rose from about 900 billion dollars in 1981 to about 2.9 trillion dollars by 1989. The entitlement core of the New Deal and Great Society order, Social Security and Medicare, was left in place; an early proposal to reduce Social Security benefits was withdrawn under congressional opposition. In 1982 the administration accepted a tax increase that reclaimed part of the 1981 cut. Its most durable changes were personnel: judges appointed across the federal courts, among them Sandra Day O'Connor, the first woman on the Supreme Court.

F. Iran-Contra: The Breach

The era's largest scandal grew out of foreign policy run in secret. In 1985 and 1986 administration officials sold arms to Iran, a government under an American arms embargo and declared a sponsor of terrorism, hoping to free American hostages held in Lebanon. Proceeds were diverted to the Contra rebels fighting Nicaragua's government, at a time when Congress had barred American military aid to the Contras. Exposed in November 1986, the dealings were examined by the Tower Commission, whose report of February 1987 found that established procedures for national security decisions had been ignored and Congress never notified. Officers of the National Security Council staff were convicted of offenses connected to the affair, convictions later overturned or vacated on appeal.

G. The Other Wars, and the Consoler

The administration fought other domestic wars. The War on Drugs brought longer and mandatory prison sentences in the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986, passed as crack cocaine spread through American cities, and the prison population rose steeply. The AIDS epidemic, first identified in 1981, killed tens of thousands of Americans during the decade. Activists charged that the administration answered the epidemic slowly. The administration pointed to rising federal spending on research and treatment and to the 1986 report of Surgeon General C. Everett Koop.

The presidency's other register appeared on January 28, 1986, when the space shuttle Challenger broke apart shortly after launch, killing its seven crew members, among them Christa McAuliffe, a schoolteacher. That evening Reagan postponed his State of the Union address and spoke to the country instead: "The future doesn't belong to the fainthearted; it belongs to the brave." The record of these years leaves the lesson's opening question open. How much of the New Deal order had the revolution actually reversed, and how much had it left standing?

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the claim of the first inaugural, and name two promises beside it. The claim, in the words of the address of January 20, 1981: "In this present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem." The address also promised to curb the size and influence of the federal establishment, to lighten a tax system it called a penalty on successful achievement, and to end the long trend of deficit piled upon deficit. Any two promises earn the second half of the credit.

2. Describe the economic program in its two halves. The legislative half was the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981, the centerpiece of supply-side economics, which cut income tax rates by about a quarter over three years and brought the top rate from 70 percent to 50 percent, beside a budget act that cut domestic spending while military spending rose, and deregulation extended. The monetary half belonged to the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker, who broke inflation by holding interest rates above 19 percent, at the cost of the 1981 to 1982 recession, unemployment reaching 10.8 percent (Bureau of Labor Statistics). Inflation fell from above 13 percent in 1980 to below 4 percent by 1983.

3. Tell the PATCO story in order, and explain the reading of it. On August 3, 1981 about 13,000 members of the Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization struck for higher pay and a shorter work week, although federal law barred strikes by federal employees and each controller had sworn an oath not to strike. Reagan read the oath to reporters and gave the strikers 48 hours to report back. On August 5 he fired the more than 11,000 who had not returned, barred them from federal service, kept the airports running with supervisors and military controllers, and the union was decertified that fall. Unions and employers read it as the era's terms because a strike against a determined employer could now cost the strikers their jobs themselves.

4. State the revolution's limits from the record. The national debt rose from about 900 billion dollars in 1981 to about 2.9 trillion dollars by 1989, as tax cuts, the military buildup, and the recession reduced revenue faster than spending was cut. Social Security and Medicare were left in place, and an early proposal to reduce Social Security benefits was withdrawn under congressional opposition. In 1982 the administration accepted a tax increase that reclaimed part of the 1981 cut. Strong answers add the counterweight: in judicial appointments, the revolution's effects ran longest.

5. What did the administration change, and what did it leave in place? Full credit answers hold both halves together. Changed: the direction of tax policy, with rates cut by about a quarter and the top rate brought from 70 percent to 50 percent in 1981; the terms of labor conflict, set by the PATCO firings of August 1981; the regulatory stance of the federal government, extended from the Carter beginnings in airlines and trucking into banking, communications, and energy; the judiciary, stocked by appointments across the federal courts; and the conduct of foreign policy in secret, exposed in the Iran-Contra affair. Left in place: the entitlement core of Social Security and Medicare; a federal establishment whose spending the deficits enlarged rather than shrank, the debt rising from about 900 billion dollars to about 2.9 trillion; and, after 1982, part of the tax cut itself. Grade the use of evidence, not the side taken on the revolution's value.
