A Changing America: Immigration, Cities, and the Sun Belt

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Door Reopened

American immigration law sorted newcomers by the country of their birth. The national origins quota system, built in the 1920s, gave large quotas to northern and western Europe and small ones elsewhere, and almost none to Asia. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, the law that ended the national origins quota system in place since the 1920s and opened American immigration chiefly to family reunification and needed skills, transforming the sources of immigration, replaced that sorting with a preference system. Relatives of citizens and residents stood at the head of the line, and workers with needed skills followed. President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the act on October 3, 1965, at the foot of the Statue of Liberty. Its supporters predicted little change in the level of immigration.

B. The Transformation in the Numbers

The prediction proved wrong. In 1970 the Census Bureau counted about 9.6 million foreign born residents, 4.7 percent of the population, a record low share. By 2010 it counted about 40 million, about 13 percent of the population, the largest immigrant population in the nation's history in absolute terms. The sources changed with the size. In 1960 the largest immigrant groups came from Europe and Canada. By the 2000s the largest came from Mexico, the Philippines, India, China, and Vietnam. Family reunification did much of the work, because each new citizen could sponsor relatives, who could later sponsor their own. Refugees admitted under separate programs, from Cuba, Southeast Asia, and the Soviet Union, added to the flow.

C. The Settlement of 1986

Growth brought argument, and the argument produced a bargain. By the 1980s several million people lived in the country without legal authorization, many of them at work. The Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986, the law that granted legal status to about three million unauthorized immigrants already in the country and made it illegal for employers knowingly to hire unauthorized workers, joined two halves. The first half was amnesty, in immigration law, a grant of legal status to people who entered or remained in the country without authorization, as the 1986 act gave to those who had arrived before 1982. About three million people gained legal status under it. The second half made it unlawful for an employer knowingly to hire an unauthorized worker. Supporters called it a settlement. Critics answered in later years that the legalization was carried out and the workplace enforcement was not.

D. A Reckoning with an Older Wrong

The same decades also returned to an older wrong. During the Second World War the federal government, acting under Executive Order 9066, had forced about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, most of them American citizens, from their West Coast homes into camps. A federal commission investigated and reported in 1983 in Personal Justice Denied. The commission concluded that the wartime decisions were not justified by military necessity and named their broad historical causes as race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership. In 1988 Congress acted on the report. The Civil Liberties Act apologized on behalf of the nation and authorized a payment of 20,000 dollars to each surviving person who had been removed. President Ronald Reagan signed the act, and the first payments went out in 1990.

E. Americans on the Move

Newcomers were only half of the remaking. Americans themselves were moving, from the older industrial North and Midwest toward the South and the West. The Sun Belt, the fast-growing region of the South and Southwest, whose population, jobs, and political weight rose from the 1970s onward on air conditioning, defense and technology work, and lower costs, drew the movers. Air conditioning made its summers workable year round. Defense plants, military bases, and technology firms supplied jobs, and land and housing cost less than in the older states. Texas, Florida, Arizona, and the Carolinas grew decade after decade, while Michigan, Ohio, and West Virginia grew slowly or lost people. Every ten years the census moved House seats and electoral votes after the movers. The South and West gained House seats in every reapportionment from 1970 onward.

F. The Older Cities Hollowed

The other end of the moving vans was the older industrial city. Deindustrialization, the long decline of manufacturing employment in the United States from the 1970s onward, as factories closed, automated, or moved abroad, emptied factory districts that had drawn earlier generations north. White flight, the movement of white families from cities to suburbs in the decades after the Second World War, accelerated by school desegregation orders and changing neighborhoods, which shrank city tax bases, had already carried much of the middle class beyond the city line. Detroit records the combined result. The Census Bureau counted about 1.85 million people in Detroit in 1950. It counted about 714,000 in 2010. Neighborhoods emptied, and the city's government shrank with its tax base.

G. Revival at the Center, and the Map That Resulted

From the 1990s, some cities told a second story beside the first. Workers and families moved into neighborhoods near the centers of the strongest cities, and investment followed. Gentrification, the movement of wealthier residents and new investment into poorer city neighborhoods, raising prices and rents and often pushing out the people who lived there, rebuilt housing and storefronts in parts of New York, Washington, and other prosperous cities. Many older industrial cities saw little of the revival. By 2000 the country's map had been redrawn by two movements at once, the newcomers admitted under the law of 1965 and its own people in motion. What that variety of origin, language, and faith required of the nation's laws and schools remained an open argument.

5. The Sun Belt's rise and the cities' decline as one movement. Strong answers hold the two ends together. Jobs, air conditioning, defense and technology work, and lower costs drew movers to the South and West, while deindustrialization and the earlier white flight emptied the older industrial cities and shrank their tax bases. Detroit's census figures carry the decline: about 1.85 million people in 1950 and about 714,000 in 2010, a loss of more than half.
