Writing Task: Which Force Redrew the Map?

The prompt. By 2020 the United States had a different population and a different map than it had in 1965. Three forces did the work. A law, the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, changed who could come. An economy in motion carried jobs and people south and west into the Sun Belt. And the factories' leaving, deindustrialization across the older industrial North and Midwest, emptied cities that earlier generations had filled. Answer this question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Which single force did most to redraw the country's map by 2020? Argue from the documents in the Changing Map File. A strong answer states what its force changed and how large the change was, follows the mechanism the documents give, and answers the strongest case for one of the other two forces. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each one supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson documents. Write the history in your own sentences, and keep every figure with the source the lesson names for it.

Evidence Bank

1. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 ended the national origins quotas and built a preference system on family reunification and needed skills, with immediate relatives of citizens admitted outside the ceilings. Its carriers predicted little change. (Activity, Station 1, summary record.)

2. The Census Bureau counted about 9.6 million foreign born residents, 4.7 percent of the population, in 1970, and about 40 million, about 13 percent, in 2010. By the 2000s the largest immigrant groups came from Mexico, the Philippines, India, China, and Vietnam, where in 1960 the largest had come from Europe and Canada.

3. The Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 granted legal status to about three million people who had arrived before 1982 and made it unlawful for employers knowingly to hire unauthorized workers. The commission report Personal Justice Denied and the Civil Liberties Act of 1988 show the same decades revising an older judgment about who had been wronged.

4. The Sun Belt grew on air conditioning, defense and technology work, and lower costs. Phoenix rose from about 107,000 people in 1950 to about 1.45 million in 2010 (Census Bureau), and the South and West gained House seats in every reapportionment from 1970 onward.

5. Deindustrialization closed, automated, or moved abroad the factories of the older industrial cities, and white flight carried families and tax base to the suburbs. Detroit fell from about 1.85 million people in 1950 to about 714,000 in 2010 (Census Bureau).

6. From the 1990s, gentrification brought wealthier residents and new investment into poorer neighborhoods of the strongest cities, raising prices and rents and often pushing out the people who lived there. Many older industrial cities saw little of the revival.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the question directly, naming one of the three forces, and rests on the size of the change that force produced rather than on which change the student finds most interesting. (Blueprint 9 and 10)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, with figures kept as the documents state them. Bank items 1 and 2 establish the law's case: a preference system whose family provisions compounded over decades, and a foreign born population rising from about 9.6 million and 4.7 percent in 1970 to about 40 million and about 13 percent in 2010. (Blueprint 9) Bank item 4 establishes the Sun Belt case: growth with named causes, Phoenix rising about thirteen fold, and House seats following the movers in every reapportionment. Bank item 5 establishes the factories' case: deindustrialization and white flight together, and Detroit losing more than half its 1950 population. (Blueprint 10) Item 3 supports any answer that weighs how the country regularized and reckoned with the population the first force admitted, and item 6 tests whether the student can place the partial city revival inside a verdict without letting it swallow the larger movements.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim through a mechanism. The law's mechanism is sponsorship compounding under family preferences. The Sun Belt's mechanism is jobs and costs drawing movers, with representation following the census. The factories' mechanism is employment and tax base leaving together, so that a city loses people, revenue, and services in one motion. An essay that lists three changes without a mechanism describes the map without explaining it.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest argument for a rival force. For the law: that its own ceilings were modest and its carriers predicted little change, answered by the preference structure and the relatives admitted outside the ceilings. For the Sun Belt: that its growth was itself fed by the newcomers the law admitted and the factories' leaving pushing movers out, answered by asking where the growth gathered and what followed it into Congress. For the factories: that Detroit's fall is one city and the Sun Belt's rise the wider fact, answered by the reach of deindustrialization across the whole older industrial region and the tax base it took with it.

Model opening for teacher reference. The law did most, because it changed the country's population itself, and every later argument in this lesson is an argument about the people it admitted. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 replaced quotas sorted by national origin with preferences for family and skills, and family sponsorship compounded: the Census Bureau counted about 9.6 million foreign born residents in 1970 and about 40 million in 2010, with the largest groups arriving from Latin America and Asia rather than Europe. The Sun Belt's rise moved Americans who were already here, a large change ably argued from item 4, and the factories' leaving emptied the cities of item 5. A full model continues in this pattern, weighs the rival it takes most seriously, and closes by naming what the verdict leaves unexplained, for the law the internal migration it did not cause.

Common weak answers. Retelling the lesson force by force describes the remaking without judging among its causes. Treating the 1965 act as the removal of all limits repeats the error Station 1 of the activity is built to catch: the act set ceilings and preferences, and its effects ran through them. Crediting one force with a change the documents assign to another, for example giving the Sun Belt credit for the foreign born figures or the law credit for Detroit's factory closings, shows the evidence bank was used as decoration. Grade the side a student takes by its evidence, never by the side itself: all three verdicts can earn full credit, and each has a model line in this guide.
