Guided Notes: A Changing America

Lesson question: How did the immigration opened in 1965 and the movement of Americans themselves remake the country's population and map by the twenty first century?

Unit question: How did the United States remake itself after 1980, in its economy, its population, and its place in the world, and what did the remaking settle and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965: the law that ended the national origins quota system in place since the 1920s and opened American immigration chiefly to family reunification and needed skills, transforming the sources of immigration

Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986: the law that granted legal status to about three million unauthorized immigrants already in the country and made it illegal for employers knowingly to hire unauthorized workers

amnesty: in immigration law, a grant of legal status to people who entered or remained in the country without authorization, as the 1986 act gave to those who had arrived before 1982

Sun Belt: the fast-growing region of the South and Southwest, whose population, jobs, and political weight rose from the 1970s onward on air conditioning, defense and technology work, and lower costs

deindustrialization: the long decline of manufacturing employment in the United States from the 1970s onward, as factories closed, automated, or moved abroad

gentrification: the movement of wealthier residents and new investment into poorer city neighborhoods, raising prices and rents and often pushing out the people who lived there

white flight: the movement of white families from cities to suburbs in the decades after the Second World War, accelerated by school desegregation orders and changing neighborhoods, which shrank city tax bases

Summary table. The door reopened: the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 ended the national origins quotas of the 1920s and built a preference system on family reunification and needed skills, signed by President Johnson on October 3, 1965, with supporters predicting little change. The numbers: the foreign born population rose from about 9.6 million and 4.7 percent in 1970 to about 40 million and about 13 percent in 2010, and the largest sources shifted from Europe and Canada toward Mexico, the Philippines, India, China, and Vietnam. The settlement and the reckoning: the 1986 act legalized about three million people and banned the knowing hire of unauthorized workers, and Personal Justice Denied led to the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, an apology and 20,000 dollars for each surviving person removed in the wartime internment. The moving country: the Sun Belt grew on air conditioning, defense and technology work, and lower costs, the South and West gained House seats in every reapportionment from 1970 onward, deindustrialization and white flight hollowed cities such as Detroit, and gentrification revived parts of the strongest centers from the 1990s.

Exit ticket 1. The 1965 act ended the national origins quota system in place since the 1920s and built a preference system that opened immigration chiefly to family reunification and needed skills.

Exit ticket 2. About 9.6 million and 4.7 percent in 1970; about 40 million and about 13 percent in 2010, both from the Census Bureau. Any two of Mexico, the Philippines, India, China, and Vietnam.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that pairs the two movements and uses the required evidence. Strong answers run the 1965 law's preference system through the foreign born figures, then turn to the internal movement: the Sun Belt grew on air conditioning, defense and technology work, and lower costs, House seats followed the movers, and Detroit fell from about 1.85 million people in 1950 to about 714,000 in 2010 as factories closed and families left for the suburbs.
