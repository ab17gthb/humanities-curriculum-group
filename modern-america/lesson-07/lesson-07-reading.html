The Great Recession and Its Long Aftermath

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Boom That Built the Collapse

For a decade after the middle 1990s, house prices rose and credit grew easy. Lenders issued subprime mortgages in large numbers, home loans made to borrowers with weak credit at higher interest rates, often with payments set to rise after the first years. The loans did not stay with their lenders. Banks bundled thousands of mortgages into securities and sold them to investors around the world, and rating agencies graded many of the bundles as safe. Financial firms borrowed heavily against the securities, so a small fall in value could wipe out their own money. Underneath the structure stood one assumption. House prices would not fall across the whole country at once. Each step paid a fee to the firm that passed the risk along, so loan volume grew even as borrowers' ability to carry the loans grew weaker.

B. The Failure in Sequence

House prices began falling in 2006 and the assumption failed. Borrowers who owed more than their houses were worth stopped paying, the securities built on their loans lost value, and no firm could value its own holdings or its partners' holdings. In March 2008 the Federal Reserve helped rescue the investment bank Bear Stearns. In September the government took over the mortgage companies Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. On September 15, 2008 the investment bank Lehman Brothers failed outright, the largest bankruptcy in American history. Within days a major money market fund broke under its Lehman losses, businesses found short term credit freezing, and officials at the Treasury and the Federal Reserve told Congress that the financial system stood days from collapse.

C. The Rescue and Its Terms

Congress answered in October 2008 with the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act, which created the Troubled Asset Relief Program, known as TARP, and authorized up to seven hundred billion dollars to steady the banking system by buying troubled assets and investing in banks. The Federal Reserve cut its main interest rate nearly to zero and lent on a new scale. The government carried the automobile industry through managed bankruptcies in 2009, and in February 2009 President Barack Obama signed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, a stimulus of about eight hundred billion dollars in spending and tax cuts meant to restart the stalled economy by putting money in consumers' hands. Supporters in both administrations said the alternative was a second Great Depression. Critics charged that the rescue saved the firms whose lending had built the collapse while their executives kept their pay.

D. The Bill as Households Paid It

The Great Recession ran from December 2007 to June 2009 by the National Bureau of Economic Research count. Unemployment reached 10.0 percent in October 2009 and about 8.7 million jobs were lost across the downturn, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported. Millions of families lost homes to foreclosure as prices fell, by roughly a quarter to a third from the 2006 peak by the Case Shiller measure, and household wealth fell by trillions of dollars in housing and retirement accounts, by the Federal Reserve's accounting. The official end in June 2009 changed little at first. Joblessness stayed near its peak into 2010, and the recovery was the slowest of the postwar era. Families who kept their jobs still owed more on their mortgages than their houses were worth.

E. The First Bill Signed

The first bill President Obama signed, on January 29, 2009, was the Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act. Lilly Ledbetter, a supervisor at a Goodyear tire plant in Alabama, learned near retirement she was paid less than men doing the same job. The Supreme Court ruled in 2007 that her claim was filed too late, because the filing clock ran from the original pay decision, not from her discovery of the gap. The act provides that an unlawful employment practice occurs each time wages, benefits, or other compensation is paid under a discriminatory decision, so the clock restarts with every paycheck.

F. Health Coverage Remade

In March 2010 Congress passed the Affordable Care Act, the largest change to American health policy since the 1960s. The law required most Americans to carry health insurance, barred insurers from denying coverage because of a prior illness or dropping it when a customer fell sick, let young adults stay on a parent's plan to age twenty six, and expanded Medicaid for people with low incomes. It passed Congress without a vote from the other party, and opponents challenged it in court and in every election after, charging that the requirement and the costs exceeded the federal government's proper reach. Supporters answered that coverage was the point. By 2016 about twenty million more people held health coverage than before the act, by the Department of Health and Human Services count. The Supreme Court upheld the law's central requirement in 2012, and repeal bills continued to reach Congress.

G. Two Answers and a Slow Recovery

The Tea Party movement rose in 2009 against the bank bailouts, the stimulus, and the health care law, demanding smaller government and lower taxes, and it reshaped Republican politics within two election cycles. Occupy Wall Street began in New York in September 2011 and spread to cities across the country, camping in financial districts to protest economic inequality in the name of the ninety nine percent against the one percent. One movement named the government as the problem. The other named concentrated wealth and the financial industry. Output and employment returned and passed their old marks, while household wealth and trust in institutions did not return on the same schedule.
