Writing Task: Who Was Rescued, Who Paid

The prompt. Answer this question in a developed essay of about 300 words: The rescue of 2008 and 2009 kept the financial system from failing. Judged from the documents, who was rescued, who paid, and who was left to the recovery? Build your answer in that order. First state what the rescue did and what it was for, from the statute. Then state the bill as households paid it, in the record's numbers. Then judge who was left to the recovery, and support the judgment with the recovery's own record: the Ledbetter Act, the health law and its coverage count, or the two protest movements' rival readings of the same years. A strong essay answers the strongest fact against its judgment. If you judge that the rescue served the public as the statute promised, you must deal with the household numbers. If you judge that it served the rescued firms first, you must deal with the statute's stated purposes and with the collapse officials said stood days away. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of October 2008 stated its purposes as restoring liquidity and stability to the financial system, used in a manner that protects home values and savings, preserves homeownership, maximizes returns to taxpayers, and provides public accountability. Section 115 released the TARP authority in stages, from 250 billion dollars to 700 billion dollars outstanding.

2. In September 2008 the government took over Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, Lehman Brothers failed on September 15, a money market fund broke, and credit froze. Officials at the Treasury and the Federal Reserve told Congress the system itself stood days from collapse.

3. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission concluded in 2011 that the crisis was avoidable, caused by failures in financial regulation, breakdowns in corporate governance, excessive borrowing by households and Wall Street, unprepared policy makers, and breaches in accountability and ethics. Commissioners who dissented filed separate views, one blaming government housing policy.

4. Unemployment reached 10.0 percent in October 2009, about 8.7 million jobs were lost, millions of families lost homes to foreclosure, and household wealth fell by trillions, by the counts of the Bureau of Labor Statistics and the Federal Reserve. The stimulus of February 2009 added about eight hundred billion dollars in spending and tax cuts.

5. The Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act, the first bill signed on January 29, 2009, restarted the pay discrimination filing clock with every paycheck, answering a Supreme Court ruling that had barred a claim as filed too late.

6. The Affordable Care Act of 2010 required most Americans to carry insurance, barred denial for prior illness, and expanded Medicaid. It passed without a vote from the other party, and about twenty million more people held coverage by 2016, by the Department of Health and Human Services count.

7. The Tea Party, from 2009, demanded smaller government and lower taxes and read the bailouts, the stimulus, and the health law as one government grown too large. Occupy Wall Street, from 2011, charged that corporations had taken bailouts from taxpayers and kept paying executive bonuses, in the name of the ninety nine percent against the one percent.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • An accurate statement of the rescue before any verdict. The statute's purposes and staged authority, bank item 1, set against the September 2008 sequence, bank item 2: the rescue was built to restore liquidity and stability under stated public conditions, at the moment officials judged the system days from collapse. (Blueprint 13)
  • The household bill stated in numbers. Bank item 4 carries the peak unemployment of 10.0 percent in October 2009, the 8.7 million jobs lost, the foreclosures, and the fall in household wealth, with the stimulus placed beside them. An essay that judges the rescue without these numbers has argued from the rescue's own record alone. (Blueprint 13)
  • A judgment about who was left to the recovery, carried by the recovery's record. The Ledbetter Act, bank item 5, shows the recovery government acting on pay discrimination first. The health law, bank item 6, shows coverage extended to about twenty million more people and a law passed on one party's votes and argued over in every election after. The two movements, bank item 7, give the judgment its rival readings, the government as the problem and corporate power as the problem. (Blueprint 14)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately and in date order, and a direct answer to the strongest fact against the judgment chosen. The Commission's verdict, bank item 3, serves both sides and damages both: it supports the charge that the failure was made by the rescued system, and its dissent records that the causes themselves were argued inside the government's own inquiry.

Model opening for teacher reference. The rescued were named in the statute before they were saved in fact. Section 2 of the Stabilization Act promised liquidity and stability for the financial system first, and protection for home values, savings, and taxpayers second, and the order held. In the autumn of 2008 the banks and the credit markets received seven hundred billion dollars of staged authority within weeks of Lehman's fall, while the households the same section promised to protect reached 10.0 percent unemployment a year later and lost homes and wealth on a schedule the recovery took years to reverse. On this record the rescue did what it was built to do, and the argument is over its design: whether a rescue that steadied the system first and reached households through a stimulus, a pay law, and a health law counts as a public rescue or as a rescue of the firms whose lending, by the Commission's verdict, had made the crisis avoidable. A full model in this line concedes the officials' judgment directly, that the system stood days from collapse in September 2008, and argues that conceding the emergency does not settle the terms on which it was answered.

Common weak answers. A verdict that the bailouts simply gave money to banks earns partial credit at most, because bank item 1 records the statute's stated purposes and staged limits and bank item 2 records the collapse officials described. An essay that uses the Commission's verdict without its dissent has read half of bank item 3. An essay that praises or condemns the health law without its coverage count, bank item 6, or without the party line vote the same item records, has argued a position rather than the record.
