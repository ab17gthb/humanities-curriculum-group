Guided Notes: The Conservative Turn: Stagflation and the Tax Revolt

Lesson question: Why did the postwar economic settlement break down in the 1970s, and who organized the politics that replaced it?

Unit question: How did the United States remake itself after 1980, in its economy, its population, and its place in the world, and what did the remaking settle and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

stagflation: the combination of stagnant economic growth, high unemployment, and high inflation that afflicted the American economy in the 1970s, defying the usual rule that inflation and unemployment moved in opposite directions

energy crisis: the shortages and sharp price rises in oil and gasoline that struck the United States in 1973 and again in 1979, when oil-producing states cut exports and revolution in Iran halted production

tax revolt: the movement of the late 1970s in which voters demanded lower taxes and limits on government spending, beginning with California's Proposition 13 in 1978 and spreading across the country

Proposition 13: the California ballot measure of 1978 that cut property taxes sharply and capped their future growth, the opening victory of the national tax revolt

New Right: the coalition of conservative activists, organizations, and voters that rose in the 1970s around lower taxes, a stronger military, and resistance to the social changes of the 1960s

Moral Majority: the political organization founded by the Reverend Jerry Falwell in 1979 to mobilize evangelical Christian voters behind conservative candidates and causes

southern strategy: the Republican electoral strategy of the Nixon years that sought white southern voters by opposing federal civil rights enforcement, busing, and the pace of change the movement had set

Summary table. The settlement: rising wages, cheap energy, and a federal government expected to manage the economy and keep unemployment low, all three failing by the middle 1970s. Stagflation and oil: inflation at 11.0 percent in 1974 and 13.5 percent in 1980 with unemployment at 8.5 percent in 1975, the combination the day's economics said should not happen together, accelerated by the 1973 embargo and the 1979 Iranian revolution, which produced shortages, sharp price rises, and gasoline lines. The trust crisis: Nixon's resignation on August 9, 1974, Ford's pardon in Proclamation 4311, the fall of Saigon in April 1975, and New York City near default that autumn. The organized answer: Carter's Crisis of Confidence diagnosis of July 15, 1979 answered from the right by the tax revolt, opened by Proposition 13 in June 1978, and by the New Right's machinery and the Moral Majority, joining southern strategy voters to suburban taxpayers for the election of 1980.

Exit ticket 1. Stagflation is the combination of stagnant economic growth, high unemployment, and high inflation that afflicted the American economy in the 1970s. Answers must carry all three parts and may add that the combination defied the usual rule that inflation and unemployment moved in opposite directions.

Exit ticket 2. Proposition 13 cut California property taxes sharply and capped their future growth in June 1978, proving at the ballot box that a tax limit could win by a wide margin. Campaigners and candidates carried the model across the country as the tax revolt, the movement in which voters demanded lower taxes and limits on government spending.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that names one cause of breakdown and one organizing body. The strongest answers pair stagflation or the energy crisis with the machinery that answered it: the New Right's direct mail and single issue organizations, the tax revolt campaigns, or the Moral Majority founded in 1979, and note the voters the coalition joined.
