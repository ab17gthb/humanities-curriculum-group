The Diagnosis File: What Had Gone Wrong by 1979?

Directions: Work through the five stations in order. Each station holds one record of the 1970s: a president's televised diagnosis, a constitutional amendment written by voters, a presidential pardon, a statistical record of the decade, and a proposed amendment that organized a politics of its own. For each record, name the diagnosis it carries of what had gone wrong. At the end, judge which diagnosis the election of 1980 rewarded and defend the judgment from the documents.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: A Crisis of Confidence, July 15, 1979

Source: Jimmy Carter, Crisis of Confidence Address, July 15, 1979 (Miller Center text), as carried in the audited TPSP record. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

... I want to talk to you right now about a fundamental threat to American democracy. I do not mean our political and civil liberties. They will endure. And I do not refer to the outward strength of America, a nation that is at peace tonight everywhere in the world, with unmatched economic power and military might.

The threat is nearly invisible in ordinary ways. It is a crisis of confidence. It is a crisis that strikes at the very heart and soul and spirit of our national will. We can see this crisis in the growing doubt about the meaning of our own lives and in the loss of a unity of purpose for our Nation. ...

In a nation that was proud of hard work, strong families, close-knit communities, and our faith in God, too many of us now tend to worship self-indulgence and consumption. Human identity is no longer defined by what one does, but by what one owns. But we've discovered that owning things and consuming things does not satisfy our longing for meaning. ...

... For the first time in the history of our country a majority of our people believe that the next 5 years will be worse than the past 5 years. Two-thirds of our people do not even vote. The productivity of American workers is actually dropping, and the willingness of Americans to save for the future has fallen below that of all other people in the Western world. ...

State Carter's diagnosis in his own terms. What does he say the fundamental threat is, and what two things does he say it is not?

Name two symptoms Carter lists as evidence, and state what habit of life he charges Americans with adopting in place of hard work.

Station 2: Proposition 13, Written Into the California Constitution, 1978

Source: California Constitution, Article XIII A, Section 1(a) (California Legislative Counsel, leginfo.legislature.ca.gov), added June 6, 1978 by Proposition 13. The sentences after the quotation are a summary of the article's assessment rules, prepared for this lesson.

SECTION 1. (a) The maximum amount of any ad valorem tax on real property shall not exceed One percent (1%) of the full cash value of such property. The one percent (1%) tax to be collected by the counties and apportioned according to law to the districts within the counties.

Summary of the article's further rules: a property's assessed value was rolled back and could then rise by no more than two percent a year until the property was sold, when it could be reassessed at its new price. The article also required a two thirds vote of the electorate for many new special taxes. The measure passed in June 1978 with about two thirds of the vote, and property tax bills fell by more than half on average.

State the limit the quoted section sets, with its number, and name the body that collects the tax under it.

This record carries its diagnosis in the form of a rule rather than an argument. What does the rule itself say had gone wrong, and whose bills does the summary say fell?

Station 3: The Pardon, September 8, 1974

Source: Gerald R. Ford, Proclamation 4311, Granting Pardon to Richard Nixon, September 8, 1974 (Ford Presidential Library), as carried in the audited TPSP record. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

... It is believed that a trial of Richard Nixon, if it became necessary, could not fairly begin until a year or more has elapsed. In the meantime, the tranquility to which this nation has been restored by the events of recent weeks could be irreparably lost by the prospects of bringing to trial a former President of the United States. The prospects of such trial will cause prolonged and divisive debate over the propriety of exposing to further punishment and degradation a man who has already paid the unprecedented penalty of relinquishing the highest elective office in the United States. ...

NOW, THEREFORE, I, Gerald R. Ford, President of the United States, pursuant to the pardon power conferred upon me by Article II, Section 2, of the Constitution, have granted and by these presents do grant a full, free, and absolute pardon unto Richard Nixon for all offenses against the United States which he, Richard Nixon, has committed or may have committed or taken part in during the period from January 20, 1969 through August 9, 1974.

State the pardon in its operative words: what kind of pardon, for what, and covering what dates?

Ford's diagnosis runs in the opposite direction from a trial. What does he say a trial would cost the country, and what penalty does he say Nixon had already paid?

Station 5: The Equal Rights Amendment, as Passed by Congress, 1972

Source: Equal Rights Amendment, House Joint Resolution 208, as passed by Congress, 1972 (Congressional Research Service text), as carried in the audited TPSP record.

Resolved by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled (two-thirds of each House concurring therein), That the following article is proposed as an amendment to the Constitution of the United States, which shall be valid to all intents and purposes as part of the Constitution when ratified by the legislatures of three-fourths of the several States within seven years of its submission by the Congress:

Section 1. Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex.

Section 2. The Congress shall have the power to enforce, by appropriate legislation, the provisions of this article.

Section 3. This amendment shall take effect two years after the date of ratification.

State what Section 1 forbids, naming both governments it binds and the ground it names.

The amendment organized two politics at once: supporters who campaigned for ratification and opponents who organized to stop it, and the seven year clock in the text ran out short of three fourths of the states. Explain, from the text, what a supporter in 1978 was racing against.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: A Crisis of Confidence

State the diagnosis, and the two things the threat is not. The fundamental threat is a crisis of confidence, striking at the heart and soul and spirit of the national will, visible in growing doubt about the meaning of people's lives and in the loss of a unity of purpose. Carter says expressly that he does not mean political and civil liberties, which will endure, and does not refer to the outward strength of America, a nation at peace with unmatched economic power and military might. Credit requires the diagnosis and both exclusions.

Name two symptoms, and the habit charged. Symptoms listed in the passage: for the first time a majority believed the next five years would be worse than the past five; two thirds of the people do not even vote; the productivity of American workers is actually dropping; the willingness to save has fallen below that of all other people in the Western world. The habit charged is the worship of self-indulgence and consumption, with human identity defined by what one owns rather than by what one does.

Station 2: Proposition 13

State the limit and the collecting body. The maximum amount of any ad valorem tax on real property shall not exceed one percent (1%) of the full cash value of the property, collected by the counties and apportioned according to law to the districts within the counties.

What does the rule say had gone wrong, and whose bills fell? A constitutional cap is itself the diagnosis: property taxation without a fixed limit had risen with assessments until the tax, not any single service it paid for, was the grievance, and the remedy voters wrote was to bind future governments rather than to replace officeholders. The summary reports that the measure passed with about two thirds of the vote and that property tax bills fell by more than half on average. The station's assessment rules are taught as a labeled summary on the station's face; the quoted Section 1(a) is verbatim from the California Constitution, Article XIII A.

Station 3: The Pardon

State the pardon in its operative words. A full, free, and absolute pardon unto Richard Nixon for all offenses against the United States which he has committed or may have committed or taken part in during the period from January 20, 1969 through August 9, 1974, granted under the pardon power of Article II, Section 2 of the Constitution.

What would a trial cost, and what penalty had Nixon paid? Ford argues that a trial could not fairly begin for a year or more, that the tranquility restored by recent events could be irreparably lost, and that the prospect would cause prolonged and divisive debate. Nixon, in Ford's words, had already paid the unprecedented penalty of relinquishing the highest elective office in the United States. Students should note the diagnosis the pardon carries: the wound, in Ford's account, was division itself, and critics of the pardon charged that it placed a former president beyond the law. Grade the reading of the document, not the side taken.

Station 4: The Decade Counted

Use the 1979 row, and explain the combination. In 1979 inflation ran at 11.3 percent while unemployment stood at 5.9 percent. Prices rising at a double digit rate alongside joblessness well above the low rates of the postwar decades is stagflation in figures: the standard economics of the day held that inflation and unemployment moved in opposite directions, so the pair standing high together is the anomaly the lesson names. The 1980 row strengthens the point, at 13.5 percent inflation and 7.2 percent unemployment.

What does the record establish about the settlement's promise? That the promise failed on its own terms. The settlement had promised managed growth with low unemployment and stable prices. The record shows prices rising at 6.2 percent as early as 1973, at 11.0 percent in 1974, and at 13.5 percent by 1980, with unemployment at 8.5 percent in 1975. A student who answers only that times were hard earns partial credit; the record's force is in the combination and the trend, not in any single figure.

Station 5: The Equal Rights Amendment

State what Section 1 forbids. Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex. The amendment binds both the federal government and the states, and the ground named is sex. Section 2 gives Congress the power to enforce the article by appropriate legislation.

What was a supporter in 1978 racing against? The clock written into the resolving clause: the article becomes valid only when ratified by the legislatures of three fourths of the several states within seven years of its submission by Congress. Passed in 1972, the amendment needed thirty eight states by 1979, and Section 3 delayed its effect for two further years after ratification. The text students read is the whole race: a supermajority of states, a deadline, and a delayed start, which is why the amendment organized ratification campaigns and a counter campaign at the same time.

Part 2: The Verdict (Grading Guide)

Carter's record diagnoses a crisis of confidence and of national purpose, a failure of spirit expressed in consumption and in lost faith in the future. Proposition 13 diagnoses government itself, its taxes rising without limit, and answers with a constitutional cap. Ford's proclamation diagnoses division as the wound and prescribes closing the Nixon account without a trial. The BLS record diagnoses a managed economy failing on its own measures, inflation and unemployment high together across the decade. The ERA diagnoses unequal rights under law on account of sex and answers with a constitutional amendment. Closing verdicts earn full credit when the diagnosis said to be rewarded is defended from at least two stations against a named rival. The strongest cases are usually made for the Proposition 13 diagnosis, since the election of 1980 was won on a program of tax cuts and limits on government that the tax revolt had already proven at the ballot box in 1978, and for a combined reading in which the BLS record supplied the grievance and the tax revolt supplied the remedy. A case for Carter's diagnosis can earn full credit only if it explains why a diagnosis the election's winner opposed still counts as rewarded, argued from the documents rather than asserted.
