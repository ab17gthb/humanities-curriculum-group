Quick Check: The Diagnosis and the Numbers

Directions: Read the passage and the background, then answer the questions.

The passage. It is a crisis of confidence. It is a crisis that strikes at the very heart and soul and spirit of our national will. We can see this crisis in the growing doubt about the meaning of our own lives and in the loss of a unity of purpose for our Nation. ... In a nation that was proud of hard work, strong families, close-knit communities, and our faith in God, too many of us now tend to worship self-indulgence and consumption.

Source: Jimmy Carter, Crisis of Confidence Address, July 15, 1979 (Miller Center text), as carried in the audited TPSP record. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

Background. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted inflation at 11.3 percent in 1979 and 13.5 percent in 1980, with unemployment at 5.9 percent in 1979 and 7.2 percent in 1980. One year before Carter spoke, in June 1978, California voters had passed Proposition 13, cutting property taxes sharply and capping them, and the tax revolt was spreading across the country.

1. In the passage, what does Carter name as the crisis, and what two losses does he say show it? Answer in your own words, using one phrase from the passage as evidence.

2. Carter charges Americans with worshipping self-indulgence and consumption in a nation once proud of hard work. State what has replaced what, in the passage's own terms.

3. The background gives the decade in Bureau of Labor Statistics figures. Explain why those figures describe a breakdown the passage never mentions, naming the combination the figures show.

4. A tax revolt campaigner in 1979 argues that the passage looks in the wrong place. State the campaigner's strongest point, using the background and the passage together.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What crisis does Carter name, and what two losses show it? A crisis of confidence. Carter says it can be seen in the growing doubt about the meaning of our own lives and in the loss of a unity of purpose for the Nation. Credit requires the named crisis, both losses, and a quoted phrase such as crisis of confidence or unity of purpose. (Blueprint 1)

2. State what has replaced what. Worship of self-indulgence and consumption has taken the place of the pride in hard work, strong families, close-knit communities, and faith in God that Carter sets against it. He states the exchange in the same passage: human identity, in the full address, is no longer defined by what one does, but by what one owns. (Blueprint 1)

3. Why do the figures describe a breakdown the passage never mentions? The passage diagnoses a failure of confidence and purpose and names no price or job figure. The background shows inflation at 11.3 percent in 1979 rising to 13.5 percent in 1980 while unemployment stood at 5.9 and then 7.2 percent: prices and joblessness high together, the combination named stagflation, which the standard economics of the day held should not occur. Students who name stagflation and tie both figures to it earn full credit. (Blueprint 1)

4. State the tax revolt campaigner's strongest point. The passage locates the failure in the country's spirit, in consumption and lost purpose, while a year earlier California voters had already located it in government itself, cutting and capping property taxes by a wide margin and starting a national tax revolt. A campaigner could say the voters had given their diagnosis at the ballot box in June 1978, and that a president reading the country's problem as its mood had missed the organized politics already replacing the settlement, the politics the election of 1980 then rewarded. Answers must work from the background's Proposition 13 record and the passage's placement of blame. (Blueprint 2)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Question 3 is the hinge diagnostic: students who can hold Carter's diagnosis and the Bureau of Labor Statistics figures in one answer have the breakdown half of the lesson. Question 4 predicts success on the writing task, because it requires the rival diagnosis to be stated from evidence rather than dismissed.
