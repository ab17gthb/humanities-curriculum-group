The Conservative Turn: Stagflation and the Tax Revolt

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Settlement That Was Expected

The generation that governed the United States after 1945 worked inside a settlement most Americans treated as permanent. Wages rose with productivity, and steady work could carry a family into the middle class. Energy was cheap, and gasoline and heating oil took a small share of a household budget. The federal government managed the economy through spending, taxes, and interest rates, and voters expected it to keep growth steady and unemployment low. Presidents were judged against that expectation, and for a quarter century the country mostly met it. By the middle 1970s all three parts of the settlement had failed at once.

B. Stagflation, Counted

The failure arrived as numbers no postwar president had faced together. Prices rose year after year while the economy stalled and jobs stayed scarce. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted inflation at 6.2 percent in 1973 and 11.0 percent in 1974, and after a brief easing the rate climbed again to 11.3 percent in 1979 and 13.5 percent in 1980. Unemployment reached 8.5 percent in 1975, stood at 5.9 percent in 1979, and was 7.2 percent in 1980. Economists named the combination stagflation. The standard economics of the day held that inflation and unemployment moved in opposite directions, so a government that fought one with the tools at hand made the other worse. Stagflation was the evidence that, for the moment, Washington could not manage the economy as the settlement had promised.

C. Oil as the Accelerant

Oil turned a difficult decade into a crisis of daily life. In October 1973, during a war between Israel and its neighbors, Arab oil producing states cut exports to the United States, and prices quadrupled within months. The United States had grown dependent on imported oil for its cars, homes, and factories, and the embargo exposed the dependence. Lines formed at gasoline stations in 1973 and 1974. In 1979 revolution in Iran halted that country's production, prices rose sharply a second time, and the lines returned, with stations rationing sales and states assigning driving days by license plate number. The two shocks together made the energy crisis, the shortages and sharp price rises in oil and gasoline that struck the United States in 1973 and again in 1979.

D. Trust Broken Before the Economics Bit

The decade opened with a wreck in politics before it became a wreck in economics. Watergate ended on August 9, 1974, when Richard Nixon resigned the presidency in a letter of one sentence. A month later President Gerald Ford issued Proclamation 4311, granting Nixon a full, free, and absolute pardon for offenses he had committed or may have committed in office. Ford argued that a trial would reopen a divisive wound; critics charged that the pardon placed a former president beyond the law. In April 1975 Saigon fell, closing the Vietnam account in defeat. That autumn New York City came within hours of defaulting on its debts, and the federal government at first refused a loan, making liberal city government look exhausted. Each episode taught voters the same lesson: institutions that had claimed competence could not deliver it.

E. A President Names a Crisis of Confidence

President Jimmy Carter answered on July 15, 1979, in a televised address remembered as his diagnosis of the decade. Carter said the fundamental threat to American democracy was not a foreign enemy or a failure of civil liberty. It was a crisis of confidence, a loss of faith that the future would be better than the past. He charged that too many Americans had come to worship self-indulgence and consumption, defining themselves by what they owned rather than by what they did, and he pointed to falling productivity and to a majority who believed the next five years would be worse than the last five. The address asked Americans to rebuild confidence and treat energy as a common effort. Critics answered that a president who blamed the country's spirit had described the symptoms while the prices kept rising.

F. The Tax Revolt Opens in California

The organized answer came from the right, and its first victory was won at the ballot box in California. Property values had risen fast in the 1970s, and rising assessments carried tax bills up with them, in some cases beyond what homeowners could pay. In June 1978 California voters passed Proposition 13 by a wide margin. The measure cut property taxes sharply, capped the tax at one percent of a property's value, and limited how fast assessed values could rise. It also required two thirds votes for many new taxes. The tax revolt spread across the country as voters demanded lower taxes and limits on government spending, and candidates learned that a promise to cut taxes could assemble voters who agreed on little else.

G. The Coalition Assembled, and the Hinge of 1980

Behind the revolt stood new machinery. Organizers of the New Right built direct mail lists, single issue groups, and research institutes that raised money and pressed a program of lower taxes, a stronger military, and resistance to the social changes of the 1960s. In 1979 the Reverend Jerry Falwell founded the Moral Majority to mobilize evangelical voters behind conservative candidates. The coalition's geography joined the white southern voters the Republican southern strategy had courted since the Nixon years to suburban taxpayers in the West and North. The argument over remedy ran alongside it. In June 1978, the same month as Proposition 13, the Supreme Court decided Bakke, striking down a medical school's fixed racial quota while allowing race as one factor in admissions. The election of 1980 stood ahead as the hinge, and the next lesson takes up the government the coalition elected.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Name the three expectations of the postwar settlement, and what happened to each. Rising wages that carried working families into the middle class; cheap energy that took a small share of a household budget; and a federal government that managed the economy and kept growth steady and unemployment low. By the middle 1970s wages had stalled against rising prices, energy had become expensive and at times unavailable, and the government's standard tools could not fight inflation and unemployment at once. Credit requires all three expectations with their failures, stated in the reading's terms.

2. Show stagflation in two different years, from the figures. In 1974 inflation ran at 11.0 percent while the economy stalled, and unemployment then reached 8.5 percent in 1975. In 1979 and 1980 inflation ran at 11.3 and 13.5 percent while unemployment stood at 5.9 percent in 1979 and 7.2 percent in 1980, high for a period of fast rising prices. The point students must make is the combination: the standard economics of the day held that inflation and unemployment moved in opposite directions, and the decade delivered both together, which is the definition of stagflation in the lesson vocabulary.

3. How were the two oil shocks alike, and how did each begin? Each sent oil and gasoline prices sharply up, produced shortages and lines at gasoline stations, and exposed the country's dependence on imported oil. The 1973 shock began when Arab oil producing states cut exports to the United States during a war between Israel and its neighbors. The 1979 shock began when revolution in Iran halted that country's oil production. Both shocks together constitute the energy crisis as the lesson defines the term.

4. State Carter's diagnosis and the tax revolt's diagnosis. Carter, in the address of July 15, 1979, diagnosed a crisis of confidence: Americans were losing faith that the future would be better, had turned toward self-indulgence and consumption, and had lost a unity of purpose, and he asked for a rebuilt national confidence and a common effort on energy. The tax revolt's diagnosis, stated by its campaigners and voters, held that government itself, its taxes and its spending, had become the burden, and that limiting taxes would discipline it. The two diagnoses located the failure in different places, in the national spirit and in the state, and the election of 1980 rewarded one of them.

5. Who organized the politics that replaced the settlement? Full credit answers name the machinery, the organizations, and the arithmetic together. The machinery was the New Right's direct mail lists, single issue groups, and research institutes, which raised money and pressed a program of lower taxes, a stronger military, and resistance to the social changes of the 1960s. The organizations included the tax revolt campaigns that won Proposition 13 in June 1978 and the Moral Majority, founded by the Reverend Jerry Falwell in 1979 to mobilize evangelical voters. The arithmetic joined white southern voters courted by the southern strategy since the Nixon years to suburban taxpayers in the West and North. A paragraph that names only Ronald Reagan earns partial credit, because the lesson's claim is that the coalition was built before it was led.
