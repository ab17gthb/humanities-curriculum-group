Quick Check: The Recount Stops, December 12, 2000

Directions: Read the passage and the background note, then answer the questions.

Source: Bush v. Gore, 531 U.S. 98 (2000), per curiam opinion (Cornell Legal Information Institute), as carried in the audited TPSP bank record bush-v-gore-2000. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

The right to vote is protected in more than the initial allocation of the franchise. Equal protection applies as well to the manner of its exercise. Having once granted the right to vote on equal terms, the State may not, by later arbitrary and disparate treatment, value one person's vote over that of another. ...

The recount process, in its features here described, is inconsistent with the minimum procedures necessary to protect the fundamental right of each voter in the special instance of a statewide recount under the authority of a single state judicial officer. Our consideration is limited to the present circumstances, for the problem of equal protection in election processes generally presents many complexities.

Background. Florida's certified margin in the presidential election of 2000 was 537 votes for George W. Bush. The Florida Supreme Court ordered a manual recount of ballots across the state, judged by the intent of the voter, with no uniform standard for judging that intent from county to county. The United States Supreme Court heard the case in December and ruled on December 12, 2000. Albert Gore had won the national popular vote. He conceded on December 13.

1. In the passage, what does the Equal Protection Clause govern besides the granting of the vote itself, and what kind of state treatment of voters does it forbid?

2. The Court found the recount inconsistent with the minimum procedures the right requires. From the background note, state the missing element in Florida's recount that produced the violation, and the certified margin that made the ruling decide the presidency.

3. Put these in order and give each date: Citizens United, Heller, Obergefell, Students for Fair Admissions, Bush v. Gore.

4. A student writes that the last sentence of the passage makes the decision a general rule for all recounts. Using the sentence itself, explain what the Court said about the reach of its own ruling, and why that limit belongs in any account of the decision.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What does the clause govern, and what treatment does it forbid? The passage states that the right to vote is protected in more than the initial allocation of the franchise and that equal protection applies as well to the manner of its exercise. Having granted the vote on equal terms, a state may not, by later arbitrary and disparate treatment, value one person's vote over that of another. Both halves are required: the clause reaches the counting, not only the casting, and the forbidden treatment is the arbitrary and disparate kind. (Blueprint 15)

2. The missing element, and the margin. The missing element was a uniform standard. Florida's recount judged ballots by the intent of the voter with no specific standards to ensure the rule's equal application from county to county, so the same kind of ballot could be counted in one county and rejected in another. The certified margin was 537 votes, which is why stopping the recount settled Florida, the Electoral College, and the presidency at once. (Blueprint 15)

3. Put the five decisions in order, with dates. Bush v. Gore, December 12, 2000. District of Columbia v. Heller, 2008. Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, 2010. Obergefell v. Hodges, June 26, 2015. Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard, June 29, 2023. (Blueprint 15)

4. What did the Court say about the reach of its ruling? The student has the sentence backward. The Court wrote that its consideration is limited to the present circumstances, because the problem of equal protection in election processes generally presents many complexities. The ruling by its own terms does not lay down a general rule for all recounts; it decides the Florida recount of 2000 on the facts before the Court. The limit belongs in any account because it is part of the holding's stated scope, and an account that omits it reports a broader decision than the Court issued. (Blueprint 15)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Question 4 is the one to read closely: the lesson's discipline is stating holdings at exactly the scope their texts claim, and a student who converts a self limited ruling into a general rule has repeated, in miniature, the error this lesson exists to correct. Question 2 separates students who can name the mechanism, the missing uniform standard, from students who remember only the outcome.
