A Polarized Nation

Modern America · Lesson 8 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Modern America, Lesson 8 of 8, the closing lesson of the unit and of the course. Teach it after Lesson 7, which covered the Great Recession and its long aftermath. This lesson takes up the sorting of the electorate, the Court settling the election of 2000, Heller and Citizens United, Obergefell, the forty five year argument from Bakke to Students for Fair Admissions, the pandemic and the CARES Act, and the election of 2020 and January 6. It closes the course by pairing every modern record with the older arguments the course began with.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “A Polarized Nation” and the guided notes, which follow the deck in sections A to G: the sorting, the court settles an election, two rights decisions, marriage in every state, the forty five year argument, the pandemic test, and the machinery tested. The activity is “The Continuity File: five modern records paired with the arguments they continue.” The notebook page is “Notebook page: the modern record, the older argument, the evidence for the pairing.” The quick check is “The Recount Stops, December 12, 2000: the passage, the margin, the limited ruling.” The writing task is “The Closing Verdict: the course's final claim about what changed and what did not.”

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Students need the course behind them: the founding amendments, the Fourteenth Amendment from the Civil War era, the New Deal answer to collapse, and the movement years and their argument over remedy. No prior knowledge of the individual decisions is assumed; each holding is stated from its own text in sequence. Teach this lesson under its strictest discipline: claims attributed to their makers, and the official record stated as the record.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (analyze connections among events: the sorting, the decisions, the pandemic, and the older arguments they continue, in order), D2.His.9 (construct arguments using historical evidence: the closing verdict pairing modern records with older arguments and answering the strongest counterclaim), D2.His.16 (assess options for individual and collective action: court settlement against political settlement weighed across the lesson); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from the sorting through the decisions to the machinery tested), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task argued from the evidence bank). The lesson is tagged to blueprint entry 15.

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to argue the lesson's question from the record: an electorate sorted into two hostile camps, measured in roll calls, county landslides, shrinking split ticket voting, and hardening party identity; Florida's certified margin of 537 votes in 2000 and Bush v. Gore stopping the recount five to four; Heller in 2008 protecting a handgun in the home and Citizens United in 2010 barring limits on independent spending, each five to four; Obergefell in 2015 taking same sex marriage licensing from zero states in 2003 to every state; Bakke in 1978 preserving race as one factor and Students for Fair Admissions in 2023 closing that settlement forty five years later; the pandemic killing more than a million Americans by CDC counts, answered by the CARES Act of March 27, 2020 at more than two trillion dollars; and the election of 2020 certified, disputed, and completed after the attack of January 6, 2021.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes: opening, question, and map (slides 1 to 3) 5 minutes; the sorting (slides 4 to 6) 6 minutes; the court settles an election (slides 7 to 8) 5 minutes; two rights decisions (slides 9 to 11) 7 minutes; marriage in every state (slides 12 to 14) 6 minutes; the forty five year argument (slides 15 to 17) 7 minutes; the pandemic test (slides 18 to 20) 6 minutes; the machinery tested, vocabulary, synthesis, and the turn to work (slides 21 to 27) 8 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes.

Slide 4 · Section A: The Sorting (divider)

On the slide: Section A · The Sorting · Two hostile camps, fewer voters in the middle, and margins thin enough for a court to decide · The Supreme Court Building, portrait view.

  • Picture: The Supreme Court Building again, this time as a portrait, photographed by the Architect of the Capitol, a work of the federal government in the public domain. It stands here because the sorting described in this section is what delivered the era's questions to that building.
  • [Begin with the definition students will reproduce in the guided notes.]
  • Political polarization is the sorting of Americans into two hostile political camps with fewer voters in the middle.
  • Insist that the sorting be shown rather than asserted.
  • Roll call records in Congress show the parties voting as blocs more often from the 1990s onward.
  • Survey series show party identity hardening and hostility toward the other camp rising.
  • Counties that once swung between the parties began delivering landslides for one side.
  • Split ticket voting, a ballot for one party's president and the other party's congressman, shrank toward vanishing.
  • A closely divided country is not new in this course; a country sorted so that nearly every contest is close and nearly every institution is contested is the era's own condition.

Slide 5 · The sorting, measured

On the slide: A · The sorting · The sorting, measured · ▪ The parties in Congress voted as blocs more often. ▪ Counties delivered landslides for one party or the other. ▪ Split ticket voting shrank. ▪ Surveys showed party identity hardening and hostility rising. ▪ In 2000 a national count came out nearly even.

  • [Work the measures in order and make students say what each one measures.]
  • The roll call measure is about institutions: members of Congress sorted into two disciplined parties, with the overlapping middle that once produced cross party coalitions largely gone from the voting record.
  • The county measure is about geography: more Americans lived in counties that voted overwhelmingly for one party, so the lived experience of a contested election disappeared from much of the map, a change the public record of election results documents.
  • The ballot measure is about individual voters, who once split tickets routinely and by this era voted one party up and down the ballot.
  • The survey measure is about feeling: party identity became a stronger marker, and negative views of the other party rose through the series the lesson names.
  • Together the four measures describe one fact from four sides, a country divided into camps before any single issue is named.
  • Note: No photograph is paired with this slide. The sorting is a measured pattern across records, not a single scene, so the slide stays text and the four measures carry it.

Slide 6 · 537 votes in Florida, 2000 (stat)

On the slide: A · The sorting · 537 votes · Florida's certified margin in the election of 2000 · A presidency settled inside one state recount · Decided the Electoral College

  • 537 votes
  • [Give this figure its full weight, because it is the sorting stated as a single number.]
  • In November 2000 the two candidates divided the national vote almost evenly, Albert Gore winning the popular vote and George W. Bush the Electoral College, and the whole contest came down to Florida, where the state eventually certified a margin of 537 votes out of nearly six million cast.
  • A margin that small cannot be recounted into certainty; every judgment about a hanging chad or a faint mark moves the total by more than the margin itself.
  • That arithmetic is why the Florida fight moved so quickly from counting rooms to courtrooms, and why the Supreme Court's decision to stop the recount, on the next section's slide, settled a presidency.
  • Teach the number beside the era's other close margins: five to four on the Court itself, in decision after decision of this lesson.
  • The country was not divided between a majority and a minority in these years. It was divided down the middle, and its institutions were built for majorities.
  • Ask: Why does a margin of 537 votes move a contest from counting rooms to courtrooms? Because every judgment about a single ballot moves the total by more than the margin itself, so the count cannot settle the result on its own.

Slide 7 · Section B: The Court Settles an Election (divider)

On the slide: Section B · The Court Settles an Election · December 12, 2000: five justices stop a recount, and the election of 2000 is over.

  • [Set the scene before the holding, because students meet Bush v. Gore as a slogan and must instead meet it as a procedure.]
  • Florida's result was too close to call on election night.
  • State law provided for recounts, the candidates sued over which ballots to count and how, and the Florida Supreme Court ordered a manual recount of the ballots machines had not read, judged by the intent of the voter.
  • The question that reached the United States Supreme Court was narrow and procedural: whether a recount conducted county by county, with no uniform standard for judging intent, treated voters equally under the Constitution.
  • The Court answered on December 12, thirty six days after the election, with the inauguration five weeks away.
  • The holding is on the next slide, in the opinion's own terms, which is how the activity at Station 1 requires students to state it.
  • Note: No photograph is paired with this divider. The famous Florida recount room photographs are press and wire photographs under copyright, and no public domain recount photograph was verified for this lesson, per the image sources record, so Bush v. Gore is taught through its text and the 537 vote record.

Slide 8 · Bush v. Gore: the recount stops

On the slide: B · The court settles an election · Bush v. Gore: the recount stops · ▪ The right was equal weight for each vote and equal dignity for each voter. ▪ The defect was that no uniform standard governed judging intent. ▪ The holding was that the recount violated equal protection. ▪ The decision was five to four and limited to the present circumstances.

  • [State the holding in the opinion's own words, which Station 1 of the activity quotes.]
  • The per curiam opinion grounds the right first: when the state legislature vests the right to vote for President in its people, that right is fundamental, and one source of its fundamental nature lies in the equal weight accorded to each vote and the equal dignity owed to each voter.
  • It then finds the defect: the recount mechanisms did not satisfy the minimum requirement for non-arbitrary treatment of voters, because Florida's command to honor the intent of the voter had no specific standards to ensure its equal application, so the same ballot could count in one county and fail in another.
  • The remedy followed: the recount stopped, Florida's certified result stood, and Gore conceded the next day.
  • Close on the sentence students most often omit: our consideration is limited to the present circumstances.
  • The Court itself declined to make the ruling a general rule for recounts, and any account that leaves that sentence out reports a larger decision than the one issued.

Slide 9 · Section C: Two Rights Decisions (divider)

On the slide: Section C · Two Rights Decisions · Heller reads the Second Amendment in 2008. Citizens United reads the First in 2010. · The Roberts Court of 2010, group portrait.

  • Picture: The Roberts Court of 2010 in group portrait, photographed by Steve Petteway for the Collection of the Supreme Court of the United States, a work of the federal government in the public domain. Top row, left to right: Sonia Sotomayor, Stephen G. Breyer, Samuel A. Alito, and Elena Kagan. Bottom row, left to right: Clarence Thomas, Antonin Scalia, Chief Justice John G. Roberts, Anthony Kennedy, and Ruth Bader Ginsburg. This is the Court that decided Citizens United that year, two years after Heller.
  • [Use the portrait to make the section's structure plain.]
  • With Congress sorted and legislatures closely divided, the era's ground rules for personal rights and for political money were set by this institution, reading amendments written in 1791.
  • Heller asked what the Second Amendment protects: a right of militias, the dissent argued, or a right of individuals, the majority held.
  • Citizens United asked what the First Amendment forbids: limits on corporate political speech, which the majority held the amendment bars when the spending is independent of a campaign.
  • In both cases the losing side warned that the majority was removing questions from democratic politics.
  • The majority's answer in Heller is the section's key sentence, and it is quoted at Station 2 of the activity: the enshrinement of constitutional rights necessarily takes certain policy choices off the table.

Slide 10 · Heller, 2008: the handgun in the home

On the slide: C · Two rights decisions · Heller, 2008: the handgun in the home · ▪ The question was a ban on usable handguns in the home. ▪ The holding protected an individual right to keep a handgun at home. ▪ Self-defense was named as the right's core use. ▪ The District's ban and its inoperable rule were struck down. ▪ The decision was five to four, on the text of 1791.

  • [Teach Heller from its question, which the opinion states in its first line and the activity quotes.]
  • The question: whether a District of Columbia prohibition on the possession of usable handguns in the home violates the Second Amendment.
  • The holding follows in the opinion's summary: the District's ban on handgun possession in the home violates the Second Amendment, as does its prohibition against rendering any lawful firearm in the home operable for the purpose of immediate self-defense, and the District had to permit Heller to register his handgun and license it for the home.
  • Two features deserve class time.
  • The first is method: the majority read the amendment's words as its authors' generation used them, and treated that reading as settling a modern city's law.
  • The second is scope: the opinion protected the handgun in the home for self-defense and acknowledged a range of other regulation as lawful, so the holding is narrower than the arguments made about it on either side.
  • State it at the scope the text claims.

Slide 11 · Citizens United, 2010: money and speech

On the slide: C · Two rights decisions · Citizens United, 2010: money and speech · ▪ The holding allowed no limits on independent spending. ▪ Corporations and unions could spend from their own treasuries. ▪ Independence from a campaign was the line the holding drew. ▪ Super PACs followed, and outside spending grew large.

  • [State Citizens United from the definition students carry in the guided notes.]
  • The decision of 2010 held that the government may not limit independent political spending by corporations and unions, which opened the way to super PACs and large outside spending in elections.
  • The majority's reasoning ran through the First Amendment: spending to publish political speech is speech, and the identity of the speaker, a corporation or a union rather than a person, does not remove the protection when the spending is independent of any candidate's campaign.
  • The dissent answered that corporate treasuries could now overwhelm the electoral debate the amendment was meant to keep open.
  • Both positions belong in the lesson, attributed to their makers.
  • The practical consequence is measurable and belongs on the record: committees that may raise and spend unlimited sums independently of candidates, the super PACs, became central spenders in the election cycles that followed, and the argument over money in politics moved from Congress, which had written the limits, to the Court, which had removed them.

Slide 12 · Section D: Marriage in Every State (divider)

On the slide: Section D · Marriage in Every State · In 2003 no state licensed a same sex marriage. In 2015 the Fourteenth Amendment required every state to.

  • [Open the section with the speed of the change, because students who grew up after 2015 often assume the Obergefell settlement was old.]
  • In 2003, when this lesson's period was already under way, no state in the Union licensed marriages between two people of the same sex.
  • Massachusetts became the first in 2004, under a ruling of its own supreme court.
  • Other states followed by ruling and by statute, while still others amended their laws and constitutions to forbid such marriages, so the country's map of a basic civil status depended on which side of a state line a couple stood.
  • The question that reached the Supreme Court was whether the Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, allowed that map to stand.
  • Justice Kennedy's opinion answered for the whole country on June 26, 2015, the same date, two years apart, as the Heller decision in this lesson's previous section, a coincidence students enjoy noticing and a reminder of how much of the era ran through one building.

Slide 13 · Obergefell, 2015: equal dignity

On the slide: D · Marriage in every state · Obergefell, 2015: equal dignity · ▪ Every state must license same sex marriages. ▪ Every state must recognize marriages from other states. ▪ The ground was the Fourteenth Amendment. ▪ The close called for equal dignity in the eyes of the law. ▪ The decision was five to four.

  • [State Obergefell in its two halves, because the activity at Station 3 tests both and each half does different work.]
  • The licensing half: the Court holds that same-sex couples may exercise the fundamental right to marry in all States, so every state must issue the licenses its own law had withheld.
  • The recognition half: there is no lawful basis for a State to refuse to recognize a lawful same-sex marriage performed in another State on the ground of its same-sex character, so a marriage valid where it was celebrated remains valid when the couple crosses a state line.
  • Then read the closing passage aloud, as the station does: the petitioners' hope is not to be condemned to live in loneliness, excluded from one of civilization's oldest institutions; they ask for equal dignity in the eyes of the law, and the Constitution grants them that right.
  • Name the phrase's ancestry for the class: equal dignity is the Civil War era amendment's claim, argued through the movement years, stated here for marriage in 2015.

Slide 14 · 0 to 50 states licensing same sex marriage (stat)

On the slide: D · Marriage in every state · 0 to 50 · states licensing same sex marriage, 2003 to 2015 · No state in 2003 · Massachusetts first in 2004 · Every state required after Obergefell

  • 0 to 50
  • [Teach this pair of numbers as the era's fastest change of legal status, and make students account for both ends of it.]
  • The zero is a fact of 2003: in the year this century's argument was already raging, not one state issued a marriage license to a same sex couple, and many states had statutes or constitutional provisions against it.
  • The fifty is a fact of June 26, 2015: after Obergefell, every state was required by the Fourteenth Amendment to license such marriages and to recognize those performed elsewhere.
  • Between the two numbers stand twelve years of state politics, rulings, statutes, ballot measures, and reversals, which the decision ended by national ruling rather than by the fiftieth state acting on its own.
  • Set the pair beside the lesson's other numbers: 537 votes, five to four, forty five years.
  • This change took twelve years from none to all; the admissions argument on the next section took forty five years to run from its settlement to its close. The era moved at both speeds at once.

Slide 15 · Section E: The Forty Five Year Argument (divider)

On the slide: Section E · The Forty Five Year Argument · Bakke settles the terms in 1978. Students for Fair Admissions closes them in 2023.

  • [Introduce the section as the place where the course's longest thread ends, and let students feel the length of it.]
  • The argument over remedy began in this course at Howard University in 1965, where President Johnson argued that the nation should seek equality as a fact and equality as a result, not equality as a right only.
  • Affirmative action turned that argument into policy, ordered for federal contractors in the revised Philadelphia Plan of 1969, in the definition students carry verbatim in this lesson's guided notes.
  • The Civil Rights unit closed with Bakke in 1978, Justice Powell's opinion striking down a set aside of seats and preserving race as one factor among others.
  • That settlement governed American college admissions for forty five years, through reaffirmations and narrowings, until June 29, 2023.
  • The next two slides state the settlement and its close as a pair, which is how Station 4 of the activity presents them and how the key requires them to be read: together, or not at all.

Slide 16 · Bakke, 1978: the settlement recalled

On the slide: E · The forty five year argument · Bakke, 1978: the settlement recalled · ▪ The set aside of sixteen of one hundred seats was struck down. ▪ The ground was disregard of individual rights. ▪ Race was preserved as a plus in an applicant's file. ▪ It was weighed against all other candidates, never on a separate track.

  • [Recall Bakke precisely, because the 2023 decision is unintelligible without both of its halves in view.]
  • The restrictive half, in the words Station 4 quotes: the fatal flaw in the Davis medical school's preferential program was its disregard of individual rights as guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment, and the set aside of sixteen of one hundred seats fell.
  • The preserving half stands in the same opinion: in a lawful admissions program, race or ethnic background may be deemed a plus in a particular applicant's file, yet it does not insulate the individual from comparison with all other candidates for the available seats.
  • Powell's settlement therefore did two things at once. It forbade the quota, the one remedy that counted results in seats.
  • It preserved the factor, the tool that let admissions officers weigh race among many qualifications.
  • American universities built their admissions systems inside that settlement for two generations of applicants.
  • Students should name the tension the settlement carried from birth: a program aimed at results, judged by a rule stated in individual terms.

Slide 17 · Students for Fair Admissions, 2023: the close

On the slide: E · The forty five year argument · Students for Fair Admissions, 2023: the close · ▪ The syllabus required strict scrutiny, no stereotype or negative, and an end point. ▪ The programs failed each criterion. ▪ They were invalid under the Equal Protection Clause. ▪ The decision was six to three, forty five years after Bakke.

  • [State the 2023 holding from the syllabus of the slip opinion, the document Station 4 quotes.]
  • The syllabus first recites the terms on which the Court had permitted race based admissions: such programs must comply with strict scrutiny, may never use race as a stereotype or negative, and must, at some point, end.
  • It then applies the terms: respondents' admissions systems fail each of these criteria and must therefore be invalidated under the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.
  • The majority's reasoning filled in the three failures: the interests the universities claimed, training leaders and enriching discussion among them, were not measurable enough for a court to review; a plus for some applicants in a fixed class operates as a negative for others; and programs justified by goals without a measure have no logical end point.
  • The dissents answered that the majority had abandoned the Fourteenth Amendment's remedial history.
  • Present both, attributed, and grade only the accuracy with which students state the holding and its grounds.

Slide 19 · The pandemic and the CARES Act

On the slide: F · The pandemic test · The pandemic and the CARES Act · ▪ More than a million Americans died, by CDC counts. ▪ Schools, businesses, and public life closed for months. ▪ The CARES Act of March 27, 2020 committed more than two trillion dollars. ▪ Vaccines were authorized in under a year. ▪ Closures and mandates were argued along the sorting's lines. · The Elizabeth High School vaccination site, March 3, 2021.

  • Picture: The same FEMA vaccination site photograph as the section divider: officials touring the community based vaccination site at Elizabeth High School, Elizabeth, New Jersey, March 3, 2021, photographed by Kenneth Wilsey of FEMA, public domain. Caption it for the site and the date, not for a hospital scene it does not show.
  • [Run the section's facts in the order the reading gives them.]
  • The disease first, defined in the guided notes: the worldwide spread of the coronavirus disease first identified in 2019, which reached the United States in 2020, killed more than a million Americans, and closed schools, businesses, and public life for months.
  • The count behind the rounded phrase is in the CDC record the activity quotes at Station 5: by April 26, 2023, more than 104 million cases and 1.1 million COVID-19-associated deaths had been reported to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

  • Vaccines followed in under a year under a government backed crash program.
  • The argument over closures, mandates, and emergency power then divided along the lines the sorting had drawn, with supporters crediting the measures with limiting the damage and critics charging that they cost more than they saved. Attribute both claims every time.

Slide 20 · 1.1 million COVID-19-associated deaths (stat)

On the slide: F · The pandemic test · 1.1 million · COVID-19-associated deaths reported to the CDC by April 26, 2023 · More than 104 million cases · The count is the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention record

  • 1.1 million
  • [Teach this number as a record, not a rounded phrase, because even a catastrophe enters the file as a document.]
  • The figure comes from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, in its Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report on surveillance after the public health emergency: by April 26, 2023, more than 104 million cases, six million related hospitalizations, and 1.1 million COVID-19-associated deaths had been reported to the CDC and summarized on its COVID Data Tracker.
  • The count passed one million during 2022, which is why the guided notes definition, written for the whole pandemic, says the disease killed more than a million Americans.
  • Give students the comparison the course has prepared them for: the pandemic's American dead exceed the American dead of the Civil War era's armies counted in the same cumulative way, and the federal answer, a statute moving more than two trillion dollars within weeks, was written at a scale only the New Deal and the Second World War had precedented in this course.
  • Note: No photograph of patients, hospitals under strain, or funerals is used anywhere in this lesson, per the image sources record. The pandemic is taught through the CDC count, the CARES Act text, and the vaccination site photograph.

Slide 21 · Section G: The Machinery Tested (divider)

On the slide: Section G · The Machinery Tested · The election of 2020 is certified, disputed, and completed. The course closes on its first arguments.

  • [Close the lesson with the machinery itself under stress, and teach this section under the lesson's strictest discipline: claims attributed to their makers, and the official record stated as the record.]
  • The election of 2020 was held during the pandemic and produced the highest turnout in more than a century.
  • Its results were counted, recounted in contested states, certified by officials of both parties, and defended in court, where challenges to the count were rejected in case after case.
  • The president who lost it claimed the election had been stolen, and many of his supporters believed the claim; the official record does not support it.
  • On January 6, 2021, the day Congress counted the electoral votes, a crowd attacked the Capitol and interrupted the count, which was completed that night.
  • The next slide states the record in that form, and the writing task's rubric holds students to the same form: attribution for claims, the record for facts.
  • Note: No photograph of January 6 is used. The widely reproduced photographs of the attack are press photographs or Capitol Police material whose public domain basis could not be verified for this lesson, per the image sources record, so the day is taught in text only, from the public record.

Slide 22 · 2020 and January 6: the record stated

On the slide: G · The machinery tested · 2020 and January 6: the record stated · ▪ The official record showed Biden winning the Electoral College and the popular vote. ▪ Results were certified by officials of both parties, and challenges were rejected in court. ▪ President Trump and his supporters claimed the election was stolen. ▪ On January 6, 2021 the Capitol was attacked, and the count was completed that night. ▪ Hundreds were prosecuted and convicted for their parts.

  • [State this slide's content slowly, in the exact form the reading uses, and tell the class why the form is the content.]
  • The official record of the election of 2020 is that Joseph Biden won the Electoral College and the popular vote, that state officials of both parties certified their results, and that challenges to the count were rejected in court after court.
  • Against that record stands a claim, attributed to its makers: President Trump and many of his supporters claimed the election had been stolen, a claim the official record does not support.
  • Both sentences belong in an honest account; neither may borrow the other's standing.
  • On January 6, 2021, a crowd that had gathered in Washington attacked the Capitol while Congress counted the electoral votes.
  • The count was completed that night, the transfer of power proceeded on January 20, and hundreds of participants were later prosecuted and convicted for their parts in the attack.
  • No event after 2023 is assigned a settled meaning beyond this record anywhere in the course, and students' verdicts are graded on evidence and attribution, never on the side they take.

Slide 23 · The unsettled present at a glance

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for marriage, remedy, and the pandemic

On the slide: The vocabulary · Words for marriage, remedy, and the pandemic · Obergefell v. Hodges · 2015: marriage licensed in every state · affirmative action · race taken into account to remedy past discrimination · COVID-19 pandemic · 2020: more than a million American deaths

  • Three more terms, in the guided notes wording, carried in these notes in full, word for word.
  • Obergefell v. Hodges: the Supreme Court decision of 2015 holding that the Fourteenth Amendment requires every state to license marriages between two people of the same sex and to recognize such marriages licensed in other states

Slide 26 · Closing synthesis: where this course ends

On the slide: Closing synthesis · Where this course ends · The subjects changed across this course: who may vote, who may marry, what the government may spend and forbid, what the country owes to those its laws once wronged. The arguments did not change their age. They were argued in 1776, 1861, 1933, 1954, and 1968, and the documents of 2000 to 2023 argue them still.

  • [Close the course by stating its ending as a finding in the terms the lesson has used throughout, sorted rather than summed.]
  • What changed can be listed and dated: an electorate sorted into two camps, a presidency settled by a court on 537 votes, a handgun right and a spending right read out of the founding amendments, a marriage right read out of the Fourteenth Amendment for all fifty states, a forty five year admissions settlement closed, and a pandemic answered at a scale the New Deal first made thinkable.
  • What did not change can also be listed: the texts being read, from 1791 and 1868; the questions being asked, over the size of government, the reach of rights, the terms of membership in the nation, and the country's place in the world; and the habit of settling them in court when the political branches divide too evenly to settle them themselves.
  • Students should leave able to pair any document of this lesson with the older argument it continues, and to defend the pairing from the document's own words.
