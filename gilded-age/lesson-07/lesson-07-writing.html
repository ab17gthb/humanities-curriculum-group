Writing Task: Help, Harm, or Both?

The prompt. Federal officials and many white reformers described assimilation as a way to help Native peoples survive. Write a paragraph that answers this question: Judged by its results, was assimilation mainly a policy of help, a policy of harm, or a policy that helped some individuals while harming Native nations as peoples? Use at least three of the evidence cards below. Your paragraph must separate stated goals from documented outcomes, and it must name at least one specific Native nation.

The cards are summaries drawn from the lesson reading. Write the history in your own sentences and quote facts, not the cards.

Card 1: The Land Count

In 1887, the year the Dawes Act passed, Native nations held about 138 million acres. By 1934, when allotment ended, about 90 million of those acres had passed out of Native hands through surplus sales, individual sales, fraud, and tax loss. Much of what remained was divided into small parcels with many heirs.

Card 2: The School Record

Boarding schools such as Carlisle, founded in Pennsylvania in 1879, removed Native children from their families, cut their hair, required English, and punished Native languages. Some students died of disease far from home. Some graduates later used their English, trades, and literacy to defend their nations in court and in print.

Card 3: The Reformers' Case

Many assimilation supporters opposed theft and violence against Native peoples and had documented real abuses by agents and settlers. Their remedy was to end tribal governments and communal landholding so that Native people would survive as individual farmers, Christians, and English speakers.

Card 4: Survival

Families taught languages and ceremonies at home where agents could not stop them. Leaders pressed treaty claims in courts and by petition. The Dakota writer Zitkala-Sa challenged assimilation in print for eastern readers. Tribal governments continued to exist on reduced land.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the question directly and judges by results, as the prompt requires. A both answer earns full credit only if it keeps the two levels separate: possible gains for some individuals (Card 2, literacy and trades later used in defense of their nations) against documented harm to nations as peoples (Cards 1 and 3, land loss and the intended end of tribal government).
  • At least three cards used accurately. Card 1 must carry its dates: about 90 million acres lost between 1887 and 1934, not an undated claim that land was taken. Card 3 must be used for stated goals, not treated as an outcome.
  • At least one specific Native nation named and placed correctly (for example, Lakota and the Black Hills or Wounded Knee, Nez Perce and the 1877 flight, Dakota and Zitkala-Sa).
  • A real counterpoint answered, not dismissed. Help claims must answer Card 1. Harm claims must answer Card 3 fairly: many supporters did oppose theft and violence, and the paragraph should explain why good intentions do not settle a judgment based on results.

Common weak answers. Judging only by intentions (the prompt asks for results); using Wounded Knee as the whole case for assimilation, when Wounded Knee was a military killing and allotment and the schools were the assimilation policy itself; treating Native peoples as one group with no nation named.

Historical note for the debrief. Allotment ended in 1934, when federal policy formally reversed course and recognized tribal governments again. Students who note that the government itself abandoned the policy have found the strongest outside confirmation of a harm or both judgment.
