Native Americans and the Closing Frontier

The Gilded Age · Lesson 7 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: The Gilded Age, Lesson 7 of 8. Lesson 6, The West, told mining, cattle, and farming as one story driven by railroads and federal land policy, with a fourth story underneath: the Native nations who held the western land under treaties and were pressed onto reservations as the other three advanced. This lesson takes up that fourth story directly, and in full. It follows what happened to Native nations after the Civil War as the United States pushed west: treaties broken and wars lost, confinement on reservations and the destruction of the buffalo, and then the policy that replaced war — assimilation, carried out on children in boarding schools and on land through the Dawes Act, answered at Wounded Knee, and survived. Lesson 8 closes the unit.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 6, The West (its section E introduced the reservation system and the shrinking map that this lesson explains from inside; its Black Hills mining story is this lesson's treaty story, told from the other side) and Lesson 1, The Engines of Industry (the railroad system of that lesson is what carried miners, settlers, soldiers, and buffalo hides across Native land). Inside the lesson, sections A and B are the taking, sections C and D are the policy, section E is the policy's violent end, and section F is the answer: the reading's last section, What Was Preserved, is the lesson's destination, not an afterthought.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Lessons 1 through 6's account of industrial growth, railroads, and the remaking of the West; the reservation system as introduced in Lesson 6. No earlier Native history is assumed; the lesson teaches its own sequence. Every figure in the lesson is taught as the reading states it — the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868 and the seizure of the Black Hills in 1877; the Little Bighorn in 1876; Carlisle opening in 1879; the Dawes Act in 1887; about 138 million acres held in 1887 and about 90 million acres lost by 1934; tens of millions of buffalo in the early 1870s and only a few hundred wild survivors by the mid-1880s; about 250 to 300 Lakota killed on December 29, 1890. Six image cautions are disclosed honestly in the notes: the Chief Joseph portrait was made in 1903, not as he appeared in 1877; the Carlisle classroom photograph is a posed federal publicity photograph from 1901, more than twenty years after the school opened; the Indian Land for Sale poster comes from 1911, inside the allotment era rather than from 1887, and the man pictured on it is not named; the Ghost Dance image is Frederic Remington's magazine illustration, not a photograph; the Tom Torlino portraits are stacked on the slide where the original pair stood side by side; and no photograph of the Wounded Knee dead is used anywhere in the lesson, by design.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (the Curtis portrait, the Johnston classroom photograph, the Choate portraits, the 1911 government poster, and the Remington illustration read as evidence, each for the kind of source it is), D2.His.2 (continuity and change in Native life and federal policy between 1868 and 1934), D2.Civ.14 (how federal policy — treaties, reservations, boarding schools, and allotment — distributed land and power, and to whom), D2.His.9 (change over time in the Native land base, from about 138 million acres to about 48 million); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, WHST.9-10.1 (assimilation judged in writing by its results, with evidence from the lesson).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain how the United States took the Black Hills and what that case shows about treaty promises; describe the reservation system by its controls and explain why the destruction of the buffalo mattered so much to Plains peoples; state what assimilation's supporters said they were doing and what the boarding schools actually did to children; explain how the Dawes Act worked and how much land Native nations lost under allotment by 1934; explain why federal agents treated the Ghost Dance as a threat and what happened at Wounded Knee on December 29, 1890; and name what Native peoples lost, resisted, and preserved — then judge assimilation, in writing, by its results.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and question (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; Section A, treaties and war (slides 4–6) 7 minutes; Section B, reservations and the buffalo (slides 7–9) 6 minutes; Section C, assimilation and boarding schools (slides 10–12) 7 minutes; Section D, the Dawes Act (slides 13–15) 7 minutes; Section E, the Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee (slides 16–18) 8 minutes; Section F, resistance and survival (slides 19–21) 6 minutes; vocabulary and closing (slides 22–24) 4 minutes, with the exit ticket collected at the bell. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. The activity is "Policy and Consequence: Assimilation on the Record," four stations, then a verdict; the notebook page is "Loss, Resistance, and Survival," the policy chart, kept for review day; the quick check is "Before and After at Carlisle," reading the Torlino portraits as evidence; the writing task is "Help, Harm, or Both? Judge assimilation by its results"; the exit ticket has three parts — explain the Dawes Act, name two losses and two preservations, and state why assimilation was pursued, with its stated goal set against one real effect.

Key vocabulary

assimilation · allotment · Dawes Act · reservation system · boarding school · Ghost Dance · Wounded Knee

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title "Native Americans and the Closing Frontier"; the standfirst — "How land was taken, cultures were attacked, and Native nations endured."

  • Picture: A pile of American bison skulls at the Michigan Carbon Works in Detroit, about 1892, waiting to be ground for fertilizer; one man stands on top of the pile and another at its base.
  • The photograph is a pile of American bison skulls at a Detroit works, about 1892, waiting to be ground for fertilizer.
  • [Keep it on screen while you frame the lesson.]
  • Every skull was once part of the food supply of the Plains peoples, and the pile is industrial in scale: the slaughter of the buffalo was a business, and its effect on Native independence was understood at the time.
  • This lesson follows what happened to Native nations after the Civil War as the United States pushed west.
  • Treaties were broken, wars were fought and lost, and then the government turned from war to a policy called assimilation, meant to end Native cultures as distinct ways of life.
  • Students should leave able to explain that policy, count its costs in land and lives, and name what Native peoples resisted and preserved.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "Why did the United States pursue assimilation, and what did Native peoples lose, resist, and preserve?"
  • [Read the lesson question aloud and keep it visible.]
  • It has two halves, and the lesson answers both.
  • Why did the United States pursue assimilation? Because officials and many white reformers concluded that Native peoples could survive only by giving up tribal government, communal land, and their own languages, and becoming farmers, Christians, and English speakers.
  • What did Native peoples lose, resist, and preserve? The losses are countable: land, food, children taken to distant schools, and lives.
  • The resistance and the preservation are just as real, and the last section of the lesson belongs to them.
  • Under the unit question, this lesson is the clearest case in the unit of who paid.
  • Industrial growth wanted western land, minerals, and railroad routes, and Native nations held legal title to much of that land.
  • The gain and the payment were connected, and students should be able to draw that connection by the exit ticket.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "War, then a policy"; the six section rows, A–F.

  • This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section.
  • Sections A and B cover the taking: treaties broken when settlers and miners wanted the land, wars that ended in surrender, confinement on reservations, and the destruction of the buffalo that made independent life on the Plains nearly impossible.
  • Sections C and D cover the policy that replaced war: assimilation, carried out on children in boarding schools and on land through the Dawes Act.
  • Section E covers the Ghost Dance, a religious movement of renewal, and its end at Wounded Knee.
  • Section F closes with resistance and survival, because the story does not end in 1890.
  • [Tell students the guided notes, the activity, and the exit ticket all follow this same order, and that the numbers in sections B and D, the buffalo count and the acreage count, are the figures the test will ask about.]

Slide 4 · Section A: Treaties and War (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "Treaty promises, and the wars that followed when they broke"; the Chief Joseph portrait at right.

  • Picture: Chief Joseph of the Nez Perce, photographed by Edward S. Curtis in 1903; a photogravure portrait, made when Chief Joseph was an older man.
  • The portrait is Chief Joseph of the Nez Perce, photographed by Edward S. Curtis.
  • [Say the date plainly: this portrait was made in 1903, when Chief Joseph was an older man, more than twenty five years after the events of this section.]
  • It is not a picture of him as he looked in 1877, and students should not treat it as one.
  • He is here because the reading names him: in 1877 the Nez Perce, led in part by Chief Joseph, fled toward Canada rather than leave their homeland in Oregon.
  • [Set the section's frame before the details.]
  • After the Civil War, the United States pushed west with soldiers, railroads, miners, and settlers.
  • Native nations held legal title to much of that land under treaties the federal government had signed.
  • The pattern of this section is what happened to those treaties when the land became valuable.

Slide 5 · Treaties broken, land taken

On the slide: four bullets — Native nations held legal title under signed treaties; the government broke its agreements again and again; in 1868, the Treaty of Fort Laramie guaranteed the Black Hills to the Lakota; gold was found, the government offered to buy, and then it seized the hills in 1877.

  • [Work through the Black Hills case slowly, because reading question one and three guided notes blanks turn on it.]
  • The Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868 guaranteed the Black Hills to the Lakota.
  • That word, guaranteed, matters: the hills were not a gift the government could take back, but Lakota land confirmed by treaty.
  • When gold was found there, the government first tried to buy the hills.
  • The Lakota refused, as was their right.
  • In 1877 the government seized them anyway.
  • Ask: "What is a treaty worth if one side may break it whenever the land turns out to be valuable?" [Let the silence do some work.]
  • Then widen the point. The Black Hills were one case of a general pattern across the West: agreements held until settlers or miners wanted the land badly enough, and then they did not.
  • Native resistance to that pattern is the next slide.

Slide 6 · Resistance, and the end of the wars

On the slide: four bullets — in 1876, the Lakota and Cheyenne defeated Custer at the Little Bighorn; the victory did not hold, because winter campaigns and hunger brought surrender within a year; in 1877, the Nez Perce fled toward Canada and were captured about 40 miles from the border; Apache resistance in the Southwest lasted until 1886.

  • [Give each resistance its due before explaining why it ended.]
  • At the Little Bighorn in 1876, the Lakota and their Cheyenne allies defeated George Custer outright.
  • It was a real victory, and it changed nothing structural: the army campaigned through the winter, food ran short, and most Lakota bands surrendered within a year.
  • In 1877 the Nez Perce chose flight over removal, traveled more than 1,000 miles, and were captured in Montana about 40 miles from Canada and safety.
  • In the Southwest, Apache leaders including Geronimo resisted removal until 1886.
  • The reading is careful about why large scale armed resistance ended by the late 1880s: not because Native peoples accepted the outcome, but because soldiers, supplies, and hunger made further war impossible.
  • [Students should repeat that distinction.]
  • Defeat by exhaustion is not consent, and the rest of this lesson is unintelligible if the class confuses the two.

Slide 7 · Section B: Reservations and the Buffalo (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "Confined to reservations, and cut off from the buffalo"; the bison skull pile photograph at right.

  • Picture: The bison skull pile from the title slide, seen closer; one man stands on top of the pile and another at its base, and both are dwarfed by it.
  • The photograph is the bison skull pile from the title slide, seen closer: one man stands on top of the pile and another at its base, and both are dwarfed by it.
  • Two systems did the work of this section, and students often merge them, so separate them now.
  • The reservation system was federal policy: it confined Native nations to defined areas under federal supervision and opened most of the West to settlement.
  • The destruction of the buffalo was a commercial slaughter that the army welcomed, and it removed the food supply that could have let Plains peoples stay independent of that system.
  • Policy confined people to a place.
  • The slaughter removed their reason and their means to leave it.
  • [By the end of the section, students should be able to explain each half on its own and then explain how the two halves worked together.]

Slide 8 · Confined: the reservation system

On the slide: four bullets — reservations were a fraction of a former homeland; reservations were often the land settlers wanted least; federal agents controlled food rations and policed movement; families were pressed to farm where farming was difficult.

  • [Define the system by its controls, because the guided notes test them.]
  • Under the reservation system, federal policy confined Native nations to defined areas of land under federal supervision.
  • Reservations were usually a fraction of a nation's former homeland, and often the land settlers wanted least.
  • Inside them, federal agents controlled food rations, policed movement, and pressed families to farm where farming was difficult.
  • [Note the dependence built into that arrangement.]
  • A nation that once fed itself now received food from an agent, and rations could be cut, as they were at Pine Ridge in 1890, with consequences this lesson reaches in section E.
  • The system also served the government directly: it let Washington open most of the West to settlement while keeping Native peoples in fixed places under federal control.
  • Confinement, dependence, and control are the three words students should carry out of this slide.

Slide 9 · A way of life destroyed in about fifteen years

On the slide: the bison skull pile photograph at right; four bullets — Plains peoples depended on the buffalo for food, clothing, shelter, and tools; in the early 1870s, tens of millions of buffalo still roamed the Plains; commercial hunters killed buffalo by the millions, and hides were shipped east by rail; by the mid-1880s, only a few hundred wild buffalo remained.

  • Picture: The pile of American bison skulls at the Michigan Carbon Works in Detroit, about 1892 — the title image, shown again beside the count it illustrates.
  • [Start with dependence, because students underestimate it.]
  • For Plains peoples the buffalo were food, clothing, shelter, and tools at once, the base of an economy and a way of life built over centuries.
  • Then run the numbers in order.
  • In the early 1870s, tens of millions of buffalo still roamed the Plains.
  • Commercial hunters, shipping hides east by railroad, killed them by the millions.
  • By the mid-1880s, only a few hundred wild buffalo remained.
  • A food source and a way of life had been destroyed in about fifteen years.
  • [Add the army's part plainly: officers welcomed the effect, because a people without food cannot stay independent for long.]
  • That sentence connects this slide to the last one.
  • The reservation system worked far more easily once hunger left Plains nations no alternative to federal rations.
  • [Reading question two asks exactly this, so make sure students can answer it in their own words.]

Slide 10 · Section C: Assimilation and Boarding Schools (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "The plan to end Native cultures, carried out on children"; the Carlisle classroom photograph at right.

  • Picture: Students in a classroom at the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania in 1901, photographed by Frances Benjamin Johnston; a posed federal publicity photograph.
  • The photograph shows students in a classroom at the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in 1901, photographed by Frances Benjamin Johnston.
  • [Name what kind of image it is: a posed federal publicity photograph, made to show the public that the school was working.]
  • Students will read it that way again at Station 2 of the activity, set against a student's own account of the same system.
  • [Date it honestly as well: Carlisle opened in 1879, so this classroom shows the system more than twenty years in, not its first days.]
  • The section turns from war to policy.
  • As the wars ended, officials and white reformers pursued assimilation, and its main instrument for children was the boarding school.
  • [Keep one question in front of the class through both slides: what did the policy's supporters say they were doing, and what did the policy actually do?]

Slide 11 · Assimilation: help, as its supporters told it

On the slide: five bullets — supporters called themselves friends of Native peoples; many supporters had documented real abuses; the remedy was to end Native cultures as distinct ways of life; Native people would survive as farmers, Christians, and English speakers; tribal governments and communal landholding would end.

  • [Present the supporters fairly before judging them, because the writing task grades students on exactly that fairness.]
  • Many reformers who backed assimilation described themselves as friends of Native peoples, and many had documented real abuses by agents and settlers.
  • Their concern was not invented.
  • Their remedy, however, was to end Native cultures as distinct ways of life.
  • Native people would survive, in this plan, by becoming farmers, Christians, and English speakers, and tribal governments and communal landholding would end.
  • So the goal itself, not only the methods, is what students must weigh.
  • A policy can oppose theft and violence and still aim at the disappearance of peoples as peoples.
  • [Hold that thought for the next two sections: the boarding schools pursued this goal with children, and the Dawes Act pursued it with land.]
  • The exit ticket asks students to state the goal and one real effect side by side.

Slide 12 · Boarding schools: Carlisle and the children

  • Picture: Tom Torlino, a Navajo student at Carlisle, photographed by the school's photographer, John N. Choate — his arrival portrait from 1882 on top, and his portrait after about three years at the school, in 1885, below. The original pair stood side by side; the slide stacks them so both fit without distortion, and the full pair is in the quick check.
  • The paired portraits are Tom Torlino, a Navajo student at Carlisle, photographed by the school's photographer, John N. Choate: on top, his arrival portrait from 1882, and below, his portrait after about three years at the school, in 1885.
  • The original pair stood side by side; the slide stacks them so both fit without distortion, and the full pair is in the quick check.
  • [The school made pairs like this as advertising, so read them as a claim, not a neutral record.]
  • Then teach the system behind the claim.
  • Beginning with Carlisle in 1879, the government and churches ran schools that removed children from their families to suppress Native languages and cultures.
  • Children had their hair cut and were punished for speaking their own languages.
  • Some died of disease far from home.
  • Others later used their English and literacy to defend their nations.
  • Both facts belong in the record.

Slide 13 · Section D: The Dawes Act (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "Allotment divides the land, and the surplus is sold"; the Indian Land for Sale poster at right.

  • Picture: The complete "Indian Land for Sale" advertisement poster, published by the United States Department of the Interior in 1911; the man pictured at its center is not named on the poster.
  • The image is a complete United States government poster from 1911, headed Indian Land for Sale.
  • [Date it plainly: it comes from inside the allotment era, twenty four years after the Dawes Act, not from 1887 itself.]
  • [Read a little of it aloud.]
  • The Department of the Interior reports selling allotted Indian land across the West in 1910, state by state and price by price, and estimates that 350,000 acres will be offered for sale in 1911.
  • 350,000 acres were estimated to be offered for sale in 1911.
  • This is the surplus land system of the next slide, advertised by the government itself as a benefit to buyers.
  • The man pictured at the center is not named on the poster, and we will not name him either.
  • [Keep the poster in mind during the section: whatever supporters said allotment would teach, this is what the policy produced in practice, a standing public sale of Native land.]

Slide 14 · How the Dawes Act worked

On the slide: five bullets — in 1887, the Dawes Act divided reservation land; individual allotments went to single families or adults; remaining tribal land was declared surplus; surplus land was sold to non-Native buyers; what remained was split into scattered parcels with many heirs.

  • [Walk the mechanism step by step, because the exit ticket asks for it in two sentences.]
  • The Dawes Act of 1887 divided reservation land into individual allotments, parcels assigned to single families or adults.
  • Land left over after allotment was declared surplus, a word students should hear in quotation marks in their heads, since the land was surplus only because the act said so.
  • The government then sold that surplus land to non-Native buyers.
  • Supporters argued that private property would teach farming and independence.
  • In practice, allotment broke up the land base that made Native self government possible, and it divided what remained into scattered parcels with many heirs, hard to use and easy to lose.
  • Ask: "Why would dividing tribal land weaken a nation even if no land had been sold at all?" [Pose the activity's question early.]
  • The acreage count on the next slide shows how much land was, in fact, sold and lost.

Slide 15 · The land count: 90 million acres

On the slide: the number "90 MILLION" in large type; the label — "acres of Native land passed out of Native hands by 1934"; the sub-line — "Native nations held about 138 million acres in 1887, the year the Dawes Act passed. The losses came through surplus sales, individual sales, fraud, and tax loss."

  • In 1887 Native nations held about 138 million acres; by 1934, about 90 million of those acres had passed out of Native hands.
  • [Let the number sit.]
  • In 1887, when the Dawes Act passed, Native nations held about 138 million acres.
  • By 1934, when allotment ended, about 90 million of those acres had passed out of Native hands.
  • That is roughly two of every three acres, gone in under fifty years, through surplus sales, individual sales, fraud, and tax loss.
  • [Connect the figure back to the promise from the last slide.]
  • Allotment was announced as a path to independence: give each family private property and farming would follow.
  • The land record shows the opposite movement, a transfer of land from Native nations to non-Native buyers on a continental scale.
  • [Reading question three and the writing task both require this figure with its dates, so students should have 138 million in 1887 and 90 million lost by 1934 in their notes verbatim before you move on.]

Slide 16 · Section E: The Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "A promise of renewal, answered with troops"; the Ghost Dance illustration at right.

  • Picture: Oglala Lakota dancers at Pine Ridge Agency — an artist's illustration by Frederic Remington, published in Harper's Weekly in 1890 for eastern readers, not a photograph.
  • The image shows Oglala Lakota dancers at Pine Ridge Agency, and students must know what kind of image it is: an artist's illustration by Frederic Remington, published in Harper's Weekly in 1890 for eastern readers, not a photograph.
  • The lesson activity asks students to weigh that distinction, because an illustration shows how the Ghost Dance was pictured for the white public, which is part of why officials feared it.
  • [There is no photograph of the Wounded Knee dead anywhere in this lesson, by design: the killing is taught through the record, not through images of bodies.]
  • The section has two movements.
  • First, a religious movement that promised renewal to peoples who had lost land, food, and children.
  • Second, the federal response to that movement, which ended on December 29, 1890, at Wounded Knee Creek.
  • [Teach both movements in that order.]

Slide 17 · The Ghost Dance: a promise of renewal

On the slide: four bullets — in 1889, Wovoka, a Paiute prophet, began teaching the Ghost Dance; the promise was that the world would be restored and the buffalo and the dead would return; the teaching spread across western reservations in 1889 and 1890; where rations were cut and land was taken, followers gathered.

  • [Introduce Wovoka accurately: a Paiute prophet who in 1889 began teaching the Ghost Dance, a religious movement that promised renewal.]
  • Dancers believed the ceremony would restore the world, bring back the buffalo and the dead, and end suffering.
  • Nothing in the teaching called for war, and students should note that the reading describes a ceremony, not an uprising.
  • Then explain the spread with the lesson's own logic.
  • The teaching moved across western reservations in 1889 and 1890 and found many followers where rations had been cut and land taken.
  • At Pine Ridge the losses named in this lesson, the Black Hills, the buffalo, the children at distant schools, were all recent and personal.
  • A promise to restore exactly what had been lost answered exactly what people there had suffered.
  • That is why the dance grew, and it is the fact federal agents either missed or refused to credit, with results on the next slide.

Slide 18 · Wounded Knee, December 29, 1890

On the slide: five bullets — agents at Pine Ridge feared the dance and called for troops; military police killed Sitting Bull during an arrest attempt; Big Foot's band was surrounded and ordered to give up its weapons; a shot was fired, and the soldiers opened fire; about 250 to 300 Lakota men, women, and children were killed.

  • [Slow down for this slide and keep the sequence exact.]
  • Federal agents at Pine Ridge feared the Ghost Dance and called for troops.
  • In December 1890, military police killed the Lakota leader Sitting Bull during an arrest attempt.
  • Two weeks later, on December 29, 1890, soldiers of the Seventh Cavalry surrounded a band of Lakota led by Big Foot at Wounded Knee Creek and ordered them to surrender their weapons.
  • A shot was fired, and the soldiers opened fire.
  • About 250 to 300 Lakota men, women, and children were killed.
  • [Use the reading's final sentence as the class's verdict and write it where students can copy it: for the Lakota, Wounded Knee was a massacre of people who had already surrendered and were under guard.]
  • [Do not rush past the silence after this slide.]
  • The next section exists because Lakota families carried this memory and their language through everything that followed.

Slide 19 · Section F: Resistance and Survival (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "What was lost, and what Native peoples preserved"; the Zitkala-Sa portrait at right.

  • Picture: Zitkala-Sa, the Dakota writer, photographed by Gertrude Käsebier in 1898 with her violin.
  • The portrait is Zitkala-Sa, the Dakota writer named in the reading, photographed by Gertrude Käsebier in 1898 with her violin.
  • Two years later her accounts of boarding school life appeared in eastern magazines, describing for white readers what the schools did to children, and challenging assimilation in print.
  • She is the right face for this section because her life holds its whole argument.
  • She was taken into the boarding school system the policy built, learned its language and its tools, and then used those tools in public to defend her people.
  • [Set the section's claim before the details.]
  • The story of this era does not end at Wounded Knee.
  • Armed resistance had ended, but resistance changed form rather than disappearing, and preservation happened in homes, in courts, and in print.
  • What Native nations lost is measured in acres, lives, and years of children taken from home.
  • What they preserved is on the next two slides.

Slide 20 · Resistance changed form

On the slide: four bullets — treaty claims were pressed in courts and by petition; writers challenged assimilation in print; Zitkala-Sa described boarding school life for eastern readers; graduates used English and literacy to defend their nations.

  • [Make the continuity explicit: the peoples who fought in section A did not stop resisting when the wars ended; they changed instruments.]
  • Leaders pressed treaty claims in courts and by petition, using the government's own documents, including the treaties it had broken, as their evidence.
  • Writers carried the case to the public.
  • Zitkala-Sa, the Dakota author in the divider portrait, described boarding school life for eastern readers and challenged assimilation in print, reaching the same magazine audience that reformers wrote for.
  • The boarding schools themselves supplied some of the tools: graduates used their English and literacy to defend their nations in court and in print, a result the schools' founders did not intend.
  • Students sometimes read this as a silver lining that softens the policy.
  • [Correct that reading.]
  • A tool taken up by its target is resistance, not a benefit the policy planned, and the writing task rewards students who keep that distinction straight.

Slide 21 · What was preserved

On the slide: four bullets — languages and ceremonies were taught at home, hidden from agents; tribal governments survived on reduced land; languages and ceremonies endured; Native nations still exist as peoples and governments today.

  • [End the survey where the reading ends.]
  • Families hid languages and ceremonies from agents and taught them at home, in the one place federal supervision could not reliably reach.
  • That single fact explains much of what survived: a language spoken to children in a kitchen does not appear in an agent's report, and it can outlast a policy.
  • Tribal governments survived on reduced land, and languages and ceremonies endured.
  • Then give students the reading's closing judgment in full, because it reframes the whole lesson.
  • What Native nations lost was measured in acres, lives, and years of children taken from home.
  • What they preserved is the reason Native nations still exist as peoples and governments today.
  • [Return to the lesson question one last time.]
  • Assimilation was pursued to end Native cultures as distinct ways of life.
  • Judged by its own goal, it failed, and the evidence of that failure is that the peoples it targeted are still here.

Slide 22 · Vocabulary: words for the policy

On the slide: four terms with definitions — assimilation; allotment; Dawes Act; reservation system.

  • [These four definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.]
  • assimilation: the process by which a person or group is made to adopt the customs and language of another culture
  • allotment: the division of tribal land into individual parcels under federal policy
  • Dawes Act: the 1887 federal law that divided reservation land into individual allotments and sold surplus land to non-Native buyers
  • reservation system: federal policy that confined Native nations to defined areas of land under federal supervision
  • Note the passive construction, made to adopt: assimilation here was imposed, not chosen.
  • Students confuse allotment and the Dawes Act most often.
  • The act is the law; allotment is the process the law set running, on reservation after reservation, until 1934.

Slide 23 · Vocabulary: words for the schools and the dance

On the slide: three terms with definitions — boarding school; Ghost Dance; Wounded Knee.

  • [Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly.]
  • boarding school: a school that removed Native children from their families to suppress Native languages and cultures
  • Ghost Dance: a religious movement that promised renewal for Native peoples and spread across western reservations in 1889 and 1890
  • Wounded Knee: the 1890 killing of Lakota men, women, and children by United States troops in South Dakota
  • The boarding school definition names the removal and the suppression, not education in general, and students should keep both parts when they use the term.
  • Ghost Dance: it was a religion, and the definition's word renewal is the one to underline: dancers sought the restoration of a world, not a military victory.
  • [Teach Wounded Knee as a place name that carries an event: when students write Wounded Knee on the test, they mean December 29, 1890, Big Foot's band, and about 250 to 300 Lakota dead.]
