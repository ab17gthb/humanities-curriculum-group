Native Americans and the Closing Frontier

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

Treaties Broken, Land Taken

After the Civil War, the United States pushed west with soldiers, railroads, miners, and settlers. Native nations held legal title to much of that land under treaties the federal government had signed. The government broke its own agreements again and again when settlers or miners wanted the land. When gold was found in the Black Hills, land guaranteed to the Lakota by the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868, the government tried to buy the hills and then seized them in 1877.

Resistance brought war. The Lakota and their Cheyenne allies defeated George Custer at the Little Bighorn in 1876. The victory did not hold. The army campaigned through the winter, food ran short, and most Lakota bands surrendered within a year. In 1877 the Nez Perce, led in part by Chief Joseph, fled toward Canada rather than leave their homeland in Oregon. Soldiers captured them in Montana, about 40 miles from the border, after a journey of more than 1,000 miles. In the Southwest, Apache leaders including Geronimo resisted removal until 1886. By the late 1880s, large scale armed resistance had ended, not because Native peoples accepted the outcome, but because soldiers, supplies, and hunger made further war impossible.

The Reservation System and the Buffalo

Federal policy confined Native nations to reservations, defined areas held under federal supervision. Reservations were usually a fraction of a nation's former homeland, often the land settlers wanted least. Federal agents controlled food rations, policed movement, and pressed families to farm where farming was difficult. The system let the government open most of the West to settlement while keeping Native peoples in fixed places under federal control.

The destruction of the buffalo made resistance and independence harder still. Plains peoples depended on the buffalo for food, clothing, shelter, and tools. In the early 1870s, tens of millions still roamed the Plains. Commercial hunters, shipping hides east by railroad, killed them by the millions. Army officers welcomed the effect, because a people without food cannot stay independent for long. By the mid 1880s, only a few hundred wild buffalo remained. A food source and a way of life built over centuries had been destroyed in about fifteen years.

Assimilation as Federal Policy

As the wars ended, federal officials and white reformers turned to assimilation: the process by which a person or group is made to adopt the customs and language of another culture. Reformers who supported assimilation often described themselves as friends of Native peoples, and many had documented real abuses. Their remedy, however, was to end Native cultures as distinct ways of life. Native people would survive by becoming farmers, Christians, and English speakers, and tribal governments and communal landholding would end.

Boarding schools carried out that plan on children. Beginning with the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in 1879, the government and churches ran schools that removed children from their families to suppress Native languages and cultures. Children traveled hundreds of miles from home, had their hair cut, and were punished for speaking their own languages. Some died of disease far from home. Others later used their English and literacy to defend their nations in court and in print. Both facts belong in the record.

The Dawes Act and Allotment

The centerpiece of assimilation was the Dawes Act of 1887. The act divided reservation land into individual allotments, parcels assigned to single families or adults, declared the remaining tribal land surplus, and sold that surplus land to non-Native buyers. Supporters argued that private property would teach farming and independence. In practice, allotment broke up the land base that made Native self government possible.

In 1887 Native nations held about 138 million acres. By 1934, when allotment ended, about 90 million of those acres had passed out of Native hands, through surplus sales, individual sales, fraud, and tax loss. Allotment also divided what remained into scattered parcels with many heirs, hard to use and easy to lose. A policy announced as a path to independence left most Native nations poorer, with far less land.

The Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee

In 1889 a Paiute prophet named Wovoka began teaching the Ghost Dance, a religious movement that promised renewal and spread across western reservations in 1889 and 1890. Dancers believed the ceremony would restore the world, bring back the buffalo and the dead, and end suffering. Where rations had been cut and land taken, the teaching found many followers.

Federal agents at Pine Ridge feared the dance and called for troops. In December 1890, military police killed the Lakota leader Sitting Bull during an arrest attempt. Two weeks later, on December 29, 1890, soldiers of the Seventh Cavalry surrounded a band of Lakota led by Big Foot at Wounded Knee Creek and ordered them to surrender their weapons. A shot was fired, and the soldiers opened fire. About 250 to 300 Lakota men, women, and children were killed. For the Lakota, Wounded Knee was a massacre of people who had already surrendered and were under guard.

What Was Preserved

The story does not end at Wounded Knee. Families hid languages and ceremonies from agents and taught them at home. Leaders pressed treaty claims in courts and by petition. Writers such as the Dakota author Zitkala-Sa described boarding school life for eastern readers and challenged assimilation in print. Tribal governments survived on reduced land, and languages and ceremonies endured. What Native nations lost was measured in acres, lives, and years of children taken from home. What they preserved is the reason Native nations still exist as peoples and governments today.

Teacher Answer Key

1. How did the United States take the Black Hills, and what does that case show about treaty promises? The Black Hills were guaranteed to the Lakota by the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868. After gold was found there, the government tried to buy the hills and then seized them in 1877. The case shows that treaty promises held only until settlers, miners, or officials wanted the land badly enough to break them.

2. Why did the destruction of the buffalo matter so much to Plains peoples? Plains peoples depended on the buffalo for food, clothing, shelter, and tools. Commercial hide hunting, carried by railroad and welcomed by army officers, reduced tens of millions of animals to a few hundred wild survivors by the mid 1880s. Losing the buffalo destroyed a food source and economy and made independent life on the Plains nearly impossible.

3. How did the Dawes Act work, and how much land did Native nations lose under the allotment policy by 1934? The Dawes Act (1887) divided reservation land into individual allotments and sold the land declared surplus to non-Native buyers. Native nations held about 138 million acres in 1887; about 90 million acres passed out of Native hands by 1934 through surplus sales, individual sales, fraud, and tax loss.

4. Why did federal agents treat the Ghost Dance as a threat, and what happened at Wounded Knee on December 29, 1890? Agents feared a large religious gathering they did not control and misread a movement about renewal as preparation for war. At Wounded Knee Creek, Seventh Cavalry soldiers surrounded Big Foot's band of Lakota, ordered them to surrender their weapons, and opened fire after a shot was fired. About 250 to 300 Lakota men, women, and children were killed.

5. The reading argues that assimilation was described by its supporters as help. Using two pieces of evidence, explain why its effects were harmful even where its supporters opposed violence and theft. Strong answers use any two: boarding schools removed children from families, cut their hair, and punished them for speaking their languages, and some children died far from home; allotment cost Native nations about 90 million acres by 1934 and broke up the land base for self government; the stated goal itself was to end tribal governments, communal landholding, and Native cultures as distinct ways of life. The point students should reach: a policy can be harmful in design and result even when some supporters present themselves as reformers.
