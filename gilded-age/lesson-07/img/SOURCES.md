# Image Sources: Lesson 7, Native Americans and the Closing Frontier

Every image in this lesson is a real public domain photograph or illustration, downloaded from Wikimedia Commons or the Library of Congress. No image was generated or altered beyond resizing for print.

## 1. bison-skulls.jpg (Activity, Station 3)
- **Title:** Pile of American bison skulls, Michigan Carbon Works, Detroit
- **Creator:** Unknown photographer
- **Date:** circa 1892
- **Repository:** Burton Historical Collection, Detroit Public Library, via Wikimedia Commons
- **Source URL:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bison_skull_pile.jpg
- **Public domain basis:** Photograph published in the United States in the 1890s; copyright expired. Wikimedia Commons tags the file Public domain (PD-US).
- **Teaching reason:** The skull pile makes the scale of the buffalo destruction countable in a way a herd photograph cannot. Skulls were a commercial product, collected and ground for fertilizer, which is the point the station teaches: the slaughter was an industry, and its effect on Plains food supply was understood at the time.

## 2. carlisle-classroom.jpg (Activity, Station 2)
- **Title:** Classroom at the Indian Industrial School, Carlisle, Pennsylvania
- **Creator:** Frances Benjamin Johnston (1864 to 1952), photographer
- **Date:** 1901
- **Repository:** Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division (LC-USZ62-115435; LCCN 96512679)
- **Source URL:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Classroom_at_the_Indian_Industrial_School,_Carlisle,_Pennsylvania_LCCN96512679.jpg (catalog: https://lccn.loc.gov/96512679)
- **Public domain basis:** Work of a United States photographer published in 1901; copyright expired. Library of Congress lists no known restrictions; Wikimedia Commons tags the file Public domain.
- **Teaching reason:** A posed federal publicity photograph. Students are asked to read it as the school wanted to be seen, then set it against a student account of the same system. The gap between the image and the account is the lesson.

## 3. ghost-dance.jpg (Activity, Station 4)
- **Title:** The Ghost Dance by the Oglala Lakota at Pine Ridge Agency, South Dakota
- **Creator:** Frederic Remington (1861 to 1909), artist
- **Date:** 1890 (published in Harper's Weekly)
- **Repository:** Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division (LCCN 90707734)
- **Source URL:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Ghost_dance_by_the_Ogallala_(sic)_Sioux_at_Pine_Ridge_Agency_..._Dakota_-_Frederic_Remington,_Pine_Ridge,_S._Dak._LCCN90707734.jpg (catalog: https://lccn.loc.gov/90707734)
- **Public domain basis:** Illustration published in the United States in 1890; copyright expired. Wikimedia Commons tags the file Public domain.
- **Teaching reason:** This is a white artist's magazine illustration, not a photograph, and the caption says so. Students use it to study how eastern readers pictured the Ghost Dance, which helps explain why agents and newspapers treated a religious movement as a threat. No photograph of the Wounded Knee dead is used in this lesson; the killing is taught through the record, not through images of bodies.

## 4. tom-torlino.jpg (Quick Check)
- **Title:** Tom Torlino, Navajo, before and after (arrival portrait, 1882; portrait after about three years at Carlisle, 1885)
- **Creator:** John N. Choate (1848 to 1902), photographer
- **Date:** 1882 and 1885
- **Repository:** Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, Yale University, via Wikimedia Commons
- **Source URL:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tom_Torlino_Navajo_before_and_after_circa_1882.jpg
- **Public domain basis:** Photographs taken in the United States in 1882 and 1885; copyright expired. Wikimedia Commons tags the file Public domain.
- **Teaching reason:** The paired portraits were made by the school's photographer to advertise assimilation, and Tom Torlino later returned home and resumed Navajo life, a fact the key supplies. Students analyze what the school wanted the pair to prove and what it cannot show. The subject is named, as he should be: he is a person in the record, not an illustration of a policy.
