Part 2: Review Questions

Lesson 1: The Engines of Industry

1. Why was the completion of the transcontinental railroad in 1869 a beginning rather than an end? State how much railroad mileage grew between 1860 and 1900 and what the network did to American markets.

2. Name the two standards the railroads set for the whole country, and state the problem each one solved.

3. Explain how steel, oil refining, the telegraph, and the corporation each served the railroad system.

Lesson 2: Big Business and Its Critics

4. Contrast Carnegie's vertical integration with Rockefeller's horizontal integration. Give one fact about each man's company.

5. How did a trust work, what did the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 ban, and why was the act weak in its early years?

6. State the laissez-faire argument and the Social Darwinism argument. Then give one answer critics made to the defenders of big business.

Lesson 3: The Workers' World

7. Why could one adult manufacturing wage often not support a family, and how does that fact help explain child labor? Use the 1900 census figure for working children.

8. Compare the Knights of Labor and the American Federation of Labor in who they organized and what they sought.

9. Choose Haymarket, Homestead, or Pullman. State what the workers wanted, what happened, and why the strike ended as it did. Name the part played by courts, troops, employers, or public opinion.

Lesson 4: The New Immigration

10. Give two push factors and two pull factors that brought the new immigrants to the United States after 1880.

11. Compare processing at Ellis Island and Angel Island. Cover how long most arrivals waited and what Asian arrivals had to prove.

12. What did the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 do, and how does it show nativism becoming federal law?

Lesson 5: The City

13. How did tenement conditions and weak sanitation work together to spread disease in a growing city?

14. What did a political machine give the residents of a poor neighborhood, and what did the machine and its boss take in return?

15. A settlement house and a political machine both served the same neighborhoods. State how their methods and their purposes differed.

Lesson 6: The West

16. The Homestead Act and railroad land grants both moved public land into private hands. State who received land under each and for what purpose.

17. Give the three reasons the cattle drives declined in the 1880s.

18. Why did mining booms turn independent prospectors into wage workers for large companies?

Lesson 7: Native Americans and the Closing Frontier

19. What did the Dawes Act do to tribal landholding? State how much land Native nations held in 1887 and how much had passed out of Native hands by 1934.

20. How did boarding schools such as Carlisle pursue assimilation in daily practice?

21. Trace the Ghost Dance to Wounded Knee. Who began the movement, why did federal agents act, and what happened on December 29, 1890?

Lesson 8: Race, Politics, and Protest

22. How did Plessy v. Ferguson and disfranchisement reinforce each other?

23. Contrast Ida B. Wells and Booker T. Washington. State what each said Black Americans should do about lynching and segregation, and name the work or speech each is known for here.

24. State two demands of the Omaha Platform, explain free silver, and explain why the election of 1896 ended the Populist Party but not its ideas.

Part 3: The Arguments of the Era

Every lesson in this unit is one side of an argument the country was having with itself. The test asks you to argue these positions, not just name them. Read each pair below and be ready to state both sides with evidence.

Laissez-faire against regulation. Defenders of the new corporations held that government should not regulate business, because competition rewarded efficiency and lowered prices. Critics answered that a monopoly ends competition itself, so only government power could restore it. The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 banned combinations in restraint of trade, but weak early enforcement left the argument open for the next era to settle.

Workers against employers. Employers treated wages, hours, and the pace of work as theirs to set, and one worker alone could simply be replaced. Workers answered by organizing, bargaining collectively, and striking. At Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman the strikes were defeated by courts, troops, injunctions, and hostile public opinion, but the question of what an employer owed a worker did not go away.

Restriction against admission. The United States admitted more than twenty million immigrants in these decades, and its factories and cities ran on their labor. Nativists answered that the new arrivals from southern and eastern Europe and from Asia threatened jobs and American institutions. Restriction won its first federal victories early, in contract labor limits and the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, while admission remained the daily practice at Ellis Island.

Assimilation against sovereignty. Federal officials said Native nations should give up tribal landholding, language, and government and live as individual farmers in the American pattern. The Dawes Act, the reservation system, and the boarding schools were built to force that change. Native peoples resisted in war, in court, and in daily cultural survival, and treated their nations as sovereign peoples with treaty rights, not as populations to be remade.

Jim Crow against protest. Southern states built a legal order of segregation and disfranchisement and defended it as settled custom, and the Supreme Court upheld it in Plessy v. Ferguson. Black Americans answered in print and in public. Ida B. Wells documented lynching as a tool of racial control, and Booker T. Washington, while accepting segregation for the present in the Atlanta Compromise, built Black economic and educational institutions under it.

Populism against the gold standard. Farmers in debt watched crop prices and the money supply shrink under deflation, and blamed railroads, banks, and a currency tied to gold alone. The Populist Party demanded free silver, railroad regulation, and a graduated income tax in the Omaha Platform. William McKinley's victory over William Jennings Bryan in 1896 ended the party, but its demands entered the program the Progressive Era later enacted in part.

Teacher Answer Key

Part 1: Key Terms (model definitions, as given in the guided notes)

Lesson 1. transcontinental railroad: a railroad line connecting the eastern rail network with the Pacific coast. Bessemer process: a method for making steel quickly and cheaply by blowing air through molten iron. refinery: a plant where crude oil is separated into useful products such as kerosene. corporation: a business owned by shareholders and treated by law as distinct from its owners. capital: money or property used to start or expand a business. telegraph: a system for sending coded messages over wires. standard time: a system of uniform time zones that replaced local solar time.

Lesson 2. trust: a combination of corporations that limits competition within an industry. monopoly: control of an industry by one company or combination, with no effective competition. vertical integration: control by one company of every stage in making and selling a product. horizontal integration: control by one company of many firms at the same stage of an industry. laissez-faire: the argument that government should not regulate business or the economy. Social Darwinism: the argument that wealth and power show natural fitness and that government aid distorts competition. Sherman Antitrust Act: the 1890 federal law that banned combinations in restraint of trade.

Lesson 3. union: an organization of workers formed to bargain with employers as a group. strike: a work stoppage meant to pressure an employer to meet workers' demands. collective bargaining: negotiation between an employer and workers organized as a group. injunction: a court order that requires or forbids a specific action. Knights of Labor: a national labor organization of the 1870s and 1880s that sought broad reform and organized many kinds of workers. American Federation of Labor: a federation of craft unions founded in 1886 that focused on wages, hours, and working conditions. child labor: paid work by children, often in factories, mines, mills, and street trades.

Lesson 4. immigrant: a person who comes to live permanently in a new country. emigrant: a person who leaves a country to live elsewhere. nativism: a policy or attitude favoring native-born residents over immigrants. Ellis Island: the federal immigration station in New York Harbor that processed most European arrivals after 1892. Angel Island: the immigration station in San Francisco Bay that processed and often detained many Asian arrivals. Chinese Exclusion Act: the 1882 federal law that suspended Chinese labor immigration and barred Chinese residents from naturalized citizenship. assimilation: the process by which a person or group adopts the customs and language of another culture.

Lesson 5. tenement: a crowded, often unsafe multi-family apartment building in a city. urbanization: the growth of cities as people move to them for work and services. political machine: an organization that controls a city or state government through favors, jobs, and votes. boss: the leader of a political machine who directs its workers and rewards. graft: the illegal use of political power for personal profit, such as kickbacks and bribes. settlement house: a community center in a poor neighborhood offering classes, childcare, and help to residents. sanitation: public measures that keep water, waste, and streets clean enough to protect health.

Lesson 6. homestead: a farm claim of up to 160 acres of public land made under the Homestead Act. land grant: public land given by the government to a railroad or other company to support construction. cattle drive: the movement of herds on foot from Texas ranches to railheads and markets. mining boom: a rapid rush of people and capital to a place where gold, silver, or another mineral was found. barbed wire: fencing wire with sharp barbs that enclosed open range cheaply. dry farming: methods of growing crops in dry regions by conserving soil moisture. reservation: land set aside by treaty or federal order for a Native nation.

Lesson 7. assimilation: the process by which a person or group is made to adopt the customs and language of another culture. allotment: the division of tribal land into individual parcels under federal policy. Dawes Act: the 1887 federal law that divided reservation land into individual allotments and sold surplus land to non-Native buyers. reservation system: federal policy that confined Native nations to defined areas of land under federal supervision. boarding school: a school that removed Native children from their families to suppress Native languages and cultures. Ghost Dance: a religious movement that promised renewal for Native peoples and spread across western reservations in 1889 and 1890. Wounded Knee: the 1890 killing of Lakota men, women, and children by United States troops in South Dakota.

Lesson 8. Jim Crow: the system of state and local laws and customs that enforced racial segregation in the South. segregation: the separation of people by race in public facilities, schools, and transportation. disfranchisement: the denial of the right to vote through law or practice. Plessy v. Ferguson: the 1896 Supreme Court decision that upheld segregation under the doctrine of separate but equal. Atlanta Compromise: Booker T. Washington's 1895 speech proposing that Black southerners accept segregation for the present and build economic strength first. Populist Party: the People's Party, founded in 1892 to represent farmers and laborers against banks, railroads, and corporations. free silver: the proposal to coin silver without limit at a ratio of 16 to 1 with gold, expanding the money supply.

Note for the teacher: assimilation and reservation appear in two lessons. Lesson 4 defines assimilation as a process a group undergoes; Lesson 7 defines it as a process a group is made to undergo, which is the federal policy sense. Accept the definition that matches the lesson in use.

Part 2: Review Questions

1. The 1869 line as a beginning. The Promontory Summit meeting completed one line, but mileage then grew from about 30,000 miles in 1860 to about 193,000 miles by 1900. The network carried heavy loads in all seasons at low cost, so sellers who had served local markets now competed in one national market.

2. Two railroad standards. Standard gauge ended the unloading and reloading of freight where lines of different gauges met. Standard time, adopted November 18, 1883, replaced local solar time with four uniform zones, which made schedules and connections across lines possible.

3. Steel, oil, wires, and money. Steel: the Bessemer process made steel by the ton, and steel rails outlasted iron rails that wore out quickly. Oil: large refineries separated crude oil into products such as kerosene more cheaply than small ones. Telegraph: managers learned distant prices in minutes, ordered cars where they were needed, and coordinated schedules. Corporation: shareholders supplied capital beyond any single owner's means while risking only what they put in.

4. Vertical against horizontal. Carnegie used vertical integration in steel, owning the mines, transport, and mills so his company paid no middleman. Rockefeller used horizontal integration in oil, combining rival refineries until Standard Oil controlled most refining capacity in the country.

5. Trusts and the Sherman Act. In a trust, stockholders handed their shares to trustees, who ran the member companies as one enterprise, setting one price and dividing markets. The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 banned combinations in restraint of trade. It was weak early because the federal government brought few cases and used the law more readily against unions than against corporations in its first years.

6. The defense of bigness and its critics. Laissez-faire held that government should not regulate business or the economy. Social Darwinism held that wealth and power show natural fitness and that government aid distorts competition. Critics answered with conduct, not theory alone: Standard Oil won secret rebates from railroads, cut prices until local rivals failed, and then raised prices once competition was gone.

7. Wages and child labor. Average manufacturing earnings were only a few hundred dollars a year, and one adult wage often could not cover rent, food, and clothing for a family, so families sent children to work. The census of 1900 counted about 1.75 million children ages 10 to 15 in paid work, about one child in five. Employers hired children because they were cheap, could reach into small spaces, and rarely protested.

8. Knights against the AFL. The Knights of Labor, led by Terence Powderly, organized many kinds of workers in one order and sought broad reform, including the eight-hour day and an end to child labor; membership reached roughly 700,000 in 1886. The American Federation of Labor, founded in 1886 and led by Samuel Gompers, was a federation of craft unions that focused on wages, hours, and working conditions for its members.

9. One great strike. Haymarket (1886): workers demanded the eight-hour day; a bomb at a Chicago meeting linked labor with violence in public opinion, leaders were tried and hanged, and the Knights were blamed and collapsed. Homestead (1892): steelworkers resisted a wage cut and the closing of their union; after a fight with Pinkerton guards, the state militia protected the reopened plant and replacement workers until the strike collapsed. Pullman (1894): workers struck over wage cuts with company rents unchanged; a federal injunction and federal troops broke the boycott, and Eugene V. Debs was jailed. Accept any one, accurately told.

10. Push and pull. Push, any two: farmland plots too small to feed a family, required military service, pogroms against Jews in Russia. Pull, any two: industrial jobs and American wages, cheap steerage tickets, letters, money, and prepaid tickets sent by earlier migrants.

11. Ellis Island against Angel Island. At Ellis Island most European arrivals were processed in a few hours, checked against the ship's manifest, and about 2 percent were excluded. At Angel Island many Asian arrivals were held in barracks for weeks and often months while officials compared their answers with witnesses' answers, and Chinese arrivals had to prove an exempt status such as merchant or son of a citizen.

12. Chinese Exclusion. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 suspended Chinese labor immigration and barred Chinese residents from naturalized citizenship. A private attitude, nativism, here became national law: for the first time the federal government excluded a group by nationality and race rather than by individual inspection.

13. Tenements, sanitation, and disease. Tenements packed whole families into rooms that often had no windows, with whole floors sharing one or two toilets and water from a single tap or pump. Privies stood close to wells, so waste seeped into drinking water and spread typhoid fever and cholera, while tuberculosis spread through crowded, unventilated rooms. Crowding supplied the contact and weak sanitation supplied the route.

14. What the machine gave and took. The machine gave jobs, fuel, food, help with officials, and quick favors that city government did not provide. In return it took votes for its candidates, and its boss and contractors took graft, kickbacks and bribes on city contracts paid for with public money.

15. Settlement house against machine. A settlement house was a community center in a poor neighborhood offering classes, childcare, and help to residents; its workers aimed at reform, teaching skills and pressing the city for services and regulation. A machine aimed at power: it traded favors and jobs for votes and profited from graft. Both met needs the city left unmet, but one sought to change conditions and the other sought to control the government that set them.

16. Two roads from public land. Under the Homestead Act of 1862, a settler family received a claim of up to 160 acres, paid a small filing fee, and earned title by living on the claim and improving it for five years. Under land grants, the government gave public land directly to railroad companies to support construction, and the companies sold much of it to raise money and settle the country their lines crossed.

17. Why the drives declined. Railroads built into Texas, so herds no longer had to walk to Kansas railheads. Barbed wire, patented in 1874, fenced the open range the drives crossed. Severe winters in the middle 1880s killed cattle by the hundreds of thousands.

18. Prospectors into wage workers. Surface deposits ran out quickly. Deep mining required shafts, pumps, and machinery that only large companies could afford, so prospectors sold their claims or went to work for wages in company mines. The Comstock Lode is the lesson's example.

19. The Dawes Act and the land base. The Dawes Act of 1887 divided reservation land into individual allotments and sold land declared surplus to non-Native buyers, ending tribal landholding as the norm. Native nations held about 138 million acres in 1887; by 1934 about 90 million acres had passed out of Native hands through surplus sales, individual sales, fraud, and tax loss.

20. Boarding schools in daily practice. Schools such as Carlisle, opened in 1879 in Pennsylvania, removed Native children from their families for years at a time. Children were punished for speaking their own languages, and the schools set out to replace Native languages and cultures with English, Christianity, and farming or trade training.

21. From the Ghost Dance to Wounded Knee. The Paiute prophet Wovoka began teaching the Ghost Dance in 1889; the movement promised renewal and spread across western reservations. Agents at Pine Ridge feared the dance and called for troops. On December 29, 1890, soldiers at Wounded Knee Creek killed about 250 to 300 Lakota men, women, and children from Big Foot's band, which had already surrendered and was under guard.

22. Plessy and disfranchisement together. Plessy made segregation constitutional under separate but equal, so segregationist governments faced no federal check from the courts. Disfranchisement through poll taxes, literacy tests, and grandfather clauses removed the Black vote that might have challenged those governments at the ballot box. Each protected the other.

23. Wells against Washington. Wells investigated lynching and published Southern Horrors in 1892, showing that many victims had committed no crime; she demanded direct public confrontation of lynching and defense of civil and political rights. Washington, in the Atlanta Compromise of 1895, proposed that Black southerners accept segregation for the present and build economic strength first. Critics answered that giving up the vote and civil rights left Black people no way to protect their gains.

24. Omaha, free silver, and 1896. Omaha Platform demands, any two: railroad regulation or government ownership of railroads, a graduated income tax, free silver, direct election of senators. Free silver meant coining silver without limit at a ratio of 16 to 1 with gold, expanding the money supply and easing farm debt under deflation. In 1896 the Democrats nominated Bryan and the Populists endorsed him, so the party lost its separate identity; William McKinley, the Republican candidate, won. The party collapsed, but many of its demands later became Progressive reforms.
