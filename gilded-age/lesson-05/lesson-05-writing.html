Writing Task: The Machine, Judged by Its Street

The prompt. It is 1890. You live in a tenement neighborhood and you are writing for a neighborhood newspaper. Write a paragraph or short essay answering this question: did the political machine help the people of your neighborhood more than it harmed them, or harm them more than it helped? Use at least two of the evidence cards below, and answer the strongest point on the other side.

The cards are summaries drawn from the lesson reading. Write the history in your own sentences, and do not copy the cards word for word.

Card 1: Help That Arrived in Person

The machine's precinct workers knew their blocks by name. They found supporters jobs on city crews, helped families facing eviction or a winter without coal, helped new immigrants become citizens, and stood by a family when a son was arrested or a wage earner died. For many poor families, this was the only organized help that ever reached them.

Card 2: The Bill the City Paid

The machine's leaders practiced graft: contractors padded the cost of paving, buildings, and supplies, and shared the extra money with the politicians who approved the bills. The Tweed Ring in New York was charged with stealing sums estimated in the tens of millions of dollars. Taxpayers paid the padded bills, and city jobs went to loyal supporters rather than to the best qualified applicants.

Card 3: What the Machine Did Not Build

While machines ran many cities, tenements still packed twenty families into buildings with one tap and shared toilets, and waste collection in poor districts stayed irregular. The systems that did change conditions, aqueducts, sewers, inspected housing, and Waring's reorganized street cleaning in 1895, came from public departments run on professional lines, often under reform administrations.

Card 4: The Settlement House Alternative

At Hull House in Chicago, opened in 1889, Jane Addams and her coworkers lived in the neighborhood, ran classes and childcare, studied local conditions, and pressed the city for playgrounds, bathhouses, factory inspection, and honest garbage collection. Settlement houses offered help without asking for a vote in return, but they depended on donations and had no power to tax or to build.

Write Your Answer

Write for a reader on your own block in 1890. Aim for about 300 words, and use only what a person in 1890 could know, except where a card gives you a later fact as evidence to weigh; if you use the 1895 street cleaning example, present it as a proposal your city should copy, not as news.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that takes one side of the help or harm question and gives the deciding reason, not a summary of both sides with no verdict.
  • At least two cards used accurately. Card 1 for the machine's personal help; Card 2 for graft and its cost to taxpayers; Card 3 for the systems the machine failed to build; Card 4 for an alternative kind of help and its limits.
  • The strongest point on the other side stated fairly and answered. A help-side essay must answer Card 2 or 3, not a weaker charge. A harm-side essay must answer Card 1 directly: the help was real, and the answer must explain why it does not settle the question, for example because help given as a favor can be withdrawn from opponents, or because the same taxes could have funded services for everyone.
  • Period discipline: a reference to Waring's 1895 reorganization presented as something an 1890 writer already knows costs the evidence point; presented as a proposal, it earns credit.

Model outline for the debrief. Harm side: claim; Card 2 (theft on a scale no basket of coal repays); Card 3 (the machine's decades in power left housing and sanitation unbuilt); answer to Card 1 (help was real but rationed by loyalty, and it was paid for with the neighborhood's own taxes, taken twice). Help side: claim; Card 1 (help arrived in person, fast, without forms or English); Card 4 as the comparison (the settlement house meant well but could not tax, build, or hire); answer to Card 2 (graft raised the price of government, but a family facing eviction weighs the help it can see against a cost spread across a whole city). Credit either verdict argued this way.
