The City

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

Lesson question: How did rapid urban growth change daily life, health, and city government?

A City Built in a Generation

Between 1860 and 1900, two of every five Americans came to live in a city. Chicago held about 112,000 people in 1860 and about 1.7 million in 1900. New York passed 3.4 million after it joined with Brooklyn in 1898. Historians call this rapid growth urbanization: the growth of cities as people move to them for work and services. Factories, railroads, and ports drew the newcomers. Some came from American farms. Many more crossed oceans, arriving from Ireland, Germany, Italy, eastern Europe, and China, or moved from the rural South. All of them needed housing, water, and work at once.

The city they built was vertical and crowded. Steel frames and elevators made the skyscraper possible, and office buildings of ten stories or more rose downtown after the 1880s. Land near the center became so valuable that builders covered nearly every foot of a lot.

Living Packed Together

Most working families rented rooms in a tenement: a crowded, often unsafe multi-family apartment building in a city. Owners divided older houses into small apartments or built cheap new blocks shaped to squeeze more rooms onto a narrow lot. A single tenement might hold twenty families. Back rooms often had no outside window. Whole floors shared one or two toilets, sometimes in the yard, and water came from a single tap or pump.

Crowding was dangerous as well as uncomfortable. Fires spread quickly through wooden stairs and air shafts, and coal stoves filled rooms with smoke. The journalist and photographer Jacob Riis, himself an immigrant from Denmark, recorded these conditions in his 1890 book How the Other Half Lives. His photographs showed middle-class readers that tenement poverty was a condition of housing and health, not simply a failure of character.

Water, Waste, and Disease

A city of millions produced waste on a scale no town had faced. Horses left manure in the streets by the ton. Households threw ashes, garbage, and dirty water into yards and gutters. Privies stood close to wells, so waste could seep into drinking water. The public measures that keep water, waste, and streets clean enough to protect health are called sanitation, and sanitation lagged far behind population.

Disease followed the water supply. Typhoid fever and cholera spread through contaminated water and milk, and tuberculosis spread through crowded, unventilated rooms. Cities responded by building on a new scale: aqueducts for clean water, sewer lines under the streets, and paved roads that could be washed. These systems worked only when a city government could plan, tax, build, and inspect, year after year. In New York in 1895, street cleaning commissioner George E. Waring put his sweepers in white uniforms, required regular routes, and separated garbage from ashes for collection. His crews, nicknamed the White Wings, showed that a disciplined public department could change a city within months.

Getting Around and Going Out

Growth also changed how a day felt. Horse-drawn streetcars, then cable cars, electric trolleys, and elevated railways let workers live farther from their jobs. Riders who could pay the fare settled in new streetcar suburbs at the edge of the city, while the poorest families stayed in the crowded center, close to work they reached on foot. Department stores filled whole downtown blocks, and theaters, dance halls, and baseball parks sold leisure by the ticket. The city was harsher than the town, and also faster and brighter.

The Machine That Ran the City

City governments were not built for this scale, and into the gap stepped the political machine: an organization that controls a city or state government through favors, jobs, and votes. At its head stood the boss: the leader of a political machine who directs its workers and rewards. In New York, William M. Tweed led the Tammany Hall machine in the late 1860s and early 1870s. Precinct workers knew their neighborhoods by name. The machine found supporters jobs, helped families with rent or coal, naturalized new immigrants, and stood by a man arrested or a widow in trouble. In return, it expected votes, and it usually got them.

The same system that delivered help also stole. Graft, the illegal use of political power for personal profit, such as kickbacks and bribes, ran through machine government. Contractors padded the cost of paving, buildings, and supplies and shared the extra money with the politicians who approved the bills. The Tweed Ring was charged with stealing sums that contemporary estimates placed in the tens of millions of dollars. Honest accounting matters in both directions. The machine's help was real, and for many poor families it was the only help available. Its theft was also real, and it raised taxes and rewarded loyalty over competence.

Reform Begins at Home

The first reformers often started inside the neighborhood itself. A settlement house was a community center in a poor neighborhood offering classes, childcare, and help to residents. Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr opened Hull House in Chicago in 1889. Its workers lived in the neighborhood, ran a kindergarten and classes, studied local conditions, and pressed the city for playgrounds, bathhouses, factory inspection, and honest garbage collection.

By the 1890s their pressure joined a wider demand for professional city administration: trained health officers, inspected tenements, civil service hiring instead of patronage jobs, and mayors elected on reform tickets, as New York elected William Strong in 1894. Reform did not end machine politics. But the argument had changed. A growing city needed services that no ward organization, however helpful to its friends, could supply fairly to everyone.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What pulled newcomers to the cities? Work in factories, railroads, and ports, and the services of a city. Newcomers came from American farms, from Ireland, Germany, Italy, eastern Europe, and China, and from the rural South. All of them needed housing, water, work, and transportation at the same time, which is why growth strained every city system at once.

2. How did tenement housing turn crowding into a health danger? Any two: rooms without outside windows or ventilation; whole floors sharing one or two toilets, sometimes in the yard; water from a single tap or pump shared by many families; fire risk from wooden stairs, air shafts, and coal stoves; crowding that spread tuberculosis and other disease. Riis documented rooms sleeping several lodgers to a room.

3. Why did water and waste require a strong city government? Aqueducts, sewers, paving, and regular collection are systems that serve whole districts at once and must be planned, taxed for, built, and inspected year after year. A single household could not keep its own water clean when a neighbor's privy stood next to the shared well. Waring's White Wings showed what a disciplined public department could do.

4. Explain both sides of the political machine. Help: jobs on city crews, rent and coal assistance, naturalization help, and support for families in trouble, delivered by precinct workers who knew the neighborhood; this help was real and often the only help available. Harm: graft through padded contracts and kickbacks, the Tweed Ring's theft estimated in the tens of millions of dollars, higher taxes, and hiring by loyalty rather than competence.

5. Did living in a neighborhood make reformers more effective? Accept either position defended with two facts. For yes: Hull House workers (from 1889) lived in the neighborhood, ran classes and childcare, studied local conditions firsthand, and pressed for specific services such as playgrounds, bathhouses, factory inspection, and garbage collection. For a qualified no: neighborhood knowledge still had to be converted into citywide systems, professional administration, and law, which required political power beyond one settlement.
