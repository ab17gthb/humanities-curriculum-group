Teacher Answer Key

SystemWhat growth changedThe problem it createdWho responded, and how
HousingCities grew from towns to millions in a generation; Chicago went from about 112,000 people in 1860 to about 1.7 million in 1900. Builders covered whole lots with tenements of twenty families.Rooms without windows, whole floors sharing toilets, one tap or pump per building, fire risk, and disease spreading through crowding.Jacob Riis documented tenement life in How the Other Half Lives (1890); settlement houses studied conditions block by block; reformers pressed for inspected tenements and housing rules.
Water and healthMillions of people and horses produced waste on a new scale, while aqueducts, sewers, and paving were built to catch up.Privies stood close to wells; waste seeped into drinking water; typhoid and cholera spread through water and milk, tuberculosis through crowded rooms.City engineers built water and sewer systems. In New York in 1895, George E. Waring reorganized street cleaning with set routes, separated collection, and sweepers in white uniforms, the White Wings.
Getting aroundHorse-drawn streetcars, cable cars, electric trolleys, and elevated railways let workers live farther from their jobs.Streets carried markets, wagons, and crowds at once; families too poor for daily fares stayed in the crowded center and walked to work.Private companies built and ran most transit lines under city franchises; riders with fare money settled in new streetcar suburbs at the edge of the city.
City governmentPolitical machines filled the gap left by small city governments, organizing whole cities through ward and precinct workers under a boss.Graft: padded contracts, kickbacks, and bribes. The Tweed Ring's theft was estimated in the tens of millions of dollars, and hiring rewarded loyalty over competence.The machine itself responded to residents personally with jobs, coal, rent help, and naturalization aid, which kept voters loyal. Reformers answered with settlement houses such as Hull House (1889), civil service hiring, trained health officers, and reform mayors such as William Strong in New York (1894).

The pattern. Strong answers name the shared pattern: every system was built for a smaller city and was overwhelmed by growth, so each problem was a systems problem. Housing, water, transit, and government all had to be rebuilt at city scale, and the fight over who would rebuild them, machines, reform administrations, or private companies, was the politics of the era.
