Writing Task: Who Decided Who Belonged?

The prompt. Between 1882 and 1924 the United States built a system for deciding which immigrants belonged. Write a short essay answering the lesson question: how did the United States decide who belonged? Your essay must argue where the real decision was made, in Congress, at the inspection stations, or in both places in different ways, and it must use at least three of the evidence cards below.

The cards summarize facts from the lesson reading and the arrival record jigsaw. Write the history in your own sentences. Do not copy card wording into the essay.

Card 1: The Law in 1882

The Chinese Exclusion Act suspended Chinese labor immigration and barred Chinese residents from naturalized citizenship. It was the first federal law to bar a named national group, and it was renewed and later made permanent.

Card 2: Hours at Ellis Island

At Ellis Island, doctors gave arrivals a seconds long first look, inspectors checked answers against the ship manifest, and most Europeans were admitted within a few hours. About 2 percent were excluded, most often on medical grounds or as likely public charges.

Card 3: Months at Angel Island

At Angel Island, Chinese arrivals claiming an exempt status were held in barracks for weeks and often months. Officials questioned applicants and witnesses separately and compared the answers for mismatches. Detainees wrote and carved poems on the barrack walls.

Card 4: The Widening Gate Law

Restriction widened in steps. An 1885 law barred contract laborers. In 1917 a literacy test and a barred zone ended most immigration from Asia. Quota laws in 1921 and 1924 cut immigration sharply and gave the smallest quotas to southern and eastern Europeans.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that takes a position on where the decision was made. A both places answer earns full credit only if it assigns each place a distinct job, for example Congress set the categories and inspectors decided individual cases inside them. A vague answer in which everyone decided a little does not.
  • At least three cards used accurately. Card 1 and Card 4 carry the Congress argument. Cards 2 and 3 carry the station argument, and the contrast between hours and months is the strongest single piece of evidence in the set.
  • The counterpoint answered, not ignored. Congress answers must explain why Angel Island questioning still decided real cases. Station answers must explain why inspectors at the two stations behaved so differently, which leads back to the laws they were enforcing.
  • Accurate use of the lesson vocabulary, with at least two of these used correctly: immigrant, nativism, Ellis Island, Angel Island, Chinese Exclusion Act, assimilation.
  • No invented evidence. Quotations, statistics, or events that appear in neither the reading, the jigsaw, nor the cards cost the evidence point.

Model line of argument. The decision was made in Congress first and at the stations second. The 1882 Act created the categories before any Chinese arrival reached San Francisco, and the widening laws of 1885, 1917, 1921, and 1924 fixed most outcomes by birthplace. Inside those categories, inspectors held real power: a chalk mark, a manifest check, or a mismatched answer in an Angel Island interview could end a case that the law had left open.
