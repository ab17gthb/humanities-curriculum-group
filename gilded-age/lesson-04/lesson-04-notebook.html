Southern and eastern EuropeansEllis Island in New York Harbor, the federal station that processed most European arrivals after 1892. About 12 million people passed through between 1892 and 1954.A seconds long medical look on the stairs, with chalk marks sending the doubtful cases to a closer examination, then standard questions checked against the ship manifest written at departure.Admission within a few hours for most arrivals. About 2 percent were excluded, most often for trachoma or another medical condition or as likely public charges.
Chinese arrivalsAngel Island in San Francisco Bay, 1910 to 1940. Before 1910, Chinese arrivals were held in a detention shed on the San Francisco waterfront.Proof of an exempt status under the Chinese Exclusion Act, such as merchant or son of a citizen. Detailed questioning of the applicant and witnesses, with answers compared for any mismatch.Detention in barracks for weeks and often months while the claim was proved or disproved. Entry was possible but slow, and some claims failed after long waits. Detainees wrote and carved poems on the barrack walls.
Other Asian arrivalsAngel Island and other West Coast ports. Japanese, Korean, and South Asian arrivals form the main groups in this lesson.Identity, status, and admissibility under the restriction laws in force, including the 1917 barred zone that ended most immigration from Asia.Many were detained and questioned, though exclusion law fell hardest and longest on the Chinese. Admission narrowed sharply after 1917 and again under the 1924 quotas.

The pattern. Strong answers locate the decision in the law, not at the dock. Congress wrote the categories first: the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 named one group, the 1917 law barred most of a continent, and the 1921 and 1924 quotas ranked nearly every birthplace. Inspectors then applied those categories case by case, which is why the same ocean crossing could end in hours at Ellis Island or in months at Angel Island.
