The New Immigration

The Gilded Age · Lesson 4 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: The Gilded Age, Lesson 4 of 8. Lessons 1 through 3 built the industrial machine — the engines, the combinations, and the workers' answer to them; this lesson follows the people the machine pulled across two oceans, and asks who the country let in. It answers its two-part question in order: sections A through E trace the new immigrants — where they came from, why they left, how they were inspected, and where they settled — and section F explains how the United States, step by step between 1882 and 1924, decided who belonged. Lesson 5, The City, picks up where this lesson's neighborhoods leave off.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 3 (the factory and mine jobs the arrivals filled are the ones Lesson 3 examined from the inside; the wage arithmetic that sent American children to work also pulled families across the Atlantic) and Lesson 5, The City (the neighborhoods named here — Little Italy, Chinatown — become that lesson's subject). Inside the lesson, the two stations are taught as a deliberate pair throughout: Ellis Island, where most Europeans were admitted within hours, against Angel Island, where many Asian arrivals were detained for weeks and months, with the contrast collected on the exit ticket and the writing task.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Lessons 1 through 3's account of industrial growth and the demand for labor; no earlier immigration history is assumed, and the lesson teaches the before-1880 pattern itself on slide 5. Every figure in the lesson is taught as the reading states it — more than 20 million arrivals between 1880 and 1920; about one American in seven foreign born by 1910; about 12 million people through Ellis Island between 1892 and 1954; about 2 percent of Ellis Island arrivals excluded; Angel Island operating from 1910 to 1940. Two image cautions are disclosed honestly in the notes: the Angel Island arrival photograph survives only as a small file and reads softer than the deck's other photographs, and the well known Angel Island interview photograph, dated 1923 by the Immigration Station Foundation, is described only as officials examining an applicant — never as a specific interrogation.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (the passenger manifest, the Ellis Island inspection account, the Angel Island poem, the 1882 cartoon, and the settlement map read as one account of arrival and exclusion), D2.His.2 (continuity and change in immigration before and after 1880), D2.Civ.13 (how inspectors applied the laws Congress wrote, and how the categories set in law decided most cases before a ship arrived); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, WHST.9-10.1 (the lesson question answered as a defended claim — station or Congress — with facts cited from the reading).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain how immigration changed after 1880 in scale and in origin; pair a push factor with a pull factor to explain one family's move; describe the Ellis Island inspection step by step and state who was excluded and why; explain how Angel Island differed and name the 1882 law behind the difference; describe settlement in ethnic neighborhoods and the uneven pace of assimilation; and trace the restriction staircase from the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 to the quota laws of 1921 and 1924.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and question (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; Section A, a larger and different migration (slides 4–6) 6 minutes; Section B, push and pull (slides 7–9) 6 minutes; Section C, Ellis Island (slides 10–12) 7 minutes; Section D, Angel Island and exclusion (slides 13–15) 8 minutes; Section E, neighborhoods, work, and assimilation (slides 16–18) 6 minutes; Section F, nativism and restriction (slides 19–21) 7 minutes; vocabulary and closing (slides 22–25) 5 minutes, with the exit ticket collected at the bell. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. The activity is "Arrival Record Jigsaw"; the notebook page is the gateway chart comparing three arrival routes; the quick check is "Questioning at Angel Island"; the writing task is "Who Decided Who Belonged?"; the exit ticket has three parts — contrast the two islands, give a push and a pull, and defend where belonging was decided.

Key vocabulary

immigrant · emigrant · nativism · Ellis Island · Angel Island · Chinese Exclusion Act · assimilation

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title "The New Immigration"; the standfirst — "More than 20 million arrivals, and a country deciding who could stay."

  • Picture: Mulberry Street in New York City, about 1900, the center of Little Italy, crowded with market stalls and shoppers; a Detroit Publishing Company photochrom held by the Library of Congress.
  • It is a good place to start, because it shows the end of a journey that this lesson will trace from the beginning.
  • Between 1880 and 1920 more than 20 million people arrived in the United States, and the arrivals changed the country as surely as the factories and railroads of the earlier lessons did.
  • [Frame the day around two movements at once.] People moved, in numbers and from places that were new in American experience.
  • And the United States, through inspectors at two island stations and through laws passed in Congress, decided which of those people belonged.
  • Students should leave able to explain both movements, and to say where the real decision was made.

Slide 2 · The essential question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "Who were the new immigrants, and how did the United States decide who belonged?"
  • Today's question has two halves, and students need both.
  • Who were the new immigrants asks about people: where they came from, why they left, and where they settled.
  • How did the United States decide who belonged asks about power: the chalk mark on a coat at Ellis Island, the months of questioning at Angel Island, and the laws that drew the lines before any ship arrived.
  • The unit question runs underneath. Industrial growth created the demand for labor that pulled migrants across two oceans, so immigration is not a side story to industrialization. It is one of its largest human consequences.
  • [Keep the lesson question visible, and return to its exact wording at the end of class.]
  • The exit ticket asks students to choose between the inspection station and Congress as the place where belonging was decided, and to defend the choice with facts.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "Arrival, inspection, and the closing door"; the six section rows, A–F.

  • This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section.
  • Section A sets the scale and the change in origins. Section B explains the movement itself, the forces that pushed people out and pulled them in.
  • Sections C and D are the heart of the lesson, a deliberate contrast. At Ellis Island most Europeans were admitted within hours. At Angel Island many Asian arrivals were detained for weeks and months.
  • Section E follows the admitted into neighborhoods and workplaces, and section F follows the opposition, as nativism turned into law step by step between 1882 and 1924.
  • [Tell students to keep the two stations separate in their notes all hour.] The lesson's central comparison, and the exit ticket, depend on that distinction never blurring into one general immigrant story.

Slide 4 · Section A: A Larger and Different Migration (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "More people, and from new places"; the Ellis Island group photograph at right.

  • Picture: A group of immigrants outside a building on Ellis Island in the early twentieth century; a federal government photograph now held by the National Archives.
  • [Use it to slow students down before the numbers begin.] Every person in a frame like this one had a village, a reason for leaving, and a destination, and the lesson will deal in millions.
  • Section A makes one claim with two parts. The migration after 1880 was larger than what came before it, and it was different in origin.
  • Earlier arrivals had come mostly from northern and western Europe. The new arrivals came mostly from southern and eastern Europe, with smaller numbers crossing the Pacific and the southern border.
  • That change in origin, more than the numbers alone, is what later sections argue about, because the argument over who belonged was largely an argument about where a person was born.

Slide 5 · Where they came from, before and after

On the slide: five bullets — before 1880, most immigrants came from Britain, Ireland, Germany, and Scandinavia; after 1880, the largest groups came from Italy, Poland, Greece, Russia, and eastern Europe; smaller numbers came from Japan, China, Korea, India, and Mexico; historians call the later arrivals the new immigrants; by 1910, about one American in seven was foreign born.

  • [Work the contrast plainly, because section A of the guided notes tests it in three blanks.]
  • Before 1880, most immigrants came from northern and western Europe: Britain, Ireland, Germany, and Scandinavia.
  • After 1880, the largest groups came from southern and eastern Europe, including Italians, Poles, Slovaks, Greeks, and Russian and eastern European Jews.
  • Smaller numbers came from Japan, China, Korea, India, and Mexico, a point students often miss when they picture immigration as only an Atlantic story.
  • The reading opens with the two paired terms students will meet again on the vocabulary slides. An immigrant is a person who comes to live permanently in a new country, and an emigrant is a person who leaves a country to live elsewhere. The same person is both, seen from opposite shores.
  • By 1910 about one American in seven was foreign born.
  • That means the change was visible on any city street in the industrial North.

Slide 6 · 20 million

On the slide: the number "20 MILLION" in large type; the label — "immigrants arrived between 1880 and 1920"; the sub-line — "By 1910 about one American in seven was foreign born. The change was in the numbers, and in where people came from."

  • More than 20 million arrivals in forty years.
  • An average of half a million people a year.
  • [Dwell on the number, because it disciplines everything else in the lesson.]
  • They arrived by ship, most of them through a small number of ports, and most of them settling in the industrial cities of the Northeast and Midwest.
  • Cities, schools, workplaces, and politics all had to absorb that flow while it was happening, not in retrospect.
  • Pair the big number with the smaller one in the subheading. One American in seven foreign born by 1910 means that immigration was not something happening at the edge of American life.
  • In the mill towns and city neighborhoods where most arrivals settled, the share was far higher than one in seven.
  • When later sections turn to conflict over wages, language, and religion, students should remember that the conflict ran at this scale, in daily life, block by block.

Slide 7 · Section B: Push and Pull (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Every departure had two sides: what drove people out, and what drew them in."

  • [A quiet note to yourself, not an apology to the class:] this section has no divider photograph.
  • The lesson's photographs show arrival and settlement, the American end of the journey. The villages people left, in Sicily, in Poland, in Guangdong, sit outside the lesson's image set, which is itself a small lesson in how the surviving record favors the receiving country.
  • The framework here is simple, and students will use it twice, in the guided notes and in the exit ticket. Push factors drove people out of a home country. Pull factors drew people toward a specific destination.
  • Neither side explains a migration alone. A family needed a reason to leave and a reason to choose the United States, plus a route, a ticket, and usually a person waiting at the other end.
  • The next two slides take the sides in order.

Slide 8 · Push: what drove people out

On the slide: five bullets — farmland was divided into plots too small to feed a family; young men faced years of required military service; landlords took much of what a harvest earned; pogroms drove Russian Jews from their homes; poverty, flooding, and disorder pushed people out of Guangdong, China.

  • [Take the push factors one at a time, and keep them concrete.]
  • In southern Italy and eastern Europe, farmland had been divided by inheritance into plots too small to feed a family, so departure was often arithmetic before it was adventure.
  • Young men faced years of required military service. Landlords took much of what a harvest earned.
  • For Russian Jews the push was violent. Pogroms were organized attacks on Jewish communities that the government allowed or encouraged, and families fled them for safety, not only for wages.
  • Chinese migrants left Guangdong province, where poverty, flooding, and disorder followed decades of war and rebellion.
  • [Stress the variety inside one word.] Push can mean hunger, conscription, rent, persecution, or flood, and different families in the same village could be pushed by different items on this list at the same time.

Slide 9 · Pull: what drew people in

On the slide: five bullets — factories, mines, mills, and railroads needed labor; American wages were several times home pay for the same work; steamship companies sold cheap tickets and advertised abroad; letters, money, and prepaid tickets pulled relatives and neighbors along; many planned to return home, and a substantial share did.

  • Pull factors worked as a chain, and students should be able to trace it.
  • Industrial employers needed labor for factories, mines, mills, and railroad crews, the same industrial growth from the unit question.
  • American wages, though low by American standards, could be several times what the same work paid at home, and that difference paid for the ticket and then for the tickets of relatives.
  • Steamship companies sold cheap tickets and advertised abroad, because migrants were a business.
  • Letters and money sent home by earlier migrants, along with prepaid tickets, pulled relatives and neighbors along the same routes, which is why whole villages sometimes reappear in a single American neighborhood.
  • [End with the complication the reading insists on.] Many men planned to work a few years and return home, and a substantial share did go back. Migration was a strategy, not always a farewell.

Slide 10 · Section C: Ellis Island (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "Hours for most, and a ferry to a new life"; the Ellis Island waiting photograph at right.

  • Picture: Immigrants with bundles and suitcases waiting to be transferred at Ellis Island on October 30, 1912, photographed by Underwood and Underwood and held by the Library of Congress.
  • It shows the station's purpose exactly: a crowd in transit, waiting to move, not a crowd being housed.
  • Ellis Island was the federal immigration station in New York Harbor that processed most European arrivals after 1892, and between 1892 and 1954 about 12 million people passed through it.
  • About 12 million people passed through Ellis Island between 1892 and 1954.
  • [Hold both facts for the contrast ahead.] For most of those 12 million, the island was an afternoon, not an ordeal: a medical look, a set of questions, and a ferry ride.
  • The small share who were excluded, about 2 percent, experienced the same rooms very differently.
  • Section C takes the crossing first, then the inspection, step by step, in the order an arrival lived it.

Slide 11 · The crossing: steerage

On the slide: four bullets — most Europeans crossed in steerage, below deck; steerage was the cheapest space, crowded dormitories for a voyage of one to two weeks; cabin passengers were inspected briefly on board; steerage passengers were ferried to Ellis Island.

  • [Start with the class system built into the ship, because it explains who met the island and who did not.]
  • Most Europeans crossed the Atlantic in steerage, the cheapest space on the ship, in crowded dormitories below deck. The voyage lasted one to two weeks.
  • Cabin passengers, who had paid more for the crossing, were inspected briefly on board and landed directly. Steerage passengers were ferried to Ellis Island for the full inspection.
  • The reading makes the point without editorializing, and students should catch it anyway. The depth of your inspection depended in part on the price of your ticket, because officials assumed that a cabin passenger was unlikely to become a public charge.
  • That assumption, money as evidence of admissibility, will return at the manifest check on the next slide, and again at Angel Island, where no ticket price could outweigh a law aimed at a person's birthplace.

Slide 12 · Inspection at Ellis Island

On the slide: five bullets — doctors watched the climb, and chalk marked the doubtful; inspectors checked answers against the ship manifest; for most, the whole process took a few hours; about 2 percent were excluded and sent back; trachoma and a likelihood of becoming a public charge were common grounds.

  • [Walk the inspection in order, because the guided notes test each step.]
  • Doctors watched arrivals climb the stairs to the registry room and marked the coats of anyone who limped, breathed hard, or looked ill with a chalk letter for a closer examination. The stairs were the first test, and it took seconds.
  • Inspectors then asked each arrival a standard set of questions and checked the answers against the ship's passenger list, called a manifest, which had been filled out at the port of departure. A mismatch raised suspicion. A match moved the line along.
  • For most Ellis Island arrivals the whole process took a few hours.
  • About 2 percent of arrivals were excluded and sent back.
  • Most often the grounds were trachoma, a contagious eye disease, other medical conditions, or an inspector's judgment that they were likely to become public charges.
  • Behind that small percent stood thousands of individual reversals at the last step of a long journey.

Slide 13 · Section D: Angel Island and Exclusion (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "The same ocean, a different welcome"; the Angel Island arrival photograph at right.

  • Picture: Immigrants arriving at the immigration station on Angel Island; a federal government photograph held by the National Archives.
  • [A caution about the image itself:] the surviving source file is small, so this portrait crop reads softer than the other photographs in the deck, but the scene is real and period.
  • Angel Island operated from 1910 to 1940 in San Francisco Bay, and its work was shaped by law in a way Ellis Island's was not.
  • Where the New York station mostly processed arrivals for admission, the San Francisco station often detained them, above all Chinese arrivals, while officials decided whether the law allowed them in at all.
  • [Set the contrast in one sentence before moving on.] Ellis Island asked, are you who the manifest says you are, and are you healthy. Angel Island asked, can you prove the law lets you enter.

Slide 14 · Detention and questioning

On the slide: the Angel Island interview photograph at right; four bullets — the station stood in San Francisco Bay from 1910 to 1940; arrivals were held in barracks for weeks, often months; questioning compared the answers of applicants and witnesses; each arrival had to prove a legal right to enter.

  • Picture: Officials examining an applicant at Angel Island; the Angel Island Immigration Station Foundation dates the well known interview photograph to 1923.
  • [Caption it honestly.] It shows exactly that scene, an applicant seated before officials. It does not show a named case or a specific interrogation, and students should not be told otherwise.
  • The routine it illustrates was slow by design. A Chinese arrival claiming an exempt status, such as merchant or son of a citizen, had to prove it.
  • Officials held arrivals in barracks for weeks and often months while they questioned the applicant and witnesses in detail, comparing answers about family and village for any mismatch.
  • One inconsistent detail about a house, a road, or a relative could sink a claim built over months.
  • [Compare that directly with the few hours on the previous section's slide, and let students sit with the distance between the two stations.]

Slide 15 · Exclusion, paper sons, and poems

On the slide: five bullets — the 1882 law suspended Chinese labor immigration; exempt claims rested on status, merchant or son of a citizen; after the 1906 fire, paper sons claimed citizen fathers; detainees brushed and carved poems on the barrack walls; Japanese, Korean, and South Asian arrivals were detained too.

  • [Give students the law first, in its full guided notes form, because the vocabulary slides return to it.]
  • The Chinese Exclusion Act was the 1882 federal law that suspended Chinese labor immigration and barred Chinese residents from naturalized citizenship, and it was the first federal law to bar a named national group.
  • Exclusion created the paperwork it then policed. Because the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and fire destroyed the city's birth and citizenship records, some Chinese migrants entered as paper sons, claiming a citizen father whose records could not be checked.
  • Inside the barracks, detainees brushed and carved poems onto the walls recording anger, boredom, and uncertainty.
  • Those poems are evidence of a kind no manifest can supply, and the activity returns to them.
  • Japanese, Korean, and South Asian arrivals also passed through Angel Island and were often detained, though exclusion fell hardest on the Chinese.

Slide 16 · Section E: Neighborhoods, Work, and Assimilation (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "Where the admitted landed, and what changed"; the Mulberry Street photograph at right.

  • Picture: Mulberry Street, about 1900, in a portrait crop of the same Detroit Publishing photochrom that opened the lesson.
  • It is the right image for this section, because admission was not the end of the story the lesson is telling. Admitted immigrants settled where the work was, and they built neighborhoods dense enough to photograph like this one.
  • Section E covers three connected subjects. The neighborhoods themselves, named places such as Little Italy and Chinatown. The work that filled them, hard and often dangerous. And the slower, uneven change inside families, as children moved toward American customs faster than their parents did.
  • [Warn students against a tidy ending here.] Settlement was not a reward ceremony after inspection. It was the beginning of a second negotiation, this one with employers, landlords, schools, and neighbors, and it ran for a generation.

Slide 17 · Neighborhoods and mutual aid

On the slide: four bullets — most settled in the industrial cities of the Northeast and Midwest; Little Italy and Chinatown were blocks built by village ties; help came from relatives, mutual aid societies, parishes, and synagogues; foreign language newspapers explained American schools and courts.

  • Settlement followed work and kin, in that order for most families.
  • Admitted immigrants settled where the work was, and most stayed in the industrial cities of the Northeast and Midwest, in neighborhoods that took names such as Little Italy and Chinatown.
  • New arrivals found rooms and jobs through relatives and through mutual aid societies, parishes, and synagogues.
  • [Pause on those institutions, because they are the lesson's answer to a fair student question:] how did a person with twelve dollars and no English start?
  • The answer is that almost nobody started alone. A brother, a cousin, or a society from the home village supplied the first room, the first job lead, and the first explanations.
  • Foreign language newspapers explained American schools, courts, and elections.
  • The neighborhood was both a shelter from the new country and a school for it, and it was both at the same time, which is why it worked.

Slide 18 · Work, school, and two speeds

On the slide: four bullets — the work waited in garment shops, steel mills, coal mines, and docks; the work was hard and often dangerous; children learned English fast in public schools; families kept home languages, foods, and holidays.

  • [Work first, because work was the reason for the address.]
  • The jobs waiting in the industrial cities were in garment shops, steel mills, coal mines, and docks, and the work was hard and often dangerous, a direct link back to the factory and mine conditions from the start of this unit.
  • Then the family change, in the reading's exact terms. Children entered public schools and learned English quickly, which could strain families when children adopted American customs faster than their parents.
  • The key term here is assimilation: the process by which a person or group adopts the customs and language of another culture.
  • [Hold students to the reading's caution when they use the word.] Assimilation rarely ran at one speed, and most families kept home languages, foods, and holidays while adding American ones.
  • It was addition and loss at once, at different rates inside the same household.

Slide 19 · Section F: Nativism and Restriction (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "Opinion first, then law, step by step"; the 1882 cartoon at right.

  • Picture: "The Only One Barred Out," a cartoon from Frank Leslie's Illustrated Newspaper of April 1, 1882, weeks before Congress passed the Chinese Exclusion Act.
  • A Chinese man sits outside a gate labeled as the gate of liberty, while a sign welcomes groups the cartoon's readers were meant to fear and bars only him. His baggage is labeled with virtues: industry, order, peace, and sobriety.
  • The cartoon criticizes exclusion, and the activity examines both its argument and its limits.
  • It opens this section because it dates the argument precisely. Opposition to the new immigration was not a late reaction to the quotas of the 1920s. It was present, organized, and already winning laws in 1882, before the largest arrivals had even landed.
  • Section F names the attitude, gives its arguments fairly, and then traces the laws that followed it.

Slide 20 · Nativism: the argument against

On the slide: four bullets — critics charged that the new arrivals were too different in religion, language, and custom; critics also claimed that new arrivals held wages down; unions backed restriction when employers used immigrants to break strikes; law followed opinion, step by step.

  • [Define the attitude in the guided notes words first.] Nativism is a policy or attitude favoring native-born residents over immigrants.
  • Then give its arguments as the reading gives them, without softening or inflating them.
  • Many native-born Americans answered the new immigration with nativism. Critics charged that the new arrivals were too different in religion, language, and custom to become Americans, and that they held wages down.
  • The wage argument had an organized base. Many unions supported restriction because employers used immigrant workers to break strikes and undercut pay, which put labor organizations in the painful position of opposing fellow workers in the name of protecting wages.
  • Students do not have to accept an argument to explain it, and the guided notes only ask them to explain it.
  • The section's last move is the important one. Law followed opinion step by step, and the next slide is that staircase.

Slide 21 · The closing door, 1882 to 1924

On the slide: five bullets — in 1882, Chinese labor immigration was suspended; in 1885, contract laborers recruited abroad were barred; in 1917, a literacy test was imposed and most of Asia was barred; in 1921 and 1924, quotas cut immigration sharply; the smallest quotas went to southern and eastern Europeans.

  • [Trace the staircase slowly, because section F of the guided notes asks students to list these steps in order, and reading question five asks them to narrate the same widening.]
  • In 1882 the Chinese Exclusion Act suspended Chinese labor immigration and barred Chinese residents from naturalized citizenship — one named group.
  • An 1885 law barred contract laborers recruited abroad.
  • In 1917 Congress required adult immigrants to pass a literacy test and barred most immigration from Asia, a continent rather than a country.
  • Quota laws in 1921 and 1924 cut immigration sharply and gave the smallest quotas to southern and eastern Europeans, the very groups whose arrival opened this lesson.
  • By the 1920s, who belonged was answered more and more by law, and the answer depended heavily on where an arrival was born.
  • The circle that began with one group in 1882 had widened to rank nearly every birthplace on earth.

Slide 22 · Vocabulary: words for the journey

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — immigrant, emigrant, nativism. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: "immigrant: a person who comes to live permanently in a new country."
  • Say: "emigrant: a person who leaves a country to live elsewhere."
  • The pair describes one person from two directions, and the small spelling difference carries the whole distinction, in for immigrant, out for emigrant.
  • [Give them the shore test:] name the shore you are standing on, and the word follows.
  • Say: "nativism: a policy or attitude favoring native-born residents over immigrants."
  • Note that the definition covers both a policy and an attitude, because section F showed the two feeding each other. The attitude supplied the voters, the policy supplied the laws, and the laws then taught the next generation what the attitude claimed was natural.

Slide 23 · Vocabulary: words for the two gates

On the slide: two terms with their definitions — Ellis Island, Angel Island. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: "Ellis Island: the federal immigration station in New York Harbor that processed most European arrivals after 1892."
  • The verb in the definition is processed, which is the right word for a station built to move people through in hours.
  • Say: "Angel Island: the immigration station in San Francisco Bay that processed and often detained many Asian arrivals."
  • The added words, and often detained, do the analytical work. Same country, same federal system, same decades, and the definitions themselves have to diverge to stay accurate.
  • [When students write the exit ticket comparison, push them to borrow this language.] A one sentence contrast built from these two definitions, plus the law named on the next slide, answers the first exit ticket question in full.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for the law and the change

On the slide: two terms with their definitions — Chinese Exclusion Act, assimilation. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: "Chinese Exclusion Act: the 1882 federal law that suspended Chinese labor immigration and barred Chinese residents from naturalized citizenship."
  • Both halves of the definition matter. The act stopped a flow of workers, and it denied the people already here a path to citizenship, which is why its effects lasted long after any single ship was turned away.
  • Say: "assimilation: the process by which a person or group adopts the customs and language of another culture."
  • Hold students to the reading's caution when they use the word. It rarely ran at one speed, it ran fastest in children and slowest in grandparents, and most families added American customs while keeping home languages, foods, and holidays, rather than trading one culture cleanly for another.

Slide 25 · Now your turn: jigsaw, notebook, quick check, and writing

On the slide: the closing title "Who decided who belonged?"; the prompt — "Return to the lesson question, then test it against the records. Keep inspection, admission, exclusion, and settlement separate."; the four work rows — Activity, Notebook, Quick check, Writing; the exit ticket line.

  • Close by returning to the lesson question: who were the new immigrants, and how did the United States decide who belonged.
  • The honest answer the lesson has built is layered. Inspectors decided individual cases, with chalk, manifests, and months of questioning. Congress decided the categories those inspectors applied, and by 1924 the categories did most of the deciding before a ship arrived.
  • [Now hand the question to the work.] In the arrival record jigsaw, groups rotate through five stations — a passenger manifest, an Ellis Island inspection account, an Angel Island poem in summary, the 1882 cartoon, and a settlement map — then build one account that keeps inspection, admission, exclusion, and settlement separate.
  • The notebook page compares the three arrival routes in a chart. The quick check takes up questioning at Angel Island, and the writing task asks the exit ticket's third question at full length: station or Congress, defended with facts.
  • Collect the exit ticket on the way out.
  • Next lesson turns from the people arriving to the city they landed in.
