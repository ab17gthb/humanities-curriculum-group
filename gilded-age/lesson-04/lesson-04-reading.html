The New Immigration

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A Larger and Different Migration

Between 1880 and 1920, more than 20 million immigrants arrived in the United States, An immigrant is a person who comes to live permanently in a new country, and an emigrant is a person who leaves a country to live elsewhere. What changed after 1880 was not only the number of arrivals but also where they came from. Earlier in the nineteenth century, most immigrants had come from northern and western Europe: Britain, Ireland, Germany, and Scandinavia. Historians call the later arrivals the new immigrants. By the 1890s the largest groups came from southern and eastern Europe, including Italians, Poles, Slovaks, Greeks, and Russian and eastern European Jews. Smaller numbers came from Japan, China, Korea, India, and Mexico. By 1910 about one American in seven was foreign born.

Why They Left, Why They Came

Every departure had two sides. Push factors drove people out. Farmland in southern Italy and eastern Europe was divided into plots too small to feed a family. Young men faced years of required military service, and landlords took much of what a harvest earned. Russian Jews fled pogroms, organized attacks on Jewish communities that the government allowed or encouraged. Chinese migrants left Guangdong province, where poverty, flooding, and disorder followed decades of war and rebellion. Pull factors drew people toward the United States. Industrial employers needed labor for factories, mines, mills, and railroad crews, and American wages, though low by American standards, could be several times what the same work paid at home. Steamship companies sold cheap tickets and advertised abroad. Letters and money sent home by earlier migrants, along with prepaid tickets, pulled relatives and neighbors along the same routes. Many men planned to work a few years and return home, and a substantial share did go back. Others came to stay.

The Crossing and Ellis Island

Most Europeans crossed the Atlantic in steerage, the cheapest space on the ship, in crowded dormitories below deck. The voyage lasted one to two weeks. Cabin passengers were inspected briefly on board. Steerage passengers were ferried to Ellis Island, the federal immigration station in New York Harbor that processed most European arrivals after 1892. There, doctors watched arrivals climb the stairs to the registry room and marked the coats of anyone who limped, breathed hard, or looked ill with a chalk letter for a closer examination, Inspectors then asked each arrival a standard set of questions and checked the answers against the ship's passenger list, called a manifest, which had been filled out at the port of departure. For most Ellis Island arrivals the whole process took a few hours, and about 2 percent of arrivals were excluded and sent back, most often for trachoma, a contagious eye disease, for other medical conditions, or because an inspector judged they were likely to become public charges. Between 1892 and 1954, about 12 million people passed through the station.

Angel Island and Exclusion

Angel Island, the immigration station in San Francisco Bay that processed and often detained many Asian arrivals, operated from 1910 to 1940. The station's work was shaped by law. The Chinese Exclusion Act, the 1882 federal law that suspended Chinese labor immigration and barred Chinese residents from naturalized citizenship, was the first federal law to bar a named national group. A Chinese arrival claiming an exempt status, such as merchant or son of a citizen, had to prove it. Officials held arrivals in barracks for weeks and often months while they questioned the applicant and witnesses in detail, comparing answers about family and village for any mismatch. Because the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and fire destroyed the city's birth and citizenship records, some Chinese migrants entered as paper sons, claiming a citizen father whose records could not be checked. Detainees brushed and carved poems onto the barrack walls recording anger, boredom, and uncertainty. Japanese, Korean, and South Asian arrivals also passed through Angel Island and were often detained, though exclusion fell hardest on the Chinese.

Neighborhoods and Work

Admitted immigrants settled where the work was. Most stayed in the industrial cities of the Northeast and Midwest, in neighborhoods that took names such as Little Italy and Chinatown. New arrivals found rooms and jobs through relatives and through mutual aid societies, parishes, and synagogues. Foreign language newspapers explained American schools, courts, and elections. Work was hard and often dangerous: garment shops, steel mills, coal mines, and docks. Children entered public schools and learned English quickly, which could strain families when children adopted American customs faster than their parents. Assimilation is the process by which a person or group adopts the customs and language of another culture. It rarely ran at one speed, and most families kept home languages, foods, and holidays while adding American ones.

Nativism and the Closing Door

Many native-born Americans answered the new immigration with nativism, a policy or attitude favoring native-born residents over immigrants. Critics charged that the new arrivals were too different in religion, language, and custom to become Americans, and that they held wages down. Many unions supported restriction because employers used immigrant workers to break strikes and undercut pay. Law followed opinion step by step. An 1885 law barred contract laborers recruited abroad. In 1917 Congress required adult immigrants to pass a literacy test and barred most immigration from Asia. Quota laws in 1921 and 1924 cut immigration sharply and gave the smallest quotas to southern and eastern Europeans. By the 1920s, who belonged was answered more and more by law, and the answer depended heavily on where an arrival was born.
