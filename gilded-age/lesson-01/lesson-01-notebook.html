Railroads and the national marketCompleted at Promontory Summit on May 10, 1869. Mileage grew from about 30,000 miles in 1860 to about 193,000 by 1900, and sellers began competing in one national market.Built with federal loans and land grants under the Pacific Railway Act of 1862. The network then grew by carrying the traffic of mines, farms, and factories.The railroads were the system itself in this row. Every other row describes something the network carried, consumed, or made necessary.
StandardsLines settled on one standard gauge, the southern roads converting in 1886. On November 18, 1883, the railroads adopted standard time in four zones in place of local solar time.Standards were worth setting only because cars and schedules crossed from one line to another. A local road with no connections needed neither.Without a common gauge, freight had to be unloaded and loaded again at every break. Without common time, printed schedules and connections across lines could not be coordinated.
SteelThe Bessemer process made steel quickly and cheaply by blowing air through molten iron. Carnegie controlled every step from ore and coke to finished rails at his Pittsburgh mills.Mills depended on the rails to bring ore, coal, and limestone together from different states and to carry finished steel to buyers nationwide.Iron rails wore out quickly under heavy traffic. Cheap steel gave the roads rails that lasted, plus stronger bridges and bigger locomotives and machines.
Oil and refiningAfter Drake's 1859 well near Titusville, refineries separated crude oil into products such as kerosene. Large refineries worked most cheaply, and Standard Oil controlled most refining capacity by the early 1880s.Refiners depended on the rails to bring in crude oil and to carry kerosene to national and overseas markets. The lowest freight rates went to the largest shippers, which rewarded size.Oil gave the roads heavy, steady freight in both directions, first in barrels and later in tank cars.
Telegraph and capitalThe transcontinental telegraph line was completed in 1861. Railroads were financed as corporations, selling stocks and bonds to investors in the United States and Europe.Telegraph lines were strung beside the tracks, and wire traffic followed the rail network. Corporate finance was developed at this scale because railroad building demanded it first.Dispatchers used the telegraph to run denser traffic safely, and managers used it to coordinate prices, cars, and shipments. Without corporate capital, no continental line could have been built at all.

The system in one sentence. Strong answers name the two way dependence the chart records. Each industry needed the rails to gather materials, reach a national market, or coordinate its work, and the railroads in turn needed cheap steel to run on, oil traffic to carry, telegraph wires to operate, and corporate capital to exist.
