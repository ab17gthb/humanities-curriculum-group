The Engines of Industry

A Road Across a Continent

On May 10, 1869, officials of the Central Pacific and Union Pacific railroads met at Promontory Summit in Utah Territory and completed the first transcontinental railroad. The Central Pacific had built east from Sacramento, California, across the Sierra Nevada. The Union Pacific had built west from Omaha, Nebraska, across the Great Plains. From that day, passengers and freight could cross the continent without leaving the rails.

The line existed because the federal government paid for much of it. The Pacific Railway Act of 1862 offered the two companies federal loans and grants of public land along the route. Even with that help, the work was immense. Crews graded roadbed, blasted tunnels through granite, and laid track by hand. The Central Pacific relied heavily on Chinese immigrant workers, who made up most of its workforce. The Union Pacific employed many Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans.

One Network, One Market

The transcontinental line mattered less as a single road than as the start of a network. Railroad mileage in the United States grew from about 30,000 miles in 1860 to about 93,000 miles in 1880 and about 193,000 miles by 1900. New lines tied mines, farms, and cattle ranges to factories and ports. Towns the rails passed by often faded. Towns on the lines became shipping points for whole regions.

A connected network changed what a business could do. Before the railroads, most goods moved slowly and at high cost by wagon, river, or canal, so most manufacturers served nearby customers. Railroads carried heavy loads in all seasons at a fraction of the wagon cost. A Chicago packinghouse could ship dressed beef to Boston in refrigerator cars cooled with ice. A Pennsylvania mill could draw iron ore, coal, and limestone from three different states. Sellers now competed in one national market, and the winners in that market were usually the firms that could produce and ship at the largest scale.

Time Itself Had to Change

Running the network required agreement on basic standards. Track gauges differed from line to line in the early years, so freight had to be unloaded and loaded again wherever the gauge changed. Companies gradually settled on one standard gauge, and in 1886 the southern railroads converted thousands of miles of track to match it in a matter of days. Time posed the same problem. Every town kept local time set by the sun, which made schedules nearly impossible to coordinate across lines. On November 18, 1883, the railroads adopted standard time, dividing the continent into four zones with uniform time inside each zone. Cities and towns soon set their clocks by railroad time.

Steel Made Scale Possible

The network ran on steel, and steel had to become cheap. Iron rails wore out quickly under heavy traffic. Steel lasted far longer, but steel had long been a costly specialty metal made in small batches. The Bessemer process changed the economics. Blowing air through molten iron burned out impurities in minutes, so a single converter could produce steel by the ton instead of by the pound. American mills took up the process in the 1870s, and output climbed steadily. Andrew Carnegie built his mills near Pittsburgh to control every step, from ore and coke to finished rails. Cheap steel then fed back into the whole system. It meant longer lasting rails, stronger bridges, and bigger machines, each of which raised the scale at which industry could operate.

Oil, Light, and the Refinery

Oil followed a similar path from novelty to system. In 1859 Edwin Drake drilled a successful well near Titusville, Pennsylvania, and set off a rush for petroleum. Crude oil by itself was worth little. A refinery separated it into useful products, above all kerosene, which lit lamps across the country and abroad. Refining rewarded size. A large refinery processed crude oil more cheaply than a small one, and the railroads gave their largest shippers lower rates. John D. Rockefeller organized Standard Oil on that logic, and by the early 1880s the company controlled most of the refining capacity in the United States. Tank cars and pipelines then replaced the wooden barrel as the normal way to move oil.

Wires, Inventions, and Capital

The telegraph tied the system together. Telegraph lines were strung beside the tracks, and the transcontinental line was completed in 1861, eight years before the railroad. A manager in Chicago could learn prices in New York within minutes, order cars, and redirect a shipment while it was still moving. Later inventions extended the pattern. The telephone, patented in 1876, sped communication inside cities. The electric lighting system Thomas Edison first operated on a district scale in New York in 1882 lengthened the working day and powered new machines.

None of this could be paid for out of ordinary profits. A railroad was the largest business enterprise of its time, and building one required capital no single owner could supply. The corporation answered that problem. Investors bought shares, and the law treated the corporation as distinct from its owners, so an investor risked only the money put in. Railroads sold stocks and bonds across the United States and Europe, and the same corporate form soon organized the steel, oil, and telegraph industries. Industrial growth was one system, in which rails carried the products of steel mills and refineries, wires coordinated the traffic, and corporations raised the money to build at continental scale.

Teacher Answer Key

1. How did the federal government help make the transcontinental railroad possible, and what did each of the two companies build? The Pacific Railway Act of 1862 gave the companies federal loans and grants of public land along the route. The Central Pacific built east from Sacramento across the Sierra Nevada. The Union Pacific built west from Omaha across the Great Plains. They met at Promontory Summit on May 10, 1869.

2. Why did one national market reward firms that produced at the largest scale? Once railroads carried goods cheaply in all seasons, a firm no longer sold only to nearby customers. It competed with sellers across the country, so the firm that produced and shipped at the largest scale usually had the lowest cost per item and won. Accept either example developed accurately. A Chicago packinghouse shipped dressed beef to eastern cities in ice cooled refrigerator cars, which only a large operation could organize. A Pennsylvania mill drew ore, coal, and limestone from three states, which rewarded mills big enough to buy and move materials at that scale.

3. What two standards did the railroads have to agree on, and what problem did each standard solve? Gauge and time. A standard track gauge ended the unloading and reloading of freight wherever two lines with different gauges met; the southern roads converted in 1886. Standard time, adopted November 18, 1883, replaced local solar time in each town with four uniform time zones, which made printed schedules and connections across lines possible.

4. The reading says steel and oil followed a similar path from novelty to system. Explain that pattern in your own words, using both industries as evidence. In each industry a technical change made large scale production cheap, and cheap production then favored the largest firms. For steel, the Bessemer process turned a costly specialty metal into a product made by the ton in minutes, and Carnegie cut costs further by controlling every step of production. For oil, crude oil had little value until refineries separated it into products such as kerosene, large refineries processed crude more cheaply than small ones, and railroad rates rewarded the largest shippers, so Standard Oil came to control most refining capacity by the early 1880s. In both cases the technology and the railroads together pushed the industry toward a few very large firms.

5. Why did railroads become the engine of a national industrial economy? Full credit answers argue that the railroads did more than carry goods. They created one national market by cutting the cost and time of transport, they set the standards (gauge and time) that let the country operate as one system, they created the demand that made cheap steel necessary and profitable, they carried the crude oil and refined products of the oil industry, their telegraph lines coordinated business across the continent, and their need for capital spread the corporation across American industry. Any three accurate facts from the reading, tied to the claim, earn full credit. Answers that only say trains were fast or carried a lot should earn partial credit, because they name the transport gain and miss the system the reading describes.
