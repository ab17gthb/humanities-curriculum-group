Writing Task: The Engine of an Economy

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Why did railroads become the engine of a national industrial economy? A strong answer does more than say that trains carried goods. It explains how the railroads changed the market other businesses sold into, the standards the country ran on, and the industries that grew up around the rails. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The transcontinental railroad was completed at Promontory Summit on May 10, 1869, with federal loans and land grants under the Pacific Railway Act of 1862. Railroad mileage grew from about 30,000 miles in 1860 to about 193,000 miles by 1900.

2. Railroads carried heavy loads in all seasons at a fraction of wagon cost. Chicago packinghouses shipped dressed beef to eastern cities in refrigerator cars, and mills drew ore, coal, and limestone from different states, so sellers competed in one national market.

3. The railroads set national standards. Lines settled on one standard gauge, and on November 18, 1883, they adopted standard time in four uniform zones in place of local solar time.

4. The Bessemer process made steel quickly and cheaply by blowing air through molten iron. Cheap steel gave the railroads rails that lasted far longer than iron, and railroad demand made large steel mills profitable.

5. Refineries separated crude oil into products such as kerosene. Railroads carried crude oil in and refined products out, and their lowest rates went to the largest shippers, which helped Standard Oil control most refining capacity by the early 1880s.

6. Telegraph lines ran beside the tracks, and the transcontinental line was finished in 1861. Managers used the wire to learn distant prices within minutes and to direct shipments already moving. Railroads were financed as corporations, which raised capital by selling shares to many investors.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the lesson question directly and treats the railroads as the engine of a system, not only as a faster way to carry freight.
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Mileage and the 1869 completion support the network claim. Refrigerator cars and multi state supply support the national market claim. Gauge and standard time support the standards claim. Bessemer steel, oil refining and rates, the telegraph, and corporate capital each support the claim that whole industries organized themselves around the rails.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. A paragraph that lists three facts without saying what each one changed has assembled evidence without making the argument, and should earn partial credit.
  • An ending that identifies what the network made possible at continental scale, such as one market, one set of standards, or industries that supplied and depended on each other.

Model opening for teacher reference. Railroads became the engine of a national industrial economy because they did not simply move the products of other industries. They created the conditions those industries grew in. Before the network, a manufacturer sold to nearby customers because wagon transport was slow and costly. Once mileage grew from about 30,000 miles in 1860 to about 193,000 by 1900, a Chicago packinghouse could sell beef in Boston and a mill could buy ore, coal, and limestone from three states, so every seller competed in one national market and the largest firms usually won it. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the standards evidence and one industry that organized itself around the rails, then closing on the system rather than the train.

Common weak answers. Speed alone (trains were faster than wagons) names a transport gain and misses the market, standards, and industry effects. Invention lists (steel, oil, and the telegraph were all important) reverse the argument of the lesson, which is that the railroads were the reason those industries took the shape they did.
