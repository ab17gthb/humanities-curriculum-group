Race, Politics, and Protest

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

After Reconstruction

When Reconstruction ended in 1877, federal troops left the South and white Democratic governments took control of the southern states. Black southerners still held citizenship under the Fourteenth Amendment and Black men held the vote under the Fifteenth Amendment, but those protections depended on enforcement, and enforcement was ending. Over the next twenty years, southern states built a new system of racial control in law, in politics, and in daily life. At the same time, farmers in the South and West built a protest movement against debt, railroad power, and falling prices. By 1896 the two struggles met in a national election. Together they set the problems that the Progressive movement would inherit.

Jim Crow Becomes Law

State legislatures passed laws that required separation by race in schools, parks, hotels, theaters, and transportation. This system took the name Jim Crow. Separation was never equal in practice. Black schools received far less public money than white schools, Black railroad cars were older and dirtier, and Black passengers who protested faced arrest. In 1892 Homer Plessy, a Black man from Louisiana, deliberately sat in a railroad car reserved for white passengers to test the state law that required separate cars. He was arrested. His case reached the Supreme Court, and on May 18, 1896, the Court decided Plessy v. Ferguson. By a vote of seven to one, the Court upheld Louisiana segregation under the doctrine of separate but equal. The decision gave constitutional protection to segregation across the South.

Taking the Vote Away

Segregation was paired with disfranchisement. Beginning with Mississippi in 1890, state conventions rewrote constitutions to remove Black men from the voter rolls without naming race directly. The devices included poll taxes that voters had to pay, literacy tests that white officials graded unfairly, and grandfather clauses that protected white voters whose grandfathers had voted before the Civil War. Registration rules, white only primaries, fraud, and intimidation did the rest. Black registration fell sharply in state after state. Without the vote, Black southerners could not defend their schools, their juries, or their safety through politics.

Violence and Protest

Violence enforced what law began. Lynch mobs killed Black people in public, often after accusations that were never tried in court, and local officials rarely punished the killers. Journalist Ida B. Wells investigated lynching after three of her friends were lynched in Memphis in 1892. In her pamphlet Southern Horrors, published that year, she showed with names, dates, and newspaper records that many victims had committed no crime and that economic competition and racial control, not justice, drove the killings. A mob destroyed her newspaper office, and she continued her campaign from the North and in Britain. Other Black leaders chose a different public strategy. In 1895 Booker T. Washington, head of Tuskegee Institute in Alabama, delivered the speech later called the Atlanta Compromise. He proposed that Black southerners accept segregation for the present and build economic strength first, through farming, trades, and business. White audiences praised the speech. Black critics, including Wells, argued that giving up the vote and civil rights left Black people with no way to protect the gains Washington hoped to build.

The Farmers' Revolt

Farmers had their own grievances. After the Civil War, prices for cotton and wheat fell for decades while the costs of seed, equipment, and railroad shipping stayed high. Farmers borrowed at high interest and repaid in dollars that grew more valuable as prices fell, a process called deflation. Many blamed railroads, banks, grain elevators, and the gold standard, which limited the money supply. In the 1870s farmers organized through the Grange and pushed states to pass Granger laws that regulated railroad and warehouse rates. Courts first upheld some regulation, and the campaign taught farmers to act together in politics.

Populism and 1896

In the 1880s Farmers' Alliances spread across the South and Plains, building cooperatives and demanding railroad regulation, a graduated income tax, and more money in circulation. In 1892 those demands became the Omaha Platform of the new Populist Party, which also called for government ownership of railroads and the direct election of senators. The Populists won votes across regions, but keeping Black and white farmers in one movement proved difficult, especially as southern Democrats used race to divide them. In 1896 the Democratic Party nominated William Jennings Bryan, whose convention speech closed by rejecting the gold standard in famous words against pressing a crown of thorns on labor and crucifying mankind on a cross of gold. Bryan campaigned for free silver across the country. The Populists endorsed him, but Republican William McKinley, backed by business money and urban workers who feared inflation, won the election. The Populist Party collapsed, yet many of its demands later became Progressive reforms. Progressives inherited both legacies: a bold program against concentrated power, and a nation that had accepted segregation and disfranchisement as settled law.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What did the Supreme Court decide in Plessy v. Ferguson, and what doctrine did it use? On May 18, 1896, the Court, seven to one, upheld Louisiana's law requiring separate railroad cars for Black and white passengers. It used the doctrine of separate but equal, holding that segregation did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment if facilities were said to be equal.

2. How did poll taxes, literacy tests, and grandfather clauses work together to disfranchise Black voters without naming race? Poll taxes priced poor Black voters out. Literacy tests let white officials fail Black applicants unfairly. Grandfather clauses exempted white voters whose grandfathers had voted before the Civil War, so the barriers fell on Black voters while protecting whites. Fraud, white primaries, and intimidation reinforced them.

3. How did Ida B. Wells and Booker T. Washington differ in their public strategies in the 1890s? Wells confronted injustice directly. She documented lynching with evidence in Southern Horrors (1892) and campaigned publicly against it. Washington, in the Atlanta Compromise (1895), publicly accepted segregation for the present and urged Black southerners to build economic strength first. Critics including Wells argued that surrendering voting and civil rights left economic gains unprotected.

4. Why did falling prices hurt farmers who owed money, and how did that grievance lead to the Populist Party? Under deflation each dollar grew more valuable, so a loan repaid later cost the farmer more in crops than when borrowed, while crop prices kept falling. Farmers organized in the Grange and the Farmers' Alliances, won Granger laws regulating railroad rates, and in 1892 formed the Populist Party on the Omaha Platform.

5. What did Progressives inherit from the 1890s? From the Jim Crow line, a legal order of segregation and disfranchisement protected by Plessy, which most white Progressives did not challenge. From the farm protest line, a tested reform program: railroad regulation, a graduated income tax, direct election of senators, and monetary reform, much of which Progressives later enacted.
