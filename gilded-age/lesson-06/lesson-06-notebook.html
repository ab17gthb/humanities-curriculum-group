Teacher Answer Key

Part of the WestHow it workedWho gainedWho paid, or resisted
MiningA strike started a mining boom: prospectors rushed in, then merchants, freight, and banks. Surface deposits ran out and company mines with expensive machinery took over, as at the Comstock Lode and Leadville.Mining companies and the towns, railroads, and merchants that supplied them. A few early prospectors struck it rich.Prospectors who sold out or became wage workers; Native nations whose treaty land held the minerals, as in the Black Hills; camps that emptied into ghost towns.
CattleTexas herds were walked on the cattle drive to Kansas railheads such as Abilene and Dodge City and shipped east by train. The system needed unfenced public grass and open trails.Ranch owners who sold cattle at northern prices, railroads, and railhead towns.Cowboys, who did dangerous work for low pay; open range ranchers when barbed wire, railroads into Texas, and hard winters ended the drives in the 1880s.
FarmingUnder the Homestead Act of 1862 a claimant paid a filing fee and lived on and improved up to 160 acres for five years to earn title. Settlers built sod houses and used steel plows, windmills, and dry farming.Families who survived the five years and earned title; railroads that sold land and carried crops; eastern buyers of cheap grain.Families who lost claims to drought and debt; farmers who stayed and depended on railroad rates, bank credit, and world prices they could not control.
Railroads and landThe government gave railroads a land grant in alternating sections along the line. Companies sold sections and borrowed against the land. Native nations were pressed onto reservations, land set aside by treaty or federal order.Railroad companies, settlers who bought near the line, and the government, whose kept sections rose in value.Native nations, whose reservations were far smaller than their former land and were reduced again when miners or settlers wanted the land.

The pattern. Strong answers name the federal government as the active hand in every row: it granted land to railroads, offered homesteads on conditions, and set and then shrank reservation boundaries, so western growth was a product of policy, not just of individual effort.
