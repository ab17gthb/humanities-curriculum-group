The West

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A West Tied to the East

After the Civil War, the American West changed faster than it ever had before. The change was not driven by settlers acting alone. It was driven by two forces working together: railroads, which connected western places to national markets, and federal land policy, which decided how public land would pass into private hands. When the first transcontinental railroad was completed in 1869, a journey that had taken months by wagon could be made in about a week by train. Mining camps, cattle towns, and farm settlements that had once stood far from any market now sat within reach of Chicago and the cities of the East.

The land those settlers occupied was not empty. Native nations lived across the Plains and the mountain West, hunted and farmed there, and held the land under treaties with the United States. Every railroad line, mine, ranch, and homestead in this lesson therefore tells two stories at once: a story of new economic opportunity, and a story of conflict over land that other people already claimed as their own.

Gold, Silver, and Boomtowns

Mining usually came first. When word spread that gold or silver had been found, a mining boom followed. Prospectors rushed in, followed by merchants, saloon keepers, freight companies, and banks. The Comstock Lode in Nevada, discovered in 1859, produced vast amounts of silver and turned Virginia City into a busy industrial town with deep mines financed by companies in San Francisco. Gold found in the Black Hills in the 1870s drew thousands of miners to camps such as Deadwood, on land the Lakota held under treaty. Leadville, Colorado, grew almost overnight around silver mines.

Boomtowns could also empty almost overnight. Surface deposits ran out quickly, and deep mining required expensive machinery that only large companies could afford. Individual prospectors sold out or became wage workers in company mines. Many camps became ghost towns. Mining pulled railroads, capital, and settlers into a region, and left behind wage labor and disputed claims on land and water.

Cattle on the Open Range

Cattle came next in much of the Plains country. At the end of the Civil War, Texas held large herds of longhorn cattle that were worth little locally and much more in northern and eastern markets. The problem was distance. Ranchers solved it with the cattle drive: cowboys moved herds on foot from Texas ranches to railheads in Kansas towns such as Abilene and Dodge City, where the animals were loaded onto trains. A drive could take two or three months, and the work was dangerous, poorly paid, and done by a workforce that included many Mexican American and Black cowboys.

The open range system depended on unfenced public grass and on trails staying open. Both conditions failed in the 1880s. Railroads built directly into Texas, which removed the need for the long drive. Farmers and ranchers fenced land with barbed wire, patented in 1874, which enclosed the open range cheaply and blocked trails and water. Severe winters in the middle 1880s killed cattle by the hundreds of thousands. Ranching did not end, but it changed into a fenced, smaller scale business based on owned or leased land.

Farming the Plains

Farmers followed the railroads onto the Plains, encouraged by the Homestead Act of 1862. The act offered a homestead: a claim of up to 160 acres of public land. It was not free land with no conditions. A claimant paid a filing fee and had to live on the claim and improve it for five years before receiving full title. Railroad companies also sold land, often on credit, and recruited settlers in the East and in Europe because settlers meant freight business for the line.

Plains farming was hard in ways eastern farming was not. Rain was scarce and uncertain. Settlers built houses of sod because timber was rare. They used steel plows to cut the tough prairie, windmills to pump water, and dry farming methods to conserve soil moisture. A run of wet years in the 1870s and early 1880s encouraged settlement farther west than the rainfall could support in dry years. When drought returned, many families lost their claims to debt and walked away. Those who stayed became commercial farmers, growing wheat and corn for distant markets and depending on railroad rates, bank credit, and world prices they could not control.

Land Policy and Its Conflicts

Underneath each of these changes sat a federal decision about land. To help railroads build, the government gave companies a land grant: public land in alternating sections along the line, in a checkerboard pattern. The companies sold their sections to settlers and used the land as security for loans, while the government kept the sections in between. To settle farm families, the government offered homesteads. To clear the way, it pressed Native nations onto a reservation: land set aside by treaty or federal order, often far smaller than the land a nation had used before, and often reduced again when miners or settlers wanted it.

By 1890 the West was no longer a separate frontier economy. Its mines supplied national industry, its ranches and farms fed eastern cities, and its land had largely passed into a system of companies, claims, fences, and reservations. Industrial growth had remade the region. Who gained, who paid, and who resisted depended on where a person stood on that map.

Teacher Answer Key

1. According to the reading, what two forces drove the rapid change in the West after the Civil War? Railroads, which connected western places to national markets, and federal land policy, which decided how public land passed into private hands. Completion of the first transcontinental railroad in 1869 cut a months long wagon journey to about a week.

2. Why did the cattle drives decline in the 1880s? Any three: railroads built directly into Texas, so herds no longer needed to walk to Kansas railheads; barbed wire, patented in 1874, fenced the open range and blocked trails and water; severe winters in the middle 1880s killed cattle by the hundreds of thousands; farming settlement closed land the drives had crossed.

3. What did a claimant actually have to do to earn full title to a homestead? File a claim for up to 160 acres, pay a filing fee, live on the claim, and improve it for five years before receiving full title. It is inaccurate to call it free land with no conditions because of the fee, the five years of residence, and the required improvements, and because many claimants also spent heavily on tools, seed, and survival in the meantime.

4. How did a railroad land grant work, and why did the checkerboard pattern matter? The government gave the company public land in alternating sections along the line. The company sold its sections and used the land as security for loans, which financed construction. The government kept the sections in between, whose value usually rose once the line was built. Settlers therefore bought from a mix of railroad, government, and private sellers, and farms and railroad land were interleaved along the route.

5. Choose one group in the reading: miners, ranchers, homestead farmers, or Native nations. Accept any group defended with two accurate facts. Miners: early prospectors could strike it rich, but most sold out or became wage workers when deep mining required company capital, and camps emptied when deposits ran out. Ranchers: Texas cattle worth little at home brought high prices at railheads, but drives ended as rails reached Texas, wire closed the range, and winter killed herds. Farmers: a claimant could earn title to 160 acres, but faced drought, debt, sod housing, and dependence on railroad rates and world prices. Native nations: land held under treaty was pressed into smaller reservations and reduced again when miners or settlers wanted it, as in the Black Hills.
