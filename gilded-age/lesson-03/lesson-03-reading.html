The Workers' World

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

The New Factory Discipline

Industrial work ran on the clock. A factory whistle set the start of the day, bells marked the breaks, and foremen enforced a pace set by machines rather than by the worker. Ten to twelve hours a day, six days a week, was a common schedule, and some steelworkers worked periodic 24-hour turns.

The work was also dangerous. Unguarded belts and gears, mine gas, and molten metal killed and maimed workers in large numbers each year. There was no workers' compensation system, and courts commonly held that a worker who accepted a dangerous job had accepted its risks.

Wages, and the Children Who Had to Work

Pay was low and unsteady. Average yearly earnings for manufacturing workers were on the order of a few hundred dollars, and layoffs could cut even that away without warning. A single adult wage often could not cover rent, food, and clothing. Families closed the gap the only way open to them: more members went to work.

That arithmetic put children into the mills. The census of 1900 counted about 1.75 million children ages 10 to 15 in paid work, roughly one child in five. They tended spinning frames, sorted slate from coal in mines, and worked in glass factories, canneries, and street trades. Employers valued them because they were cheap, could reach into small spaces around machinery, and rarely protested. Most left school early. Child labor was one of the ways the system balanced its books, by paying adults less than a family needed to live.

Why Workers Combined

A single worker who asked for higher wages or a shorter day could simply be replaced. Alone, workers had no bargaining power. Together they might. A union let workers negotiate as a group, a practice called collective bargaining, and a strike, the refusal to work, gave the group its leverage: if all the spinners stopped at once, production stopped with them.

Employers resisted organization itself. Companies fired union members, circulated blacklists of organizers, required pledges not to join a union, and hired replacement workers during strikes.

Two Roads: The Knights and the AFL

The Knights of Labor, founded in 1869 and led in the 1880s by Terence Powderly, took the broad road. The Knights organized many kinds of workers in the same order: skilled and unskilled, factory hands and farmers, and, unusually for the time, many women and Black workers. Their program reached beyond wages to broad reform: the eight-hour day, the end of child labor, and eventual worker ownership of industry. Membership surged after successful railroad strikes in 1885, to roughly 700,000 by some contemporary estimates in 1886.

The American Federation of Labor, founded in 1886 under Samuel Gompers, took the narrower road. The AFL was a federation of craft unions, each organizing one skilled trade, such as cigarmakers, iron molders, or carpenters. It concentrated on wages, hours, and working conditions, and built unions with dues and strike funds meant to survive a long fight.

Three Defeats

On May 4, 1886, during a national campaign for the eight-hour day, a Chicago labor meeting in Haymarket Square was ending when police moved in to disperse it. Someone, never identified, threw a bomb into the police ranks. The blast and gunfire killed seven police officers and at least four civilians, by the counts most often given; totals differ across accounts. Eight anarchist leaders were tried for murder on the theory that their speeches had encouraged the act. Four were hanged. No evidence connected any of them to the bomb itself. In the public mind, labor and violence were now linked. The Knights of Labor, who had not called the meeting, were blamed too, and their membership collapsed within a few years.

At Homestead, Pennsylvania, in 1892, the issue was union survival itself. Henry Clay Frick, running the Carnegie Steel works while Andrew Carnegie was in Scotland, set out to break the union and cut wages. When the contract expired, Frick locked the workers out and brought in about 300 armed Pinkerton guards by barge. Workers met them in a day-long gun battle on July 6 in which about ten men died, by the counts most often given; totals differ. The Pinkertons surrendered. The victory lasted days. Pennsylvania sent some 8,000 militia, the plant reopened with replacement workers under military protection, and the strike collapsed by November. Steel remained largely unorganized for a generation.

The Pullman strike of 1894 began in a company town. George Pullman cut wages at his railroad car works near Chicago during the depression of the 1890s but did not cut the rents in his company town. His workers struck in May 1894 and appealed to Eugene V. Debs and the American Railway Union, which voted to boycott all trains carrying Pullman cars, tying up rail traffic across much of the country. A federal court issued an injunction ordering the union to stop interfering with the trains and the mail, and President Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago over the governor's objection. About thirty people died in clashes, by the estimate most often given; counts differ. Debs was jailed for violating the injunction, the union was destroyed, and the strike ended in defeat.

The pattern across the three defeats is the lesson. Employers hired private guards, replacement workers, and lawyers. Governments supplied militia, troops, and court orders. Newspapers commonly presented strikers as dangerous radicals, turning public opinion against them. Unions that stretched beyond what their members and funds could hold broke under the strain. The AFL's narrower unions survived.

Employer reason: children were cheap, could reach into small spaces around machinery, and rarely protested, so employers actively valued and hired them. 2. How did the Knights of Labor and the American Federation of Labor differ in whom they organized and in what they tried to win?
