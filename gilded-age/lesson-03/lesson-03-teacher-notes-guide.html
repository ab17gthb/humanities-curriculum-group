The Workers' World

The Gilded Age · Lesson 3 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: The Gilded Age, Lesson 3 of 8. Lesson 2 showed how big business built combinations of its own; this lesson follows the answer from below, as workers build a combination of their own and then test it against employers, governments, and public opinion. It answers its two-part question in order: sections A through D explain why workers organized, and section E explains, through Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman, why collective action so often ended in defeat.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 2 (the Sherman Act's use against unions, introduced there, returns here as the injunction at Pullman; the corporation's combination is answered by the union's combination) and the rest of the unit's story of who gained, who paid, and who resisted. Inside the lesson, the two union roads are taught as a deliberate pair throughout: the Knights of Labor reaching for broad reform and the American Federation of Labor narrowing the fight to wages, hours, and conditions, with the comparison collected on slide 17 and tested by the defeats in section E.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Lesson 2's account of the corporation, the trust, and the Sherman Act; the Civil War and Reconstruction background from prior units. Every figure in the lesson is taught as the reading states it — ten to twelve hours a day, six days a week; a few hundred dollars a year; about 1.75 million children ages 10 to 15 in the census of 1900; roughly 700,000 Knights by some contemporary estimates in 1886; about 300 Pinkerton guards and some 8,000 militia at Homestead; about thirty deaths at Pullman, by the estimate most often given. Four image dates are disclosed honestly in the notes: the title spinning room is from May 1911, the breaker boys from 1911, the Gompers portrait from 1911, and the Debs portrait from 1897, all just after the events at the heart of the lesson.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (the reading, the Hine photographs, and the Harper's Weekly engraving read as one account of workers' organizing and defeat), D2.Eco.3 (how low wages and replaceability shaped what workers and employers could do), D2.Eco.8 (the union, the blacklist, and the injunction as institutional answers to a labor problem), D2.Civ.13 (how courts and troops settled labor disputes in this period); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, WHST.9-10.1 (the lesson question answered as a ranked claim about the causes of defeat, with facts cited from the reading).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to describe the factory day — the clock, the machine pace, and danger with no workers' compensation; explain why a wage of a few hundred dollars a year put about 1.75 million children to work by 1900; state the logic of combination — alone, replaceable; together, leverage through collective bargaining and the strike — and the four ways employers answered it; compare the Knights of Labor and the American Federation of Labor on whom they organized and what they tried to win; and explain what happened at Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman and name the pattern the three defeats share.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and question (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; Section A, the factory day (slides 4–6) 6 minutes; Section B, wages and child labor (slides 7–10) 9 minutes; Section C, why workers combined (slides 11–13) 6 minutes; Section D, two roads (slides 14–17) 9 minutes; Section E, three defeats (slides 18–23) 11 minutes; vocabulary and closing (slides 24–26) 4 minutes, with the exit ticket collected at the bell. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. The activity is "Strike Evidence Stations: Haymarket, Homestead, Pullman"; the notebook page is the three defeats chart; the quick check is "A Little Spinner"; the writing task is "Why Did Collective Action Fail?"; the exit ticket has three parts — why workers organized, what force other than the employer beat one strike, and why the AFL survived.

Key vocabulary

union · strike · collective bargaining · injunction · Knights of Labor · American Federation of Labor · child labor

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title "The Workers' World"; the standfirst — "Long days, low wages, and the strikes that tested what workers could win together."

  • Picture: The spinning room at the Magnolia Cotton Mills in Mississippi, photographed by Lewis Hine in May 1911: long rows of spinning frames run the length of the room, belts and wheels turn overhead, and workers stand scattered down the aisles, some of them plainly children.
  • [Say the date plainly.] This photograph comes a few years after the strikes at the heart of the lesson, and it shows the same world those strikes grew out of.
  • That room is the lesson in one image.
  • Industrial work paid the wages that kept families alive, and it also set the terms: the hours, the pace, the danger, and the arithmetic that sent children to the frames.
  • Today students follow the workers from that room to the unions they built, and then to the three defeats that tested what organizing could win.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "Why did workers organize, and why did collective action so often end in defeat?"
  • [Put the lesson question at the center of the hour and keep it visible.] It has two halves, and the lesson answers them in order.
  • Why did workers organize? Sections A through D answer that: the factory day, a wage that could not feed a family, children put to work, and the two union strategies workers built in response.
  • Why did collective action so often end in defeat? Section E answers that with three cases: Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman.
  • The unit question underneath frames the whole Gilded Age: how did industrial growth transform American life, and who gained, who paid, and who resisted?
  • This lesson is the unit's clearest case of the last two groups. The workers paid, in hours, in health, and sometimes in their lives, and the workers also resisted, together.
  • That is why their defeats, and not only their conditions, are the story today.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "A workforce organizes"; the five section rows, A–E.

  • This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section.
  • We start where the workers started, on the factory floor, with the clock, the machine pace, and the danger.
  • Then the pay: wages so low that families sent children into the mills to close the gap.
  • Then the response, because a worker alone had no power and workers together might.
  • Section D compares the two great strategies of the period, the Knights of Labor reaching for broad reform and the American Federation of Labor narrowing its goals to wages, hours, and conditions.
  • Section E puts both strategies, and the movement generally, to the test in three defeats between 1886 and 1894.
  • Tell students the exit ticket draws on sections C, D, and E, so the reasons for organizing and the forces that defeated the strikes both need to land today.

Slide 4 · Section A: The Factory Day (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "Work set by the clock, paced by the machine"; the cotton mill photograph at right.

  • Picture: The interior of a cotton mill, taken by Lewis Hine for the National Child Labor Committee in 1908: rows of spinning frames and belts, with almost no people in the frame.
  • [Name the date honestly.] It comes just after the years this lesson covers, and it shows the same machinery, the same belts and spinning frames, and the same machine-set pace the reading describes.
  • Look at what fills the frame. There are almost no people in it, and that is the point.
  • A spinning room was a system of machines arranged in rows, and the workers fitted themselves into the spaces the machines left.
  • Before unions, before strikes, students need to feel the basic fact of this section: industrial work was not the worker's craft slowed down or sped up.
  • It was a new kind of work entirely, run on the employer's clock, at the machine's pace, and the rest of the lesson grows out of that change.

Slide 5 · The clock set the day

On the slide: five bullets — workers worked ten to twelve hours a day, six days a week; a factory whistle set the start of the day; bells marked the breaks and foremen watched the pace; the pace was set by machines, not by the worker; some steelworkers worked periodic 24-hour turns.

  • [Work the guided notes blanks in order.] A normal industrial schedule was ten to twelve hours a day, six days a week.
  • 10 to 12 hours a day, six days a week.
  • The reading adds a fact students remember: some steelworkers worked periodic 24-hour turns, a full day and night at the furnace when the shift changed.
  • Some steelworkers worked periodic 24-hour turns.
  • The day was bounded by the employer's signals, a whistle to start and bells for breaks, not by daylight or by the task at hand.
  • Inside those hours, the pace belonged to the machine. A spinner or a molder did not decide how fast to work; the frames and the line decided, and foremen enforced the pace the machines set.
  • Contrast this with the farm and craft work most students' ancestors, and many of these workers themselves, had known, where the worker set the rhythm and the seasons set the hours.
  • The clock discipline came first, historically and in this lesson, because it explains why the wage bargain felt so one-sided.

Slide 6 · Danger was part of the job

On the slide: four bullets — unguarded belts and gears, mine gas, and molten metal made the work dangerous; workers were killed and maimed in large numbers each year; there was no workers' compensation system; courts held that taking the job meant accepting its risks.

  • The reading's second paragraph is short, and its legal point is the one students must carry out of section A.
  • The hazards were physical and constant: unguarded belts and gears that caught sleeves and hair, gas in the mines, molten metal in the mills.
  • Workers were killed and maimed in large numbers each year.
  • What turned injury into catastrophe was the law around it. There was no workers' compensation system, no insurance fund that paid a hurt worker or a dead worker's family as a matter of course.
  • Courts commonly held that a worker who accepted a dangerous job had accepted its risks, a doctrine lawyers called assumption of risk.
  • [Put the consequence plainly.] An injured worker could lose the family's main wage, have no claim on the employer, and face the poorhouse.
  • Danger was not an accident of the system. Under the rules of the time, its cost was assigned, in advance, to the worker.

Slide 7 · Section B: Wages and Child Labor (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "A wage that could not feed a family, and the children who closed the gap"; the Hine spinner photograph at right.

  • Picture: Lewis Hine's little spinner in a Georgia cotton mill, photographed about 1909 for the National Child Labor Committee: a small, barefoot girl in an apron beside spinning frames as tall as she is.
  • Students already know this photograph: it is the quick check image. [Use the familiarity.] In the quick check they read the photograph for scale, the small girl beside frames as tall as she is.
  • Now give them the economics behind it. Hine captioned photographs of this kind with the observation that some girls were so small they had to climb onto the frame to reach the threads.
  • She is barefoot, she wears an apron with pockets for her tools, and she stands in an aisle built for machinery, not for children.
  • This section answers the question her presence raises: why was a child standing there at all? The answer is arithmetic, and it starts with what her parents earned.

Slide 8 · A few hundred dollars a year

On the slide: four bullets — average yearly earnings were on the order of a few hundred dollars; pay was low, and layoffs could cut it away without warning; one adult wage often could not cover rent, food, and clothing; families closed the gap by sending more members to work.

  • [Slow down on the reading's careful wording, because the guided notes test it.]
  • Average yearly earnings for manufacturing workers were on the order of a few hundred dollars.
  • Average yearly earnings: on the order of a few hundred dollars.
  • That phrase, on the order of, tells students the figure is a scale, not a precise salary: a few hundred dollars for a full year of ten and twelve hour days.
  • The wage was also unsteady. Layoffs could cut even that away without warning, in a period with no unemployment insurance.
  • Set the wage against the family's bills. A single adult wage often could not cover rent, food, and clothing, the three necessities the reading names.
  • Families closed the gap the only way open to them: more members went to work, wives taking in laundry or boarders, older children leaving school for the mill.
  • Child labor, the subject of the next two slides, was not a separate scandal pasted onto the system. It was how the system's wage arithmetic balanced at the kitchen table.

Slide 9 · 1.75 million child workers

On the slide: the number "1.75 MILLION" in large type; the label — "children ages 10 to 15 in paid work, census of 1900"; the sub-line — "Roughly one child in five. The count is the wage system made visible: pay adults less than a family needs to live, and children take up the difference."

  • 1.75 million children ages 10 to 15 were in paid work in the census of 1900.
  • Roughly one child in five.
  • [Dwell on this number, because it is the census, not a reformer's pamphlet.]
  • The census of 1900 counted about 1.75 million children ages 10 to 15 in paid work, roughly one child in five in that age group.
  • Have students picture their own school scaled up: in a school of a thousand children in those grades, about two hundred would be at work instead, on any given day, in the year 1900.
  • The reading is precise about where they worked. They tended spinning frames in the textile mills, sorted slate from coal in the mines, and worked in glass factories, canneries, and street trades, selling papers and running errands in the cities.
  • Emphasize that the figure counts only ages 10 to 15 and only paid work, so it understates children's labor in the home and on family farms.
  • It is a floor, not a ceiling, and it was the official count of the United States government.

Slide 10 · Children the system could use

On the slide: the breaker boys photograph at right; three bullets — spinners tended the frames and breaker boys sorted slate from coal; children were cheap, able to reach into small spaces, and slow to protest; most child workers left school early.

  • Picture: Breaker boys at a coal breaker near South Pittston, Pennsylvania, photographed by Lewis Hine in 1911, sitting astride the coal chutes and picking slate and rock out of the coal as it rushes beneath them.
  • [Give the date plainly.] It falls a decade after the census count on the previous slide, and the boys are doing the exact work the reading names.
  • The reading gives the employer's reasons in one line, and the guided notes test two of them. Children were cheap.
  • They could reach into small spaces around machinery that adult hands could not. And they rarely protested.
  • Add the family's reason from the last slide: their wages were needed at home.
  • The cost landed on the children themselves. Most left school early, which fixed them in the worst paid work for life, the point the quick check asks students to argue from Hine's spinner photograph.

Slide 11 · Section C: Why Workers Combined (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Alone, a worker could be replaced. Together, workers had something an employer had to reckon with."

  • [Pause at the turn in the lesson.] Sections A and B described conditions: the hours, the danger, the wage, the children.
  • Conditions alone, however bad, do not produce a labor movement. Plenty of workers endured them without organizing, because organizing was risky and often illegal in effect if not in name.
  • This section gives the logic that turned endurance into combination.
  • It is an argument about power, and students should be able to state it in one sentence by the end of the slide after next: one worker has nothing to bargain with, because one worker can be replaced; workers acting together control the one thing the employer needs, the labor itself, and stopping it together is what gives their demands weight.
  • Everything in sections D and E, both union strategies and all three defeats, is a test of that single idea against employers, courts, and troops.

Slide 12 · Alone, replaceable; together, leverage

On the slide: four bullets — one worker asking alone could simply be replaced; a union let workers negotiate as a group; collective bargaining meant the group negotiated at once; a strike stopped production, and that was the leverage.

  • [Teach the three vocabulary words here in use, because the definitions on the vocabulary slides will then land as summaries.]
  • A union is an organization of workers formed to bargain with employers as a group.
  • Its whole premise is the first bullet: a single worker who asked for higher wages or a shorter day could simply be replaced, so alone, workers had no bargaining power. Together they might.
  • Collective bargaining is negotiation between an employer and workers organized as a group, and it changes the employer's calculation, because refusing the group means losing the whole workforce at once.
  • The strike is the enforcement behind the negotiation: a work stoppage meant to pressure an employer to meet workers' demands.
  • If all the spinners stopped at once, production stopped with them.
  • [Hold students to the logic.] The union did not ask the employer to be kind. It made refusal expensive, which is a different and more durable kind of argument.

Slide 13 · How employers answered organization

On the slide: four bullets — employers fired union members; blacklists circulated the names of organizers; workers were made to pledge they would not join a union; employers hired replacement workers during strikes.

  • Employers understood the logic on the previous slide as clearly as the workers did, and they resisted organization itself, before any particular demand was on the table.
  • [Walk the four answers in the reading's order.]
  • Companies fired union members, which made joining itself a risk to the family's rent.
  • They circulated blacklists of organizers, shared lists that kept a known union man from being hired anywhere in an industry or a town.
  • They required pledges not to join a union as a condition of employment, contracts workers signed because the alternative was no job.
  • And during strikes they hired replacement workers, which is why a strike was always a race between the union's funds and the employer's ability to restart production without it.
  • This slide is the seed of section E. In the three defeats, students will watch these private weapons joined by public ones: militia, troops, and court orders.

Slide 14 · Section D: Two Roads (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "The Knights reached for broad reform. The AFL narrowed the fight to win it."; the Powderly portrait at right.

  • Picture: Terence Powderly, who led the Knights of Labor through their great surge in the 1880s; the print is a colorized version of the standard likeness from his years in office.
  • Two organizations dominate this section, and they disagreed about almost everything except the need to organize.
  • The Knights of Labor took the broad road: one order for many kinds of workers, and a program that reached past wages to reforming the industrial system itself.
  • The American Federation of Labor took the narrow road: skilled workers organized craft by craft, pursuing wages, hours, and conditions, with treasuries built to last.
  • [Frame the comparison as a genuine strategic argument, not a right answer and a wrong one.]
  • In 1886 the Knights were enormous and the AFL was a handful of unions. By the mid 1890s the positions had reversed, and section E explains much of why.

Slide 15 · The Knights of Labor: the broad road

On the slide: five bullets — the Knights were founded in 1869 and led in the 1880s by Terence Powderly; they organized skilled and unskilled workers, factory hands, and farmers in one order; they included many women and Black workers, unusual for the time; their goals were the eight-hour day, an end to child labor, and worker ownership; they counted about 700,000 members by 1886.

  • [Give the Knights their full due before their fall, because the guided notes ask for both their reach and their numbers.]
  • The Knights of Labor, in the definition students will copy, are a national labor organization of the 1870s and 1880s that sought broad reform and organized many kinds of workers.
  • Founded in 1869 and led in the 1880s by Terence Powderly, the Knights organized skilled and unskilled workers in the same order, factory hands and farmers together, and, unusually for the time, many women and Black workers.
  • Their program reached beyond wages to broad reform: the eight-hour day, the end of child labor, and eventual worker ownership of industry.
  • About 700,000 members by 1886.
  • Membership surged after successful railroad strikes in 1885, to roughly 700,000 by some contemporary estimates in 1886.
  • Hold the phrase contemporary estimates; the Knights kept loose records, and honest numbers for them are always approximate.

Slide 16 · The AFL: the narrow road

On the slide: the Gompers portrait at right; four bullets — the AFL was founded in 1886 under Samuel Gompers; it was a federation of craft unions, one skilled trade each; it concentrated on wages, hours, and working conditions; its dues and strike funds were built to survive a long fight.

  • Picture: Samuel Gompers, photographed in 1911, late in a tenure that ran from the AFL's founding in 1886 until his death in 1924; he was younger, and the federation far smaller, in the years this lesson covers.
  • The American Federation of Labor is, in the guided notes definition, a federation of craft unions founded in 1886 that focused on wages, hours, and working conditions.
  • Each craft union organized one skilled trade — cigarmakers, iron molders, carpenters — and kept its own affairs.
  • Gompers deliberately refused the Knights' broad program. No campaigns to remake the industrial system; instead, enforceable gains, won by collective bargaining and defended by money.
  • The AFL built unions with dues and strike funds meant to survive a long fight.
  • That unglamorous sentence is the strategic heart of the section, and the exit ticket will ask students to use it to explain why the AFL was still standing in 1900.

Slide 17 · Two roads, side by side

On the slide: five bullets — the Knights organized many kinds of workers in one order and sought broad reform; the AFL organized skilled crafts in separate unions and put wages and hours first; the Knights surged fast and stretched wide; the AFL grew slowly and kept money in reserve; the three defeats tested which road held up.

  • [Consolidate the comparison before the defeats, because students need it organized for the reading's second question and for the exit ticket.]
  • On membership: the Knights put many kinds of workers in one order, skilled beside unskilled, and welcomed groups other unions excluded; the AFL federated separate craft unions of skilled workers and left the unskilled largely outside.
  • On goals: the Knights sought broad reform of the industrial order itself, up to worker ownership; the AFL concentrated on wages, hours, and working conditions that a contract could deliver this year.
  • On structure: the Knights surged to perhaps 700,000 members on the strength of strike victories, with thin funds and loose control; the AFL collected dues and strike funds so a local could hold out.
  • About 700,000 members was the Knights' peak, reached on strike victories with thin funds.
  • Neither road was foolish. Breadth gave the Knights numbers and moral reach; narrowness gave the AFL endurance.
  • The next section shows what each quality was worth when employers, governments, and newspapers combined against them.

Slide 18 · Section E: Three Defeats (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "Haymarket, 1886. Homestead, 1892. Pullman, 1894. One pattern."; the Haymarket engraving at right.

  • Picture: The famous Harper's Weekly engraving of the Haymarket bomb, published May 15, 1886, eleven days after the event.
  • [Warn students, as the activity does,] that the engraving compresses the speech, the explosion, and the shooting into a single moment; it is evidence about what the public saw and feared as much as about what happened.
  • That warning sets up the whole section. Between 1886 and 1894, three of the largest labor actions in American history ended in complete defeat, and the writing task will ask students to explain why.
  • Take the cases in chronological order and keep the same questions for each: what did the workers want, what force was brought against them, and what broke first — their funds, their numbers, or their standing with the public?
  • By Pullman, students should be naming the pattern before you do.

Slide 19 · Haymarket, Chicago, 1886

On the slide: five bullets — workers campaigned nationally for the eight-hour day; on May 4 a bomb was thrown into the police ranks; the thrower was never identified; eight leaders were tried for their speeches and four were hanged; the Knights, who had not called the meeting, were blamed.

  • [Teach Haymarket with the reading's care about what is known and what is not.]
  • On May 4, 1886, during a national campaign for the eight-hour day, a Chicago labor meeting in Haymarket Square was ending when police moved in to disperse it.
  • Someone, never identified, threw a bomb into the police ranks.
  • Seven police officers and at least four civilians were killed, by the counts most often given; totals differ across accounts.
  • Eight anarchist leaders were tried for murder on the theory that their speeches had encouraged the act, and four were hanged.
  • No evidence connected any of them to the bomb itself.
  • Then the consequence that matters for the labor movement: in the public mind, labor and violence were now linked.
  • The Knights of Labor, who had not called the meeting, were blamed too, and their membership collapsed within a few years.
  • A movement can be defeated by association, without losing a vote or a battle.

Slide 20 · Homestead, 1892

On the slide: the Frick portrait at right; four bullets — Frick ran the works while Carnegie was in Scotland; he imposed a lockout and a wage cut aimed at the union itself; about 300 Pinkerton guards arrived by barge; workers won the battle, and 8,000 militia ended the strike.

  • Picture: Henry Clay Frick, from a Bain News Service photograph made years after 1892; Frick directed the company's side at Homestead while Andrew Carnegie was in Scotland.
  • [Students should never write that Carnegie ran the fight in person.]
  • The issue at Homestead was union survival itself. Frick set out to break the union and cut wages, locked the workers out when the contract expired, and brought in about 300 armed Pinkerton guards by barge before dawn on July 6.
  • About 300 Pinkerton guards arrived by barge.
  • Workers met them in a day-long gun battle in which about ten men died, and the Pinkertons surrendered.
  • The victory lasted days. Pennsylvania sent some 8,000 militia, the plant reopened with replacement workers under military protection, and the strike collapsed by November.
  • Some 8,000 militia reopened the plant under military protection.
  • Steel remained largely unorganized for a generation. Private force lost the battle; public force won the strike.

Slide 21 · Pullman, 1894: the company town

On the slide: the Pullman photograph at right; four bullets — Pullman cut wages at the car works but did not cut rents in his company town; his workers struck in May 1894; the American Railway Union boycotted Pullman cars; rail traffic stalled across much of the country.

  • Picture: Troops in formation outside the Arcade Building in Pullman during the strike, with residents watching from the foreground; a contemporary photograph from 1894, and the orderliness of the scene is itself the point about organized public force.
  • The strike began in a company town. George Pullman cut wages at his railroad car works near Chicago during the depression of the 1890s but did not cut the rents in the town he owned, where his workers were required to live.
  • His workers struck in May 1894 and appealed to Eugene V. Debs and the American Railway Union.
  • The union voted to boycott all trains carrying Pullman cars, and rail traffic tied up across much of the country.
  • Note the scale change from Homestead. A boycott that stopped the nation's trains, and with them the mail, was no longer a local dispute, and it drew a national answer, which is the next slide.

Slide 22 · Debs and the injunction

On the slide: the Debs portrait at right; four bullets — a federal court ordered the boycott stopped; President Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago; the troops came over the governor's objection; Debs was jailed for violating the injunction and the union was destroyed.

  • Picture: Eugene V. Debs in 1897, three years after the Pullman strike, already the most famous labor leader in the country because of it.
  • The legal tool that ended the strike is a vocabulary word, so define it in use: an injunction is a court order that requires or forbids a specific action.
  • A federal court issued an injunction ordering the American Railway Union to stop interfering with the trains and the mail.
  • President Cleveland then sent federal troops to Chicago over the governor of Illinois's objection, a state government protesting that it had not asked for help and did not want it.
  • About thirty people died in clashes, by the estimate most often given.
  • Debs refused to obey the injunction and was jailed for violating it; [be precise] that he was jailed for defying the court order, not for leading a strike.
  • The union was destroyed, and the strike ended in complete defeat.

Slide 23 · The pattern in the defeats

On the slide: five bullets — employers used private guards, replacement workers, and lawyers; governments supplied militia, troops, and court orders; newspapers presented strikers as dangerous radicals; unions that stretched past their members and funds broke; the AFL's narrower unions survived.

  • [End the section with the reading's own synthesis, which the notebook page and the writing task both build on.]
  • Across the three defeats the combination repeats. Employers hired private guards, replacement workers, and lawyers.
  • Governments supplied militia, troops, and court orders — state force at Homestead, federal force at Pullman, and the courts at both Haymarket and Pullman.
  • Newspapers commonly presented strikers as dangerous radicals, turning public opinion against them, which cost the Knights their membership without any battle at all.
  • And unions that stretched beyond what their members and funds could hold broke under the strain, while the AFL's narrower unions survived.
  • Have students fill the Three Defeats at a Glance table from this slide before you move on: for each strike, what the workers wanted, and why it ended in defeat.
  • The writing task then asks them to rank these causes and defend the ranking, so the pattern needs to be theirs, not yours.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for organizing

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — union, strike, collective bargaining. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test's vocabulary matching draws on them.

  • Say: "union: an organization of workers formed to bargain with employers as a group."
  • The definition's weight sits in its last three words: the group is the invention, the thing that made refusal expensive for an employer.
  • Say: "strike: a work stoppage meant to pressure an employer to meet workers' demands."
  • Note that the definition says pressure, not persuasion; a strike works by stopping production, not by winning an argument.
  • Say: "collective bargaining: negotiation between an employer and workers organized as a group."
  • Students met all three terms in section C, in use, before meeting them here as definitions.
  • That order is deliberate: the vocabulary names machinery they have already watched run, and stall, in the three defeats.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for the fight and the law

On the slide: four terms with their definitions — injunction, Knights of Labor, American Federation of Labor, child labor. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: "injunction: a court order that requires or forbids a specific action."
  • At Pullman it forbade the boycott, and Debs went to jail for violating it, which makes this the rare vocabulary word with a man's imprisonment inside it.
  • Say: "Knights of Labor: a national labor organization of the 1870s and 1880s that sought broad reform and organized many kinds of workers."
  • Say: "American Federation of Labor: a federation of craft unions founded in 1886 that focused on wages, hours, and working conditions."
  • Students should hear the contrast inside the two definitions themselves: broad reform against wages, hours, and conditions.
  • Say: "child labor: paid work by children, often in factories, mines, mills, and street trades."
  • The practice section B counted at 1.75 million children in the census of 1900.

Slide 26 · Now your turn: stations, notebook, quick check, and writing task

On the slide: the closing title "Judge the pattern yourself"; the prompt — "Return to the lesson question: why did workers organize, and why did collective action so often end in defeat?"; the four work rows — Activity, Notebook, Quick check, Writing; the exit ticket line.

  • The notebook page is the three defeats chart they fill as they go: what the workers wanted, what the other side did, and how it ended and what it proved; it returns on review day.
  • The quick check, A Little Spinner, asks them to read Hine's photograph against the wage facts from section B.
  • The writing task states the lesson question as an argument: which single cause best explains why collective action failed, argued from at least two defeats, with the strongest counterargument answered.
