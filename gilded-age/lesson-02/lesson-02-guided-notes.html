Guided Notes: Big Business and Its Critics

Lesson question: Did big business build modern America, capture it, or do both at once?

Unit question: How did industrial growth transform American life, and who gained, who paid, and who resisted?

Key Terms

trust: a combination of corporations that limits competition within an industry

monopoly: control of an industry by one company or combination, with no effective competition

vertical integration: control by one company of every stage in making and selling a product

horizontal integration: control by one company of many firms at the same stage of an industry

laissez-faire: the argument that government should not regulate business or the economy

Social Darwinism: the argument that wealth and power show natural fitness and that government aid distorts competition

Sherman Antitrust Act: the 1890 federal law that banned combinations in restraint of trade

Two Organizers at a Glance. Carnegie: vertical integration, owning mines, transport, furnaces, and mills; result, costs below rivals and control of steel production. Rockefeller: horizontal integration and the trust, combining rival refiners under trustees; result, control of about 90 percent of oil refining at its peak and power to set prices.

Exit ticket 1. A trust is a combination of corporations that limits competition within an industry; a monopoly is control of an industry by one company or combination, with no effective competition. A trust could produce a monopoly when the trustees ran all member companies as one and eliminated rivalry among them.

Exit ticket 2. Leave business alone is laissez-faire: the argument that government should not regulate business or the economy. Won a fair contest is Social Darwinism: the argument that wealth and power show natural fitness and that government aid distorts competition.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any position supported by one accurate fact, such as lower kerosene prices or large scale steel and oil production for built; monopoly pricing power, secret rebates, or weak Sherman Act enforcement for captured; one of each for both at once.
