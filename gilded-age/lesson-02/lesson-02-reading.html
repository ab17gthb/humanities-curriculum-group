Big Business and Its Critics

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A New Kind of Company

The industries built after the Civil War were too large for one owner to run alone. A steel company needed mines, furnaces, mills, and railroads. An oil company needed wells, pipelines, refineries, and tank cars. Businessmen met those costs by forming corporations, which sold shares to investors and could raise far more money than any single family held. By the 1880s the largest corporations were doing something more. They were absorbing the competition itself, until in some industries only one combination was left standing.

Two Ways to Take Control

Andrew Carnegie built his steel empire mainly by vertical integration: control by one company of every stage in making and selling a product. Carnegie Steel bought iron mines and coal fields, the railroads and ships that carried the ore, and the furnaces and mills that turned it into rails and beams. Owning each stage, it paid no middleman, and its costs fell below those of rivals who bought materials from others.

John D. Rockefeller built Standard Oil mainly by horizontal integration: control by one company of many firms at the same stage of an industry. Rockefeller persuaded, bought, or drove out rival refiners until Standard controlled most of the nation's oil refining. In 1882 his lawyers devised the trust: a combination of corporations that limits competition within an industry. Stockholders in about forty companies handed their shares to trustees, who ran the companies as one enterprise. The trust could set one price, divide markets, and close inefficient plants. Other industries copied the device, in sugar, whiskey, lead, and farm machinery.

The end point of either road could be a monopoly: control of an industry by one company or combination, with no effective competition. Standard Oil refined about 90 percent of the nation's oil at its peak. A firm with that share did not have to take the price the market offered. It could set the price itself.

The Case for Bigness, and the Case Against It

Defenders of the combinations made a serious argument. Large firms were efficient. They bought in bulk, ran the newest machinery around the clock, and cut waste. Kerosene, once a luxury, became cheap enough for working families to light their homes, in part because refining at scale cost less per gallon. Defenders also pointed to philanthropy. Carnegie, who wrote that the rich should use their fortunes for the public good, paid for thousands of public libraries. Rockefeller funded universities and medical research.

Critics answered that the record included more than efficiency. Standard Oil had won secret rebates from railroads, lowering its shipping costs below what rivals paid on the same line. It had cut prices in one town until local refiners failed, then raised them once competition was gone. A small competitor could not match a firm willing to lose money in one market while earning it in fifty others. Farmers and merchants complained that railroads and trusts together decided what their crops and goods were worth. Workers pointed out that efficiency gains rarely reached their pay. The writer Henry Demarest Lloyd argued in Wealth Against Commonwealth (1894) that combinations enriched a few owners by taking wealth the whole community had helped to create.

Ideas That Defended the New Order

Two ideas gave the defenders a philosophy. The first was laissez-faire: the argument that government should not regulate business or the economy. Under this view, markets corrected themselves, regulation protected the inefficient, and a law against a successful company punished success itself.

The second was Social Darwinism: the argument that wealth and power show natural fitness and that government aid distorts competition. The English writer Herbert Spencer applied ideas about competition in nature to human society, and the American professor William Graham Sumner popularized the argument in the United States. Be precise here. Social Darwinism was an argument made by Spencer, Sumner, and their followers about society. It was not a doctrine taught by Charles Darwin, whose work concerned species in nature. Its defenders held that the owners of great fortunes had won a fair contest, and that helping those who lost would weaken the whole society. Critics replied that a contest in which one side received secret railroad rebates was not a fair test of fitness at all.

A Law Against Trusts

In 1890 Congress answered with the Sherman Antitrust Act: the 1890 federal law that banned combinations in restraint of trade. The act made it a federal crime to form a combination that restrained trade or to monopolize an industry. Its language was broad, and Congress left it to the courts to decide what the words meant in practice.

For its first decade, the law did little against big business. Courts read it narrowly. In 1895 the Supreme Court held that a sugar combination controlling about 98 percent of refining could not be broken up under the act, because manufacturing was not trade between the states. In the same period, the act was used against labor unions, whose strikes courts treated as combinations restraining trade. The trust question stayed open. Big business had plainly built much of modern America: its steel, its oil, its national markets. Whether it had also captured the country, its prices, its railroads, and its politics was the argument the next generation inherited.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Explain the difference between vertical integration and horizontal integration, and name the businessman connected to each. Vertical integration is control of every stage in making and selling a product, from raw materials to the finished good; the reading connects it to Andrew Carnegie in steel. Horizontal integration is control of many firms at the same stage of an industry; the reading connects it to John D. Rockefeller, who combined rival oil refiners.

2. What was a trust, and how did it let separate companies act as one? A trust was a combination of corporations that limits competition within an industry. Stockholders handed their shares to a small board of trustees, who ran the member companies as one enterprise, setting one price, dividing markets, and closing plants.

3. Two arguments for the combinations, and two against. For: large firms were more efficient, buying in bulk and running modern plants, which lowered prices such as the price of kerosene; owners also gave fortunes to public purposes, including libraries, universities, and medical research. Against: combinations used unfair methods, including secret railroad rebates and temporary price cutting to destroy local rivals before raising prices again; and they left farmers, merchants, small competitors, and workers with no real power over prices, rates, or pay.

4. Why did the Sherman Antitrust Act have so little effect in its first decade? Courts read the act narrowly. In 1895 the Supreme Court refused to break up a sugar combination controlling about 98 percent of refining, ruling that manufacturing was not interstate trade. At the same time, the act was used against labor unions, treating strikes as combinations in restraint of trade.

5. Did big business build modern America, capture it, or do both at once? Accept any of the three positions defended with two accurate facts. Built: national steel and oil industries, cheaper kerosene, large scale production. Captured: monopoly pricing power, secret rebates, weak enforcement of the Sherman Act, political influence. Both at once: the strongest answers usually pair a building fact with a capture fact and note that the same combination produced both.
