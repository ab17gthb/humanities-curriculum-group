Big Business and Its Critics

The Gilded Age · Lesson 2 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: The Gilded Age, Lesson 2 of 8. Lesson 1 showed how the railroad became a system that rewarded the largest scale; this lesson follows that logic of size into the great firms themselves. It is framed all hour as an argument with two honest sides: big business plainly built much of modern America, and its critics said it had also captured the country, its prices, its railroads, and its politics. Lesson 3 takes up the answer from below, as organized labor builds a combination of its own.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 1 (the corporation and the logic of size are introduced there; Rockefeller's Standard Oil is named there as the case of size winning) and Lesson 3, where labor answers bigness with a combination of its own — which is why this lesson ends on the Sherman Act being used against unions. Inside the lesson, the two organizers — Carnegie in steel and Rockefeller in oil — are taught as a deliberate pair throughout: same goal, control of an industry, reached by opposite routes, and the organizers chart on slide 23 collects the comparison.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Lesson 1's account of the national market and the corporation; the Civil War and Reconstruction background from prior units. Every figure in the lesson is taught as the reading states it — about forty companies in the 1882 trust, 90 percent of oil refining at Standard Oil's peak, 98 percent of sugar refining in the 1895 case, and the Sherman Antitrust Act in 1890. Three image dates are disclosed honestly in the notes: Carnegie's portrait is from 1913, after he ran his steel company; the octopus cartoon "Next!" is from 1904, more than twenty years after the trust was formed; and "The Bosses of the Senate" is from January 23, 1889, one year before the Sherman Act.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (the reading, the portraits, and the two Puck cartoons read as one account of the argument over bigness), D2.Eco.3 (how scale and market control change what firms can do and who wins), D2.Eco.8 (the trust and the Sherman Act as institutional answers to a market problem), D2.Civ.13 (how a broad law's meaning is settled by the courts that interpret it); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, WHST.9-10.1 (the lesson question answered as a claim — built, captured, or both — with facts cited from the reading).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain why industries outgrew the single owner and how corporations raised money by selling shares; define vertical integration through Carnegie's steel chain and horizontal integration through Rockefeller's refining combination; explain how the 1882 trust worked and what a monopoly is; state the defenders' case (efficiency, cheap kerosene, philanthropy) and the critics' case (secret rebates, prices cut then raised, gains that rarely reached workers' pay) at full strength; define laissez-faire and Social Darwinism and say why the second is not Darwin's own doctrine; and explain what the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 banned, why the Supreme Court's 1895 sugar ruling weakened it, and how the same law was used against labor unions in its first decade.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and question (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; Section A, why companies grew (slides 4–5) 5 minutes; Section B, two ways to take control (slides 6–10) 12 minutes; Section C, the argument over bigness (slides 11–13) 8 minutes; Section D, ideas that defended the new order (slides 14–16) 7 minutes; Section E, the Sherman Antitrust Act (slides 17–20) 8 minutes; vocabulary, organizers, and closing (slides 21–24) 5 minutes, with the exit ticket collected at the bell. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. The activity is "The Trust Hearing: Big Business on Trial, 1890"; the quick check is "The Bosses of the Senate"; the writing task is "Built It, Captured It, or Both?"; the exit ticket has three parts — define trust and monopoly, name the two defending ideas, and answer the lesson question with one fact.

Key vocabulary

trust · monopoly · vertical integration · horizontal integration · laissez-faire · Social Darwinism · Sherman Antitrust Act

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title "Big Business and Its Critics"; the standfirst — "How a few combinations came to run whole industries, and how the country argued about it."

  • Picture: The Homestead Steel Works in Pennsylvania, photographed by the Detroit Publishing Company in about 1900 to 1910: a riverfront of mills, furnaces, and smokestacks on a scale no single workshop owner could have built.
  • That scale is the subject of this lesson.
  • After the Civil War, the largest American industries stopped being run by single owners and became corporations and then combinations, until in oil and steel a handful of men could set prices for the whole country.
  • [Frame the day as an argument with two honest sides.] Big business plainly built much of modern America.
  • Its critics said it had also captured the country, its prices, its railroads, and its politics.
  • Students will weigh both claims against the evidence and answer the lesson question for themselves at the end.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "Did big business build modern America, capture it, or do both at once?"
  • Today's question is the narrower one on the slide, and the unit question runs beneath it as the frame.
  • This lesson answers the unit question through one industry-spanning argument.
  • Industrial growth created a new kind of company, the combination, and the country then argued about who gained from it, who paid for it, and who resisted it.
  • The gainers were real: owners of great fortunes, and also families who could suddenly afford kerosene light.
  • The payers were real too: small refiners driven out, farmers and merchants who took prices set by others, and workers whose pay rarely rose with the efficiency gains.
  • [Keep the three choices in the question visible all hour.] Built, captured, or both at once.
  • By the closing slide students must pick one and defend it with facts, so every section should be heard as evidence for one side or the other.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "One argument over bigness"; the five section rows, A–E.

  • This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section.
  • Section A explains why companies grew: the new industries cost more than any one owner held.
  • Section B gives the two roads to control, vertical integration in steel and horizontal integration in oil, ending in the trust and, at the limit, monopoly.
  • Section C stages the argument itself, the defenders' case from efficiency and philanthropy against the critics' case from secret rebates and destroyed rivals.
  • Section D names the two ideas that defended the new order as a philosophy.
  • Section E closes with the country's first legal answer, the Sherman Antitrust Act, and why it did so little for ten years.
  • Tell students the organizers chart and the exit ticket both draw on sections B and E, so names, methods, and numbers need to land today.

Slide 4 · Section A: Why Companies Grew (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "Industries too large for one owner to build or to run alone"; the Homestead Steel Works photograph at right.

  • Picture: The same Homestead Steel Works image from the title slide, cropped on the mill buildings: chimneys, sheds, and rail lines running the length of the riverfront.
  • Use it to make the opening problem concrete. A steel company did not need one furnace.
  • It needed iron mines and coal fields, railroads and ships to move the ore, and furnaces and mills to turn it into rails and beams.
  • An oil company needed wells, pipelines, refineries, and tank cars.
  • No single family fortune covered costs on that scale, and no single owner could manage all of it.
  • So the form of business itself had to change before the size of business could change.
  • That is the one idea this short section carries: first the corporation, then the combination.
  • Everything else in the lesson — the trusts, the arguments, and the law — grows out of that change in form.

Slide 5 · A new kind of company

On the slide: five bullets — after the Civil War, industries outgrew the single owner; corporations sold shares to investors; shares raised more money than any one family held; by the 1880s, the largest firms went further; they began absorbing the competition itself.

  • [Teach the sequence slowly, because the guided notes test it in the first two blanks.]
  • Businessmen met the new costs by forming corporations, which sold shares to investors and could raise far more money than any single family held.
  • A shareholder risked only the price of the shares, so thousands of strangers would put money into an enterprise none of them could have funded alone.
  • That solved the money problem.
  • Then, by the 1880s, the largest corporations were doing something more, and this is the turn the whole lesson hangs on.
  • They were absorbing the competition itself, buying or crushing rival firms until in some industries only one combination was left standing.
  • A corporation competing harder was one thing. A corporation ending competition was another, and Americans spent the rest of the century arguing about the difference.
  • Section B names the two methods they used.

Slide 6 · Section B: Two Ways to Take Control (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "Carnegie took every stage in steel; Rockefeller took every rival in oil"; the Rockefeller portrait at right.

  • Picture: John D. Rockefeller, painted by Eastman Johnson in 1895, when Standard Oil stood near its peak.
  • Section B is the mechanical heart of the lesson, and students should leave it able to define both methods without hesitation.
  • Andrew Carnegie built his steel empire mainly by owning every stage of production.
  • John D. Rockefeller built Standard Oil mainly by combining every firm at one stage, refining.
  • One man went up and down the chain, the other went across it.
  • The section then introduces the legal device Rockefeller's lawyers built in 1882, the trust, and the end point both roads could reach, monopoly.
  • The guided notes end the section with an organizers chart comparing the two men, so teach them as a pair throughout: same goal, control of an industry, reached by opposite routes.

Slide 7 · Carnegie: control every stage

On the slide: the Carnegie portrait at right; five bullets — vertical integration was Carnegie's method in steel; he bought iron mines and coal fields; he owned the railroads and ships that carried the ore; he owned the furnaces and mills that finished it; paying no middleman, his costs fell below his rivals.

  • Picture: Andrew Carnegie in 1913, late in life and long after he had sold his steel company; use it as a portrait of the man, not as a picture of the 1880s.
  • His method was vertical integration: control by one company of every stage in making and selling a product.
  • Carnegie Steel bought iron mines and coal fields, the railroads and ships that carried the ore, and the furnaces and mills that turned it into rails and beams.
  • [Walk the chain aloud in order, mine to mill, because the order is the definition.]
  • Owning each stage, the company paid no middleman, and its costs fell below those of rivals who bought materials from others at marked-up prices.
  • Note what vertical integration did not require: Carnegie did not have to buy every other steel firm.
  • He could defeat them on cost, ton by ton, while they paid suppliers he owned.

Slide 8 · Rockefeller: control every rival

On the slide: the Rockefeller portrait at right; four bullets — horizontal integration was Rockefeller's method in oil; he persuaded, bought, or drove out rival refiners; Standard Oil came to control most of the nation's refining; he held one stage of the industry almost entire.

  • Picture: The same Eastman Johnson portrait of Rockefeller from 1895, used again because his method is the mirror image of Carnegie's and the comparison is the point.
  • Rockefeller built Standard Oil mainly by horizontal integration: control by one company of many firms at the same stage of an industry.
  • He did not begin by owning wells or pipelines. He began at the refinery gate.
  • He persuaded, bought, or drove out rival refiners until Standard controlled most of the nation's oil refining.
  • [Keep the three verbs distinct, because they describe three different experiences for the rivals.]
  • Some were persuaded with fair offers. Some were bought under pressure they could feel. Some were driven out by price cuts and railroad arrangements they could not match.
  • Students often flatten this into "he bought everyone." He did not.
  • The mixture of offer, pressure, and ruin is exactly what the critics in section C will describe.

Slide 9 · 1882: the trust

On the slide: five bullets — Rockefeller's lawyers devised a new device; stockholders in about forty companies handed over their shares; trustees ran the companies as one enterprise; the trust set one price, divided markets, and closed plants; sugar, whiskey, lead, and farm machinery firms copied it.

  • In 1882 Rockefeller's lawyers devised the trust: a combination of corporations that limits competition within an industry.
  • [Explain the mechanics plainly, because the word confuses students.]
  • Stockholders in about forty companies handed their shares to a small board of trustees and received trust certificates in return.
  • About forty companies handed their shares to the trustees in 1882.
  • The trustees now held the voting power of every member company, so they ran the companies as one enterprise.
  • The trust could set one price, divide markets among its own plants, and close inefficient plants without any rival taking the business.
  • Legally the companies still existed. In practice they no longer competed.
  • Then widen the lens. Other industries copied the device, in sugar, whiskey, lead, and farm machinery, which is why Americans of the era spoke of "the trusts" as a general class, not one oil company.
  • The word trust in this lesson never means confidence; it names this specific arrangement.

Slide 10 · 90 percent of oil refining

On the slide: the number "90 PERCENT" in large type; the label — "of the nation's oil refining, held by Standard Oil at its peak"; the sub-line — "The end point of either road could be a monopoly: one combination, no effective competition, and the power to set the price itself."

  • 90 percent of the nation's oil refining was held by Standard Oil at its peak.
  • [Dwell on the number, because it converts a definition into a fact students can argue with.]
  • The end point of either road, vertical or horizontal, could be a monopoly: control of an industry by one company or combination, with no effective competition.
  • Ask: "What changes at 90 percent that has not already changed at 50?"
  • At half the market, a firm still fears the other half. At nine tenths, the remaining refiners are too small to discipline its prices.
  • A firm with that share did not have to take the price the market offered. It could set the price itself, and every lamp in America burned at a price one board of trustees had chosen.
  • Hold that thought. The defenders in section C will answer that Standard also made kerosene cheap.
  • Both claims rest on the same 90 percent.

Slide 11 · Section C: The Argument Over Bigness (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Defenders pointed to efficiency and generosity. Critics pointed to rebates and ruined rivals."

  • This section is a debate, and it should be taught as one, with each side given its strongest form before either is judged.
  • The defenders were not fools or villains. Large firms really were more efficient, and their owners really did give away fortunes for libraries, universities, and medical research.
  • The critics were not jealous outsiders either. Henry Demarest Lloyd, who wrote the era's most famous attack, was a wealthy Chicago journalist arguing that combinations took wealth the whole community had helped to create.
  • Tell students the reading question asks for two arguments on each side, so they should be collecting them as matched pairs: efficiency against secret rebates, cheap kerosene against prices raised after rivals died, philanthropy against the question of how the fortune was made.
  • The exit ticket will force a verdict, but no verdict is worth anything until both cases have been heard at full strength.

Slide 12 · The case for bigness

On the slide: five bullets — large firms were efficient; they bought in bulk and ran the newest machinery around the clock; kerosene fell from a luxury to a household light; Carnegie paid for thousands of public libraries; Rockefeller funded universities and medical research.

  • [Give the defenders their full case first.]
  • Large firms were efficient. They bought in bulk, ran the newest machinery around the clock, and cut waste that smaller firms carried.
  • The result students can picture is kerosene. Once a luxury, it became cheap enough for working families to light their homes, in part because refining at scale cost less per gallon.
  • A family reading by a cheap, bright lamp did not experience Standard Oil as an abstraction; it experienced it as light.
  • Defenders also pointed to philanthropy. Carnegie, who wrote that the rich should use their fortunes for the public good, paid for thousands of public libraries.
  • Rockefeller funded universities and medical research.
  • Take the argument seriously before turning to the critics: if bigness lowered prices and built libraries, what exactly was the objection?
  • The next slide is the objection, and it is about methods, not size alone.

Slide 13 · The case against it

On the slide: the octopus cartoon at right; five bullets — secret rebates from railroads cut Standard's shipping costs; Standard cut prices in one town until local refiners failed; then it raised prices once competition was gone; farmers and merchants took the prices they were given; efficiency gains rarely reached workers' pay.

  • Picture: "Next!", drawn by Udo Keppler for Puck in 1904, more than twenty years after the trust was formed; it shows how the critics' image of Standard Oil hardened over time, an octopus gripping industry and government alike.
  • Now the record behind the image. Standard Oil had won secret rebates from railroads, lowering its shipping costs below what rivals paid on the same line.
  • It had cut prices in one town until local refiners failed, then raised them once competition was gone.
  • A small competitor could not match a firm willing to lose money in one market while earning it in fifty others.
  • Farmers and merchants complained that railroads and trusts together decided what their crops and goods were worth, and workers pointed out that efficiency gains rarely reached their pay.
  • The writer Henry Demarest Lloyd argued in Wealth Against Commonwealth, in 1894, that combinations enriched a few owners by taking wealth the whole community had helped to create.

Slide 14 · Section D: Ideas That Defended the New Order (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "Two arguments that turned a business practice into a philosophy"; the Spencer photograph at right.

  • Picture: Herbert Spencer in 1889, the English writer whose name anchors this section.
  • Sections A to C described what the combinations did and how Americans argued about it.
  • Section D goes one level deeper, to the ideas that told defenders they were not merely rich but right.
  • Two ideas gave the defenders a philosophy, and students must keep them separate, because the exit ticket asks them to identify each one inside a single spoken sentence.
  • The first concerns government: it should stay out. The second concerns people: wealth proves fitness.
  • [Warn students now about the most common error in this section.] The second idea carries Darwin's name, but it was an argument about society made by Spencer and by the American professor William Graham Sumner.
  • Charles Darwin's work concerned species in nature, and he did not teach a doctrine about railroads, wages, or poor relief.

Slide 15 · Laissez-faire and Social Darwinism

On the slide: six bullets — laissez-faire held that government should not regulate business; markets correct themselves, and regulation protects the inefficient; Social Darwinism held that wealth and power show natural fitness; Spencer applied nature's competition to society; Sumner popularized the argument in the United States; Darwin himself did not teach it, and his work concerned species in nature.

  • [Define both ideas with precision, in the guided notes' own terms.]
  • The first was laissez-faire: the argument that government should not regulate business or the economy.
  • Under this view, markets corrected themselves, regulation protected the inefficient, and a law against a successful company punished success itself.
  • The second was Social Darwinism: the argument that wealth and power show natural fitness and that government aid distorts competition.
  • The English writer Herbert Spencer applied ideas about competition in nature to human society, and the American professor William Graham Sumner popularized the argument in the United States.
  • Be precise here, as the reading insists. Social Darwinism was an argument made by Spencer, Sumner, and their followers about society.
  • It was not a doctrine taught by Charles Darwin, whose work concerned species in nature.
  • Its defenders held that the owners of great fortunes had won a fair contest, and that helping those who lost would weaken the whole society.

Slide 16 · Was the contest fair?

On the slide: four bullets — defenders said the winners had won a fair contest; helping the losers would weaken the whole society; critics answered that one side received secret railroad rebates; a rigged contest is not a test of fitness.

  • End the section with the critics' reply, because it is the sharpest single sentence in the reading and it turns the defenders' strongest word against them.
  • The defenders' claim rested on the word fair. If the owners of great fortunes had won a fair contest, then their wealth proved fitness, and government aid to the losers only distorted a natural result.
  • Critics replied that a contest in which one side received secret railroad rebates was not a fair test of fitness at all.
  • [Make students see the structure of that answer.] It does not reject the idea of a contest, or even of fitness. It attacks the evidence.
  • A race tells you who is fastest only if both runners start at the same line, on the same track, under the same rules. Rebates moved the starting line.
  • Once students grasp that, they can evaluate Social Darwinism as an argument rather than repeat it as a slogan, which is what the exit ticket will ask.

Slide 17 · Section E: The Sherman Antitrust Act (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title at left; the standfirst — "Congress answers in 1890; the courts answer back for a decade"; "The Bosses of the Senate" cartoon at right.

  • Picture: "The Bosses of the Senate," drawn by Joseph Keppler for Puck on January 23, 1889, one year before the law in this section. Giant money bags labeled for the steel, copper, oil, iron, and sugar trusts loom over small senators, and the entrance marked for the people is bolted shut.
  • Keep that image in mind as the political setting for the Sherman Act.
  • Congress was not acting in calm detachment; it was acting under public pressure, with cartoons like this one telling voters that the trusts already owned the Senate.
  • The act Congress passed in 1890 was broad, angry, and vague, and Congress left its meaning to the courts.
  • This section is the story of what the courts did with it.
  • Students should track two facts for the reading question: how the Supreme Court treated a business combination, and how the same law was used against labor unions.

Slide 18 · A law against trusts, 1890

On the slide: five bullets — Congress passed the Sherman Antitrust Act in 1890; it banned combinations in restraint of trade; monopolizing an industry became a federal crime; its language was broad; Congress left the meaning to the courts.

  • In 1890 Congress answered with the Sherman Antitrust Act: the 1890 federal law that banned combinations in restraint of trade.
  • [Read the act's two moves separately.]
  • First, it made it a federal crime to form a combination that restrained trade. Second, it made it a crime to monopolize an industry.
  • On paper, that covered the trust exactly: the trustees of forty companies, setting one price, were a combination restraining trade if the words meant anything.
  • But notice the weakness built into the law from the start. Its language was broad, and Congress left it to the courts to decide what the words meant in practice.
  • Congress had named a target without defining it, in a country whose judges were largely sympathetic to the laissez-faire ideas from section D.
  • A law is only as strong as the institution that interprets it.
  • The next two slides show what interpretation did to this one during its first decade.

Slide 19 · 98 percent of sugar refining

On the slide: the number "98 PERCENT" in large type; the label — "of sugar refining, held by a combination the Supreme Court refused to break up in 1895"; the sub-line — "The Court ruled that manufacturing was not trade between the states, so the act did not reach it."

  • 98 percent of sugar refining was held by a combination the Supreme Court refused to break up in 1895.
  • [Dwell on this number beside the 90 percent from section B, because together they measure the law's first decade.]
  • In 1895 the Supreme Court held that a sugar combination controlling about 98 percent of refining could not be broken up under the Sherman Act, because manufacturing was not trade between the states.
  • [Follow the logic with students.] The sugar trust manufactured sugar. Manufacturing happened inside one state, at the refinery.
  • Trade between the states began afterward, when the sugar shipped.
  • So the combination that controlled nearly all American refining stood outside the act, even though its product crossed every state line in the country.
  • A combination holding 98 percent was, if anything, a clearer monopoly than Standard Oil's 90.
  • The law had been written against combinations in restraint of trade, and the Court had read the words narrowly until almost nothing was restrained.
  • This is the first of the two facts the reading question requires.

Slide 20 · A law used against unions

On the slide: five bullets — for its first decade, the act did little against big business; courts read it narrowly, case after case; the same act was used against labor unions; strikes were treated as combinations restraining trade; the trust question stayed open.

  • The second fact is the bitter irony of the decade, and students should not miss it.
  • In the same period when the act could not reach a sugar combination holding 98 percent of refining, the act was used against labor unions, whose strikes courts treated as combinations restraining trade.
  • A strike, in this reading, was workers combining to stop work, and stopped work restrained trade.
  • So the one combination the law reliably punished was the combination of working people, the group with the least power in the whole story.
  • Step back and close the section. The trust question stayed open.
  • Big business had plainly built much of modern America: its steel, its oil, its national markets.
  • Whether it had also captured the country, its prices, its railroads, and its politics was the argument the next generation inherited, and it is the argument students now take up in the hearing simulation.

Slide 21 · Vocabulary: words for control

On the slide: four terms with their definitions — trust, monopoly, vertical integration, horizontal integration. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Say: "trust: a combination of corporations that limits competition within an industry."
  • The device of 1882, in which stockholders handed their shares to trustees.
  • Say: "monopoly: control of an industry by one company or combination, with no effective competition."
  • The end point, measured in this lesson at 90 percent of oil refining and 98 percent of sugar refining.
  • Say: "vertical integration: control by one company of every stage in making and selling a product."
  • Carnegie's road through mines, transport, furnaces, and mills.
  • Say: "horizontal integration: control by one company of many firms at the same stage of an industry."
  • Rockefeller's road through rival refiners.
  • The paired memory hook is simple: vertical goes up and down the chain of production, horizontal goes across the row of competitors.

Slide 22 · Vocabulary: words for the argument and the law

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — laissez-faire, Social Darwinism, Sherman Antitrust Act. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: "laissez-faire: the argument that government should not regulate business or the economy."
  • The hands-off principle that told Congress to leave the combinations alone.
  • Say: "Social Darwinism: the argument that wealth and power show natural fitness and that government aid distorts competition."
  • The philosophy of Spencer and Sumner, which students must not attribute to Charles Darwin himself.
  • Say: "Sherman Antitrust Act: the 1890 federal law that banned combinations in restraint of trade."
  • The country's first legal answer, weakened in its first decade by narrow court rulings and used instead against labor unions.
  • Notice how the three terms form the lesson's triangle. Two ideas defended bigness, and one law opposed it, and for ten years the ideas were stronger than the law.
  • Students who can state all three definitions cold are ready for the organizers chart and the exit ticket.

Slide 23 · Two organizers at a glance

On the slide: six bullets — Carnegie built in steel by vertical integration; he owned mines, transport, furnaces, and mills; his costs fell below his rivals, and he controlled steel production; Rockefeller built in oil by horizontal integration and the trust; he combined rival refiners under trustees; he held about 90 percent of refining and the power to set prices.

  • Use this slide to fill in the organizers chart at the end of the guided notes, row by row, and make students say the contrasts aloud.
  • Andrew Carnegie, in steel, used vertical integration, owning mines, transport, furnaces, and mills; the result was costs below rivals and control of steel production.
  • John D. Rockefeller, in oil, used horizontal integration and the trust, combining rival refiners under trustees; the result was control of about 90 percent of oil refining at its peak and the power to set prices.
  • About 90 percent of oil refining, and the power to set prices, was Rockefeller's result.
  • The chart looks simple, and that is its use: it is the fastest revision tool in the lesson.
  • But press one level deeper before moving on. Carnegie's control rested on cost; rivals could survive, they just could not match his price and live.
  • Rockefeller's control rested on combination; rivals inside the trust stopped being rivals at all.
  • Two different kinds of power, one industry each, and one lesson question covers both.

Slide 24 · Now your turn: The Trust Hearing and exit ticket

On the slide: the closing title "Big Business on Trial"; the prompt — "Return to the lesson question: did big business build modern America, capture it, or do both at once? The hearing is where you argue it."; the four work rows — Activity, Notebook, Quick check, Writing; the exit ticket line.

  • Close by handing the lesson to its work.
  • The activity is The Trust Hearing: Big Business on Trial, 1890. Each group takes one witness card, prepares that witness from the evidence in today's lesson, and testifies before a Senate hearing committee deciding how to use the new Sherman Act.
  • Stress that witnesses must argue from the record: rebates, prices, libraries, percentages.
  • The notebook page then asks students to weigh the defenders' case and the critics' case side by side in writing.
  • The quick check covers the mechanics: the two kinds of integration, the trust device, and the Sherman Act and its weak first decade.
  • Finally the writing task returns to the lesson question, defended with two facts from the reading.
  • Note: Next lesson, organized labor answers bigness with a combination of its own.
