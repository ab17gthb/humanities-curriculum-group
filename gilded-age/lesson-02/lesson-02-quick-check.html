Quick Check: The Bosses of the Senate

Directions: Study the cartoon and the background note, then answer the questions.

Background. Joseph Keppler drew this cartoon for Puck magazine in January 1889, one year before Congress passed the Sherman Antitrust Act. In the Senate chamber, giant figures with money bags for bodies are labeled for the trusts: steel, copper, oil, iron, sugar, tin, coal, and others. They enter through a wide door marked for monopolists. The senators below them are drawn small. At the back, the entrance to the public gallery, the people's entrance, is bolted and barred, and a sign above the chamber rewrites a famous line from the Gettysburg Address to say the Senate belongs to the monopolists, by the monopolists, and for the monopolists.

trust: a combination of corporations that limits competition within an industry

monopoly: control of an industry by one company or combination, with no effective competition

1. What details in the cartoon show that the trusts, not the voters, held power in the Senate?

2. Keppler drew the trusts as money bags much larger than the senators. What argument is he making about the relationship between economic power and political power?

3. A defender of the trusts answers Keppler this way: The large combinations won a fair contest, their wealth shows their fitness, and government should leave business alone. Name the two ideas the defender is using, and explain why a critic in 1889 would call the contest unfair.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What details show that the trusts, not the voters, held power in the Senate? [Blueprint 3: Trusts, monopoly, and the Sherman Antitrust Act] The trusts enter as giant money bags through their own wide entrance, labeled for monopolists, and loom over the chamber. The senators are drawn small beneath them. The people's entrance to the gallery is bolted and barred, and the galleries are empty, so the public is shut out while the trusts have the floor. The rewritten motto over the chamber states the point directly.

2. What argument is Keppler making about economic and political power? [Blueprint 3: Trusts, monopoly, and the Sherman Antitrust Act] That concentrated economic power converts into political power. A combination that controls an industry can also control the lawmakers who are supposed to regulate it, which is why a Sherman Act passed by this Senate might not be enforced against the interests drawn standing over it. Proficient answers note the date: the cartoon appeared in 1889, before the act, as an argument that monopoly was already a political problem, not only a pricing problem.

3. Name the two ideas the defender is using, and why would a critic call the contest unfair? [Blueprint 4: Laissez-faire, Social Darwinism, and their critics] Government should leave business alone is laissez-faire: the argument that government should not regulate business or the economy. Wealth shows fitness is Social Darwinism: the argument that wealth and power show natural fitness and that government aid distorts competition. A critic would call the contest unfair because the combinations did not win on equal terms: they held monopoly power, received secret railroad rebates that lowered their costs below rivals' costs, and, on Keppler's evidence, influenced the Senate itself. Note for teachers: Social Darwinism was a period argument made by figures such as William Graham Sumner and popularizers of Herbert Spencer, not a doctrine of Charles Darwin.

Teacher note. This check is formative. The common weak answer on question 1 lists the money bags without the bolted people's entrance, which is the detail that turns size into an argument about representation. Question 3 is the blueprint 4 item; if students name the ideas but cannot state the unfairness evidence, reteach the rebate system from the reading before the writing task.
