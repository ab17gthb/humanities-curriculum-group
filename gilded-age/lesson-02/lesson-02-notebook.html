Teacher Answer Key

SubjectHow it workedWho gainedWho paid, and how
Carnegie and vertical integration (steel)Carnegie Steel owned every stage of production: iron mines, coal fields, railroads and ships, furnaces and mills. Owning each stage removed middlemen and cut costs below rivals.Carnegie and his investors; buyers of steel, who got rails and beams from a low cost producer; later, the public, through Carnegie's library philanthropy.Rival steel firms that had to buy materials from others and could not match his costs; workers whose employer faced little competition for their labor.
Rockefeller, horizontal integration, and the trust (oil)Rockefeller combined rival refiners at the same stage of the industry. In the 1882 trust, stockholders handed shares to trustees, who ran about forty companies as one, setting prices and dividing markets. Secret railroad rebates lowered its shipping costs.Rockefeller and the trust's owners; consumers at first, through cheaper kerosene.Independent refiners driven out or bought cheaply; consumers later, when the sole seller could raise prices; shippers who paid full rates while the trust paid less.
Laissez-faire and Social DarwinismTwo arguments defended the new order. Laissez-faire held that government should not regulate business or the economy. Social Darwinism, argued by Herbert Spencer and popularized by William Graham Sumner, held that wealth and power show natural fitness and that government aid distorts competition. It was an argument about society, not a doctrine of Charles Darwin.The owners of the great combinations, whose fortunes the arguments treated as proof of fitness and whose businesses the arguments shielded from regulation.Critics, workers, and small competitors, whose losses the arguments treated as the natural result of a fair contest, even when rebates and monopoly power had shaped the result.
The Sherman Antitrust Act (1890)The 1890 federal law banned combinations in restraint of trade and made monopolizing an industry a federal crime. Congress left the broad language to the courts, which read it narrowly.In principle, competitors, consumers, and the public, who gained a legal tool against trusts. In its first decade the act was used most clearly against labor unions.Almost no trust paid at first. In 1895 the Supreme Court refused to break up a sugar combination controlling about 98 percent of refining, ruling manufacturing was not interstate trade.

The pattern. Defenders used competition to mean the contest itself: if a firm won, its victory proved efficiency and fitness. Critics used it to mean the conditions of the contest: a market stays competitive only if rivals can enter and survive, which secret rebates, predatory pricing, and monopoly power destroyed.
