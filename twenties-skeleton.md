# The Twenties Unit: Assignment Skeleton

_Fifth built unit for the Knoweth curriculum and planner site, sitting in course order after Imperialism and World War I and before the Depression and New Deal. Modeled on the Progressive Era pilot and the Gilded Age unit. Every lesson has the same working plate so teachers can pick pieces without losing the line of the unit._

## How to read this

Status marks:

- **HAVE**: an audited source or image already exists in the TPSP bank
- **BUILD**: create for this unit
- **LINK**: use the TPSP bank rather than rebuilding it

## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- How did the prosperity of the 1920s change American life, and why was that prosperity weaker than it looked?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's working estimate is 12 instructional days before teacher picks. The unit runs from the election of 1920, where the Imperialism and World War I unit ended, through the crash of October 1929. It opens with the country Harding's normalcy described: tired of war, reform, and crusades, and ready to make money. It ends at the crash, with a deliberate distinction the last lesson teaches and the test enforces: the crash began the Depression but did not by itself cause all of it, and the weaknesses that turned a crash into a depression were visible all decade.

**The plate in this build**

1. **Reading**: 750 to 900 words with five questions and a teacher key
2. **Guided notes**: keyed to the reading and lesson, with the vocabulary set verbatim and a teacher key
3. **Activity**: stations, jigsaw, map work, simulation, debate, or structured chart work in the locked print design
4. **Notebook page**: a single organizer students keep
5. **Quick check**: a short formative tied to the blueprint
6. **Writing task**: a paragraph or short essay with a scaffold and a teacher key or rubric

TPSP source analyses and photo sets are linked where the audited bank already carries them. They are not rebuilt merely to fill a folder.

**Six state-neutral era topics**

1. The new economy: production, consumption, and credit
2. Mass media and national culture
3. The automobile and the reshaped map of daily life
4. Politics, Prohibition, and the limited state
5. Race, migration, and Black cultural and political movements
6. Belonging and belief: restriction, the Klan, and the culture wars

## Lesson 1: The Business of America

**Lesson question:** How did mass production and mass consumption remake American economic life in the 1920s?

**Reading scope:** Ford's moving assembly line at Highland Park and the falling price of the Model T, the five-dollar day and what Ford expected in return, electrification of factories and homes, the appliance flood, national advertising and the selling of desire, installment buying and consumer credit, chain stores and the pressure they put on local merchants, and radio as a selling medium from its first years. The reading treats production and consumption as one system: goods got cheap because they were standardized, and standardized goods needed a nation of buyers taught to want them.

**Activity concept:** Consumer culture stations. Students work through a Model T price series, a Ford five-dollar-day account, an appliance advertisement, an installment contract, and a chain-store price comparison, then judge which element did the most to create a consumer culture and defend the judgment with the station evidence.

**Vocabulary:** assembly line, mass production, Model T, productivity, installment buying, advertising, chain store

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby closing passage. **LINK** (the decade's wealth seen from inside, in the bank's audited text).

## Lesson 2: Mass Media and the Celebrity Age

**Lesson question:** How did radio, movies, and the popular press give Americans a shared national culture for the first time?

**Reading scope:** The first commercial broadcasts and the rise of network radio, the movie palace and the coming of the talkies in 1927, tabloid newspapers and photojournalism, sports as national theater with Babe Ruth and Jack Dempsey, Charles Lindbergh's 1927 flight and the manufacture of the hero, fads from mah-jongg to flagpole sitting, and the advertising money that paid for nearly all of it. The reading asks throughout whose voices the new media carried and whose they left out, including Black and immigrant audiences building media of their own.

**Activity concept:** One week of media, 1927. Students rotate through a network radio schedule, a talkie review, a tabloid front page, a World Series sports page, and the Lindbergh coverage, then chart what a family in Ohio and a family in Georgia would both have seen that week and what neither would have seen.

**Vocabulary:** mass media, broadcast, radio network, talkie, tabloid, celebrity, fad

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP material on Lindbergh, Ruth, and the popular press where the bank carries it. **LINK**.

## Lesson 3: America on Wheels

**Lesson question:** How did the automobile change where Americans lived, how they spent their time, and what they expected from life?

**Reading scope:** Registration numbers and the spread of car ownership beyond the wealthy, the Good Roads movement and federal highway building, the first suburbs built for drivers and the commuting pattern they created, the decline of the streetcar and the small town the railroad had built, courtship moving from the parlor to the car and the alarm that followed, tourism, tourist camps, and the motel, filling stations and roadside business, and the costs in deaths, debt, and stranded farmers and rail towns. The reading keeps the car tied to the other systems of the decade: oil, rubber, glass, credit, and advertising.

**Activity concept:** One car, five changes. Students trace the effects of one family's first car on housing, courtship, work, leisure, and town business, working from registration data, a road map before and after paving, a tourist camp advertisement, and a mother's letter about dating, then rank the changes by how far they reached.

**Vocabulary:** suburb, commuter, paved highway, filling station, tourism, motel, dating

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP automobile and road photographs where the bank carries them. **LINK**.

## Lesson 4: The Politics of Prosperity

**Lesson question:** What did the Republican governments of the 1920s do at home and abroad, and what did they deliberately leave undone?

**Reading scope:** Harding and normalcy in office, the Ohio Gang and the Teapot Dome scandal and what it showed about the era's ethics, Coolidge and the doctrine that the business of America is business, Treasury Secretary Mellon's tax cuts and the case made for them, the Fordney-McCumber Tariff, and abroad a policy of independence without isolation in name only: the Washington Conference and naval disarmament, war debts and the Dawes Plan circle of loans, the Kellogg-Briand Pact, and refusal to join the League or the World Court. The lesson closes with Hoover, associational cooperation between government and business, and the election of 1928, where Al Smith's Catholicism, Prohibition, and city background split the country along the decade's culture lines and set up Lesson 6.

**Activity concept:** The business of government simulation. Student groups staff the era's decision desks, oil leases, tax policy, the tariff, the naval conference, and the peace pact, issue a decision on each from period documents, then compare their calls with what the administrations actually did and what followed.

**Vocabulary:** Teapot Dome, tax cut, tariff, isolationism, Washington Conference, war debt, Kellogg-Briand Pact

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP material on Teapot Dome, the Washington Conference, and the 1928 election where the bank carries it. **LINK**.

## Lesson 5: The Noble Experiment

**Lesson question:** Why did Prohibition fail to stop drinking, and what did it build instead?

**Reading scope:** The Eighteenth Amendment and the Volstead Act as the war unit left them, the scale of the enforcement problem, speakeasies and the changed social place of drinking, bootlegging supply chains from Canadian and coastal smuggling to home stills, Al Capone and the syndicate organization of crime, violence as a business cost, police and Prohibition agents on the take, the Wickersham Commission's divided verdict, and the repeal movement's strange coalition of wet immigrants, businessmen tired of the income tax argument reversed, and women who had led the dry crusade and now led the Association Against the Prohibition Amendment. The reading is careful with numbers: consumption fell, especially at first, and cirrhosis deaths dropped, and the law still failed on its own terms by the decade's end.

**Activity concept:** The enforcement ledger. Students work through the Volstead Act's key clauses, a speakeasy raid report, a Capone organization chart, a dry appeal, and a repeal pamphlet, then build the ledger the Wickersham Commission faced: what the law achieved, what it cost, and what it built, each entry tied to a document.

**Vocabulary:** Prohibition, Volstead Act, bootlegger, speakeasy, organized crime, racketeering, repeal

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP material on Capone, the dry movement, and enforcement where the bank carries it. **LINK**.

## Lesson 6: The Culture Wars

**Lesson question:** What were the fights over immigration, religion, and science really about: who counted as a real American, and whose knowledge counted as true?

**Reading scope:** The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 and the National Origins Act of 1924 and the ethnic ranking written into them, eugenics and the science claimed for restriction, the second Ku Klux Klan as a national, marketed movement against Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and bootleggers as much as against Black Americans, its 1925 march on Washington and its collapse in scandal, fundamentalism and the anti-evolution laws, the Scopes trial in Dayton in 1925 with Bryan and Darrow, and Sacco and Vanzetti, the anarchists whose case ran from 1920 to the executions of 1927 and drew judgment from the whole world. The reading treats the decade's tolerance and its intolerance as one story, since the same years produced both the Harlem Renaissance and the quota act.

**Activity concept:** The belonging docket jigsaw. Half the class builds the restriction case and its opposition from the quota tables, a Klan membership appeal, and the 1925 march photograph; half builds the belief case from the Scopes testimony and the Sacco and Vanzetti letters and verdict coverage. The whole class then argues what single question connects the docket, and where the evidence for each side is strongest.

**Vocabulary:** quota system, National Origins Act, eugenics, Ku Klux Klan, fundamentalism, evolution, anarchism

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP material on the 1924 act, Scopes, and Sacco and Vanzetti where the bank carries it. **LINK**.

## Lesson 7: The Great Migration and the Harlem Renaissance

**Lesson question:** What did Black Americans build in the cities of the North, and how did that building answer the country that had excluded them?

**Reading scope:** The Great Migration continuing from the war years, with the definition carried over verbatim from the Imperialism and World War I unit, its pushes in Jim Crow, the boll weevil, and flood and its pulls in northern wages and the vote, the Chicago Defender's role in spreading the news, Black neighborhoods and the housing fight in Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, and Harlem, the Harlem Renaissance in literature and music with Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Duke Ellington, and Bessie Smith, the New Negro as a claim about dignity and self definition, Marcus Garvey and the UNIA as mass organizing with its Black Star Line and its collapse, and the NAACP's anti-lynching campaign and the Dyer bill's fate in the Senate. The reading holds achievement and danger together: the same decade that built Harlem also saw the 1921 destruction of Tulsa's Greenwood district, which the lesson names plainly.

**Activity concept:** Migration account jigsaw. Groups work from a Defender call to move north, a migrant's letter, an Urban League report, a Hughes poem, and a Garvey speech, then assemble one account that separates what pushed people out, what pulled them north, and what they built when they arrived.

**Vocabulary:** Great Migration, New Negro, Harlem Renaissance, jazz, blues, UNIA, Chicago Defender

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP material on Hughes, Garvey, and the Defender where the bank carries it. **LINK**.

## Lesson 8: The Weak Spots and the Crash

**Lesson question:** Why was the prosperity of the 1920s uneven from the start, and what exactly broke in October 1929?

**Reading scope:** The farm depression that ran the whole decade, overproduction, falling prices, the McNary-Haugen bills and Coolidge's vetoes, sick industries in coal and textiles, the labor movement's decline under the open shop drive, welfare capitalism, and company unions, income concentration at the top and the installment debt at the bottom, the Florida land boom and its 1926 collapse as the warning nobody priced in, stock speculation and buying on margin, the bull market of 1928 and 1929, and the crash itself from Black Thursday to Black Tuesday. The reading ends by separating the crash from the Depression: a market collapse was not yet a decade of collapse, and the bridge between them belongs to the next unit.

**Activity concept:** The boom audit. Students audit the decade sector by sector, working from a farm price series, a coal miner's account, a welfare capitalism company pamphlet, an installment debt table, and a margin-buying explainer with the October 1929 headlines, marking each sector sound, strained, or broken before the crash, then defend the audit against the headline claim that the economy had looked strong.

**Vocabulary:** overproduction, open shop, welfare capitalism, company union, speculation, buying on margin, Black Tuesday

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP material on farm prices, margin buying, and the crash where the bank carries it. **LINK**.

## Changes from the working map

- The unit opens in 1920 rather than 1919 because the Imperialism and World War I unit already teaches the strikes, the race violence, and the Red Scare of 1919 inside its home front and aftermath lessons, and closes with the election of 1920 and normalcy. Repeating that ground here would cost a lesson. Teachers who run the units back to back get one continuous line from the war to the crash.
- The Great Migration is taught as a continuing movement rather than a wartime event only, and its guided-notes definition is carried over verbatim from the Imperialism and World War I unit so a student who took both units meets the same words.
- Prohibition gets its own lesson rather than sharing one with organized crime as a subtopic, because enforcement failure is the decade's clearest case study of the limited state, and the same argument about government capacity runs through Lesson 4 and Lesson 8.
- The crash closes the unit but the Depression does not open it. The last lesson and blueprint entry 15 teach the distinction on purpose, since students routinely merge the two; the next unit starts where this one stops.
