The Second New Deal

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. 1935: A Second Season of Laws

The critics of the last lesson set the conditions for what followed. Recovery had not arrived, Long's and Townsend's movements were demanding security the first New Deal had not provided, and the Court was striking down its laws. In 1935, Roosevelt and Congress answered with a second wave of statutes built to last.

The difference lay in purpose. The laws of 1933 had been emergency measures, written in weeks to stop a collapse. The laws of 1935 were written as a settlement: permanent duties the federal government would carry in ordinary times too. A work program, an insurance system, and a labor law redrew what Americans could expect from their government, and a tax measure announced who would pay.

B. The WPA at Work

The largest new program in daily life was the Works Progress Administration, created in May 1935 under Harry Hopkins. It put the unemployed to work directly, on projects proposed by states and towns and paid for by Washington. Its crews built and repaired roads, schools, courthouses, and airports. Across its life to 1943 the WPA employed about eight and a half million people; at its height about three million were on its payroll at once.

Hopkins defended the program as work relief rather than a dole: a job, a wage, and something built at the end of it. Critics answered that some projects were make-work. Its record stands in nearly every town, in roads, school buildings, and public grounds.

C. Federal One: Work for Artists and Writers

One part of the WPA hired for culture rather than construction. Its arts projects, grouped as Federal One, employed actors, musicians, artists, and writers. The Federal Theatre Project staged plays across the country, many of them free. The Federal Writers' Project produced guidebooks to the states and collected life histories, among them interviews with more than two thousand formerly enslaved people.

The arts projects were the smallest part of the WPA and drew the sharpest attacks in Congress, which ended the theatre project in 1939. They still marked a change: a jobless actor now counted as out of work in the same sense as a jobless carpenter, and the country's culture had become a public charge.

D. The Social Security Act

The Social Security Act, signed on August 14, 1935, was the season's most durable law. Its center was old-age insurance: a payroll tax on wages, paid by workers and matched by employers, funding a monthly benefit the retired worker would draw as a matter of right. The act stated the entitlement in its text (cuts marked with ellipses): "Every qualified individual ... shall be entitled to receive, with respect to the period beginning on the date he attains the age of sixty-five, or on January 1, 1942, whichever is the later ... an old-age benefit (payable as nearly as practicable in equal monthly installments)."

Around that center the act built two more protections. Unemployment insurance, run jointly with the states, would pay temporary benefits to workers who lost their jobs. Federal grants helped the states pay aid to dependent children, the blind, and the old who had no insurance. The three parts shared one idea: the risks of old age, job loss, and a father's death were no longer to fall on a family alone.

E. Who Was Left Out

The settlement had a boundary written into the law. The act's definition of employment left out agricultural labor and domestic service in a private home. Those fields held a large share of all Black workers, above all in the South, and a large share of working women. The exclusion was defended then as administration: reporting and taxing farm and household wages. Its effect was plain: the workers most exposed to poverty stood outside the new insurance at its founding.

Later amendments widened the system, bringing most farm and domestic workers inside during the 1950s. In 1935 the boundary was part of the settlement itself, and any honest account of the law has to name the people it left outside.

F. The Wagner Act and the Labor Board

The National Labor Relations Act, the Wagner Act, was signed on July 5, 1935. It guaranteed workers the right to organize unions of their own choosing and to bargain collectively, and it made acts by employers, such as firing a worker for union activity, unfair labor practices forbidden by law. Its enforcement agency, the National Labor Relations Board, ran the elections in which workers chose their union and heard charges against employers.

Until 1935 the right to organize had rested on declarations employers could mostly ignore. The Wagner Act gave the right an enforcement agency and a procedure. Workers used it at once; the organizing drives of the next lesson began from the ground this law prepared.

G. The Wealth Tax and the Settlement

The Revenue Act of 1935, which newspapers called the wealth tax, raised income tax rates at the very top, taking the highest rate to seventy-five percent on the largest incomes, and raised taxes on large estates and corporations. It paid for only part of the new programs. Its larger purpose was political, answering Long's charge that the New Deal had left great fortunes untouched.

Together, the laws of 1935 changed the standing question of American government. Before them, a worker's old age, a lost job, and a fight to organize were private troubles. After them, the federal government had accepted permanent responsibility for each, funded by taxes and run by standing agencies. That acceptance, with the exclusion written beside it, is the settlement the rest of the unit will judge.

Teacher Answer Key

1. How did the purpose of the 1935 laws differ from that of the 1933 laws, and what were the three main laws? The 1933 laws were emergency measures, written in weeks to stop a collapse. The 1935 laws were written as a settlement: permanent duties the federal government would carry in ordinary times too. The three main laws were the Works Progress Administration (work relief on a national scale), the Social Security Act (old-age insurance, unemployment insurance, and aid), and the National Labor Relations Act (enforceable labor rights). The Revenue Act of 1935, the wealth tax, accompanied them.

2. What did the WPA do, who ran it, and how many did it employ? The Works Progress Administration, created in May 1935 under Harry Hopkins, hired the unemployed directly onto projects proposed by states and towns and paid for by Washington: roads, schools, courthouses, and airports, plus the Federal One arts projects. Across its life to 1943 it employed about eight and a half million people, with about three million on the payroll at its height. Hopkins defended it as work relief rather than a dole: a job, a wage, and something built at the end of it, against the critics' charge that some projects were make-work.

3. State the three protections of the Social Security Act and how old-age insurance was paid for. Old-age insurance, paying a monthly benefit to retired workers as a matter of right; unemployment insurance, run jointly with the states, paying temporary benefits to workers who lose their jobs; and federal grants to the states for aid to dependent children, the blind, and the old without insurance. Old-age insurance was paid for by a payroll tax on wages, paid by workers and matched by their employers, with benefits under the original text beginning at sixty-five and monthly payments written to start in 1942.

4. Who was left outside at the founding, and why must the exclusion be named? Workers in agricultural labor and domestic service in a private home, fields that held a large share of all Black workers, above all in the South, and a large share of working women. The exclusion was defended then as administrative, a problem of reporting and taxing farm and household wages. The reading insists it be named because the law's protection is the lesson's subject: a settlement described only by whom it covered misstates the record, since the workers most exposed to poverty stood outside the insurance at its founding. Later amendments brought most of these workers inside during the 1950s.

5. How did the Wagner Act change the right to organize in practice? Before 1935 the right rested on declarations employers could mostly ignore. The Wagner Act guaranteed workers the right to organize unions of their own choosing and to bargain collectively, defined acts such as firing a worker for union activity as unfair labor practices forbidden by law, and created the National Labor Relations Board to run representation elections and hear charges. The right therefore gained an enforcement agency and a procedure, and workers used it at once in the organizing drives that followed. Full credit requires the board and at least one enforcement power, not the guarantee alone.
