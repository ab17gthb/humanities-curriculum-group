Too Little, Too Much

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Attacked From Both Sides

By the start of 1935 the New Deal had run for two years without ending the Depression. Millions remained out of work, and the recovery was thin and uneven. By 1935 the judgments arrived from opposite directions at once.

From one side, mass movements argued that the New Deal did too little, saying recovery would never come while a few men held most of the nation's wealth. From the other side, wealthy opponents and the Supreme Court argued that it did too much, taking powers left to Congress and the states.

B. Huey Long and Share Our Wealth

The loudest voice on the first side belonged to Huey Long, Louisiana's former governor and now a United States senator. In a national radio address on February 23, 1934, he announced the Share Our Wealth Society. Its clubs soon claimed millions of members.

The plan capped large fortunes at $50,000,000 to fund a guaranteed floor for every family. Long stated the promise in the address: "We do not propose to divide it up equally. We do not propose a division of wealth, but we propose to limit poverty that we will allow to be inflicted upon any man's family." The guarantee was family wealth of around $5,000, enough for a home, an automobile, and a radio, with a pension of thirty dollars a month.

Long was shot in the state capitol in September 1935 and died two days later. A senator broadcasting to millions could build a national following on the charge that the New Deal had left great fortunes untouched, and the White House feared a challenge in 1936.

C. Townsend and the Old-Age Pension

Dr. Francis Townsend, a retired California physician, built his movement on one idea. The federal government would pay two hundred dollars a month to every citizen over sixty. Each pensioner had to retire and spend the whole payment within the month, so it passed at once into local stores. The spending, he argued, would end the slump as retirees left jobs to younger workers.

Townsend clubs organized in nearly every congressional district, and petitions reached Congress by the million. Economists named the weakness plainly: the pension would have cost a large share of the national income. Old age was where the Depression lasted longest and charity reached least, and the old were now a force Congress could not safely ignore.

D. Coughlin and the Radio Audience

Father Charles Coughlin, a Catholic priest broadcasting from Royal Oak, Michigan, reached the largest audience of the three. His weekly talks had backed Roosevelt early in the New Deal. By 1934 he had turned on the administration, attacking bankers and the Federal Reserve, and in November founded the National Union for Social Justice to organize his listeners. Summaries of the broadcasts describe a program of monetary change and public control of key industries, framed as a fight against a small circle of financiers.

The three movements competed for many of the same followers. Opponents called such leaders demagogues, men who rise on simple promises spoken to fear and hope. The word is a judgment, best tested man by man against what each actually proposed.

E. The American Liberty League

The attack from the other direction had money and standing but no mass membership. The American Liberty League, founded in 1934, drew its leaders from wealthy businessmen and conservative Democrats. The League held that the New Deal threatened property, piled up debt, and moved toward government control of business.

Defending great wealth in a depression made the League an easy target with no following among voters. Its claim that the New Deal had passed the Constitution's limits was also a lawyer's claim, about to be heard in the Supreme Court.

F. Schechter: The Court Strikes the Recovery Act

On May 27, 1935, the Court decided the case of a Brooklyn poultry company charged under the live poultry code of the National Industrial Recovery Act. The unanimous decision in Schechter v. United States struck the act at two points. The syllabus states the first: "Extraordinary conditions, such as an economic crisis, may call for extraordinary remedies, but they cannot create or enlarge constitutional power." Congress, it continues, "is not permitted by the Constitution to abdicate, or to transfer to others, the essential legislative functions with which it is vested." Congress had let the president approve codes for whole industries with no standards of its own.

The second point was commerce. The company's chickens had crossed state lines to reach New York, but the code punished how they were sold and slaughtered inside the state, which the Court held lay beyond the commerce power of Congress. The industrial plan of the early New Deal vanished in a day. The day was remembered as Black Monday: the Court ruled against the government in two other cases as well.

G. Butler and the Lesson of the Two Fronts

The farm program fell next. The Agricultural Adjustment Act paid farmers to produce less, funded by a tax on farm processors. In United States v. Butler, decided in January 1936, the Court struck the tax down: Congress could not use its taxing power to regulate farm production, a field left to the states. After the two decisions, the Court would plainly set the New Deal's boundaries.

The two fronts had closed together. One side demanded more than the New Deal had given, above all security in old age and a curb on great fortunes. The other side's argument now stood in constitutional law. The Second New Deal of 1935 answered both pressures, moving toward the protections the critics demanded in statutes written to survive the Court.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Explain Long's plan, including the fortune cap, the $5,000 guarantee, and the pension. Long proposed to cap large fortunes, in the February 1934 address allowing no one man to own more than $50,000,000, and to use what was taken from the top to guarantee every family a floor below which it could not fall. The guarantee as he stated it was family wealth of around $5,000, enough for a home, an automobile, a radio, and the schooling of the children. Beside it the address proposed an old-age pension of thirty dollars a month. He was careful to say the plan did not divide wealth equally or guarantee equality; it limited the poverty a family would be allowed to suffer. Answers that give the plan as simple equal division have missed the distinction Long himself drew.

2. Why was the Townsend Plan politically powerful despite the cost objection? The two conditions were that the pensioner give up regular work and spend the whole two hundred dollars within the month. Economists objected that paying the pension to every citizen over sixty would cost a large share of the national income. The political fact set apart from the arithmetic is organizational: Townsend clubs stood in nearly every congressional district and petitions carried millions of signatures, and old age was where the Depression lasted longest and private charity reached least, so members of Congress faced an organized constituency on the one need the New Deal had not yet answered.

3. Test the word demagogue against one leader, using two facts. A demagogue, by the lesson definition, gains power by arousing fears, prejudices, and hopes with simple promises rather than by answering arguments. A strong answer picks one man and argues the fit or the misfit from evidence. For Long: the promise of a guaranteed family floor was simple and spoke to hope, but he stated figures, a fortune cap, a $5,000 guarantee, and a pension amount, and answered the cost question by naming the fortunes the money would come from. For Townsend: the single figure of two hundred dollars was simple, but the plan named its mechanism, retirement plus required spending, even if the cost arithmetic failed. For Coughlin: the fight framed against a small circle of financiers spoke to fear and prejudice more than to a costed program, and his movement rested on a radio voice rather than a drafted bill. Any verdict earns credit when two accurate facts do the work.

4. State the two holdings of Schechter and what each destroyed. On delegation, the Court held that Congress may not abdicate or transfer its essential legislative functions; the Recovery Act let the president approve industry codes without congressional standards, which surrendered the lawmaking power itself. This destroyed the code system of the National Recovery Administration. On commerce, the Court held that the poultry company's local selling and slaughtering, after the birds had come to rest in New York, lay beyond the federal commerce power and within the authority of the states. Together the holdings removed the legal basis of the early New Deal's industrial plan in a single unanimous decision.

5. How did attack from both directions shape the Second New Deal? Full credit answers hold both pressures in one paragraph. The mass movements, Long's clubs, the Townsend petitions, and Coughlin's audience, demanded more than the first New Deal had given, above all security in old age and a curb on great fortunes, and they made inaction politically dangerous in the run up to 1936. The Liberty League argued the New Deal had gone too far, and the Supreme Court wrote part of that argument into law in Schechter and Butler, striking the recovery and farm acts. The 1935 laws answered both fronts at once: they moved toward the permanent protections the movements demanded, and they were drafted as statutes of Congress resting on the taxing and commerce powers in forms meant to survive the Court. Partial credit for one front only.
