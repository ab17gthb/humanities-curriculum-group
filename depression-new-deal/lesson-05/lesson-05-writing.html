Writing Task: Attack From Every Direction

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Why did the New Deal draw attack from every direction by 1935, and what did the attacks reveal about the country? A strong answer does more than list critics. It separates the two directions of attack, the charge that the New Deal did too little and the charge that it did too much, and it explains what the existence of both attacks at once shows about the United States in 1935. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. Huey Long announced the Share Our Wealth Society in a national radio address on February 23, 1934. The plan capped large fortunes to guarantee every family wealth of around $5,000, with an old-age pension of thirty dollars a month. Its clubs claimed millions of members before Long was assassinated in September 1935.

2. The Townsend Plan proposed two hundred dollars a month to every citizen over sixty who retired and spent the payment within the month. Townsend clubs organized in nearly every congressional district, and petitions reached Congress by the million, even though economists objected that the pension would have cost a large share of the national income.

3. Father Charles Coughlin's broadcasts reached tens of millions. After backing Roosevelt early, he broke with the administration and in November 1934 founded the National Union for Social Justice. Summaries of the broadcasts describe a program of monetary change and public control of key industries, framed as a fight against a small circle of financiers.

4. The American Liberty League, founded in 1934 by wealthy businessmen and conservative Democrats, charged that the New Deal threatened property, piled up public debt, and moved toward government control of business. It had money and standing but no mass membership.

5. In Schechter v. United States (1935), a unanimous Supreme Court struck down the National Industrial Recovery Act, holding that Congress had given away its lawmaking power and reached business beyond its authority. The syllabus held that an economic crisis cannot create or enlarge constitutional power.

6. In United States v. Butler (1936), the Court struck down the Agricultural Adjustment Act's processing tax, holding that Congress could not use its taxing power to regulate farm production, a field left to the states.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the lesson question directly and holds both directions of attack in view, too little and too much, rather than treating the critics as one group. (Blueprint 9 and Blueprint 10)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, drawn from both directions. Bank items 1 through 3 support the too little side: Long's fortune cap and clubs, the Townsend petitions, Coughlin's audience and break with Roosevelt. (Blueprint 9) Bank items 4 through 6 support the too much side: the Liberty League's charges and the two Court decisions. (Blueprint 10) A full credit essay draws on both sides of the bank.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to what the attacks revealed, such as a mass public demanding security the first New Deal had not provided, organized by radio and clubs, and a constitutional system that limited the federal answer at the very moment the demand peaked.
  • An ending that answers the second half of the question in its own terms, not a restatement of the claim.

Model opening for teacher reference. The New Deal drew attack from every direction by 1935 because it stood between a public that wanted more security than it had delivered and a constitutional order that limited how much it could deliver at all. From below, the pressure was organized and enormous. Huey Long's Share Our Wealth clubs claimed millions of members behind a fortune cap and a guaranteed family floor, and the Townsend Plan turned the old into petitioners in nearly every congressional district behind two hundred dollars a month. From above, the pressure was narrower and, in the end, stronger in law: the Liberty League charged that the New Deal threatened property and piled up debt, and a unanimous Supreme Court struck down the recovery act in Schechter, holding that the emergency itself could not enlarge constitutional power. A full model continues in this pattern, adds Butler, and closes on what the two fronts together revealed.

Common weak answers. Critics listed without directions (Long, Townsend, Coughlin, the League, and the Court all opposed Roosevelt) merges opponents who wanted opposite things. Movements only cannot explain why the administration also had to fear the courts. Court only cannot explain why the 1935 laws moved toward the movements' demands rather than away from them.
