Hoover and the Limits of Voluntarism

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

Hoover's Beliefs

Herbert Hoover came to the presidency in 1929 with a reputation earned organizing food relief in wartime Europe, where his programs had fed millions. He believed in rugged individualism, Hoover's belief that Americans should succeed through their own effort and that direct federal relief would weaken character and self reliance. Relief given by the federal government, in his view, would turn citizens into dependents and unbalance the budget.

His method followed from those beliefs. Voluntarism, Hoover's policy of meeting the Depression through voluntary cooperation by business, charities, and local government rather than through federal relief programs, asked each part of the country to carry its share by agreement rather than by law. The president would summon business, charity, and government leaders and win pledges from them. Business would hold wages and keep building. Charities and cities would feed the hungry. The federal government would encourage and coordinate, and it would not pay relief.

What Hoover Did First

Hoover acted earlier than the later caricature of a do nothing president allows. In 1929 he backed the Federal Farm Board, which lent money to farm cooperatives to hold crops off the market and steady prices. He pressed business leaders to maintain wages and construction. He expanded federal public works, government funded construction projects such as dams, roads, and public buildings, built to provide jobs as well as lasting structures, The great dam on the Colorado River that later carried his name was the largest of these projects.

The Hawley-Smoot Tariff

One measure worked against recovery. The Hawley-Smoot Tariff, the 1930 law that raised United States tariffs to the highest level in the country's history, and drew retaliation that cut world trade, answered farm and factory demands for protection from foreign goods. Hoover signed it in June 1930, after more than a thousand economists had petitioned him to veto it. Trading partners raised their own tariffs in return, American exports fell further, and farmers lost foreign buyers they could not replace. A policy meant to protect American jobs shrank the markets American workers sold into.

Lending at the Top

As banks failed by the thousand, Hoover moved further. In January 1932 he signed the law creating the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, the federal agency created in 1932 that lent public money to banks, railroads, and insurance companies in the hope that stability at the top would restore jobs below. The reasoning ran downward. A saved bank would lend again, a saved railroad would keep its crews, and credit would reach families at the bottom in time. In July 1932 the Emergency Relief and Construction Act authorized the corporation to lend money to states for relief and to fund more public works, Hoover's one step toward federal relief, taken as loans rather than grants.

The lending did not reach the bottom fast enough. Banks used federal loans to repair their own balance sheets, and many were too weak or too frightened to lend. State relief loans were limited, and states already in debt could borrow only so much. Pledged wages were cut across industry by 1931 and 1932, and charities and city relief funds were exhausted. Measured against a collapse that had put about one worker in four out of work, it was not enough, and it arrived in the wrong order, help for institutions first and relief for families last.

The Bonus Army

In the summer of 1932 the gap became visible in the capital. The Bonus Army, the veterans of the First World War who camped in Washington in the summer of 1932 demanding early payment of their promised service bonus, and were driven out by federal troops, had come to ask Congress to pay a bonus voted in 1924 and not due until 1945. Thousands of veterans, many with families, camped across the city, the largest camp on the Anacostia flats. Congress refused, and most drifted home, but a few thousand stayed. On July 28, after clashes with police, Hoover ordered the army to clear the camps. Federal troops under General Douglas MacArthur drove the veterans out with tanks, cavalry, and tear gas and burned the Anacostia camp. Photographs of the burning camp fixed the president's image for voters judging him in November.

The Election of 1932

The election of 1932 was less a contest of programs than a verdict on conditions. The Democrats nominated Governor Franklin D. Roosevelt of New York, who promised a new deal for the American people and campaigned as a man willing to experiment. Hoover defended his record and warned that Roosevelt's promises would unbalance the budget. Voters in a country with about one worker in four out of a job were not voting on a theory. Roosevelt won about 57 percent of the popular vote and carried forty-two of the forty-eight states.

The Interregnum

The Constitution of the day left four months between election and inauguration. The interregnum, the period between Roosevelt's election in November 1932 and his inauguration in March 1933, when the outgoing and incoming presidents shared responsibility for a deepening crisis and neither acted decisively, became the worst winter of the Depression. Hoover urged Roosevelt to join in statements that would steady banks and commit the incoming administration to a balanced budget. Roosevelt refused to bind himself before taking office. State by state, governors closed banks to stop runs, until by inauguration morning the banking system had shut down. A generation later the inauguration moved from March to January. What the new president did with the emergency is the next lesson.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Explain voluntarism, and name the belief behind it. Voluntarism was Hoover's policy of meeting the Depression through voluntary cooperation by business, charities, and local government rather than through federal relief programs. The president summoned leaders, won pledges, and publicized them, while the federal government encouraged and coordinated. Behind it stood rugged individualism, his belief that Americans should succeed through their own effort and that direct federal relief would weaken character and self reliance, unbalance the budget, and expand federal power.

2. Name three things Hoover did before 1932, and explain why the do nothing description is inaccurate. Any three, accurately stated. He backed the Federal Farm Board of 1929, lending to farm cooperatives to hold crops and steady prices. He won pledges from business to maintain wages and construction. He expanded federal public works spending, including the great Colorado River dam. He signed the Hawley-Smoot Tariff in 1930. The description is inaccurate because Hoover intervened repeatedly and earlier than most presidents would have in an earlier era. The accurate charge is about scale and design, not inactivity.

3. Explain the reasoning behind lending at the top, and give two reasons the help did not reach the bottom. The Reconstruction Finance Corporation lent public money to banks, railroads, and insurance companies in the hope that stability at the top would restore jobs below: a saved bank would lend again and a saved railroad would keep its crews. It fell short because banks used loans to repair their own balance sheets and were too weak or frightened to lend, railroad crews stayed smaller, state relief loans under the Emergency Relief and Construction Act were limited and conditional, and pledged wages were cut anyway while charity and city funds ran out.

4. How did the Bonus Army episode damage Hoover beyond one day? Thousands of veterans camped in Washington asking early payment of a bonus voted in 1924 and due in 1945. On July 28, 1932, federal troops under General Douglas MacArthur drove the remaining veterans out with tanks, cavalry, and tear gas and burned the Anacostia camp. Photographs of the burning camp reached voters across the country and fixed the image of a president using the army against veterans and their families in the election season, confirming the charge that his program protected institutions while families went without.

5. What did Hoover do, and why did his program fall short? Full credit answers combine the record and the limits. Hoover organized voluntary cooperation, aided farm cooperatives, expanded public works, signed a protective tariff that cut world trade, created the RFC to lend at the top, and took one loan based step toward state relief in July 1932. The program fell short because it was measured for a smaller emergency: voluntary pledges broke under falling prices, charity and city funds were exhausted, lending at the top did not become lending or jobs at the bottom, and the banking system failed state by state through the interregnum while about one worker in four stood out of work. Answers that repeat only the do nothing caricature, or only the list of actions, earn partial credit.
