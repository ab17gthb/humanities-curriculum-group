Guided Notes: Hoover and the Limits of Voluntarism

Lesson question: What did President Hoover do about the Depression, and why did his program fall short of the collapse?

Unit question: How did the New Deal change the relationship between Americans and their federal government, and what did it leave undone?

Key Terms

voluntarism: Hoover's policy of meeting the Depression through voluntary cooperation by business, charities, and local government rather than through federal relief programs

rugged individualism: Hoover's belief that Americans should succeed through their own effort and that direct federal relief would weaken character and self reliance

Reconstruction Finance Corporation: the federal agency created in 1932 that lent public money to banks, railroads, and insurance companies in the hope that stability at the top would restore jobs below

public works: government funded construction projects such as dams, roads, and public buildings, built to provide jobs as well as lasting structures

Hawley-Smoot Tariff: the 1930 law that raised United States tariffs to the highest level in the country's history, and drew retaliation that cut world trade

Bonus Army: the veterans of the First World War who camped in Washington in the summer of 1932 demanding early payment of their promised service bonus, and were driven out by federal troops

interregnum: the period between Roosevelt's election in November 1932 and his inauguration in March 1933, when the outgoing and incoming presidents shared responsibility for a deepening crisis and neither acted decisively

Summary table. Beliefs: rugged individualism held that Americans should succeed through their own effort and that direct federal relief would weaken character and self reliance, so voluntarism assigned relief to business cooperation, charities, and local government. Actions: the Federal Farm Board, wage and construction pledges, expanded public works, the Hawley-Smoot Tariff, the RFC lending to banks, railroads, and insurance companies, and the Emergency Relief and Construction Act loans to states. Limits: pledges broke, the tariff cut world trade, banks repaired their balance sheets instead of lending, charity and city funds were exhausted, and the Bonus Army clearing fixed Hoover's public image. The verdict of 1932: Roosevelt won about 57 percent of the popular vote and forty-two states, and the banking system then shut down state by state through the interregnum.

Exit ticket 1. Hoover intervened repeatedly, through the Farm Board, public works, the tariff, the RFC, and relief loans to states. The accurate criticism is that his program was too small and wrongly ordered for the collapse, not that he did nothing.

Exit ticket 2. Any two. Banks used federal loans to repair their own balance sheets and were too weak or frightened to lend. Railroad crews stayed smaller. State relief loans were limited and offered as loans to states already in debt. Pledged wages were cut across industry anyway, and charity and city relief funds were exhausted.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that pairs the record of action with the limits and uses at least two facts. The strongest answers name voluntarism as the design, the RFC as its largest instrument, and the scale of the collapse, about one worker in four out of work, as the measure it failed.
