Writing Task: Judging Hoover's Program

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: What did President Hoover do about the Depression, and why did his program fall short of the collapse? A strong answer does two things at once. It states the record fairly, including the actions the do nothing caricature leaves out, and it explains the shortfall from the design of the program and the size of the collapse, not from Hoover's character. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. Hoover's policy was voluntarism: business, charities, and local government were to carry relief by voluntary cooperation, coordinated from Washington, while the federal government refused direct relief. Behind it stood rugged individualism, the belief that direct federal relief would weaken character and self reliance.

2. In 1929 the Federal Farm Board lent to farm cooperatives to hold crops and steady prices, and Hoover expanded federal public works, including the great dam on the Colorado River.

3. The Hawley-Smoot Tariff, signed in June 1930, raised tariffs to the highest level in the country's history. Trading partners retaliated and cut world trade, and American exports fell further.

4. The Reconstruction Finance Corporation, created in 1932, lent public money to banks, railroads, and insurance companies in the hope that stability at the top would restore jobs below. Banks often used the loans to repair their own balance sheets and did not lend. In July 1932 the Emergency Relief and Construction Act let the corporation lend to states for relief, as loans rather than grants.

5. In the summer of 1932 the Bonus Army of veterans camped in Washington asking early payment of a bonus voted in 1924 and due in 1945. On July 28 federal troops drove the remaining veterans out and burned the Anacostia camp, and photographs of the burning camp reached voters across the country.

6. Roosevelt won the election of 1932 with about 57 percent of the popular vote and forty-two states. Through the interregnum winter the banking system closed state by state, and by inauguration morning in March 1933 it had effectively shut down, while about one worker in four stood out of work.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the lesson question and judges the program by its design and its scale, not by the president's intentions or character. (Blueprint 3 and Blueprint 4)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank items 1 through 3 support Hoover's program as designed and enacted: voluntarism and rugged individualism, the Farm Board and public works, and the Hawley-Smoot Tariff. (Blueprint 3) Bank items 4 through 6 support the limits: lending that stopped at the top, the Bonus Army, the election verdict, and the interregnum banking collapse. (Blueprint 4) A full credit essay draws on both groups.
  • A fair statement of the record. An essay that calls Hoover inactive without reckoning with the Farm Board, the public works expansion, and the RFC has repeated the caricature the lesson corrects, and should earn partial credit however strong its limits argument is.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive link is the order of help: the program steadied institutions and encouraged local relief while the need sat with families whose wages, deposits, and local funds were already gone.

Model opening for teacher reference. Hoover did more than the caricature allows, and what he did was not enough, and both halves of that sentence are the history. He organized the country's relief around voluntarism, lent to farm cooperatives through the Farm Board, expanded public works up to the great Colorado River dam, and by 1932 was lending public money through the Reconstruction Finance Corporation to banks, railroads, and insurance companies. Every instrument, though, was a loan, a pledge, or an encouragement, and each one depended on the health of the institutions it was meant to steady. Banks used federal loans to repair their own balance sheets, charities and city funds ran out as donations fell, and the collapse the program faced had put about one worker in four out of work. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the tariff or the Bonus Army, and closes on the interregnum, when the banking system shut down state by state while two presidents shared a crisis neither would act on alone.

Common weak answers. The caricature (Hoover did nothing and let people starve) fails the record test and cannot explain why his defenders pointed to real programs. The defense brief (Hoover did a great deal and was unlucky) states the record without the limits, and cannot explain the election result or the interregnum. A chronology with no judgment lists the presidency without answering why the program fell short, which is the question asked.
