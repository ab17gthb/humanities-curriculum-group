The Human Cost of the Depression

The Depression and New Deal · Lesson 1 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: The Depression and New Deal, Lesson 1 of 8 — the opening lesson of the unit. It begins with the Depression already under way. The Twenties unit closed with the crash of 1929, and this unit takes up what the crash became. Teach it first, before the lessons on Hoover, Roosevelt and the First Hundred Days, and the rest of the New Deal, because it builds the problem the New Deal was invented to answer.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “The Human Cost of the Depression” and the guided notes, which follow the deck's order exactly — the collapse in numbers, bank failures and deflation, breadlines and charity, Hoovervilles and eviction, family strain, the Dust Bowl, and private or public, closing with The Human Cost at a Glance table. The activity is “The Cost Ledger: an unemployment series, a bank failure, a Hooverville, a migrant photograph, a family budget.” The notebook page asks students to rebuild the human cost at a glance in their own words. The quick check is “The Run and the Line: the bank run, deflation, the breadline, and the Hooverville.” The writing task is “A Public Emergency: the closing question of the reading as an essay.”

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Students need the crash of 1929 from the Twenties unit and a general sense of banks, wages, rent, and mortgages. Everything else is taught in order: the production fall and unemployment estimates, the bank run and deflation, breadlines and exhausted charity, eviction and Hoovervilles, family strain, and the Dust Bowl migration west.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.14 (analyze multiple and complex causes and effects — production, banking, prices, charity, housing, and family life weighed together as one collapse), D2.His.13 (continuity and change — earlier downturns compared with a collapse that deepened year after year), D2.Econ.2 (explain how scarcity and incentives shaped choices — doubling up, postponing marriage, riding freight trains, and driving west); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from measured collapse to lived cost), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task: argue whether the collapse was a private misfortune or a public emergency).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain how industrial production fell by nearly half, why about one worker in four was out of work in 1933, how a bank run could close a sound bank, how deflation made debts heavier, why private charity ran out in front of growing lines, what a Hooverville was, how families doubled up and postponed plans, and how drought and dust drove farm families west on Route 66.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening, question, and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; the collapse in numbers (slides 4–6) 7 minutes; bank failures and deflation (slides 7–10) 8 minutes; breadlines and charity (slides 11–12) 5 minutes; Hoovervilles and eviction (slides 13–14) 5 minutes; family strain (slides 15–16) 5 minutes; the Dust Bowl (slides 17–18) 5 minutes; private or public (slides 19–20) 5 minutes; glance, vocabulary, and the turn to work (slides 21–24) 5 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. Warn students early that the guided notes end with The Human Cost at a Glance table, which they will rebuild from the record.

Key vocabulary

Great Depression · deflation · bank run · unemployment · breadline · Hooverville · Dust Bowl

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: the title photograph filling the slide (a dust storm approaching Spearman, Texas, on April 14, 1935); the unit and lesson line “The Depression and New Deal · Lesson 1”; the title “The Human Cost of the Depression”; the standfirst — “How deep the collapse cut, and how families lived through it.”

  • Picture: A dust storm approaching Spearman, Texas, on April 14, 1935, from the National Archives and NOAA photo collections.
  • Open a unit that begins with the Depression already under way. The Twenties unit closed with the crash of 1929, and this unit takes up what the crash became: not a bad season on the stock market but a decade of collapse in banks, factories, farms, and family budgets.
  • This lesson measures that collapse and then follows it into homes.
  • Students will work with figures first, because the figures are the argument. Production cut nearly in half, about one worker in four out of work, and about nine thousand banks gone are not background color.
  • They are the evidence the whole unit rests on, and the exit ticket will ask students to use them.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the question — “How deep did the Depression cut, and how did ordinary families live through it?”; below it, the unit question: “How did the New Deal change the relationship between Americans and their federal government, and what did it leave undone?”

  • [Read the lesson question aloud,] then the unit question beneath it as the frame for the whole unit.
  • The lesson question has two halves, and students should hear both. The first half asks for depth, which means numbers stated accurately and labeled honestly as estimates where the record is an estimate.
  • The second half asks how families lived through it, which means the human record: breadlines, evictions, doubled up households, freight trains, and migration west.
  • Notice that this lesson does not yet answer the unit question. It builds the problem the New Deal was invented to answer.
  • By the end of the hour students should be able to pose the closing question of the reading in their own words: whether a collapse this size was a private misfortune to be waited out, or a public emergency the government had a duty to answer.
  • The next lessons take up the men who had to answer it.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title “A collapse, measured and lived”; seven rows — A, The collapse in numbers (production nearly halved, one worker in four out); B, Bank failures and deflation (runs, lost savings, debts growing heavier); C, Breadlines and charity (free food, and funds running out); D, Hoovervilles and eviction (scrap shacks on land families did not own); E, Family strain (doubled up, postponed, riding the freights); F, The Dust Bowl (drought, dust storms, and Route 66 west); G, Private or public (the question the whole unit answers).

  • This map follows the guided notes section by section, so students can use the letters on screen to find their place in their notes all hour.
  • Sections A and B give the collapse in its economic form: falling production and mass unemployment, then bank failures and deflation working together.
  • Sections C, D, and E follow the collapse into daily life: the breadline, the eviction and the Hooverville, and the rearranged family.
  • Section F moves to the southern Plains, where drought turned an economic collapse into an ecological one and sent families west.
  • Section G closes the lesson by posing the question the rest of the unit answers.
  • Tell students the summary table at the end of their guided notes, The Human Cost at a Glance, asks them to rebuild exactly this map from the record, row by row, in their own words.

Slide 4 · Section A: The Collapse in Numbers (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section A”; the title “The Collapse in Numbers”; the line — “More than three years of shrinking, measured plainly.”

  • [Set the habit for this section before any figures.] The numbers in this lesson come from the records of the period, and the government of the early 1930s counted some of them loosely.
  • Where the reading gives a figure as an estimate, students should repeat it as an estimate. That caution does not weaken the story. It strengthens it, because the scale is not in doubt even where the last digit is.
  • The Great Depression, in the guided notes definition, is the severe economic collapse that began in the United States in 1929 and lasted through the 1930s, marked by bank failures, mass unemployment, and falling prices.
  • Hold that definition in view. This section covers the first two marks, falling output and mass unemployment.
  • The bank failures belong to the next section, and the falling prices run through both.

Slide 5 · Production cut nearly in half

On the slide: five bullets — Industrial production fell by nearly half between 1929 and 1932. Steel mills ran at a small fraction of capacity. Railroads carried far less freight. The downturn deepened year after year. It did not end in a season, as earlier downturns had.

  • Give students the production figure first and let it sit. Industrial production, the output of the nation's factories and mines, had fallen by nearly half between 1929 and 1932.
  • A fall of that size means the economy was not running slowly. It was running at about half its former size.
  • Steel mills ran at a small fraction of their capacity, and railroads carried far less freight, which is how a factory figure becomes a national figure: mills that make less steel order less coal, railroads that carry less freight employ fewer crews, and each cut pulls the next one behind it.
  • Earlier downturns in American history had ended in a season or a year. This one deepened year after year, which is the plainest reason contemporaries stopped calling it a depression in the older, smaller sense and began treating it as something new.
  • Students should write the production fall and its dates into section A of the notes.

Slide 6 · One worker in four

On the slide: the big figure “1 in 4”; the label — “workers out of work in 1933, by contemporary estimate”; the note — “About 25 percent unemployment at the depth of the collapse · In industrial cities, the share ran higher.”

  • 1 in 4 — workers out of work in 1933, by contemporary estimate
  • About 25 percent unemployment at the depth of the collapse
  • In industrial cities, the share ran higher
  • Dwell on what this figure counts, because the guided notes test the definition. Unemployment is the condition of people who are able to work and seeking work but cannot find a job.
  • Contemporary estimates, counted loosely by the standards of a later age, put about one worker in four out of work in 1933.
  • The estimates do not count the farmer whose crop would not sell, the worker cut to half hours, or the man who had stopped looking, so the loss of income in the country was larger than the figure on this slide.
  • In industrial cities the share ran higher than one in four. Behind each figure stood a wage that had stopped, and behind the stopped wage stood rent, food, and children.
  • Ask: Say that sentence in your own words, once, before moving on. (The rest of the lesson is that sentence worked out in detail.)

Slide 7 · Section B: Bank Failures and Deflation (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (a crowd outside a Michigan bank, February 1933); the section label “Section B”; the title “Bank Failures and Deflation”; the lines — “A crowd outside a Michigan bank, February 1933.”

  • Picture: A crowd outside a bank during the Michigan banking crisis of February 1933. The photographer is not credited in the holding record, which students should hear as an honest limit on what the record says.
  • What the crowd is doing is the subject of this section. These are not customers in the ordinary sense. They are depositors who fear their bank is about to fail, gathered to take their money out before the doors close.
  • Section B covers the two mechanisms that turned a market collapse into a decade of ruin for families who never owned a share of stock: the bank run, which destroyed savings directly, and deflation, which quietly raised the weight of every debt in the country.
  • Both mechanisms are in the vocabulary list, and both are tested, so students should have pencils ready for definitions as well as figures.

Slide 8 · A run could close a sound bank

On the slide: the photograph at right (a crowd outside a Michigan bank, February 1933); five bullets — A rumor said the bank held loans that could not be repaid. Depositors gathered to withdraw all at the same time. No bank kept all its deposits in cash. Most deposits were out on loan. The doors closed, and deposits were frozen.

  • Picture: The Michigan bank run photograph from the divider, in February 1933. Depositors gathered outside a bank they feared would fail.
  • In the guided notes definition, a bank run is a rush of depositors to withdraw their money from a bank at the same time, driven by fear that the bank will fail.
  • Walk the mechanism slowly, because students often reverse the cause. The run did not begin because the bank had already failed. It began with a fear or a rumor, and the fear itself could do the work.
  • No bank kept all its deposits in cash, because most deposits were out on loan to farmers, merchants, and home buyers, which is what a bank is for.
  • A bank therefore could not pay every depositor on the same morning even if every loan it held was good. When the cash ran out, the doors closed, deposits were frozen, and most frozen deposits in failed banks were never fully repaid.
  • About nine thousand banks failed in the decade, which is the next slide's figure.

Slide 9 · 9,000 banks failed

On the slide: the big figure “9,000”; the label — “banks failed in the Depression decade”; the note — “No federal insurance protected deposits · Families lost savings built over years.”

  • 9,000 — banks failed in the Depression decade
  • No federal insurance protected deposits
  • Families lost savings built over years
  • Put the figure together with the mechanism from the last slide. About nine thousand banks failed in the United States during the decade.
  • Each failure was local, a named bank on a named street, and each one took deposits with it.
  • No federal insurance protected depositors, so a family that had saved the price of a farm, a store, or a year of schooling could lose the whole of it in one afternoon without having borrowed anything or speculated in anything.
  • That last point does real work in the closing argument, so students should mark it. The banking collapse did not punish only the reckless. It reached careful savers through a system failure, and it destroyed the local supply of credit at the same time, because a closed bank makes no new loans to the farmers and merchants of its town.
  • Both losses belong in section B of the notes.

Slide 10 · Debts that grew heavier as prices fell

On the slide: five bullets — Prices fell across the economy, year after year. Wheat and cotton could return less than the cost of growing them. The dollars owed stayed the same. Each payment took a larger share of a smaller income. Waiting to spend paid, so buyers waited.

  • In the guided notes definition, deflation is a general fall in prices across the economy, which raises the real burden of debts and rewards waiting to spend.
  • Students tend to hear falling prices as good news, so work the farm example until the mechanism is plain. A farmer who borrowed at the high prices of the 1920s sold his crop at the low prices of the 1930s and owed the same number of dollars.
  • Wheat and cotton sometimes returned less than the cost of growing them. The debt did not change on paper, and it grew heavier every year in the only terms that counted, bushels and bales.
  • The second half of the definition belongs in the notes as well: when prices will be lower next month, a buyer who can wait has reason to wait, and waiting cuts demand, which cuts jobs, which cuts prices again.
  • Deflation is a spiral, not a discount.

Slide 11 · Section C: Breadlines and Charity (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (unemployed men outside a Chicago soup kitchen, February 1931); the section label “Section C”; the title “Breadlines and Charity”; the lines — “Unemployed men outside a Chicago soup kitchen, February 1931.”

  • Picture: A National Archives record, number 541927, showing unemployed men queued outside a depression soup kitchen opened in Chicago, in February 1931.
  • Note its date. This is early in the collapse, before the worst of the bank failures, and already the line reaches down the sidewalk.
  • Section C covers hunger as a public sight in the richest country in the world, and it covers the machinery that answered hunger in 1931: private charity, churches, missions, and city agencies.
  • Students should watch for the turn inside the section. Charity was not idle and it was not stingy. It raised money, opened kitchens, and fed thousands of people a day.
  • It failed anyway, because donations fell with incomes while the lines grew, and that failure is one of the load bearing facts in the closing question.

Slide 12 · Free food, and funds running out

On the slide: the photograph at right (unemployed men outside a Chicago soup kitchen, February 1931); five bullets — A line of people waited for free food. The line was run by a charity, a church, or a city agency. Soup kitchens fed thousands a day. Donations fell as incomes fell. Relief funds ran out in front of growing lines.

  • Picture: The Chicago soup kitchen line from the divider, February 1931, National Archives record 541927.
  • In the guided notes definition, a breadline is a line of people waiting for free food given out by a charity, a church, or a city agency.
  • Lines like the one in the photograph stretched along sidewalks in every large city, and the men in them were often men who had held skilled jobs a year or two earlier.
  • Name the system that fed them accurately: it was private and local, built from donations, church kitchens, missions, and city relief funds.
  • Then name its limit, which the reading states in one sentence students should copy. By 1931 and 1932 donations had fallen with incomes, and many charities and city relief funds were running out of money in front of lines that kept growing.
  • A relief system funded by the incomes of the community fails exactly when the community's incomes fail. That sentence explains much of what follows in this unit, from the limits of voluntarism in the next lesson to the federal relief programs after it.

Slide 13 · Section D: Hoovervilles and Eviction (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (scrap shacks on vacant land, Portland, Oregon, 1936); the section label “Section D”; the title “Hoovervilles and Eviction”; the lines — “Scrap shacks on vacant land, Portland, Oregon, 1936.”

  • Picture: A Hooverville in Portland, Oregon, in July 1936, by Arthur Rothstein, working for the federal Farm Security Administration.
  • The date deserves a sentence: this camp was still standing, and still being added to, more than six years after the crash.
  • Section D covers the loss of homes in its two forms. The first is legal and quick: eviction for unpaid rent and foreclosure for an unpaid mortgage.
  • The second is what families built afterward, on vacant land at the edges of cities, from whatever the city discarded.
  • The guided notes ask students to name the building materials and the president whose name the camps carried, so teach both as facts of the record, not as color.
  • A shantytown that lasts for years is a housing system, and students should be ready to describe it as one.

Slide 14 · Out of the apartment, onto vacant land

On the slide: the photograph at right (a Hooverville in Portland, Oregon, 1936); five bullets — Families faced eviction for rent unpaid and foreclosure on the mortgage. Shacks were built of scrap wood, tin, packing boxes, and cardboard. The largest camps held hundreds of families. The camps had no running water, no sewers, and no legal claim to the ground. The camps were named in bitter reference to President Hoover.

  • Picture: Rothstein's Hooverville photograph from the divider, Portland, Oregon, July 1936.
  • In the guided notes definition, a Hooverville is a shantytown built by homeless people from scrap wood, tin, and cardboard during the Depression, named in bitter reference to President Hoover.
  • The name was an argument addressed to the president and to voters: the man under whom the collapse deepened owned its most visible shame.
  • The camps themselves were more organized than the word shantytown suggests. Residents laid out streets between the shacks and in the largest camps chose informal mayors.
  • What no organization could supply was infrastructure and legal standing. The camps had no running water and no sewers, and the families had no legal claim to the ground beneath them, so a city could clear a camp whenever it chose.
  • Students should connect this section backward: the road to the Hooverville usually ran through a stopped wage, an emptied relief fund, and an eviction notice, in that order.

Slide 15 · Section E: Family Strain (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section E”; the title “Family Strain”; the line — “The collapse rearranged who lived where, who worked, and who waited.”

  • This section has no single photograph and no single figure, and that is worth saying to students, because the strain it covers was recorded in smaller records: marriages postponed, births postponed, households doubled, and teenagers gone from the breakfast table.
  • The family was the last relief system standing after wages, savings, and charity had failed, and families stretched themselves to absorb relatives and to feed extra mouths on smaller incomes.
  • They also broke under the load in predictable ways. Men who had lost jobs rode freight trains looking for work, and hundreds of thousands of teenagers left home the same way, some hunting work and some sparing their families one more mouth to feed.
  • Teach the section as a set of household decisions, each one rational on its own terms and each one a cost.
  • The guided notes ask for the decisions and their causes together.

Slide 16 · Doubled up, postponed, and riding the freights

On the slide: five bullets — Families doubled up, taking in relatives. Marriages were postponed, and the birth rate fell. Men rode freight trains looking for work. Hundreds of thousands of teenagers left home. Women became the earners families depended on.

  • Work through the list as causes and effects, because the exit ticket tests them in pairs.
  • Families doubled up in apartments and houses, taking in relatives who had lost their own homes, which is the Hooverville's alternative and the reason the camps did not hold everyone who lost housing.
  • Young couples postponed marriage because they could not set up a household, and the birth rate fell as couples postponed children, so the Depression is visible in the census as well as in the breadline.
  • Men rode freight trains from town to town looking for work, and hundreds of thousands of teenagers left home the same way, some seeking work and some sparing their families one more mouth to feed.
  • Women who found work in laundries, kitchens, and offices often became the earners their families depended on, even as many employers refused to hire married women.
  • Each line on this slide is a plan a family gave up or remade.

Slide 17 · Section F: The Dust Bowl (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (dust over the Texas Panhandle, March 1936); the section label “Section F”; the title “The Dust Bowl”; the lines — “Dust over the Texas Panhandle, photographed in March 1936.”

  • Picture: Dust over the Texas Panhandle in March 1936, by Arthur Rothstein for the Farm Security Administration, from the Library of Congress FSA file.
  • Section F moves the lesson from the cities to the southern Great Plains, where the collapse met a second disaster that no banker had caused and no bank failure could explain.
  • Drought settled over the region in the early 1930s, and farming practices that had broken the prairie sod left the topsoil loose. Wind did the rest.
  • Students should hold the two disasters apart in their notes, because the reading does: the farm economy had already collapsed on prices before the rain stopped, and the dust then destroyed the land itself.
  • Families who lost both a market and a farm had one asset left, a car or a truck, and one road west.

Slide 18 · The fields blew away, and families drove west

On the slide: the photograph at right (a farmer and his sons walking in the face of a dust storm, Cimarron County, Oklahoma, April 1936); five bullets — Drought settled over the southern Plains. Wind carried dry topsoil away in storms. Storms darkened the sky at noon. Families loaded cars and trucks and drove west. Families followed Route 66 toward California, hoping for field work.

  • Picture: A farmer and his sons walking in the face of a dust storm in Cimarron County, Oklahoma, in April 1936, by Arthur Rothstein for the Farm Security Administration.
  • In the guided notes definition, the Dust Bowl is the region of the southern Great Plains where drought and poor farming practices turned topsoil to dust in the 1930s, driving many farm families from their land.
  • Name the states in the reading's account: parts of Texas, Oklahoma, Kansas, Colorado, and New Mexico.
  • Farm families who had already watched prices collapse now watched their fields blow away, and many drove west on Route 66 toward California hoping for work picking fruit and vegetables.
  • What many found was crowded camps, low wages, and hostility from the communities they entered, which the activity takes up through the most famous FSA photograph of all, Dorothea Lange's Migrant Mother, made at Nipomo, California, in March 1936.

Slide 19 · Section G: A Private Misfortune or a Public Emergency (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section G”; the title “A Private Misfortune or a Public Emergency”; the line — “The question the whole unit follows.”

  • The lesson closes where the reading closes, with a judgment students now have the evidence to make.
  • [Set the two answers side by side and refuse, for one more slide, to settle it.] On one account, hard times were a private misfortune: families endured, neighbors helped, charities fed, and better times eventually returned, as they had after earlier downturns.
  • On the other account, a collapse that closed banks, emptied factories, and drove families from homes and land in every state at once was a public emergency, of a size that private endurance could not answer.
  • Students have watched the private machinery work and fail in this lesson: savings destroyed by a system failure, charity funds emptied in front of growing lines, families stretched to doubling and still breaking.
  • The writing task will make them argue the question in full, with the figures and the photographs as evidence.

Slide 20 · A failure of the whole system at once

On the slide: four bullets — It reached savers, steady workers, and prudent borrowers. Banks, factories, farms, and homes failed together. Private charity ran out of money in front of growing lines. The next lesson takes up the president who had to answer it first.

  • State the reading's closing claim plainly, because the exit ticket and the writing task both build on it.
  • A collapse that closed banks, emptied factories, drove one worker in four from a job, and pushed families out of their homes and off their land was not a run of private bad luck.
  • It was a failure of the whole economic system at once, reaching people who had saved carefully, worked steadily, and borrowed prudently.
  • That is why the question at the end of the reading is a real question and not a slogan. If the failure was systemic, then a remedy at the scale of the system was at least arguable, and the argument itself is the subject of this unit.
  • The next lesson begins with President Hoover, the man who had to answer the question first, with his own beliefs about what the federal government should and should not do.
  • Students should leave today able to state his problem fairly, in the figures from this lesson.

Slide 21 · The human cost at a glance

On the slide: the title “Four records, one cost”; four rows — The collapse (production nearly halved; one worker in four out; wages stopped across whole cities); Banks and prices (runs, 9,000 failures, deflation; savings lost; debts grew heavier); Food and shelter (breadlines, exhausted funds, Hoovervilles; hunger in public; homes lost); Families and land (doubling up, freight trains, dust and Route 66; plans postponed; farms blown away).

  • This slide mirrors the final table in the guided notes, The Human Cost at a Glance, and students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • The four rows are the records. The collapse covers the production fall and the unemployment estimates, and what it cost was wages stopping across whole cities.
  • Banks and prices covers the runs, the nine thousand failures, and deflation, and what it cost was savings lost in an afternoon and debts growing heavier as prices fell.
  • Food and shelter covers the breadlines, the exhausted relief funds, eviction, and the Hoovervilles.
  • Families and land covers doubling up, postponed marriages and births, the freight trains, and the Dust Bowl migration west.
  • Grade the table generously on wording and strictly on pairing: each entry must say what happened and what it cost, in the student's own words.

Slide 22 · Vocabulary: words for the collapse

On the slide: four terms with their definitions — Great Depression, deflation, bank run, unemployment. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Say: “Great Depression: the severe economic collapse that began in the United States in 1929 and lasted through the 1930s, marked by bank failures, mass unemployment, and falling prices”
  • Say: “deflation: a general fall in prices across the economy, which raises the real burden of debts and rewards waiting to spend”
  • Say: “bank run: a rush of depositors to withdraw their money from a bank at the same time, driven by fear that the bank will fail”
  • Say: “unemployment: the condition of people who are able to work and seeking work but cannot find a job”
  • The three marks in the Great Depression definition are the structure: bank failures, mass unemployment, and falling prices.
  • Note that fear, not failure, starts a bank run. Both halves count in unemployment; a person who has stopped seeking work falls outside the measure, which is why the estimates understate lost income.

Slide 23 · Vocabulary: words for the human record

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — breadline, Hooverville, Dust Bowl. These definitions match the guided notes word for word.

  • Say: “breadline: a line of people waiting for free food given out by a charity, a church, or a city agency”
  • Say: “Hooverville: a shantytown built by homeless people from scrap wood, tin, and cardboard during the Depression, named in bitter reference to President Hoover”
  • Say: “Dust Bowl: the region of the southern Great Plains where drought and poor farming practices turned topsoil to dust in the 1930s, driving many farm families from their land”
  • The breadline definition names the three givers because relief in 1931 was private and local, and its limits are the argument.
  • The materials list belongs in the Hooverville definition, because scrap construction is what made the camps clearable and uninsurable as homes.
  • Note the double cause in the Dust Bowl definition, drought and poor farming practices together, because students who blame the weather alone miss half of it.
