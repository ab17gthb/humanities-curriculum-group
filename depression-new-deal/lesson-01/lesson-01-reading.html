The Human Cost of the Depression

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

The Collapse in Numbers

By the winter of 1932 to 1933, the American economy had been shrinking for more than three years. The Great Depression, the severe economic collapse that began in the United States in 1929 and lasted through the 1930s, marked by bank failures, mass unemployment, and falling prices, did not end in a season, as earlier downturns had. Industrial production, the output of the nation's factories and mines, had fallen by nearly half between 1929 and 1932. Steel mills ran at a small fraction of their capacity, and railroads carried far less freight.

Unemployment, the condition of people who are able to work and seeking work but cannot find a job, rose past every figure the country had known. Contemporary estimates, counted loosely by the standards of a later age, put about one worker in four out of work in 1933. In industrial cities the share ran higher. Behind each figure stood a wage that had stopped, and behind the stopped wage stood rent, food, and children.

Bank Failures and Deflation

The banking system failed alongside the factories. When depositors feared that a bank held bad loans, a bank run, a rush of depositors to withdraw their money from a bank at the same time, driven by fear that the bank will fail, could begin within an hour. No bank kept all its deposits in cash, so a run could close a sound bank as well as a weak one. About nine thousand banks failed in the decade, and families who had saved for years lost their deposits entirely, because no federal insurance protected them.

Falling prices made every debt heavier. Deflation, a general fall in prices across the economy, which raises the real burden of debts and rewards waiting to spend, cut farm prices so low that wheat and cotton sometimes returned less than the cost of growing them. A farmer who had borrowed at high prices in the 1920s now sold his crop at low prices in the 1930s and owed the same number of dollars. Buyers who could wait had reason to wait, and waiting cut demand still further.

Breadlines and Charity

Hunger became a public sight in the richest country in the world. A breadline, a line of people waiting for free food given out by a charity, a church, or a city agency, stretched along sidewalks in every large city. Soup kitchens run by churches, missions, charities, and city agencies fed thousands of people a day. By 1931 and 1932, however, donations had fallen with incomes, and many charities and city relief funds were running out of money in front of lines that kept growing.

Hoovervilles and Eviction

Families who lost wages often lost their homes next. Landlords evicted tenants who could not pay rent, and banks foreclosed on homeowners who could not meet a mortgage. On vacant land at the edges of cities, homeless families built shacks from scrap wood, tin, packing boxes, and cardboard. These shantytowns were called Hoovervilles, a shantytown built by homeless people from scrap wood, tin, and cardboard during the Depression, named in bitter reference to President Hoover. Large Hoovervilles held hundreds of families, with no running water, no sewers, and no legal claim to the ground beneath them.

Family Strain

The collapse rearranged family life. Families doubled up in apartments and houses, taking in relatives who had lost their own homes. Young couples postponed marriage because they could not set up a household, and the birth rate fell as couples postponed children. Men who had lost jobs rode freight trains from town to town looking for work, and hundreds of thousands of teenagers left home the same way, some seeking work and some sparing their families one more mouth to feed. Women who found work in laundries, kitchens, and offices often became the earners their families depended on, even as many employers refused to hire married women.

The Dust Bowl

On the southern Great Plains, nature completed what the economy had begun. The Dust Bowl, the region of the southern Great Plains where drought and poor farming practices turned topsoil to dust in the 1930s, driving many farm families from their land, stretched across parts of Texas, Oklahoma, Kansas, Colorado, and New Mexico. Drought settled over the region in the early 1930s, and wind carried the dry topsoil away in storms that darkened the sky at noon. Farm families who had already watched prices collapse now watched their fields blow away. Many loaded their belongings into cars and trucks and drove west on Route 66 toward California, where they hoped for work picking fruit and vegetables, and often found crowded camps and low wages instead.

A Private Misfortune or a Public Emergency

A collapse that closed banks, emptied factories, drove one worker in four from a job, and pushed families out of their homes and off their land was not a run of private bad luck. It was a failure of the whole economic system at once, reaching people who had saved carefully, worked steadily, and borrowed prudently. That scale posed the question this unit follows from lesson to lesson. Was a collapse this size a private misfortune, to be endured by each family until better times returned, or a public emergency that the government had a duty to answer? The next lesson begins with the president who had to answer it first.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Use two figures from the reading to show the depth of the collapse, and explain what each figure measured. Industrial production had fallen by nearly half between 1929 and 1932, measuring the output of factories and mines. Contemporary estimates put about one worker in four out of work in 1933, measuring unemployment among people able to work and seeking work. The bank figure, about nine thousand bank failures in the decade, is also full credit. Each answer must say what the figure measured, not only repeat the number.

2. Explain how a bank run could close a bank, and why deflation made debts harder to pay. A bank run is a rush of depositors withdrawing at the same time from fear the bank will fail. No bank kept all deposits in cash, because most deposits were out on loan, so a run could force even a sound bank to close before it could collect its loans. Deflation is a general fall in prices. Wages and crop prices fell while the dollars owed stayed the same, so each payment demanded a larger share of a smaller income, and the real burden of the debt rose.

3. Give two reasons why charity could not keep up with the need by 1931 and 1932. Donations fell as incomes fell, so charities took in less money at the very moment demand rose. City relief funds and charity budgets were exhausted in front of lines that kept growing, because the need covered millions of families across the whole country for years, not a single town for a single winter. Accept either reason stated accurately, plus the scale point that unemployment reached about one worker in four.

4. Name three ways the Depression changed family life, with the economic cause behind each. Any three, with causes. Families doubled up after eviction or foreclosure took their homes. Couples postponed marriage and children because wages had stopped and a new household could not be supported. Men and teenagers rode freight trains because no work existed at home. Women became family earners in laundries, kitchens, and offices because a husband's wage had disappeared, even where employers refused married women.

5. Was a collapse this size a private misfortune or a public emergency? Full credit answers argue from scale. The strongest answers note that the collapse reached people who had saved carefully, worked steadily, and borrowed prudently, that it closed about nine thousand banks, cut production nearly in half, put about one worker in four out of work, and drove families from homes and land, which no private charity or family budget could answer. Answers that argue the private side must still reckon with the figures. A paragraph that asserts an opinion without three facts from the reading earns partial credit.
