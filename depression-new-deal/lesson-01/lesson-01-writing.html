Writing Task: A Public Emergency

The prompt. Answer the closing question of the lesson reading in a developed essay of about 300 words: Was the Depression a private misfortune to be endured by each family, or a public emergency the government had a duty to answer? A strong answer does more than state a side. It measures the collapse first, with figures, and then shows what happened to families, and it explains why private charity and family budgets could or could not carry a collapse of that size. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. Industrial production fell by nearly half between 1929 and 1932. Contemporary estimates put about one worker in four out of work in 1933, and the government of the day counted unemployment loosely, so the figure is an estimate.

2. About nine thousand banks failed during the decade. No bank kept all its deposits in cash, so a bank run could close a sound bank, and no federal insurance protected deposits. Families lost savings they had built over years in a single afternoon.

3. Deflation cut farm prices so low that wheat and cotton sometimes returned less than the cost of growing them, while the dollars a farmer owed stayed the same.

4. Breadlines and soup kitchens run by charities, churches, and city agencies fed thousands of people a day. By 1931 and 1932 donations had fallen with incomes, and relief funds were running out in front of lines that kept growing.

5. Eviction and foreclosure pushed families into Hoovervilles, shantytowns built from scrap wood, tin, and cardboard, named in bitter reference to President Hoover, with no running water and no sewers.

6. Families doubled up, postponed marriage and children, and sent men and teenagers onto freight trains. On the southern Great Plains, drought turned topsoil to dust, and farm families drove west on Route 66 toward California.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the question directly and rests on the scale of the collapse, not on sympathy alone. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank items 1 through 3 support the depth of the collapse: the production fall, the unemployment estimate, the bank failures, and deflation. (Blueprint 1) Bank items 4 through 6 support the human cost and the failure of private relief: the breadlines and exhausted charity funds, the Hoovervilles, the rearranged families, and the Dust Bowl migration. (Blueprint 2) A full credit essay draws on both groups.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive link is capacity: a collapse covering millions of families in every state at once exceeded what donations, city budgets, and family savings could carry, which the exhausted relief funds of 1931 and 1932 demonstrate in the record rather than in the abstract.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest argument on the other side, such as the claim that relief was a local and family duty, answered from the figures rather than dismissed.

Model opening for teacher reference. The Depression was a public emergency because its causes sat above the level of any family. Industrial production fell by nearly half in three years, about nine thousand banks failed, and a depositor could lose a lifetime of savings in an afternoon at a bank that had made no mistake of its own, because no bank kept all its deposits in cash and no federal insurance stood behind them. A misfortune that reaches careful savers, steady workers, and prudent borrowers at the same time, in every state, is not private in any useful sense. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the breadline and Hooverville evidence and the exhausted charity funds, answering the local duty argument from the figures, and closing on the scale of what the collapse required.

Common weak answers. Sympathy only (people suffered terribly) describes the cost without measuring it or addressing who could answer it. Figures only (production fell, banks failed) measures the collapse without the human record or the capacity argument. A claim with no other side stated earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a judgment between two positions, not a description of one.
