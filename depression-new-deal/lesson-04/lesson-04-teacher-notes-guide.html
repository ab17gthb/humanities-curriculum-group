Relief, Recovery, and the Alphabet Agencies at Work

The Depression and New Deal · Lesson 4 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: The Depression and New Deal, Lesson 4 of 8. It follows Lesson 3, which established the banking rescue and the Hundred Days season, and it follows those signatures home into people's lives. Teach it fourth, before Lesson 5 takes up the critics of 1935, because it supplies the record — what each agency did, and how far its help reached — that the critics argue over from both sides.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “Relief, Recovery, and the Alphabet Agencies at Work” and the guided notes, which follow the deck's order exactly — two kinds of relief, Hopkins and the FERA, the CCC camps, the CWA winter, the PWA builds, the NRA codes, and measuring the reach, closing with the agencies at a glance table. The activity is “Agency Case Files: five Americans matched to the agency built for each case.” The notebook page asks students to rebuild the agencies chart, kind of relief and who was missed. The quick check is “Relief at Work: the distinction, the CCC check, and the 1934 measure.” The writing task is “How Far Did Help Reach: the lesson question as an essay.”

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Students need Lesson 1 — the scale of the collapse — Lesson 2 — Hoover's voluntarism and lending at the top — and Lesson 3 — the Hundred Days laws signed at speed, including the industry codes and relief money moving through the states. Everything else is taught in order: the direct relief / work relief distinction, the FERA, CCC, CWA, PWA, and NRA designs, and the gaps and unemployment measure of 1934.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.14 (analyze multiple and complex causes and effects — need, design, and reach weighed agency by agency), D2.His.16 (assess options for individual and collective action — direct relief against work relief as competing answers to the same emergency), D2.Econ.13 (explain how public policies affect markets — codes setting prices, wages, and hours, and construction funding through private contractors); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from the relief distinction to the agency survey to the honest measure), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task: the ledger judgment, reach and limits held in one argument).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to distinguish direct relief from work relief, explain how the FERA sent federal money through the states under Harry Hopkins from May 1933, what the CCC paid its quarter million young men and how the thirty-dollar wage was split, why the CWA hired about four million people for one winter and then ended, how the PWA under Harold Ickes funded large construction built by private contractors, how the NRA's codes of fair competition and the Blue Eagle worked and what they could not do, and why, after all of it, about one worker in five was still jobless in 1934, with camps segregated, relief uneven, and women reached narrowly.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening, question, and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; two kinds of relief (slides 4–5) 5 minutes; Hopkins and the FERA (slides 6–7) 5 minutes; the CCC camps (slides 8–10) 8 minutes; the CWA winter (slides 11–13) 7 minutes; the PWA builds (slides 14–15) 5 minutes; the NRA codes (slides 16–18) 7 minutes; measuring the reach (slides 19–21) 5 minutes; glance, vocabulary, synthesis, and the turn to work (slides 22–26) 3 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. Tell students at the start to write one distinction at the top of their notes — direct relief against work relief — because every agency in the lesson stands on one side of that line or works both sides of it.

Key vocabulary

direct relief · work relief · Civilian Conservation Corps · Federal Emergency Relief Administration · Civil Works Administration · Public Works Administration · National Recovery Administration

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

What the New Deal did at the kitchen table.”

  • Picture: Lunchtime at CCC Camp TVA #22, on the hill above Fall Creek, south of the Clinch River, near Esco, Tennessee, photographed by Lewis Hine on November 17, 1933, a National Archives record marked public domain as a work of the United States federal government. It shows a camp meal, not a relief office; caption it honestly.
  • Open by moving the unit down from the Capitol to the kitchen table.
  • Lesson 3 ended with laws signed at speed in the Hundred Days.
  • This lesson follows those signatures home: into a relief office deciding a family's grocery order, into a camp where a young man's pay envelope is split before he sees it, onto a winter work crew, and onto a bridge site that will not finish for years.
  • The photographs in this deck come from the agencies' own records: camp tables, a tree going into the ground, a dam face, and an NRA parade.
  • One distinction organizes everything that follows, and students should write it at the top of their notes now: direct relief against work relief, help given against help earned.
  • Every agency in the lesson stands on one side of that line or works both sides of it.
  • The closing argument of the lesson, about how far help reached, is graded agency by agency along that line.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the question — “What did the New Deal agencies actually do in people's lives, and how far did their help reach?”; below it, the unit question: “How did the New Deal change the relationship between Americans and their federal government, and what did it leave undone?”

  • Read the lesson question aloud and point at its two halves, because the exit ticket and the writing task both grade the two halves separately.
  • The first half is concrete: what did the agencies actually do, in named lives, with dollar figures attached.
  • A grocery order is an answer.
  • A monthly check of twenty five dollars sent home is an answer.
  • A speech about recovery is not.
  • The second half is a measurement: how far did the help reach, against the size of the need.
  • That measurement must be honest in both directions.
  • Millions helped is a fact.
  • One worker in five still jobless in 1934 is also a fact, and an answer that carries only one of them has not answered the question this lesson asks.
  • Connect the question to the unit question beneath it.
  • This lesson supplies the unit's first evidence about what the New Deal left undone: the gaps in delivery, the groups outside the designs, and the unemployment that no agency of 1933 cured.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title “Two kinds of relief, five agencies, one honest measure”; seven rows — A, Two Kinds of Relief (direct help against paid work, the lesson spine); B, Hopkins and the FERA (federal money through the states, from May 1933); C, The CCC Camps (a quarter million young men, thirty dollars a month); D, The CWA Winter (four million hired for the winter of 1933 to 1934); E, The PWA Builds (Ickes, large construction, carefully and slowly); F, The NRA Codes (fair competition codes and the Blue Eagle); G, Measuring the Reach (millions helped, and one in five still jobless).

  • This map follows the guided notes section by section.
  • Section A lays down the distinction the whole lesson runs on, direct relief against work relief.
  • Sections B through F then take the agencies one at a time in the order a citizen would have met them: the relief office first, then the camps, then the emergency winter jobs, then the great construction program, then the codes that governed the shop or factory a worker returned to.
  • Section G steps back and measures, and its figures should be anticipated from the start so students collect evidence for the measurement as they go.
  • Tell the class which initials they must be able to expand and explain by the end of the hour: FERA, CCC, CWA, PWA, and NRA, five agencies, five different answers to the same emergency.
  • The activity at the end of the lesson, Agency Case Files, will hand each group one American and ask which of these five doors that person knocks on, and the map on this slide is the office directory for that work.

Slide 4 · Section A: Two Kinds of Relief (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section A”; the title “Two Kinds of Relief”; the line — “Help given and help earned. Every agency in this lesson stands on one side of that line, or works both.”

  • Teach this section as the lesson's operating system, because every later argument runs on it.
  • By the spring of 1933 the country had millions of families with no income at all, and two different needs hiding inside the single word relief.
  • Some needs were immediate and bodily: food this week, rent this month, coal before the cold.
  • Other needs were about standing and habit: a man idle for two years was losing his skills, his health, and his place in his own household, and a grocery order did nothing for any of that.
  • The New Deal's relief chiefs, above all Harry Hopkins, argued about the two needs constantly.
  • The argument produced the architecture students are about to study: some agencies handing help directly to families, others building jobs so that help arrived as a wage.
  • Neither side won the argument outright, which is why the system that emerged by the winter of 1933 contains both kinds of agency running at once, sometimes in the same town, serving neighbors on the same street.

Slide 5 · Given, or earned

On the slide: five bullets — Direct relief is money, food, or goods given with no work required.; Work relief is paid jobs on public projects.; When there is no food tonight, direct relief fits.; After a year idle, a job and a wage fit.; Hopkins preferred work where work could be organized.

  • Carry both definitions exactly as the guided notes print them.
  • Direct relief is this: “direct relief: money, food, or goods given directly to people in need without work required in return”.
  • Work relief is this: “work relief: government paid jobs on public projects, given to unemployed people instead of direct relief payments”.
  • Then make the distinction do work with the reading's two cases, and ask the class to decide each one before revealing the reading's answer.
  • Ask: A family with no food tonight — which kind of relief fits, and why? It cannot wait for a project to be approved, a crew to be hired, and a first payday to arrive, so direct relief fits, and its speed is its virtue.
  • Ask: A healthy man idle for a year — which kind fits, and why? He needs the job itself, the wage he earned, the proof to his family and himself that his labor still counts, so work relief fits, and its dignity is its virtue.
  • Roosevelt and Hopkins preferred work relief where work could be organized and direct relief where it could not, and that preference, half practical and half moral, explains the designs of every agency that follows in this lesson.

Slide 6 · Section B: Hopkins and the FERA (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section B”; the title “Hopkins and the Federal Emergency Relief Administration”; the line — “Federal money, sent through the states, to families in need, starting in May 1933.”

  • Introduce the man before the agency, because the FERA cannot be understood apart from Harry Hopkins.
  • Hopkins was a social worker by training, formed in settlement houses and New York relief work, and he brought a caseworker's instincts to a national emergency: move money fast, decide family by family, and treat delay as a form of harm.
  • Congress created the Federal Emergency Relief Administration in May 1933, inside the Hundred Days, and Roosevelt put Hopkins at its head within days.
  • The design he ran was a partnership with a built in flaw, and students should see both halves clearly.
  • Federal money finally stood behind relief, which no state budget could do alone in 1933.
  • State and local offices still decided who received what, which kept the program close to the families it served and also imported every local standard, generous or mean, into a federal program.
  • Section B covers the money, the partnership, and the unevenness that followed from it.

Slide 7 · Money through the states

On the slide: five bullets — The FERA was created in May 1933 and led by Harry Hopkins.; The FERA sent federal relief money to the states.; The states passed the money on to people in need.; Washington paid part of the cost, and the states paid the rest.; The FERA delivered direct relief on a national scale.

  • State the definition exactly.
  • The Federal Emergency Relief Administration is this: “Federal Emergency Relief Administration: the 1933 agency, led by Harry Hopkins, that sent federal relief money to the states to pass on to people in need”.
  • Then follow a dollar, because the route of the money is the section.
  • Congress appropriated federal funds.
  • The FERA in Washington allotted them to state relief administrations, matching state spending rather than replacing it.
  • State and city caseworkers then decided, household by household, who received grocery orders, rent help, and cash.
  • Within weeks money was flowing to offices that had nearly run dry over the winter.
  • Two consequences deserve equal time.
  • The scale was new: for the first time the federal government accepted direct responsibility for feeding citizens who had committed no crime and lost no war.
  • The unevenness was also new in kind: because the states paid part and set the standards, the same empty pantry drew generous help in one state and very little in another, a gap section G will measure as a limit of the whole system.

Slide 8 · Section C: The CCC Camps (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (lunch time at CCC Camp TVA #22, near Esco, Tennessee); the section label “Section C”; the title “The Civilian Conservation Corps”; the line — “Young men, camps, trees, and a pay envelope split between the camp and the kitchen table at home.”

  • Picture: Lunch time at CCC Camp TVA #22, near Esco, Tennessee, photographed by Lewis Hine on November 17, 1933, a National Archives record marked public domain. The crowd around the cook tent is being served.
  • Set up the CCC as the most popular program of the early New Deal, and ask why as the section's running question.
  • The corps was created in the first weeks of the Hundred Days, before the FERA, and it joined two emergencies in one design.
  • The first emergency was idle young men, riding freight trains, gathering in hobo camps, and frightening a country that feared what a generation without work might become.
  • The second emergency was the land itself: eroded farms, cut over forests, and parks without roads or fire protection.
  • The corps took the young men to the land.
  • They would live in camps under Army discipline, work under foresters and park officials, and send most of their pay home, which turned a conservation program into a relief program for two addresses at once.
  • The popularity followed from the visibility.
  • A CCC camp was something a town could see, photograph, and point to, and the trees its boys planted are still standing in state and national parks across the country.

Slide 9 · A quarter million young men

On the slide: the photograph at right (a CCC enrollee planting a tree, circa April 1933); five bullets — About 250,000 young men enrolled within months.; They planted trees, fought fires, and built park roads and trails.; The camps were run by the Army.; Enrollment was mainly for young unmarried men.; The work was directed by foresters and park officials.

  • Picture: A CCC enrollee planting a tree, circa April 1933, a National Archives record marked public domain as a work of the United States federal government.
  • Carry the definition exactly.
  • The Civilian Conservation Corps is this: “Civilian Conservation Corps: the 1933 program that employed young men in camps to plant trees, fight fires, and build park roads and trails, paying thirty dollars a month, most of it sent home”.
  • Then give the camp day its texture from the record, because students retain programs they can picture.
  • Reveille before dawn, Army cots and Army food, a workday spent planting seedlings on eroded hillsides, digging firebreaks, stringing park trails, or building the rustic roads and shelters that park visitors still use.
  • Evenings brought classes for the many enrollees who had left school early.
  • The limits belong on this slide too, before the money slide makes the program sound complete.
  • Enrollment was mainly for young unmarried men, which closed the corps to women, to older men, and to fathers, the very groups other agencies served badly as well.
  • The corps was a superb answer for one slice of the unemployed.
  • The lesson's honesty standard requires naming the slices it never touched, and section G returns to the racial limits of enrollment as well.

Slide 10 · $30 a month: the CCC wage

On the slide: the big figure “$30 a month”; the label — “the CCC wage, with twenty five dollars sent straight home”; the note — “Relief twice over: the enrollee fed and housed in camp, the family paid at home.”

  • $30 a month — the CCC wage, with twenty five dollars sent straight home
  • Relief twice over: the enrollee fed and housed in camp, the family paid at home
  • Do the household arithmetic on this slide, because thirty dollars is the most instructive number in the lesson.
  • The enrollee kept five dollars for his own small spending.
  • Twenty five dollars traveled home every month, in a government check a mother could plan around: rent covered or nearly covered, groceries bought without the relief office, a younger brother kept in shoes and in school.
  • Multiply the check by the enrollment and the corps becomes a national income system for a quarter million families, delivered without a single caseworker interview.
  • That design explains the double description in the reading.
  • The corps was work relief for the young man, whose wage was earned on a hillside.
  • It was direct relief for his family, whose check arrived with no work required of them at all.
  • Ask: Which side of the section A line does the CCC stand on? Let the correct answer be both, with the line drawn through the middle of one pay envelope.
  • That answer is the standard the case file activity will reward.

Slide 11 · Section D: The CWA Winter (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section D”; the title “A Winter of Work with the CWA”; the line — “November 1933: winter coming, millions idle, and Hopkins hires four million people in a matter of weeks.”

  • Frame the CWA as the emergency inside the emergency.
  • By the autumn of 1933 the FERA was feeding families and the CCC had taken its quarter million young men, and still the general unemployment figures had barely moved.
  • Winter was the killing season of the Depression calendar: construction stopped, farm work ended, heating bills arrived, and families that had stretched resources since spring ran out of everything at once.
  • Hopkins proposed to meet the winter head on by hiring the unemployed directly, at wages, on any useful work a town could start at once.
  • Roosevelt approved the Civil Works Administration in November, funded by shifting money from other programs, and the hiring that followed was the fastest mass employment in American history to that date.
  • Section D covers what those millions did, why the work was deliberately small, and why the same president who created the program in November killed it the following spring, a decision students should be ready to judge rather than merely record.

Slide 12 · Four million hired for one winter

On the slide: five bullets — The CWA was created in November 1933 as an emergency program.; It hired about four million people for the winter of 1933 to 1934.; Its small projects repaired schools, built roads, and staffed offices.; Speed was the point, so wages arrived before the coldest months.; The program ended in spring 1934, over cost.

  • State the definition exactly.
  • The Civil Works Administration is this: “Civil Works Administration: the emergency program that hired about four million unemployed people for the winter of 1933 to 1934 on small public projects”.
  • Then confront the joke students may bring into the room, that CWA work meant men paid to rake leaves.
  • Leaf raking happened, on purpose, where nothing better could start in time.
  • Beside it stood school repairs, road grading, ditch digging, sewer work, and, easy to miss, the white collar projects that hired teachers, clerks, artists, and nurses, including the laid off teacher of the case file activity.
  • The program's real product was not any single project.
  • Its product was four million paydays delivered during the months when private hiring was zero.
  • Judge it by that standard and the CWA looks less like waste and more like a bridge: expensive, temporary, and built for exactly one crossing.
  • The next slide takes the size of the hiring as its subject, and the shutdown argument belongs there too.

Slide 13 · 4 million: the CWA winter

On the slide: the big figure “4 million”; the label — “people hired by the CWA for the winter of 1933 to 1934”; the note — “Then shut down in the spring of 1934, over cost and fear of a permanent federal workforce.”

  • 4 million — people hired by the CWA for the winter of 1933 to 1934
  • Then shut down in the spring of 1934, over cost and fear of a permanent federal workforce
  • Let the size of this number register against the lesson's other figures.
  • The CCC, a triumph of enrollment, held a quarter million young men.
  • The CWA hired sixteen times that number, in weeks, through state and local offices that Hopkins drove relentlessly.
  • No peacetime government had ever employed civilians at this speed.
  • Then ask the harder question the number invites: if the hiring worked, why end it?
  • Roosevelt's reasons are in the reading and should be stated fairly.
  • The cost frightened him, and the winter's bills ran far beyond early estimates.
  • He also feared creating a permanent class of federal jobholders, a fear his relief chiefs shared in principle even while they defended the winter's work.
  • The shutdown threw the same millions back onto direct relief, which is the lesson's clearest case of a program succeeding at its aim and still ending.
  • Students will meet the permanent answer to this problem in Lesson 6, when the Works Progress Administration of 1935 takes the CWA's insight and builds it to last for years instead of months.

Slide 14 · Section E: The PWA Builds Slowly (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (drillers on the construction of Hoover Dam, October 3, 1932); the section label “Section E”; the title “The Public Works Administration Builds Slowly”; the line — “Harold Ickes, careful review, honest bidding, and structures built to stand for generations.”

  • Picture: Drillers on the construction of Hoover Dam, photographed on October 3, 1932, a National Archives record marked public domain. Honest caption: this photograph is dated 1932, before the Public Works Administration was created, and it is used in this lesson to show the scale of federal dam construction work of the period. It is not a PWA project photograph; the PWA construction site photograph on the next content slide is the PWA image in this lesson.
  • Introduce the PWA as the CWA's opposite in temperament, and pair the two men in students' minds: Hopkins the sprinter, Ickes the surveyor.
  • Harold Ickes, the Secretary of the Interior, received the largest single appropriation of the Hundred Days era for public works, and he was determined that not one dollar of it would be stolen, wasted, or spent on a project that collapsed in scandal.
  • Where Hopkins hired first and organized later, Ickes reviewed first: engineering checked, bids compared, contracts audited, local sponsors examined for honesty and competence.
  • The result was a program perpetually accused of slowness by the same newspapers that praised its buildings.
  • Both judgments were earned, and the section asks students to hold them together.
  • A bridge that opens two winters late does nothing for the family hungry this winter.
  • A bridge built honestly carries traffic for a century.
  • The New Deal, characteristically, refused to choose, and ran the fast program and the slow program side by side through the same emergency.

Slide 15 · Built by contractors, built to last

On the slide: the photograph at right (a PWA construction site in Washington, D.C., circa 1933); five bullets — The PWA was created in June 1933 and led by Harold Ickes.; It funded dams, bridges, schools, and courthouses.; Its projects were built by private contractors.; The contractors hired the workers.; Careful review made the jobs arrive slowly.

  • Picture: A PWA construction site in Washington, D.C., circa 1933, a National Archives record marked public domain. The project sign in the photograph reads “P.W.A. Federal Works Agency, Public Works Administration, Recorder of Deeds, D.C. Bldg., Project No. D.C.1034F.”
  • Carry the definition exactly.
  • The Public Works Administration is this: “Public Works Administration: the 1933 agency, led by Harold Ickes, that funded large construction projects such as dams, bridges, schools, and courthouses, built by private contractors”.
  • The employment route is the detail students most often miss, and the case files test it: the PWA did not hire workers directly.
  • It funded a project, a city courthouse, a state bridge, a federal dam, and private contractors bidding for the construction hired the engineers, carpenters, and laborers.
  • A construction engineer like the Oregon case in the activity therefore knocks on the PWA's door by way of a contractor's payroll, months after appropriation, which is precisely the program's strength and weakness in one fact.
  • Ickes also insisted that PWA projects be genuinely useful and durably built, and the surviving record vindicates the standard daily: schools, hospitals, water systems, and bridges from these years remain in ordinary use.
  • Its wages were real wages, paid to some of the longest idle skilled workers in the country, and its buildings kept paying the public long after the emergency ended.

Slide 16 · Section F: The NRA Codes (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (the NRA Blue Eagle code poster, circa 1934); the section label “Section F”; the title “The National Recovery Administration and the Blue Eagle”; the line — “Codes for every industry, a blue eagle in the window, and an experiment in planning the whole economy.”

  • Picture: The NRA Blue Eagle poster, circa 1934, a National Archives record marked public domain and marked on its face as property of the United States government. The poster itself carries the year 1934 and reads “NRA Code, Retail Trade.”
  • Widen the lens for the final agency, because the NRA is not a relief agency at all, and students should notice the category change.
  • The FERA fed people.
  • The CCC, CWA, and PWA employed people.
  • The NRA tried to cure the disease the others were treating: an economy in which competition itself had turned destructive, firms cutting prices below cost, then cutting wages to survive, then watching their own customers lose the income to buy anything.
  • The remedy was coordination from Washington.
  • Each industry would write a code of fair competition, fixing minimum wages, maximum hours, and price rules, and the government would approve and enforce the codes as law.
  • Firms that signed displayed the Blue Eagle, and the administration asked the public to buy only where the eagle flew, turning shopping into a patriotic act.
  • Section F covers the codes at their height, the genuine gains they delivered, and the weaknesses already visible by 1934: complexity, favoritism toward large firms, and prices held above what the jobless could pay.

Slide 17 · Codes of fair competition

On the slide: the photograph at right (female film industry workers carrying a large American flag in a National Recovery Administration parade, New York City, 1933); five bullets — The NRA was created in 1933 under the Recovery Act.; Its codes set prices, wages, and hours by industry.; The codes allowed workers to organize.; Complying firms displayed the Blue Eagle.; Shoppers were asked to buy where the eagle flew.

  • Picture: Female film industry workers carrying a large American flag as part of a National Recovery Administration parade, New York City, in 1933, a World Telegram photograph held by the Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division, and marked public domain. The Commons version is cropped and flipped from the Library of Congress original.
  • State the definition exactly.
  • The National Recovery Administration is this: “National Recovery Administration: the agency created in 1933 to write and enforce codes of fair competition for each industry, setting prices, wages, and hours and allowing workers to organize”.
  • Then explain the code system as a citizen met it in 1933 and 1934, which was as a symbol before it was a statute.
  • The Blue Eagle appeared in shop windows, on packages, on movie screens, and in a national parade of support in New York that autumn.
  • To display it, a firm signed its industry code and the president's blanket agreement on wages and hours.
  • Child labor largely vanished from coded industries, hours shortened, and minimum wages appeared in trades that had never known them.
  • The right to organize, written into the recovery law, gave unions a federal sentence to quote to employers for the first time.
  • Students should record those gains before the criticism on the next slide, because the reading's judgment on the NRA is double, and a one sided account of the eagle, either as triumph or as folly, will fail the writing rubric.

Slide 18 · What the codes could not do

On the slide: five bullets — The codes grew complicated and hard to enforce.; Large firms often wrote codes in their own favor.; Small firms complained of paperwork and cost.; Code prices could sit above what idle buyers could pay.; The experiment reached most of American industry.

  • Weigh the experiment honestly, as contemporaries had to by the end of 1934.
  • Start with enforcement: hundreds of codes, thousands of pages of rules, and a small staff to police them meant that compliance rested heavily on publicity and pressure, which faded as the first enthusiasm cooled.
  • Move to fairness inside the system: the industries wrote their own codes in the first instance, and large firms with lawyers shaped rules that smaller competitors then had to obey, a grievance small business owners carried to Congress in volume.
  • End with the economic contradiction students should be able to state unaided.
  • Raising wages helps workers buy.
  • Raising prices by code takes the same raise back at the counter, especially for the unemployed buyer the whole system existed to rescue.
  • The NRA therefore closes the agency survey on the lesson's characteristic note: a real reach, millions of workers under codes, joined to real limits that no amount of blue eagles could paper over.
  • The Supreme Court ends the experiment in Lesson 5, unanimously, and students should arrive at that lesson knowing what exactly was struck down.

Slide 19 · Section G: Measuring the Reach (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section G”; the title “Measuring the Reach”; the line — “Millions helped and hired. Camps segregated, relief uneven, and one worker in five still jobless in 1934.”

  • Close the lesson with the measurement its question demands, and set the standard for the discussion plainly: praise and criticism both require figures.
  • By the end of 1934 the agencies of this lesson had fed families in every state, placed a quarter million young men in camps, carried four million people through a winter on wages, set a great construction program in motion, and placed most of American industry under codes.
  • A teacher who hides that record teaches cynicism, not history.
  • The same record carries its gaps in the open.
  • The camps were segregated.
  • Relief differed by state and by race.
  • The designs served young men best and reached women narrowly.
  • And the summit figure of the whole lesson stands on the next stat slide: unemployment in 1934, after two years of the largest relief effort the country had ever mounted, still stood at about one worker in five.
  • Section G teaches students to hold the whole ledger at once, which is the exact skill the unit test and the final lesson on legacy will grade.

Slide 20 · The gaps in the record

On the slide: five bullets — CCC camps were segregated by race.; Black enrollment was held far below the Black share of the jobless.; Relief differed by state, and often by race within a state.; Most agencies served men first.; Women were reached through a narrow set of jobs.

  • Name each gap with its mechanism, because vague criticism teaches nothing.
  • The CCC camps were segregated by race as a matter of administration, in a Jim Crow country whose armed services were also segregated, and Black enrollment was held to a share far below the Black share of the unemployed, so the program's famous check home arrived in Black households far less often than need required.
  • FERA relief ran through state and local offices, which meant local racial practice set federal payment levels in much of the South, and payments to Black families were commonly lower for identical need.
  • The gender gap was designed rather than local: the CCC excluded women by rule, the construction programs hired in male trades, and women's work relief was confined to a narrow band of sewing, clerical, and service projects.
  • Students should be precise about the conclusion.
  • These gaps do not erase the millions helped.
  • They define who the new federal presence reached last and least, which is the unfinished agenda the later lessons, on the Second New Deal and on legacy, take up directly.

Slide 21 · 1 in 5: still jobless in 1934

On the slide: the big figure “1 in 5”; the label — “workers still unemployed in 1934, after two years of New Deal relief”; the note — “Down from about one in four in 1933, and far above any level known before 1929.”

  • 1 in 5 — workers still unemployed in 1934, after two years of New Deal relief
  • Down from about one in four in 1933, and far above any level known before 1929
  • End the content on the honest number.
  • One worker in five unemployed in 1934 represents progress against the one in four of 1933, and students should say so: the direction was right, and the agencies of this lesson did the moving that can be measured.
  • The same figure represents the limit of everything taught today.
  • No level remotely like one in five had existed in the United States before 1929, in depressions that the country remembered as severe.
  • Families did not experience the figure as a statistic.
  • They experienced it as the fifth house on the street, the brother still riding freight, the relief order renewed again.
  • Teach the figure as the bridge to the rest of the unit.
  • It is why critics in Lesson 5 could gather millions by arguing the New Deal did too little, why Roosevelt returned in 1935 with the larger Second New Deal of Lesson 6, and why the final lesson will judge that the Depression itself was ended not by any agency in this room but by the war spending of the next decade.

Slide 22 · The agencies at a glance

On the slide: the title “Five agencies, two kinds of relief”; four rows — FERA (direct relief through the states; fed families in every state; uneven by state); CCC and CWA (work relief, camps and winter jobs; young men paid; four million through one winter); PWA (large construction by contractors; lasting buildings; jobs arrived slowly); NRA (industry codes and the Blue Eagle; wages and hours set; codes favored large firms).

  • This slide mirrors the closing table of the guided notes, and students should complete that table now, agency by agency, from memory where they can.
  • The rows are grouped by the lesson spine: direct relief first, in the FERA row; work relief next, split between the fast programs, CCC and CWA, and the slow building program, PWA; and the recovery experiment, the NRA, last, because it treated the economy rather than the household.
  • Check the table with the two questions that expose shallow answers.
  • Ask: Which row would a widowed mother with three small children appear in, and why can it be no other row? The FERA row, because camp, construction, and code all presuppose a worker who can leave home.
  • Ask: Which rows ended or were designed to end quickly, and what did ending cost? The CWA row above all, whose spring shutdown returned millions to direct relief.
  • Grade the table generously on wording and strictly on the relief distinction, because the quick check and the case files grade the same distinction in application.

Slide 23 · Vocabulary: words for relief

On the slide: four terms with their short definitions — direct relief, work relief, Civilian Conservation Corps, Federal Emergency Relief Administration. The full definitions below match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Say: “direct relief: money, food, or goods given directly to people in need without work required in return”
  • Say: “work relief: government paid jobs on public projects, given to unemployed people instead of direct relief payments”
  • Say: “Civilian Conservation Corps: the 1933 program that employed young men in camps to plant trees, fight fires, and build park roads and trails, paying thirty dollars a month, most of it sent home”
  • Say: “Federal Emergency Relief Administration: the 1933 agency, led by Harry Hopkins, that sent federal relief money to the states to pass on to people in need”
  • Four terms, matching the guided notes word for word, and students should copy the definitions because the unit test draws on them.
  • Press the pair at the top hardest, because everything else in the lesson hangs on it.
  • Students who define direct relief as government help generally, or work relief as any job, will misfile every case in the activity.
  • The definitions turn on one phrase each: without work required in return, against jobs given instead of direct relief payments.
  • The CCC definition carries its wage inside it, thirty dollars a month with most sent home.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for the agencies

On the slide: three terms with their short definitions — Civil Works Administration, Public Works Administration, National Recovery Administration. The full definitions below match the guided notes word for word.

  • Say: “Civil Works Administration: the emergency program that hired about four million unemployed people for the winter of 1933 to 1934 on small public projects”
  • Say: “Public Works Administration: the 1933 agency, led by Harold Ickes, that funded large construction projects such as dams, bridges, schools, and courthouses, built by private contractors”
  • Say: “National Recovery Administration: the agency created in 1933 to write and enforce codes of fair competition for each industry, setting prices, wages, and hours and allowing workers to organize”
  • Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly.
  • Two contrasts in these definitions earn emphasis before the exit ticket.
  • The CWA definition is the only one in the set built around a season, the winter of 1933 to 1934, and this agency was a bridge over one winter, not a settlement.
  • The PWA definition is the only one that names its leader and its method together, Harold Ickes and construction by private contractors.
  • The NRA definition ends with the right to organize, a clause students should flag, because Lesson 6 returns to it when the Wagner Act makes that right permanent law in 1935.

Slide 25 · Answering the question

On the slide: four bullets — In people's lives, the agencies delivered grocery orders, camp checks, winter wages, and bridges.; Their reach covered millions fed, hired, and put under codes within two years.; Their gaps included segregated camps, uneven relief, and women reached narrowly.; The measure is that one worker in five was still jobless in 1934.

  • Run the synthesis as a ledger with two columns, and build it from the class's own answers before summarizing.
  • The credit column is concrete and should be recited concretely: a mother's grocery order renewed by a federal dollar, a CCC check for twenty five dollars arriving monthly in a mill town kitchen, four million paydays in the worst winter of the century, an engineer back at a drafting table on a bridge that still stands, a factory hand working shorter hours under a code with a union card newly protected.
  • The debit column is equally concrete: the Black enrollee turned away from a full camp, the relief payment cut by a local office's prejudice, the teacher whose winter job ended by design in March.
  • The lesson question asks what the agencies did and how far the help reached, and the ledger is the answer form: reach measured in named lives, limits measured the same way.
  • Students who can keep both columns in one paragraph are ready for the writing task, and for Lesson 5, where the critics of 1935 argue over exactly this ledger from both sides.
