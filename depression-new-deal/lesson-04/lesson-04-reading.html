Relief, Recovery, and the Alphabet Agencies at Work

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Two Kinds of Relief

The New Deal agencies of 1933 rested on a distinction every relief worker soon learned. Direct relief meant money, food, or goods given directly to people in need without work required in return. Work relief meant government paid jobs on public projects, given to unemployed people instead of direct relief payments. The two forms answered different needs. A family with no food tonight needed direct relief at once. A healthy man idle for a year needed a job more than a handout. Roosevelt and his relief chief, Harry Hopkins, preferred work relief where work could be organized, and direct relief where it could not. The agencies in this lesson divide along that line.

B. Hopkins and the Federal Emergency Relief Administration

Congress created the Federal Emergency Relief Administration in May 1933 and placed Harry Hopkins, a former social worker from New York, at its head. The agency sent federal relief money to the states to pass on to people in need. The federal government paid part of each state relief dollar and required the states to pay the rest. Hopkins moved fast. Within weeks, money was flowing to state relief offices that had nearly run dry. FERA was direct relief on a national scale, and it kept millions of families fed and housed. State standards differed widely, so the same need could draw generous help in one state and very little in another.

C. The Civilian Conservation Corps

The Civilian Conservation Corps, created in the first weeks of the Hundred Days, took young men out of the cities and into camps in the country. Within months about a quarter million young men were enrolled. They planted trees, fought fires, controlled erosion, and built park roads and trails. Each man received thirty dollars a month, and most of it, twenty five dollars, went straight home to his family. The corps therefore worked as relief twice over. The enrollee ate, slept, and worked under discipline in the camp, and his mother received a monthly check that paid rent and bought food at home. The camps were run by the Army, and enrollment was limited mainly to young unmarried men, so the program reached one part of the unemployed and left the rest to other agencies.

D. A Winter of Work with the CWA

In November 1933, with winter coming and millions still idle, Hopkins created the Civil Works Administration as an emergency work program. The CWA hired about four million unemployed people for the winter of 1933 to 1934 on small public projects. Men and women repaired schools, built roads, and staffed offices. The point was speed. A job that began in December paid wages through the coldest months, when private hiring stopped and family budgets broke. Roosevelt ended the program in the spring of 1934 because its cost frightened him and because he feared a permanent class of federal jobholders. Work relief could be hired by the millions in a matter of weeks, but stopping it threw the same millions back onto direct relief.

E. The Public Works Administration Builds Slowly

The Public Works Administration, created in June 1933 and led by Secretary of the Interior Harold Ickes, took the opposite approach to the CWA. Ickes funded large construction projects such as dams, bridges, schools, and courthouses, built by private contractors who hired the workers. He insisted on careful review and honest bidding before he released money. Projects therefore started slowly, and critics complained that PWA money sat unspent while men stood idle. Ickes answered that a dam or a courthouse would serve the public for generations and that public money wasted on a failed project would discredit the whole New Deal. By the middle of the decade its record stood in concrete across the country. Its jobs came later than the CWA jobs, but the buildings lasted.

F. The National Recovery Administration and the Blue Eagle

The National Recovery Administration, created under the National Industrial Recovery Act in June 1933, tried to lift the whole economy at once. It wrote and enforced codes of fair competition for each industry, setting prices, wages, and hours and allowing workers to organize. Businesses that accepted their code displayed the Blue Eagle, the agency symbol, in windows and on products, and the government asked shoppers to buy only where the eagle flew. At its height the code system covered most of American industry. It raised wages and shortened hours in many trades. It also grew complicated and hard to enforce. Large firms often wrote codes that favored themselves, small firms complained of the paperwork, and prices set by code could hold goods above what idle buyers could pay.

G. Measuring the Reach

By the end of 1934 the agencies had touched millions of lives. FERA money had fed families in every state, the CCC had employed hundreds of thousands of young men, and the CWA had carried four million people through one winter. The reach was real, and so were the gaps. The CCC camps were segregated by race, and Black enrollment was held far below the Black share of the unemployed. Relief payments differed by state and often by race within a state. Most agencies served men first and reached women through a narrow set of jobs. Above all, the numbers at the top did not move enough. Unemployment in 1934 still stood at about one worker in five, far above any level the country had known before 1929. The agencies had changed what the federal government did every day. They had not yet ended the Depression.
