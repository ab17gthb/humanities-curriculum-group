Part 3: The Arguments of the Era

Emergency or misfortune. The unit's first argument is about what a depression is. Hoover treated the collapse as a misfortune to be weathered by private effort with limited federal help. Roosevelt treated it as a public emergency that licensed federal action. Every later fight in the unit, over agencies, critics, courts, and budgets, is this argument returning in a new form.

Experiment and settlement. The first New Deal of 1933 was a season of experiments, many of them temporary and some of them struck down. The second, in 1935, built permanent systems for old age, unemployment, and labor rights. Judging the New Deal means keeping the two seasons separate, because they were built for different purposes.

Who was inside the settlement. The New Deal's benefits ran through local delivery and founding exclusions, so its reach differed by race, sex, region, and occupation. The unit treats that unevenness as part of the record, not an exception to it, and the test asks about it directly.

The recovery that waited. The New Deal relieved, employed, and reformed, and the Depression still outlasted it. Full employment came with war spending. The honest verdict the unit builds toward is that the New Deal changed the country permanently and ended the Depression only in part, and both halves of that sentence are required.

Teacher Answer Key

Part 1: Key Terms

Lesson 1: The Human Cost of the Depression

Great Depressionthe severe economic collapse that began in the United States in 1929 and lasted through the 1930s, marked by bank failures, mass unemployment, and falling prices
deflationa general fall in prices across the economy, which raises the real burden of debts and rewards waiting to spend
bank runa rush of depositors to withdraw their money from a bank at the same time, driven by fear that the bank will fail
unemploymentthe condition of people who are able to work and seeking work but cannot find a job
breadlinea line of people waiting for free food given out by a charity, a church, or a city agency
Hoovervillea shantytown built by homeless people from scrap wood, tin, and cardboard during the Depression, named in bitter reference to President Hoover
Dust Bowlthe region of the southern Great Plains where drought and poor farming practices turned topsoil to dust in the 1930s, driving many farm families from their land

Lesson 2: Hoover and the Limits of Voluntarism

voluntarismHoover's policy of meeting the Depression through voluntary cooperation by business, charities, and local government rather than through federal relief programs
rugged individualismHoover's belief that Americans should succeed through their own effort and that direct federal relief would weaken character and self reliance
Reconstruction Finance Corporationthe federal agency created in 1932 that lent public money to banks, railroads, and insurance companies in the hope that stability at the top would restore jobs below
public worksgovernment funded construction projects such as dams, roads, and public buildings, built to provide jobs as well as lasting structures
Hawley-Smoot Tariffthe 1930 law that raised United States tariffs to the highest level in the country's history, and drew retaliation that cut world trade
Bonus Armythe veterans of the First World War who camped in Washington in the summer of 1932 demanding early payment of their promised service bonus, and were driven out by federal troops
interregnumthe period between Roosevelt's election in November 1932 and his inauguration in March 1933, when the outgoing and incoming presidents shared responsibility for a deepening crisis and neither acted decisively

Lesson 3: Roosevelt and the First Hundred Days

bank holidaythe closing of all the nation's banks by presidential proclamation on March 6, 1933, so that examiners could sort sound banks from failed ones before reopening
Emergency Banking Actthe law passed by Congress on March 9, 1933, that gave the president broad power over banking and allowed sound banks to reopen under federal license
fireside chatone of President Roosevelt's radio addresses to the nation, delivered in plain conversational language from the White House
Hundred Daysthe first phase of Roosevelt's presidency, from March 9 to June 16, 1933, in which Congress passed an exceptional volume of New Deal legislation
Glass-Steagall Actthe 1933 law that separated commercial banking from investment banking and created federal insurance of bank deposits
Agricultural Adjustment Actthe 1933 law that paid farmers to reduce production of basic crops and livestock in order to raise farm prices toward parity
Tennessee Valley Authoritythe federal corporation created in 1933 to build dams, control flooding, and bring electricity and development to the Tennessee River valley

Lesson 4: Relief, Recovery, and the Alphabet Agencies at Work

direct reliefmoney, food, or goods given directly to people in need without work required in return
work reliefgovernment paid jobs on public projects, given to unemployed people instead of direct relief payments
Civilian Conservation Corpsthe 1933 program that employed young men in camps to plant trees, fight fires, and build park roads and trails, paying thirty dollars a month, most of it sent home
Federal Emergency Relief Administrationthe 1933 agency, led by Harry Hopkins, that sent federal relief money to the states to pass on to people in need
Civil Works Administrationthe emergency program that hired about four million unemployed people for the winter of 1933 to 1934 on small public projects
Public Works Administrationthe 1933 agency, led by Harold Ickes, that funded large construction projects such as dams, bridges, schools, and courthouses, built by private contractors
National Recovery Administrationthe agency created in 1933 to write and enforce codes of fair competition for each industry, setting prices, wages, and hours and allowing workers to organize

Lesson 5: The Critics: Long, Townsend, Coughlin, and the Court

Share Our WealthHuey Long's program and movement, begun in 1934, which promised to cap large fortunes and guarantee every family a homestead and a minimum income
Townsend PlanDr. Francis Townsend's proposal that the federal government pay two hundred dollars a month to every citizen over sixty, on condition the money be spent within the month
old-age pensiona regular payment made by a government to people who have reached a set age and left regular work
demagoguea leader who gains power by arousing popular fears, prejudices, and hopes with simple promises, rather than by answering arguments
American Liberty Leaguethe organization founded in 1934 by wealthy businessmen and conservative Democrats to oppose the New Deal as a threat to property and the Constitution
Schechter v. United Statesthe 1935 Supreme Court decision that struck down the National Industrial Recovery Act, holding that Congress had given away its lawmaking power and reached business beyond its authority
United States v. Butlerthe 1936 Supreme Court decision that struck down the Agricultural Adjustment Act's processing tax as an unconstitutional use of federal power over farm production

Lesson 6: The Second New Deal

Works Progress Administrationthe agency created in 1935, led by Harry Hopkins, that employed about eight and a half million people over its life building roads, schools, and airports and funding arts and writing projects
Social Security Actthe law of August 1935 that created old-age insurance, unemployment insurance with the states, and aid to dependent children and other vulnerable groups
old-age insurancethe part of Social Security that pays a monthly benefit to retired workers, funded by payroll taxes on workers and their employers
unemployment insurancethe joint federal and state system, created in 1935, that pays temporary benefits to workers who lose their jobs
payroll taxa tax taken from workers' wages and matched by employers, used to fund Social Security
National Labor Relations Actthe 1935 law, called the Wagner Act, that guaranteed workers the right to organize unions and bargain collectively and created a board to enforce those rights
collective bargainingnegotiation between an employer and a union chosen by the workers over wages, hours, and working conditions

Lesson 7: Labor Rising and the Recession of 1937

collective bargaining (carried from Lesson 6)negotiation between an employer and a union chosen by the workers over wages, hours, and working conditions
sit-down strikea strike in which workers stop work and remain inside the factory, holding the plant so that replacement workers cannot be brought in
Congress of Industrial Organizationsthe federation formed in 1935 to organize workers by whole industry, including the unskilled, rather than by craft
United Auto Workersthe union of automobile workers, recognized by General Motors in February 1937 after the Flint sit-down strike
court packingthe name opponents gave Roosevelt's 1937 proposal to add a new justice to the Supreme Court for each sitting justice over seventy, which would have let him appoint up to six
Roosevelt recessionthe sharp economic downturn of 1937 to 1938, caused in part by federal spending cuts and new payroll taxes pulling money out of a weak economy
deficit spendinggovernment spending beyond the revenue it collects, financed by borrowing, used to put money into an economy in a slump

Lesson 8: What the New Deal Changed and What It Did Not

New Deal coalitionthe alliance of white southerners, northern city voters, union members, farmers, and increasingly Black voters that kept the Democratic Party in national power for a generation after 1932
Fair Labor Standards Actthe 1938 law that set a federal minimum wage, required overtime pay past forty hours a week, and restricted child labor
safety netthe set of government programs that catch people who fall into poverty through old age, unemployment, disability, or the loss of a family earner
redliningthe practice by which federal housing officials and lenders marked minority neighborhoods as risky on maps and denied loans there, keeping Black families from buying homes on equal terms
repatriationthe removal of people of Mexican descent, including many United States citizens, to Mexico during the Depression, carried out by local and federal authorities under economic pressure
Securities and Exchange Commissionthe federal agency created in 1934 to regulate the stock markets and require honest public reporting by companies selling stock
Federal Deposit Insurance Corporationthe federal agency created in 1933 that insures bank deposits, so that depositors are repaid by the government if their bank fails

Part 2: Review Questions

1. How deep did the collapse run by 1933? Give three measures. By the standard historical estimates, unemployment reached about one worker in four in 1933, industrial production fell by roughly half from 1929, and thousands of banks failed, with farm prices falling so far that many crops returned less than the cost of growing them. Any three accurate measures from the reading earn credit.

2. Why did deflation make the Depression worse for debtors? Debts stay fixed in dollars while prices and incomes fall, so each payment costs more in real terms. Farmers and homeowners owed the same mortgage on smaller earnings, which pushed foreclosures and bank losses higher in a circle.

3. Name three forms the human cost took in everyday life, and the evidence for each from Lesson 1. Breadlines and soup kitchens, as charities and cities fed lines of unemployed people. Hoovervilles, the scrap built shantytowns where homeless families lived. Dust Bowl migration, as drought and dust drove Plains farm families west. Eviction, doubled up households, and postponed marriages and births also earn credit.

4. What did voluntarism ask of business, charity, and local government, and what did it refuse? It asked business to hold wages and keep workers, charities to feed the hungry, and cities and states to run relief, all by cooperation and pledge rather than law. It refused direct federal relief payments to individuals, which Hoover held would weaken character and unbalance the budget.

5. Hoover did more than the caricature of him allows. Name three federal actions he took. Any three: the Federal Farm Board supporting farm prices, expanded public works spending, the Reconstruction Finance Corporation lending to banks and railroads in 1932, the Emergency Relief and Construction Act authorizing federal relief loans to states. The Hawley-Smoot Tariff also counts as an action, though its effects cut the other way.

6. Why did the Bonus Army episode damage Hoover beyond its size? Veterans asking early payment of a promised bonus were driven from Washington by federal troops under his command in the summer of an election year. The photographs and reports fixed the public image of a president using soldiers against hungry veterans while refusing relief.

7. How did the bank holiday and the Emergency Banking Act work as a pair? The holiday closed every bank on March 6, 1933, stopping the runs. The Act of March 9 let examiners sort the banks and licensed only sound ones to reopen, with federal lending behind them, so reopening itself became evidence of soundness.

8. What did the first fireside chat do that no law could do? It explained the banking system in plain language to a national radio audience and asked people to trust the reopened banks. Deposits flowed back in the days after, supplying the confidence the rescue depended on.

9. Name four laws of the Hundred Days and the problem each one answered. Any four: the Emergency Banking Act for the bank runs, the Agricultural Adjustment Act for farm prices, the Tennessee Valley Authority for a flooded and unpowered river region, the National Industrial Recovery Act for industrial collapse and codes, the Glass-Steagall Act for banking structure and deposit insurance. The Civilian Conservation Corps, created in the same season, answers unemployment.

10. State the difference between direct relief and work relief, and give one agency of each kind. Direct relief gives money, food, or goods to people in need with no work required; the Federal Emergency Relief Administration worked this way through the states. Work relief pays wages for jobs on public projects; the Civilian Conservation Corps, Civil Works Administration, and Public Works Administration are the lesson's examples.

11. Why was the Civil Works Administration shut down after one winter? It hired about four million people quickly in the winter of 1933 to 1934. Roosevelt ended it in the spring of 1934 because its cost frightened him and because he feared a permanent class of federal jobholders, and its workers fell back on direct relief.

12. In what ways did the agencies' help reach Americans unevenly? CCC camps were segregated, relief payments varied by state and locality and were often lower for Black families, and women were reached through a narrow set of jobs. The programs were federal in funding and local in delivery, and the local customs came with them.

13. What did Long, Townsend, and Coughlin each say the New Deal had failed to do? Long said it had left great fortunes untouched and promised through Share Our Wealth to cap them and guarantee every family a homestead and income. Townsend said it had left the old without support and promised two hundred dollars a month to every citizen over sixty. Coughlin said it had failed to break the power of bankers and money interests. All three charged the New Deal with doing too little.

14. What did the Liberty League and the Supreme Court charge from the other direction? They charged that the New Deal did too much, going beyond the Constitution. The League organized wealthy opposition. The Court struck down the National Industrial Recovery Act in Schechter in 1935 and the Agricultural Adjustment Act in Butler in 1936.

15. Why did pressure from both directions push Roosevelt toward a Second New Deal? The mass movements showed him millions of voters ready to follow more sweeping promises, and the Court showed him his first program being dismantled. Answering the first group and outlasting the second both required a larger, more durable settlement in 1935.

16. What three permanent systems did the Social Security Act set running? Old-age insurance paying retired workers from payroll taxes, unemployment insurance run jointly with the states paying temporary benefits to job losers, and aid to dependent children and other vulnerable groups. The old-age insurance system is the one still at the center of American retirement.

17. Who was left out of Social Security at the start, and why does the exclusion matter for judging the law? Agricultural and domestic workers were excluded, an exclusion that left out a large share of Black workers and many women workers. The exclusion counts in judging the law because its universality, the quality that made it durable, was partial from its first day, and the missing workers were those with the least bargaining power.

18. What did the Wagner Act guarantee, and what machinery did it create to make the guarantee real? It guaranteed workers the right to organize unions of their own choosing and to bargain collectively, and it created the National Labor Relations Board to run elections and police unfair labor practices. The board is what turned a stated right into an enforceable one.

19. Why did the sit-down strike work at Flint when ordinary strikes had failed in the auto industry? By staying inside the plant, the strikers kept replacement workers out and kept the company from running its machines with other hands, and the governor refused to clear the plant by force. After forty-four days General Motors recognized the United Auto Workers.

20. What did Roosevelt propose in February 1937, and how did the fight end? He proposed adding a new Supreme Court justice for each sitting justice over seventy, up to six new seats. The Senate defeated the plan as an attack on the Court. In the same season the Court upheld the Wagner Act and Social Security and began sustaining New Deal laws, and retirements soon let him appoint justices by the ordinary route.

21. What caused the Roosevelt recession, in the administration's own later judgment? The government pulled support away too soon. Federal spending was cut and the new Social Security payroll taxes began collecting before benefits flowed, taking money out of a weak economy, and production and employment fell sharply into 1938.

22. Name four changes from the New Deal that are still operating today. Any four: federal insurance of bank deposits through the FDIC, regulation of stock markets by the SEC, Social Security, unemployment insurance, federal minimum wage and overtime law under the Fair Labor Standards Act, the Tennessee Valley Authority's power system, and federal protection of the right to organize.

23. What is the strongest evidence that the New Deal did not end the Depression? Unemployment never fell below fourteen percent in any year of the 1930s and stood near nineteen percent again in 1938. Full employment arrived only with the enormous spending of the Second World War.

24. State the two measures by which Lesson 8 says the New Deal should be judged, and give a verdict under each. Against the collapse it answered, it was relief and reform on a scale the country had never attempted, and it kept a democratic government standing through a collapse that broke governments elsewhere. Against full recovery and equal treatment, it stopped short: the Depression outlasted it, and its benefits were written with exclusions. A verdict that uses both measures and evidence from the unit earns full credit.
