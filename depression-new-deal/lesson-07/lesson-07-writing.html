Writing Task: Two Ledgers of One Year

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Why did 1937 bring both labor's greatest victories and the New Deal's hardest year? A strong answer keeps the two ledgers separate before it joins them. It explains what workers won and how they won it, and it explains what the President lost and why he lost it, in the plants, in the Senate, and in the economy. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The CIO, formed in 1935 under John L. Lewis, organized by industry rather than by craft. Flint's sit-down strikers held the Fisher Body plants for forty-four days, from December 30, 1936, and on February 11, 1937, General Motors recognized the United Auto Workers and agreed to bargain.

2. Governor Frank Murphy sent the National Guard to Flint to keep order and refused to use it to evict the strikers. Union membership climbed from under three million in 1933 toward nine million by the end of the decade.

3. At Republic Steel in Chicago on Memorial Day, May 30, 1937, police fired on a march of strikers and their families and killed ten people. A Senate committee found the police violence unjustified.

4. On February 5, 1937, Roosevelt proposed an added justice for each Supreme Court justice over seventy who did not retire, up to six. Opponents named it court packing. The Senate killed the bill in July 1937, while the Court upheld the Wagner Act in April and Social Security in May.

5. Federal spending cuts and the new Social Security payroll taxes together pulled money out of a weak economy. Industrial production fell by about a third between the summer of 1937 and the spring of 1938, and unemployment climbed back toward nineteen percent. In the spring of 1938 the administration returned to deficit spending.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the lesson question: why the victories and the hard year arrived together, not merely that both happened. (Blueprint 13, Blueprint 14)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, with at least one from labor's ledger (bank 1, 2, 3) and at least one from the New Deal's ledger (bank 4, 5). An essay that covers only Flint has written half the year. (Blueprint 13, Blueprint 14)
  • Explanation that connects evidence to claim: the sit-down mechanism and the governor's refusal explain the victory, and the plan's design and the two withdrawals of money explain the losses. A list of dated events without the mechanisms earns partial credit.
  • The Memorial Day killings placed correctly, as the cost inside labor's victory and the limit on what the Wagner Act could protect in 1937, not as a separate topic. (Blueprint 13)

Model opening for teacher reference. The year 1937 rewarded the Americans who acted together in the plants and punished the president who acted alone in Washington. The workers' instrument was the sit-down strike, backed by the Wagner Act and organized by the CIO by industry: at Flint the men held the Fisher Body plants for forty-four days, Governor Murphy refused to evict them, and General Motors recognized the United Auto Workers, the first breach in mass production's open shop. The president's instruments failed him in the same months. His court packing plan, announced February 5, 1937, asked the Senate to enlarge the Supreme Court for reasons the message itself would not state, and the Senate killed it in July. A full model continues in this pattern through the recession's two withdrawals before closing on the year's distribution of power.

Common weak answers. A victory narrative (Flint, membership, the Wagner Act upheld) that never reaches the court fight or the recession answers a question the lesson did not ask. A failure narrative that treats the recession as proof the New Deal was collapsing misses the April and May decisions sustaining its two largest laws. Treating the Memorial Day dead as a labor defeat that cancels Flint confuses a cost with a verdict; the lesson's point is that both entries stand in the same year's ledger.
