Guided Notes: Labor Rising and the Recession of 1937

Lesson question: Why did 1937 bring both labor's greatest victories and the New Deal's hardest year?

Unit question: How did the New Deal change the relationship between Americans and their federal government, and what did it leave undone?

Key Terms

sit-down strike: a strike in which workers stop work and remain inside the factory, holding the plant so that replacement workers cannot be brought in

Congress of Industrial Organizations: the federation formed in 1935 to organize workers by whole industry, including the unskilled, rather than by craft

United Auto Workers: the union of automobile workers, recognized by General Motors in February 1937 after the Flint sit-down strike

collective bargaining (carried from Lesson 6): negotiation between an employer and a union chosen by the workers over wages, hours, and working conditions

court packing: the name opponents gave Roosevelt's 1937 proposal to add a new justice to the Supreme Court for each sitting justice over seventy, which would have let him appoint up to six

Roosevelt recession: the sharp economic downturn of 1937 to 1938, caused in part by federal spending cuts and new payroll taxes pulling money out of a weak economy

deficit spending: government spending beyond the revenue it collects, financed by borrowing, used to put money into an economy in a slump

G. Labor's ledger, any of: the Wagner Act upheld by the Supreme Court; General Motors recognizing the United Auto Workers after Flint; membership climbing from under three million toward nine million; the CIO organizing whole industries. The New Deal's ledger, any of: the court packing bill defeated in the Senate; the President's authority after the landslide spent; the Roosevelt recession, with production down about a third and unemployment back toward nineteen percent.

The Year at a Glance. Won in the plants: the Flint settlement of February 11, 1937, GM recognizing the UAW; the CIO's industrial organizing; membership under three million in 1933 toward nine million by 1939. Paid on the ground: the Memorial Day Massacre at Republic Steel, ten killed on May 30, 1937; the fight to hold the Flint plants, including the Battle of the Running Bulls. Lost in Washington: the court packing plan, proposed February 5, 1937 and killed in the Senate in July, even as the Court upheld the Wagner Act and Social Security. Learned by 1938: cutting spending and starting payroll taxes together pulled money out of a weak economy and produced the Roosevelt recession; the administration returned to deficit spending in the spring of 1938.

Exit ticket 1. The workers stayed inside and held the plant and its machines, so the company could not bring in replacement workers and run production without ordering a fight to retake the building, which employers and governors were unwilling to order. The strikers also stayed together and organized, with food and defense committees, instead of scattering to a picket line.

Exit ticket 2. Federal spending cuts that shrank the relief rolls, and the new Social Security payroll taxes begun in January 1937, which took money out of pay for benefits not payable monthly until 1940. Both pulled money out of the economy at the same time.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer joining one labor victory with one New Deal loss and a fact for each. The strongest answers set Flint and the membership climb beside the Senate's defeat of court packing and the recession, and note that the Court upheld the Wagner Act and Social Security in the same months the President lost the court fight.
