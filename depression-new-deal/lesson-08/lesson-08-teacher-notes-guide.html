What the New Deal Changed and What It Did Not

The Depression and New Deal · Lesson 8 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: The Depression and New Deal, Lesson 8 of 8, the final lesson of the unit. It follows Lesson 7, which closed with labor's victories, the court packing defeat, and the Roosevelt recession. Teach it last, before the review day and the unit test, because this lesson steps back from the sequence of events to judge the whole New Deal: the changed country built across Lessons 3 to 7, the work left undone, and the two measures by which a fair verdict can be reached.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “What the New Deal Changed and What It Did Not” and the guided notes, which follow the deck's order exactly — a government present in daily life, security for old age and unemployment, rights at work and a floor under wages, the physical record, the New Deal coalition, what it did not do, and two measures of judgment, closing with the account at a glance table. The activity is “The Verdict Brief: the changed state, the physical record, the recovery numbers, the exclusions.” The notebook page asks students to set out the account in chart form with their measure named. The quick check is “The Number on His Arm: Lange's lumber worker, the two agencies, the exclusions.” The writing task is “The Verdict: the lesson and unit questions as an essay, with the TPSP DBQ as its linked extension.”

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Students need the whole unit in outline: the collapse of 1933 from Lesson 1, the Hundred Days from Lesson 3, relief and work relief from Lesson 4, the Social Security Act and the Wagner Act from Lesson 6, and Flint, the court fight, and the recession from Lesson 7. Nothing new is introduced as narrative; every fact in this lesson was taught earlier in the unit, and the new work is weighing, sorting, and judging it.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (analyze connections among events — deposit insurance, Social Security, labor rights, and the wage floor forming one permanent settlement), D2.His.16 (assess options for individual and collective action — judging a program against the collapse it answered and against full recovery and equal treatment), D2.Civ.14 (explain how economic and political decisions affect citizens — the exclusions written into founding laws and the coalition bargain behind them); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from the changed country through the unfinished work to the two measures), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task: a verdict that names its measure and answers the other side).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to name the permanent federal presence built by the FDIC, the SEC, Social Security, the labor board, and the Fair Labor Standards Act, describe the safety net and its edges, inventory the physical record still in daily use, name the five groups of the New Deal coalition and the price of holding it together, state the recovery boundary — unemployment never below 14 percent in any year of the decade, with full employment only on war spending — document the exclusions of the founding laws, the segregated camps, redlining, and repatriation, and deliver a verdict on the New Deal that names the measure it rests on.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening, question, and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; a government present in daily life (slides 4–5) 4 minutes; security for old age and unemployment (slides 6–8) 6 minutes; rights at work (slides 9–11) 5 minutes; the physical record (slides 12–13) 4 minutes; the coalition (slides 14–15) 4 minutes; what it did not do (slides 16–20) 10 minutes; two measures, the account, vocabulary, and the turn to work (slides 21–27) 12 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. Tell students at the start that the lesson is built in two halves on purpose, the achievements at full strength and then the limits at full strength, because the exit ticket and the writing task require both.

Key vocabulary

New Deal coalition · Fair Labor Standards Act · safety net · redlining · repatriation · Securities and Exchange Commission · Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Title: What the New Deal Changed and What It Did Not

On the slide: the title photograph filling the slide (a Tennessee Valley Authority power shovel at work on a roadway at the Norris Dam site, October 30, 1933, photographed by Lewis Hine); the unit and lesson line “The Depression and New Deal · Lesson 8”; the title “What the New Deal Changed and What It Did Not”; the standfirst — “The changed country, the unfinished work, and the two measures of judgment.”

  • Picture: A Tennessee Valley Authority power shovel at work on a roadway at the Norris Dam site on October 30, 1933, photographed by Lewis Hine for a federal project and held by the National Archives. Caption it as Norris Dam construction in the New Deal's first year, not as a generic construction scene.
  • This photograph is the right opening image for the unit's final lesson because the physical record is one of the two things students must weigh today: what the New Deal built, in concrete and in law, is still standing and still running.
  • The other thing is what it did not do.
  • The Depression was not ended by the New Deal, and the protections it built were not extended to everyone.
  • [Open by naming the discipline of the hour.] Students must hold both records in one judgment and name the measure they are judging by, against the collapse of 1933 or against full recovery and equal treatment.
  • That discipline, naming the measure, is the skill the writing task grades.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the question — “What did the New Deal change for good, what did it leave undone, and how should it be judged?”; below it, the unit question: “How did the New Deal change the relationship between Americans and their federal government, and what did it leave undone?”

  • Notice that today's question is nearly the unit question itself, and say so: this lesson is where the whole unit is judged.
  • It has three parts, and the exit ticket requires all three.
  • What did the New Deal change for good: the federal role, the physical record, the political settlement.
  • What did it leave undone: full recovery, which waited for war spending, and equal treatment, which its founding laws withheld from whole groups of Americans.
  • And how should it be judged, which is the part students most want to skip, because a verdict requires choosing a measure.
  • Two measures run through the lesson and through the reading's closing section: against the collapse it answered, and against full recovery and equal treatment.
  • A student may defend either verdict.
  • A student may not decline to have one, or hide the measure the verdict rests on.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title “The changed country, then the unfinished work”; seven rows — A, A government present in daily life (deposits insured; stock markets regulated); B, Security for old age and unemployment (Social Security and the safety net); C, Rights at work, a floor under wages (the labor board; the Fair Labor Standards Act, 1938); D, The physical record (dams, bridges, schools, parks, and airports); E, The New Deal coalition (five groups, one party, a generation in power); F, What it did not do (unemployment never below 14 percent; the exclusions); G, Two measures of judgment (against the collapse, or against full recovery).

  • The map follows the guided notes section by section.
  • Sections A through E build the changed country in order: the federal government's new presence in daily life through deposit insurance and securities regulation; the safety net of Social Security; labor rights enforced and a wage floor set in 1938; the physical record of dams and buildings; and the New Deal coalition that held power for a generation.
  • Section F then states the limits as plainly as the record allows: unemployment never below fourteen percent in any year of the decade, full employment only with war spending, and the exclusions, agricultural and domestic workers outside Social Security, segregated camps, redlining, and repatriation.
  • Section G closes with the two measures of judgment.
  • Warn students now that the lesson is built in this order on purpose: the limits are taught after the achievements, at full strength, and neither half is a footnote to the other.

Slide 4 · Section A divider: A Government Present in Daily Life

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (President Roosevelt signing the Social Security Act on August 14, 1935, with members of Congress standing behind him); the section label “Section A”; the title “A Government Present in Daily Life”; the lines — “Standing rules, run by standing agencies.” and “Not emergency measures for one season.”

  • Picture: President Roosevelt signing the Social Security Act on August 14, 1935, with members of Congress standing behind him, held by the Library of Congress. The signing belongs to section B, and it opens this section because it is the era's emblem of the larger change treated first: a federal government that stayed.
  • Make the contrast with 1933 concrete for students.
  • The emergency agencies of the Hundred Days were built to be temporary, and some, like the Civil Works Administration, were shut within months.
  • The agencies of this section were built the other way, as permanent parts of the state: a deposit insurance corporation, a securities regulator, and behind them a new expectation that the national government stood inside economic life, guaranteeing, policing, and paying, as a normal condition rather than a crisis exception.
  • Sections A and B together cover that settlement; section A takes the two regulators of money itself.

Slide 5 · Two guarantees over money

On the slide: five bullets — The FDIC, created in 1933, insured bank deposits by the federal government.; A failed bank no longer wiped out a family's savings.; The SEC, created in 1934, regulated stock markets and required honest reporting.; Companies selling stock answered for their published figures.; Both agencies still operate, a lifetime after the emergency.

  • Teach the two agencies as a pair, because a working family met them as a pair: one guarded what the family saved, the other policed what the family might buy.
  • Carry the definitions exactly: “Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation: the federal agency created in 1933 that insures bank deposits, so that depositors are repaid by the government if their bank fails”.
  • Deposit insurance ended the bank run as a rational act: with the guarantee behind the deposit, a rumor no longer required a run to the teller.
  • Carry the definition exactly: “Securities and Exchange Commission: the federal agency created in 1934 to regulate the stock markets and require honest public reporting by companies selling stock”.
  • Its weapon was disclosure: a company could sell risky stock, but not on false figures.
  • Students should register the common form: in both cases the government did not take over the business; it set the floor of honesty and solvency beneath it, permanently.

Slide 6 · Section B divider: Security for Old Age and Unemployment

On the slide: the section label “Section B”; the title “Security for Old Age and Unemployment”; the line — “The largest piece of the new presence, and the start of the safety net.”

  • If deposit insurance is the New Deal's quietest permanence, Social Security is its largest.
  • Set out what the Act of August 1935 actually built, because students often shrink it to a pension: old-age insurance for retired workers, funded by payroll taxes on workers and employers; unemployment insurance run jointly with the states; and aid to dependent children and other vulnerable groups.
  • Then name the principle, which the reading states and the guided notes test.
  • Before 1935, old age without savings, or a lost job in a slack season, was a private misfortune met by family, charity, or the county poor farm.
  • After 1935, the federal government accepted these as standing risks of a working life and insured against them as a matter of law.
  • That acceptance, more than any single benefit check, is the change in the relationship between Americans and their government that the unit question asks about.
  • The exclusions written into the Act belong to section F; here, build the structure first.

Slide 7 · The safety net

On the slide: five bullets — Old-age insurance paid a monthly benefit at retirement.; Unemployment insurance paid benefits between jobs, run with the states.; Aid reached dependent children and other vulnerable people.; Payroll taxes funded the net, earned as a right of work.; The net had edges, and some Americans stood outside it from the start.

  • Read the guided notes definition aloud, because it is the term in its most useful form.
  • Carry the definition exactly: “safety net: the set of government programs that catch people who fall into poverty through old age, unemployment, disability, or the loss of a family earner”.
  • Note the four falls the definition names: old age, unemployment, disability, and the loss of a family earner, and have students match each to a program from the lesson before it: old-age insurance, unemployment insurance, and aid to dependent children.
  • The funding deserves one clear sentence: the net was paid for by payroll taxes, contributions from wages and employers, which is why its benefits were understood as earned, a right of work rather than a gift of relief, and why they proved politically durable when relief programs were repealed.
  • End on the definition's limit, stated in the reading and developed in section F: a net has edges, and in 1935 the edges were drawn so that agricultural and domestic workers fell outside them.

Slide 8 · A card in every wallet

On the slide: five bullets — Workers registered for Social Security numbers from 1936.; The wage record followed the worker from job to job.; Monthly benefits began in 1940.; The number became a piece of American identity.; An unemployed worker of 1939 carried his number tattooed on his arm.

  • This slide makes the abstraction physical, and it previews the quick check.
  • From late 1936, workers across the country registered for Social Security numbers, tens of millions of them, in one of the largest administrative undertakings the federal government had ever attempted; employers reported wages against those numbers, and the worker's benefit account followed him from job to job for life.
  • Monthly benefits began in 1940.
  • The quick check photograph, by Dorothea Lange in Oregon in August 1939, shows an unemployed lumber worker with his Social Security number tattooed on his arm, and students should be asked what the tattoo proves before they are told: a man tattoos a number on his skin only if he expects the number, and the program behind it, to be permanent and valuable for the rest of his life.
  • Ask: What does the tattoo prove? That the worker expected the number, and the program behind it, to be permanent and valuable for the rest of his life.
  • The same photograph shows the decade's other truth, a man still migrant and unemployed in 1939, which is section F arriving early; let students notice it and hold the thought.

Slide 9 · Section C divider: Rights at Work and a Floor Under Wages

On the slide: the section label “Section C”; the title “Rights at Work and a Floor Under Wages”; the line — “The board behind the union, and a national minimum beneath the wage.”

  • Section C joins the two labor settlements of the unit: the right to organize, taught in the last two lessons, and the wage and hour floor, which arrives here because its law is the New Deal's last.
  • The Wagner Act's labor board had been enforcing workers' choice of a union since 1935, and the Supreme Court secured it in April 1937; by 1938 the right to organize was a standing fact of industrial life.
  • What workers could not yet claim was any national standard for the wage itself or the length of the week.
  • The Fair Labor Standards Act of June 1938 supplied it, and it is the right closing statute for the New Deal's legislative record: minimum wage, overtime, and child labor limits, set in Washington for the whole country.
  • Teach the section as the completion of a structure: the Act of 1935 protected the worker's organization; the Act of 1938 protected the worker's pay envelope.

Slide 10 · Stat: 25 cents, the first federal minimum wage

On the slide: the big figure “25 cents”; the label — “the first federal minimum wage, set by the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938”; the note — “Overtime pay required past forty hours a week · Child labor restricted · The last major law of the New Deal.”

  • 25 cents — the first federal minimum wage, set by the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938
  • Overtime pay required past forty hours a week; child labor restricted; the last major law of the New Deal
  • Put the figure in a worker's terms before judging it.
  • Twenty-five cents an hour, for a forty hour week, is ten dollars a week, in a decade when millions had worked for less or not at all; the Act scheduled the minimum to rise to forty cents within seven years.
  • Carry the definition exactly: “Fair Labor Standards Act: the 1938 law that set a federal minimum wage, required overtime pay past forty hours a week, and restricted child labor”.
  • Each of its three provisions reversed an older settlement: the wage had been set by each employer's market, the week had run past sixty hours in many trades, and children had worked in mills and canneries within living memory.
  • Note also what the Act excluded, because it previews section F: farm labor and domestic service were left outside the wage floor too, so the same exclusions that mark Social Security mark the New Deal's final statute.

Slide 11 · The board behind the right

On the slide: five bullets — The labor board ran union elections and policed coercion.; Employers were bound to bargain with the workers' chosen union.; The Supreme Court secured the Act in April 1937.; By 1939, nine million union members stood behind the settlement.; Rights at work had become a standing part of the federal role.

  • This slide closes section C by following the enforcement, because a right with a board behind it is a different thing from a right in a speech.
  • The National Labor Relations Board conducted representation elections, investigated charges that employers had fired or spied on union members, and ordered remedies; after the Supreme Court upheld the Wagner Act in April 1937, in the Jones and Laughlin case from the last lesson, compliance spread through industries that had sworn never to bargain.
  • The membership figure belongs here: from under three million members in 1933 toward nine million by the end of the decade.
  • Connect that growth to the coalition of the next sections: organized labor was no longer a pressure group outside politics but a constituency inside the governing party, and its presence in the New Deal coalition is one reason the settlement survived its maker.
  • Students should leave the section able to say what the federal government did at work by 1939: it insured the election, enforced the contract table, and set the floor under the wage.

Slide 12 · Section D divider: The Physical Record

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (Lewis Hine's power shovel at the Norris Dam site in October 1933, the same image as the title slide in a portrait crop); the section label “Section D”; the title “The Physical Record”; the lines — “Dams, bridges, schools, parks, and airports,” and “in daily use for generations.”

  • Picture: Lewis Hine's power shovel at the Norris Dam site in October 1933, the same image as the title slide in a portrait crop. Caption it as Norris Dam construction, not as a generic worksite.
  • It introduces the most durable evidence in the lesson: the built country.
  • Relief checks were spent and gone; a dam is an argument that renews itself every year it stands.
  • Teach the inventory wide, because students underrate its range: the great river dams, Norris and Grand Coulee and Boulder among them; electrification carried into farm country; bridges, schools, courthouses, post offices, and airports in nearly every county in the nation; roads paved; park lodges and trails built by the CCC and the WPA's workers.
  • The Tennessee Valley Authority is the type case: one federal corporation remaking a whole river region's floods, power, and fertilizer.
  • The verdict relevance comes at the end of the section and in the activity's second file: construction spending is the one kind of Depression expenditure whose return is still being collected.

Slide 13 · What the traveler of 1940 used

On the slide: five bullets — River dams rose on the Tennessee, the Columbia, and the Colorado.; Power lines reached farm country that private companies had passed over.; Bridges, schools, courthouses, and airports stood in every region.; Park trails and lodges were built by relief workers.; The structures outlasted the arguments that built them.

  • Run the inventory as a journey, following the reading.
  • A traveler crossing the United States in 1940 drove on roads the New Deal's programs had paved, crossed bridges they had built, landed at airports they had laid out, passed schools and courthouses they had raised in towns that could not have bonded them in 1933, and at night saw farmhouses lit by power lines the rural electrification program had strung into country the private utilities had judged unprofitable.
  • The Tennessee Valley Authority's dams held back the Tennessee River's floods and sold current at rates the private companies then had to meet.
  • Two teaching points.
  • First, scale: this was not a scattering of monuments but a national building program carried out as relief, employing millions, which is why its remains are everywhere rather than somewhere.
  • Second, judgment: in the activity's Verdict Brief, the physical record is the file that weighs by time, since every decade of use adds to the return on money spent in the worst years of the century.

Slide 14 · Section E divider: The New Deal Coalition

On the slide: the section label “Section E”; the title “The New Deal Coalition”; the line — “The settlement had a political foundation, and the foundation lasted a generation.”

  • Every structure in sections A through D rested on votes, and this section names them.
  • Roosevelt's achievement as a politician was not only winning in 1932 but assembling a majority broad enough to govern through the decade and beyond it: the New Deal coalition.
  • Teach the coalition as a bargain among groups that agreed on little except the party that delivered to them.
  • White southerners supplied the congressional seniority that chaired the committees.
  • Northern city voters supplied the electoral votes of the great states.
  • Union members, newly organized by the millions, supplied organization and turnout.
  • Farmers supplied the heartland.
  • And Black voters in the northern cities, heirs of the party of Lincoln, moved toward the Democrats in this decade and stayed.
  • The bargain had a price, and the price was charged to Black southerners, who remained disfranchised inside the coalition's own stronghold.
  • Name that here, before section F, so students meet the exclusions as a cost of the settlement and not as an unrelated scandal.

Slide 15 · Five groups, one party

On the slide: five bullets — White southerners held committee power in Congress.; Northern city voters supplied the great states' electoral votes.; Union members supplied organization, turnout, and members by the million.; Farmers received supports for prices and credit.; Black voters in the North were moving toward the Democratic Party.

  • Work the list as the guided notes definition gives it, in full.
  • Carry the definition exactly: “New Deal coalition: the alliance of white southerners, northern city voters, union members, farmers, and increasingly Black voters that kept the Democratic Party in national power for a generation after 1932”.
  • A generation is the operative measure: five straight presidential elections, from 1932 through 1948, and congressional majorities that long outlasted Roosevelt himself.
  • Ask: What did each group receive? Delivery is what held an alliance of groups with opposed interests together: farm supports and rural power for the farmers and the South; labor rights and relief for the cities and the unions; growing federal attention for Black northern voters.
  • Then put the contradiction plainly, because it is the coalition's central fact: the same alliance included the white South, whose price was federal restraint toward segregation and disfranchisement.
  • The exclusions of section F were not accidents that befell the settlement from outside; several of them were terms on which the coalition itself was held together.

Slide 16 · Section F divider: What It Did Not Do

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (Dorothea Lange's unemployed lumber worker of August 1939, seated with his wife in a migrant camp in Oregon, his Social Security number tattooed on his arm); the section label “Section F”; the title “What It Did Not Do”; the lines — “Unemployment never below fourteen percent.” and “The protections did not reach everyone.”

  • Picture: Dorothea Lange's unemployed lumber worker of August 1939, the quick check image, photographed for the Farm Security Administration in Oregon. Caption it as the Lange lumber worker with his Social Security number tattooed on his arm, not as a generic migrant portrait.
  • It is shown here first so its argument can frame the section: in the decade's last summer, ten years after the crash, a skilled woods worker was still following the bean harvest out of a migrant camp, with his Social Security number tattooed on his arm.
  • Permanence and insufficiency stand in the same frame, which is section F in one image.
  • State the section's two charges in order and without softening either.
  • The recovery charge is numerical: unemployment never fell below fourteen percent in any year of the 1930s, and full employment arrived only with war spending.
  • The treatment charge is documentary: the settlement's exclusions were written into its founding laws, its camp rosters, its housing maps, and its removal drives, and they fell on identifiable Americans by name and category.
  • Students who want to acquit the New Deal must answer this section, not skip it.

Slide 17 · Stat: 14 percent, the best year of the decade

On the slide: the big figure “14 percent”; the label — “the lowest unemployment reached in any year of the 1930s, in 1937”; the note — “About 25 percent in 1933 · About 19 percent again in 1938 · Full employment only with war spending in the 1940s.”

  • 14 percent — the lowest unemployment reached in any year of the 1930s, in 1937
  • About 25 percent in 1933; about 19 percent again in 1938; full employment only with war spending in the 1940s
  • This is the single most important number in the lesson, and it should be taught as a boundary, not a detail.
  • Unemployment fell impressively from its depth, about twenty-five percent in 1933, and students should credit the fall.
  • But in the best year of the decade, 1937, it reached only about fourteen percent, roughly one worker in seven still idle in the eighth year of the emergency, and the recession then drove it back toward nineteen percent.
  • In 1939 it stood near seventeen percent.
  • Full employment, the condition in which anyone seeking work can find it, arrived in the 1940s, and it arrived on war spending: the federal government finally spent at the scale the slump had required all along, building an army and a war economy.
  • The conclusion students must be able to state without prompting is the unit's boundary line: the New Deal relieved, reformed, and partially recovered the country, and the war, not the New Deal, ended the Great Depression.
  • The World War II unit owns the mobilization itself.

Slide 18 · The exclusions written in

On the slide: five bullets — Social Security in 1935 left agricultural and domestic workers out.; The exclusion fell hardest on Black workers and on women.; CCC camps were segregated, and relief was uneven under local control.; The same exclusions marked the wage law of 1938.; The exclusions were written into the laws at the time, not discovered later.

  • Teach the exclusions as design, because the activity's fourth file asks students to prove design from evidence, and the habit is the lesson's discipline.
  • The Social Security Act's omission of agricultural and domestic workers was a provision of the statute, affecting a majority of Black workers in the South and a large share of working women, and historians have long connected it to the southern committee chairmen whose votes the bill required.
  • The Civilian Conservation Corps enrolled its young men in segregated camps as a matter of administration.
  • Relief run through local officials varied with local prejudice, generous in one county and grudging in the next.
  • The Fair Labor Standards Act repeated the pattern in 1938, leaving farm and domestic workers outside the wage floor.
  • The point to fix is the one the reading makes in its last sentence on the subject: the lines the new state drew around its protections were drawn deliberately, by identifiable men, for identifiable reasons of coalition and prejudice, and the Americans outside the lines knew it at the time.

Slide 19 · Redlining, and removal

On the slide: the photograph at right (a federal housing security map of 1937, a city divided into graded zones by federal appraisers, from the records of the Federal Home Loan Bank Board); five bullets — Federal housing maps graded neighborhoods by race.; Minority districts were marked risky, and loans were denied there.; White buyers built equity while Black buyers were kept out.; Repatriation removed people of Mexican descent to Mexico.; Many of the removed were United States citizens.

  • Picture: A federal housing security map of 1937, from the records of the Federal Home Loan Bank Board at the National Archives: the instrument itself, a city divided into graded zones by federal appraisers. The source record does not name the city, so do not name one. Caption it as a 1937 federal housing map, not as a map of a specific city.
  • Carry the definitions exactly: “redlining: the practice by which federal housing officials and lenders marked minority neighborhoods as risky on maps and denied loans there, keeping Black families from buying homes on equal terms”.
  • The consequence ran for generations, because the home was the ordinary family's one road to equity: denied the federally backed loan in the 1930s and 1940s, Black families were denied the asset whose appreciation financed the white suburbs.
  • Carry the definition exactly: “repatriation: the removal of people of Mexican descent, including many United States citizens, to Mexico during the Depression, carried out by local and federal authorities under economic pressure”.
  • Raids, coerced departures, and county relief offices that paid for one way tickets south did the work, and the fear of the drive emptied neighborhoods far beyond those actually removed.
  • Note: No verified public domain photograph of repatriation in progress is reproduced in this lesson; the widely reproduced images could not be verified as public domain, so the removal is taught in text drawn from the reading.

Slide 20 · Removed under pressure

On the slide: five bullets — In the early 1930s, jobs were scarce and scapegoats were sought.; Local raids and county drives pushed families south.; Citizens of the United States were among the removed.; Estimates of the removed run into the hundreds of thousands.; The new state of sections A to E was not built for them.

  • Give repatriation its own slide, because it is the exclusion students know least and the one that most tests the lesson's argument about who the new state was for.
  • In the early Depression years, as unemployment climbed, people of Mexican descent, immigrants and citizens alike, were pressed to leave: workplace raids in Los Angeles and other cities, county programs that chartered trains and paid fares on condition the riders not return, and relief denied to families who stayed.
  • Large numbers of those removed were United States citizens, many of them children born in this country, carried to a Mexico they had never seen.
  • Estimates of the total removed run into the hundreds of thousands; the surviving records do not support a single exact figure, and students should be taught to say so rather than to memorize one.
  • The placement of this fact shapes the verdict: it happened mostly before 1933, under Hoover and local authorities, and the New Deal did not organize it; but the New Deal's settlement, which distributed protection by category and place, left the removed and their descendants outside its account as well.

Slide 21 · Section G divider: Two Measures of Judgment

On the slide: the section label “Section G”; the title “Two Measures of Judgment”; the line — “The verdict changes with the measure. Name the measure.”

  • Close the teaching where the reading closes, with judgment itself as the subject.
  • Two measures have been available all lesson, and students have been using both without naming them.
  • The first measures the New Deal against the collapse it answered: the banks of 1933, the unemployment of 1933, the helplessness of the federal government of 1933.
  • Against that measure the record of sections A through E is overwhelming: stabilization, millions at work, permanent protections, a built country.
  • The second measures it against full recovery and equal treatment: a normally working economy and protections extended to all Americans.
  • Against that measure, the record of section F is decisive the other way.
  • Neither measure is dishonest, and the choice between them is the actual content of a century of argument about the New Deal.
  • What the lesson forbids is the unmeasured verdict, the praise or condemnation that never says what standard it is applying.
  • The next two slides run each measure at full strength, in order.

Slide 22 · Measured against the collapse

On the slide: five bullets — In 1933, banks were failing and a quarter of workers were idle.; The banking system was stabilized and deposits were insured.; Millions were put to work, and a physical country was built.; Permanent protections were written into law.; A governing coalition lasted a generation.

  • Run the favorable measure at its strongest, because students must be able to state it before they are asked to answer it.
  • In March 1933 the banking system was shut, a quarter of the labor force was idle, farm prices had halved, and the federal government possessed neither the machinery nor the accepted duty to answer any of it.
  • By 1939 the banks were stable and their deposits insured by the federal government; the securities markets were policed; millions had been put to work, and their work stood in dams, schools, and parks; old age and unemployment had become insured risks; labor rights were enforced and a floor stood under wages; and the political coalition behind the settlement had won five straight presidential elections and would shape the country for a generation.
  • Measured against where the country stood when Roosevelt took the oath, the New Deal was the largest act of relief, recovery, and reform the nation had ever attempted, and most of its structure is still in place.
  • That is the case.
  • The next slide is the other case, and it is also true.

Slide 23 · Measured against full recovery

On the slide: five bullets — Unemployment stayed at or above 14 percent in every year of the decade.; The recession of 1937 to 1938 came inside the recovery.; Full employment arrived only with war spending.; The exclusions were written into laws, rosters, and maps.; On this measure, the New Deal stopped short.

  • Now run the second measure at full strength.
  • The New Deal's own stated work was recovery, and by the plainest test it was not achieved: unemployment never fell below about fourteen percent in any year of the 1930s, the administration's own fiscal turn in 1937 produced a recession inside the recovery, and the economy did not reach full employment until the federal government spent on war at the scale the Depression had always required.
  • The second count is equal treatment.
  • The settlement protected Americans by category and drew its categories so that agricultural and domestic workers, Black southerners under segregation, Black home buyers facing redlined maps, and families of Mexican descent under repatriation pressure stood outside or beneath the protections their neighbors received.
  • A student who adopts this measure and judges the New Deal a partial, bounded achievement that stopped short of its promise has read the evidence correctly; so has the student on the other measure.
  • The writing task grades the naming of the measure and the answering of the other side, not the verdict chosen.

Slide 24 · The account at a glance

On the slide: the title “Changed, built, held, undone”; four rows — Changed (FDIC, SEC, Social Security, labor rights, the wage floor — a permanent federal presence in economic life); Built (dams, bridges, schools, parks, and airports — construction still in daily use generations later); Held (the New Deal coalition, five straight elections — national power for a generation after 1932); Undone (unemployment never below 14 percent; the exclusions — recovery waited for war; protection was not equal).

  • This slide mirrors the closing table of the guided notes, The Account at a Glance, and students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • Changed: the federal role, deposit insurance from 1933, securities regulation from 1934, Social Security from 1935, enforced labor rights, and the wage and hour floor of 1938, a permanent presence where ten years earlier there had been almost none.
  • Built: the physical record, dams on three great rivers and buildings in every county, still in daily use.
  • Held: the political settlement, the New Deal coalition in national power for a generation.
  • Undone: two entries, recovery, with unemployment never below fourteen percent in any year of the decade and full employment reserved to war spending; and equal treatment, with the exclusions of the founding laws, the segregated camps, the redlined maps, and repatriation.
  • Grade the table on the presence of both undone entries: a table with only the recovery entry has missed half of section F.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for the settlement

On the slide: four terms with their definitions — New Deal coalition, Fair Labor Standards Act, safety net, redlining. The full definitions below match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Say: “New Deal coalition: the alliance of white southerners, northern city voters, union members, farmers, and increasingly Black voters that kept the Democratic Party in national power for a generation after 1932”
  • Say: “Fair Labor Standards Act: the 1938 law that set a federal minimum wage, required overtime pay past forty hours a week, and restricted child labor”
  • Say: “safety net: the set of government programs that catch people who fall into poverty through old age, unemployment, disability, or the loss of a family earner”
  • Say: “redlining: the practice by which federal housing officials and lenders marked minority neighborhoods as risky on maps and denied loans there, keeping Black families from buying homes on equal terms”
  • Four terms, matching the guided notes word for word, and students should copy the definitions because the unit test draws on them.
  • Notice for students that this set holds the settlement and its shadow together: two terms for the coalition and its laws, one for the protection, and one for the exclusion, which is the shape of the whole lesson in four lines.

Slide 26 · Vocabulary: words for the record and the removal

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — repatriation, Securities and Exchange Commission, Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. The full definitions below match the guided notes word for word.

  • Say: “repatriation: the removal of people of Mexican descent, including many United States citizens, to Mexico during the Depression, carried out by local and federal authorities under economic pressure”
  • Say: “Securities and Exchange Commission: the federal agency created in 1934 to regulate the stock markets and require honest public reporting by companies selling stock”
  • Say: “Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation: the federal agency created in 1933 that insures bank deposits, so that depositors are repaid by the government if their bank fails”
  • Three more terms, in the guided notes wording, carried in these notes in full.
  • Pair the two agencies for memory, as the lesson taught them: 1933 insured what a family saved, 1934 policed what a company sold.
  • And have students state the repatriation definition's hardest phrase aloud, including many United States citizens, because the verdict in the writing task depends on not losing it.
