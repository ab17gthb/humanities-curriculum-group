Writing Task: The Verdict

The prompt. Answer the lesson question, and with it the unit question, in a developed essay of about 300 words: What did the New Deal change for good, what did it leave undone, and how should it be judged? A strong answer names its measure before it gives its verdict. It covers the changed federal role and the physical record on one side, and the unfinished recovery and the exclusions on the other, and it answers the strongest argument against its own verdict. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the Verdict Brief files. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Linked extension. This task pairs with The Primary Source Project premium DBQ 5, "Did the New Deal end the Great Depression?" (DBQ180), which takes up the recovery question with a full document set. Teachers running the extension assign the DBQ in place of, or after, this task; the DBQ is a linked resource and is not reproduced here.

Evidence Bank

1. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (1933) insured bank deposits, and the Securities and Exchange Commission (1934) required honest reporting by companies selling stock. Both still operate, as standing rules rather than emergency measures.

2. The Social Security Act of 1935 created old-age insurance funded by payroll taxes and unemployment insurance run with the states, the core of a safety net. The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 set a minimum wage of twenty-five cents an hour, overtime past forty hours, and limits on child labor.

3. The physical record: dams on the Tennessee, Columbia, and Colorado rivers, the Tennessee Valley electrified, and bridges, schools, courthouses, airports, roads, and park buildings across the country, much of it still in daily use.

4. The New Deal coalition of white southerners, northern city voters, union members, farmers, and increasingly Black voters kept the Democratic Party in national power for a generation after 1932, through five straight presidential elections.

5. Unemployment fell from about twenty-five percent in 1933 to about fourteen percent in 1937, then rose toward nineteen percent in the recession of 1937 to 1938 and stood near seventeen percent in 1939. It never fell below fourteen percent in any year of the decade; full employment came only with war spending.

6. The exclusions: Social Security left out agricultural and domestic workers at the start; the CCC ran segregated camps; relief under local control was uneven; federal housing officials redlined minority neighborhoods out of loans; and repatriation removed people of Mexican descent, including United States citizens, to Mexico.

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Open with your measure and your claim, develop both sides of the record, answer the counterargument, and close with the verdict restated in one sentence.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A measure stated before the verdict, and a claim that follows from it: judged against the collapse of 1933, or against full recovery and equal treatment. Either measure can earn full credit; an essay with no measure has given an opinion that cannot be checked. (Blueprint 15)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, covering both sides of the account: the changed state and the physical or political record (bank 1, 2, 3, 4) on one side, and the recovery numbers or the exclusions (bank 5, 6) on the other. (Blueprint 15)
  • The counterargument stated at full strength and answered. Against a favorable verdict: a decade that never brought unemployment below fourteen percent and wrote racial exclusions into its founding laws. Against an unfavorable verdict: the comparison belongs with 1933 and with the Hoover record, and no earlier administration built permanent protections on this scale.
  • The boundary fact placed correctly: full employment arrived with war spending, which belongs to the next unit. An essay that credits the New Deal with ending the Depression has failed the lesson's central evidence, whatever its measure. (Blueprint 15)

Model opening for teacher reference. Judged against the collapse it answered, the New Deal changed the United States permanently and mostly for the better. In 1933 a family's savings lived or died with its local bank; by the end of the decade deposits were insured by the FDIC, stock sellers answered to the SEC, retired workers drew Social Security, and a floor stood under wages. The government a citizen met in daily life was a different government from the one Herbert Hoover had presided over, and the dams, schools, and airports of the physical record are still in use. A full model continues by running the other side of the record, the recovery numbers and the exclusions, and by answering the counterargument its measure invites, before the verdict is restated.

Common weak answers. A balance sheet without a measure (three good things, three bad things, a shrug) assembles the record without judging it, which is the one thing this task exists to require. A celebration that omits bank 5 and bank 6 has not read the decade's numbers or its maps. A condemnation that omits bank 1 through bank 4 cannot explain why the settlement lasted a generation or why its programs are still running. Treating the war's full employment as a New Deal achievement reverses the lesson's closing fact.
