Guided Notes: What the New Deal Changed and What It Did Not

Lesson question: What did the New Deal change for good, what did it leave undone, and how should it be judged?

Unit question: How did the New Deal change the relationship between Americans and their federal government, and what did it leave undone?

Key Terms

New Deal coalition: the alliance of white southerners, northern city voters, union members, farmers, and increasingly Black voters that kept the Democratic Party in national power for a generation after 1932

Fair Labor Standards Act: the 1938 law that set a federal minimum wage, required overtime pay past forty hours a week, and restricted child labor

safety net: the set of government programs that catch people who fall into poverty through old age, unemployment, disability, or the loss of a family earner

redlining: the practice by which federal housing officials and lenders marked minority neighborhoods as risky on maps and denied loans there, keeping Black families from buying homes on equal terms

repatriation: the removal of people of Mexican descent, including many United States citizens, to Mexico during the Depression, carried out by local and federal authorities under economic pressure

Securities and Exchange Commission: the federal agency created in 1934 to regulate the stock markets and require honest public reporting by companies selling stock

Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation: the federal agency created in 1933 that insures bank deposits, so that depositors are repaid by the government if their bank fails

G. Measured against the collapse: the New Deal stabilized the banking system, insured deposits, regulated securities, put millions to work, and wrote Social Security, labor rights, and a wage floor into permanent law; the fact may be any of these, stated accurately. Measured against full recovery and equal treatment: unemployment stayed at or above fourteen percent in every year of the decade, full employment waited for war spending, and the settlement's exclusions (agricultural and domestic workers, segregated camps, redlining, repatriation) left whole groups outside the protection it built.

The Account at a Glance. Changed: deposits insured by the FDIC from 1933; securities regulated by the SEC from 1934; Social Security old-age and unemployment insurance from 1935; Wagner Act labor rights enforced; the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 setting a minimum wage, overtime past forty hours, and child labor limits. Built: dams on the Tennessee, Columbia, and Colorado; bridges, schools, courthouses, airports, roads, and park structures in daily use for generations; the Tennessee Valley electrified. Undone, recovery: unemployment never below fourteen percent in any year of the decade; the 1937 to 1938 recession inside the recovery; full employment only with war spending. Undone, equal treatment: agricultural and domestic workers excluded from Social Security at the start; segregated CCC camps and uneven local relief; redlining in federal housing credit; Mexican repatriation, including citizens.

Exit ticket 1. Any permanent change with its instrument: insured savings (FDIC), honest stock reporting (SEC), a monthly old-age benefit and unemployment insurance (Social Security Act), an enforceable right to organize (Wagner Act and its board), or a minimum wage and overtime (Fair Labor Standards Act).

Exit ticket 2. Unemployment never fell below fourteen percent in any year of the 1930s. Accept also the sharper form: full employment arrived only with the war spending of the 1940s.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that runs one change and one limit with facts, and lands on a judgment that uses a measure. The strongest answers state the measure explicitly, against the collapse or against full recovery and equal treatment, and note that the other measure gives the opposite reading.
