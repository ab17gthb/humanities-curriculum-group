Roosevelt and the First Hundred Days

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Inauguration Day, March 4, 1933

Franklin D. Roosevelt took the oath of office on March 4, 1933, with the banking system shut or failing in state after state. Governors had closed banks to stop withdrawals, and in many cities no bank was open at all. About one worker in four had no job. Roosevelt spoke to a crowd in Washington and, by radio, to millions more, and he treated fear itself as part of the emergency. He said, in words quoted from the Franklin D. Roosevelt Presidential Library transcript, that the only thing we have to fear is fear itself, and he called that fear a nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror which paralyzes needed efforts to convert retreat into advance. The sentence worked as policy and not only as comfort. A president who named fear as the enemy could ask the country to accept strong federal action, and he promised action at once.

B. The Bank Holiday

On March 6, two days after the inauguration, Roosevelt proclaimed a bank holiday and closed every bank in the country. The closing stopped the rush of withdrawals that had been draining sound banks along with weak ones. With the doors shut, federal examiners could inspect each bank, sort the sound from the failed, and decide which banks could safely reopen. Roosevelt acted under an old wartime trading law, and he knew Congress had to confirm and expand his power at once. State holidays already in force had shown that closing could stop a run, but only a national closing could stop runs everywhere at once. The holiday was a gamble. If the public lost confidence while the banks stood closed, the reopening could fail, and with it the new presidency.

C. The Emergency Banking Act and the Reopening

Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act on March 9, in a single day, with debate measured in hours. The law gave the president broad power over banking, confirmed the bank holiday, and allowed sound banks to reopen under federal license. Banks found unsound stayed closed. The Treasury then licensed banks to reopen in stages, the largest city banks first. When the first banks opened, lines formed again, but this time people stood in line to put money back in. Deposits exceeded withdrawals in city after city. The rescue worked because the government had inspected before it promised, and because the president explained the plan before the doors opened.

D. The First Fireside Chat

On Sunday evening, March 12, Roosevelt spoke on the radio in the first of his fireside chats. He began, in the words of the FDR Library transcript, by saying he wanted to talk for a few minutes with the people of the United States about banking. He explained in plain language that a bank does not keep deposits in a vault but puts money to work in loans. He described what the government had done during the holiday and what would happen next. He closed by asking listeners to keep their money in the reopened banks, saying that the machinery to restore the financial system had been provided and that together the country could not fail. Tens of millions listened. The next day the deposits kept growing.

E. Farming and the Valley

The Hundred Days then moved beyond banking. The Agricultural Adjustment Act of May 1933 paid farmers to reduce production of basic crops and livestock in order to raise farm prices toward parity, the level at which farm income would buy what it had bought before the First World War. In its first season the program paid farmers to plow under growing cotton and to reduce pig herds, a painful step taken because unsold surpluses had driven prices below the cost of growing the crop. The Tennessee Valley Authority, created the same month, took up a whole river region. It built dams, controlled flooding, and brought electricity and development to the Tennessee River valley, where private power companies had left most farm homes without current.

F. Industry and Banking Reform

The National Industrial Recovery Act of June 1933 created a program to write codes of fair competition for each industry, setting wages, hours, and prices and protecting the right of workers to organize. The Glass-Steagall Act, passed the same week, separated commercial banking from investment banking and created federal insurance of bank deposits, so that a depositor in a failed bank would be repaid by the government up to a set limit. Together the two laws showed the range of the season. One tried to plan recovery industry by industry. The other rebuilt the rules of banking. Congress also set the end of Prohibition in motion by proposing the amendment that the states ratified before the year ended.

G. A New Expectation

The special session ran from March 9 to June 16, 1933, the period remembered as the Hundred Days. Congress passed bill after bill, often with little debate, and the president signed each one into a country that had expected far less from Washington only a year before. Two judgments belong together. Speed and confidence were real achievements. At the same time, most of the programs were experiments. Nobody in March 1933 could know whether paying farmers to produce less, or planning industry by code, would raise prices, restore jobs, or last. What had changed for good was the expectation itself. Americans now looked to the federal government to act in a crisis, and to act first.
