The Culture Wars

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A Line Drawn in Numbers

Immigration rose sharply after the First World War, and Congress moved to close the door. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 limited each nationality to 3 percent of the people born in that country and counted in the census of 1910, with total admissions of about 357,000 a year. The Immigration Act of 1924, known as the National Origins Act, cut much deeper. Its quota system allowed each country 2 percent of its foreign born counted in the census of 1890, and total admissions fell to 164,667 a year. The choice of 1890 was the policy. That census reached back to a time before the great movement from southern and eastern Europe, so the arithmetic ranked nations before any individual was judged. Germany received 51,227 places a year and Great Britain and Northern Ireland 34,007, while Italy received 3,845, Russia 2,248, and Greece the minimum of 100. The same law barred nearly all immigration from Asia by excluding aliens who were ineligible for citizenship.

A Science Claimed for the Border

Supporters defended the ranking as science. Eugenics claimed that ability and character were inherited and that human groups could be ranked by ancestry. Harry Laughlin of the Eugenics Record Office served the House immigration committee as an expert agent, and he presented figures arguing that the newer immigrants filled prisons and asylums at higher rates than the older stock. Madison Grant, in his 1916 book The Passing of the Great Race, praised the supposed virtues of northern Europeans and warned against mixture with other peoples. The claims treated a passport as a measure of a person, and the quota tables wrote that treatment into federal law. Some scientists of the day disputed the eugenics findings, and later research discarded them, but in the 1920s the ranking carried the authority of expert testimony in the Capitol itself.

The Klan as a National Business

The second Ku Klux Klan was founded in 1915 by William J. Simmons on Stone Mountain in Georgia, in the same year that the film The Birth of a Nation glorified the first Klan. Professional promoters then sold it as a business. Recruiters called kleagles kept part of each ten dollar initiation fee, and members bought their robes from a Klan supplier. The order presented itself as the defender of white Protestant America. Its declared enemies included Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and bootleggers, as well as Black Americans, and it backed those declarations with parades, boycotts, intimidation, and violence. Membership estimates for the peak years run into the millions, and the order was strong in Indiana, Ohio, and Oregon as well as in the South. On August 8, 1925, some 30,000 members paraded down Pennsylvania Avenue in Washington. The collapse came from inside. Indiana Grand Dragon D. C. Stephenson was convicted of murder in 1925 in the death of Madge Oberholtzer, and from prison he exposed the officials he had worked with. Membership fell away through the rest of the decade.

The Bible, the Schoolroom, and Dayton

Fundamentalism defended a literal reading of the Bible against both modern theology and modern science, and its supporters wrote that defense into law. Tennessee passed the Butler Act in March 1925, which made it a crime for a public school teacher to teach that human beings had descended from a lower order of animals. The American Civil Liberties Union offered to defend a test case, and John Scopes, a young teacher in Dayton, agreed to stand trial. Clarence Darrow led the defense. William Jennings Bryan, three times a candidate for president, joined the prosecution. The judge barred most scientific testimony, so on July 20, in a session moved outdoors because of the heat and the crowd, Darrow questioned Bryan as an expert on the Bible. Bryan conceded that the days of creation in Genesis could be periods rather than twenty four hour days. The jury convicted Scopes and he was fined 100 dollars. Bryan died in Dayton five days after the trial ended. The Tennessee Supreme Court later upheld the law but reversed the conviction on a technicality in the fine.

Two Men in a Massachusetts Prison

On April 15, 1920, robbers shot and killed a paymaster and his guard during a payroll robbery in South Braintree, Massachusetts. On May 5, police arrested Nicola Sacco, a shoemaker, and Bartolomeo Vanzetti, a fish seller. Both were Italian immigrants and anarchists, and anarchism is the belief that government itself should be abolished. Both men were carrying guns when arrested, and both gave false answers under early questioning, which they later explained as fear of arrest for their political activity. A jury at Dedham convicted both men of murder on July 14, 1921, in a trial before Judge Webster Thayer that turned on disputed identification and firearms evidence. Appeals ran for six years and all were denied. By 1927 the case had drawn protests across Europe and Latin America. Governor Alvan Fuller appointed a committee headed by Harvard president A. Lawrence Lowell, which reported that the evidence supported the verdict, and Fuller refused clemency. At sentencing, Vanzetti told the court that he was suffering because he was a radical and because he was an Italian. Both men were executed on August 23, 1927.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What did the census of 1890 do inside the National Origins Act? The act set each country quota at 2 percent of its foreign born counted in the census of 1890. Because that census predated the heavy immigration from southern and eastern Europe, northern and western European countries received large quotas and the newer source countries received very small ones. Germany received 51,227 places and Great Britain and Northern Ireland 34,007, while Italy received 3,845, Russia 2,248, and Greece 100. The ranking was built into the formula before any individual applicant was considered. [Blueprint 8]

2. How did eugenics give the quota system a defense that simple prejudice could not give it? Eugenics presented the ranking of nationalities as a scientific finding rather than a preference. Harry Laughlin served the House immigration committee as an expert agent and presented figures claiming the newer immigrants filled prisons and asylums at higher rates. Madison Grant argued in print that northern Europeans carried superior inherited traits. A claim delivered as expert testimony and data could be cited in debate as fact, which let supporters describe exclusion as a conclusion drawn from evidence. The reading also notes that the findings were disputed at the time and discarded later. [Blueprint 8]

3. In what ways was the second Ku Klux Klan run like a business, and how did it fall? Professional promoters recruited members on commission. Kleagles kept part of each ten dollar initiation fee, and members bought robes from a Klan supplier, so growth produced income for the organization and its recruiters. The order sold itself as the defender of white Protestant America against Catholics, Jews, immigrants, bootleggers, and Black Americans. It fell after Indiana Grand Dragon D. C. Stephenson was convicted of murder in 1925 in the death of Madge Oberholtzer; from prison he exposed cooperating officials, and the scandal and infighting drained membership through the rest of the decade. [Blueprint 9]

4. What happened when Darrow questioned Bryan on July 20, 1925, and why did that exchange reach further than the verdict itself? In the outdoor session, Darrow examined Bryan as an expert on the Bible. Under questioning about Genesis, Bryan conceded that the days of creation could be periods rather than twenty four hour days, which undercut the strict literal position he had come to defend. The verdict itself only fined Scopes 100 dollars and was later reversed on a technicality. The exchange was reported nationwide and fixed the public meaning of the trial as a contest over whose account of human origins would be taught, and it followed Bryan to his death in Dayton five days later. [Blueprint 9]

5. What were the fights really about? Full credit answers name both halves of the lesson question and support each with evidence. On belonging: the quota formula ranked whole nations by the census of 1890, eugenics supplied a claimed scientific ranking of peoples, and the Klan sold a definition of the real American as white and Protestant. On knowledge: fundamentalist law decided which account of human origins a public school could teach, and the Scopes trial tested that decision in public. Sacco and Vanzetti can support either half, since Vanzetti told the court he suffered as a radical and as an Italian, and the case turned on whether the court had judged the men or their identities and beliefs. Answers that retell one episode without connecting it to belonging or knowledge earn partial credit. [Blueprint 8 and 9]
