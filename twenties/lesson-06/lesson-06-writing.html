Writing Task: Who Counted, and Whose Knowledge Counted

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: What were the fights over immigration, religion, and science really about: who counted as a real American, and whose knowledge counted as true? A strong answer covers both halves of the question. It shows how the decade tried to fix the definition of the real American, and how it tried to fix the definition of true knowledge, in law, in print, and in court. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, with at least one piece from each half of the bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

Belonging.

1. The National Origins Act of 1924 set each country quota at 2 percent of its foreign born counted in the census of 1890. Germany received 51,227 places a year, Great Britain and Northern Ireland 34,007, Italy 3,845, Russia 2,248, and Greece 100. The law also barred nearly all immigration from Asia.

2. Harry Laughlin of the Eugenics Record Office served the House immigration committee as an expert agent and presented figures ranking the newer immigrants below the older stock. Madison Grant's The Passing of the Great Race, published in 1916, argued that northern Europeans carried superior inherited traits.

3. The second Ku Klux Klan sold membership through commissioned recruiters and reached membership estimates in the millions. Its leader Hiram Evans wrote in 1926 that the Klan spoke for the old pioneer stock and did not try to represent any people but these. Some 30,000 members paraded in Washington on August 8, 1925. The order collapsed after Indiana leader D. C. Stephenson was convicted of murder in 1925.

Belief.

4. Tennessee passed the Butler Act in March 1925, which made it a crime to teach that human beings had descended from a lower order of animals. John Scopes stood trial at Dayton in July 1925 and was convicted and fined 100 dollars.

5. On July 20, 1925, Clarence Darrow questioned William Jennings Bryan as an expert on the Bible. Bryan conceded that the days of creation in Genesis could be periods rather than twenty four hour days. Bryan died in Dayton five days after the trial ended, and the Tennessee Supreme Court later upheld the law while reversing the conviction on a technicality.

6. Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti, Italian immigrant anarchists, were convicted of murder in 1921 on disputed identification and firearms evidence. Appeals were denied for six years, the Lowell committee upheld the verdict in 1927, and both men were executed on August 23, 1927. At sentencing, Vanzetti said he was suffering because he was a radical and because he was an Italian.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the lesson question: the fights were attempts to fix who counted as a real American and whose knowledge counted as true. A claim covering only immigration, or only Scopes, earns partial credit regardless of how well it is developed. (Blueprint 8 and 9)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, with at least one from the Belonging half and at least one from the Belief half. The 1890 census base and the quota figures support the belonging claim. (Blueprint 8) The Laughlin testimony and the Evans old stock definition support either the ranking claim or the respectability claim. (Blueprint 8 and 9) The Butler Act and Bryan concession support the knowledge claim, and Vanzetti sentencing statement supports the claim that a court judged identity. (Blueprint 9)
  • Explanation that connects each piece of evidence to the claim. An essay that lists a quota figure, a Klan parade, and a trial date without saying what definition each one tried to fix has assembled facts without making the argument, and should earn partial credit.
  • An ending that names the shared structure: in each fight, an institution, Congress, a legislature, a mass organization, or a court, claimed the authority to settle a definition, and the people placed outside that definition answered back.

Model opening for teacher reference. The fights of the 1920s were really about definition, and about which institution held the power to define. At the border, Congress defined the real American by arithmetic. The National Origins Act set quotas at 2 percent of the census of 1890, a year chosen because it counted the country before Italians, Poles, and Russians had arrived in large numbers, so Germany received 51,227 places a year and Italy 3,845. No official had to call any applicant unfit. The formula had done the ranking in advance, and eugenics testimony in Congress dressed that ranking as science. A full model continues in this pattern, turning to the belief half with the Butler Act and Bryan concession at Dayton or with Vanzetti sentencing statement, and closes on the shared structure rather than on any single episode.

Common weak answers. Retelling the Scopes trial as a colorful story, monkeys, heat, and famous lawyers, without the Butler Act's definition of lawful knowledge misses the belief half of the question. Treating the quota act as a simple limit on numbers, without the 1890 base year, misses the belonging half, because the limit alone did not rank anyone. Treating Sacco and Vanzetti as only a murder case ignores the evidence the lesson is built on.
