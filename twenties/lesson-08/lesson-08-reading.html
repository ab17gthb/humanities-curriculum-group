The Weak Spots and the Crash

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

Prosperity With Weak Places

The 1920s produced prosperity. Factory output climbed, and millions of families bought cars, radios, and appliances their parents never owned. But the prosperity was uneven from the start. Farmers, coal miners, and textile workers spent much of the decade in a depression of their own, ordinary families bought on installment debt, and income at the top flowed into speculation. The market that broke in October 1929 broke over these weak places.

The Farm Depression Ran All Decade

During the First World War, the government urged farmers to plant every acre, and high prices rewarded them. Many borrowed to buy land, machinery, and tractors. When the war ended, European farms recovered and demand fell, but American production stayed high. The result was overproduction: farmers grew more than buyers would purchase at prices covering their costs. Wheat averaged about $2.16 a bushel in 1919, about $1.03 in 1921, and about $1.04 in 1929, while debts stayed at wartime levels.

Farm organizations backed the McNary-Haugen bills, to support crop prices at home while surplus crops were sold abroad at world prices. Congress passed the bill in 1927 and 1928, and President Calvin Coolidge vetoed it both times. Roughly a quarter of the population still lived on farms, so the farm depression left much of the country unable to buy what factories made.

Sick Industries: Coal and Textiles

Coal was a sick industry. Oil and natural gas took heating customers away, and mines could produce more coal than the country would buy. Operators closed mines, cut wages, and fought the miners' union. Contemporary estimates put the fall in mine employment at roughly a quarter between 1923 and 1929. Textiles were sick differently: New England mills faced newer southern mills paying lower wages, and the industry could weave more cloth than customers wanted.

Labor Loses Ground

Union membership fell from about five million in 1920 to about three and a half million in 1929. Employers organized an open shop drive, which they called the American Plan. Under an open shop, a worker could be hired without joining a union, and in many shops only by promising not to join one. Courts backed employers with injunctions against strikes.

Some large employers took a second approach, welfare capitalism. They offered pensions, paid vacations, stock purchase plans, and company housing, hoping workers who received these benefits would see no need for a union. Many of the same firms set up company unions, which discussed grievances with management but were organized and financed by the employer. A company union could not bargain from an independent position or strike with real strength.

Paid at the Top, Borrowed at the Bottom

Income concentrated at the top. A Brookings Institution study estimated that in 1929 the richest five percent of Americans received about thirty percent of the nation's personal income. The wealthy could not spend fast enough to absorb factory output, so consumption depended on everyone else, whose wages rose slowly. Credit filled the gap: cars, furniture, and radios were sold on installment plans, a small payment at purchase and the rest monthly, and by contemporary estimates most durable goods were bought this way by 1929.

Florida Gave the Warning

The first speculative boom of the decade to collapse was in Florida land. Through 1925, buyers purchased lots, often on a small binder payment, expecting to resell within weeks at a higher price. Prices rose because the next buyer was expected, not because the land had changed. In 1926 the buyers stopped coming. Prices collapsed, banks that had lent on land failed, and in September 1926 a hurricane struck Miami, killing an estimated 370 people.

A Market Built on Borrowed Money

Stock prices climbed in 1928 and 1929. The Dow Jones Industrial Average reached 381 on September 3, 1929, roughly triple its level five years earlier. Speculation drove much of the rise. Speculators bought not for dividends but to resell at a higher price, usually buying on margin. Buyers paid part of the price, sometimes an estimated ten percent, and borrowed the rest from brokers against the stock itself. Estimates put brokers' loans at about $8.5 billion by the fall of 1929. Margin magnified a fall as well as a rise: when prices dropped, brokers demanded more cash, and buyers who could not pay were sold out immediately.

October 1929

The break began on Thursday, October 24, Black Thursday. Nearly thirteen million shares changed hands, and the ticker tape reporting prices ran hours behind. A bankers' pool bought to steady prices, and Richard Whitney placed a large order for U.S. Steel. The buying steadied the market for two days. On Monday, October 28, prices fell sharply again. On Tuesday, October 29, Black Tuesday, about 16.4 million shares were traded, the heaviest selling the exchange had ever recorded, and leading stocks lost roughly an eighth of their remaining value in one day. By mid November the average stood near 199, about half its September peak.

A Crash Is Not Yet a Depression

End with what the crash was not. Only a small minority of Americans owned stock, by most estimates a few percent of the population, so the crash did not wipe out most families' savings. Turning this blow into a decade of mass unemployment required more: bank failures, collapsing demand, and later policy choices. That bridge from crash to Depression is the subject of the next unit.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Why did farm prices stay low through the whole decade, and what did the McNary-Haugen bills propose? What happened to them? Wartime demand and high prices had pushed farmers to expand production and take on debt for land and machinery. When European farms recovered, demand fell, but expanded acreage and tractors kept American production high, so overproduction held prices down. Wheat averaged about $2.16 a bushel in 1919, about $1.03 in 1921, and about $1.04 in 1929, while farm debts stayed at wartime levels. The McNary-Haugen bills proposed federal support for crop prices at home, with surplus crops sold abroad at world prices. Congress passed the bill in 1927 and again in 1928, and President Coolidge vetoed it both times.

2. Coal and textiles were both called sick industries, but they were sick in different ways. Explain the problem in each industry and how owners responded at workers' expense. Coal lost its market: oil and natural gas took heating customers, and mines already built could produce more coal than buyers wanted. Textiles kept a market but had too much capacity, and New England mills faced newer southern mills paying lower wages. In both industries owners protected their position on the backs of workers. Coal operators closed mines, cut wages, and fought the miners' union, and mine employment fell by roughly a quarter between 1923 and 1929 by contemporary estimates. Mill owners cut wages, closed, or moved south.

3. Explain how the open shop drive and welfare capitalism, including company unions, worked toward the same result for independent unions. Both reduced the reasons and the ability of workers to organize independently. The open shop drive, promoted as the American Plan, kept union membership from being a condition of employment and often required workers to promise not to join a union at all; courts backed employers with injunctions, and membership fell from about five million in 1920 to about three and a half million in 1929. Welfare capitalism attacked the motive instead: pensions, vacations, stock plans, and company housing were offered so workers would feel provided for without a union. Company unions completed the structure by giving workers an organization that management created and financed, which could discuss grievances but could not bargain independently or strike with real strength. The benefits reached only a minority of workers.

4. What happened in Florida in 1926, and what should it have shown stock buyers about speculation and credit? The Florida land boom, in which buyers purchased lots on small binder payments expecting to resell at a profit, collapsed in 1926 when new buyers stopped arriving. Land prices fell, banks that had lent on land failed, and the September 1926 hurricane struck Miami, killing an estimated 370 people. The lesson available to stock buyers was that prices held up by expected resale and borrowed money fall as soon as the next buyer disappears, and that lenders who financed the boom are exposed when it ends. Margin buyers in 1929 repeated the Florida pattern in stocks.

5. Why was the prosperity of the 1920s uneven from the start, and what exactly broke in October 1929? Full credit answers cover both halves of the question. Uneven from the start: farmers spent the decade under overproduction and low prices while carrying wartime debts, with McNary-Haugen vetoed twice; coal and textiles were sick industries whose owners cut wages and jobs; union membership fell under the open shop drive while welfare capitalism and company unions substituted employer controlled benefits for independent bargaining; income concentrated at the top, a Brookings estimate putting about thirty percent of personal income with the richest five percent in 1929, while ordinary consumption ran on installment debt. What broke in October 1929: a stock market lifted by speculation and margin credit. On Black Thursday, October 24, nearly thirteen million shares traded and the ticker fell hours behind; a bankers' pool steadied prices briefly; on Black Tuesday, October 29, about 16.4 million shares were sold and the market lost roughly an eighth of its remaining value in a day; by mid November the Dow Jones average was about half its September peak of 381. The closing distinction is required for full credit: only a small minority of Americans owned stock, so the crash alone did not create a decade of depression. Bank failures, collapsing demand, and later policy choices built that bridge, and they belong to the next unit.
