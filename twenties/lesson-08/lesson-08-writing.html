Writing Task: Uneven From the Start

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Why was the prosperity of the 1920s uneven from the start, and what exactly broke in October 1929? A strong answer has two halves. The first shows that whole sectors and groups were weak all decade, using evidence about farms, coal and textiles, labor, income, or household debt. The second states precisely what broke in October 1929 and how margin credit turned a fall in prices into a collapse. End by separating the crash from the Depression. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. Wheat averaged about $2.16 a bushel in 1919, about $1.03 in 1921, and about $1.04 in 1929, while farm debts stayed at wartime levels. The McNary-Haugen bills to support crop prices passed Congress in 1927 and 1928 and were vetoed by President Coolidge both times. Roughly a quarter of the population still lived on farms.

2. Coal lost heating customers to oil and natural gas, and mine employment fell by roughly a quarter between 1923 and 1929 by contemporary estimates. Textile mills had more capacity than demand, and New England mills faced lower wage southern mills.

3. Union membership fell from about five million in 1920 to about three and a half million in 1929. Employers promoted the open shop, and large firms offered welfare capitalism benefits and set up company unions that management organized and financed.

4. A Brookings Institution study estimated that the richest five percent of Americans received about thirty percent of personal income in 1929. Most durable goods, including cars, furniture, and radios, were bought on installment plans by the end of the decade.

5. The Florida land boom, built on binder payments and expected resale, collapsed in 1926. Banks that had lent on land failed. Stock speculation repeated the pattern: the Dow Jones Industrial Average reached 381 on September 3, 1929, and brokers' loans were an estimated $8.5 billion that fall.

6. On Black Thursday, October 24, 1929, nearly thirteen million shares traded and a bankers' pool steadied prices for two days. On Black Tuesday, October 29, about 16.4 million shares traded. By mid November the Dow Jones average stood near 199, about half its peak. Only a small minority of Americans owned stock.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the lesson question: prosperity was uneven from the start, and a specific, identifiable thing broke in October 1929.
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, with at least one piece from the first half and one from the second. Wheat prices, the double veto of McNary-Haugen, and the farm population support the farm claim (Blueprint 12). Coal employment, textile overcapacity, falling union membership, the open shop, and company unions support the industry and labor claims (Blueprint 13). The Brookings income estimate, installment buying, the Florida collapse, and brokers' loans support the unequal prosperity and speculation claim (Blueprint 14). The share volumes of Black Thursday and Black Tuesday, the fall from 381 to about 199, and the small minority of stock owners support the crash claim (Blueprint 15).
  • A working explanation of the margin mechanism: buyers paid part of the price and borrowed the rest against the stock, so falling prices brought demands for cash, forced sales, and further falls. An essay that says prices fell because people panicked, without the loan mechanism, has skipped the second half of the question and should earn partial credit.
  • A closing distinction between the crash and the Depression. Because only a small minority of Americans owned stock, the crash alone did not create a decade of mass unemployment. Bank failures, collapsing demand, and later policy built that bridge, and they belong to the next unit.

Model opening for teacher reference. The prosperity of the 1920s was uneven from the start because it rested on sectors that were already weak and on income that never reached most buyers. Farmers spent the decade producing more than the country would purchase at prices covering their costs. Wheat that averaged about $2.16 a bushel in 1919 averaged about $1.04 in 1929, and when Congress twice passed the McNary-Haugen bills to support crop prices, President Coolidge vetoed them both. A full model continues in this pattern, adding one labor or credit fact from the bank, then turning to October 1929: a market carried by margin loans, in which a fall in prices forced sales that pushed prices lower still, until about 16.4 million shares changed hands on Black Tuesday. It closes on the distinction: the crash broke the speculative market and the credit under it, and it did not by itself make the Depression.

Common weak answers. Treating the crash as the cause of every weakness in the essay reverses the lesson: the farm problem, the sick industries, and labor's decline all predate 1929. Listing the decade's problems without the October mechanism answers only the first half of the prompt. Ending in 1932 or later, with mass unemployment treated as an October 1929 result, merges the crash with the Depression, which the prompt asks students to separate.
