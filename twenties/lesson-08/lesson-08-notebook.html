Teacher Answer Key

SectorWhat looked strong in 1929What the audit foundOne figure or fact to remember
FarmsFarms were mechanized and highly productive, and wartime expansion had made American agriculture look like a growth industry.Overproduction held crop prices down all decade while debts stayed at wartime levels. The McNary-Haugen remedy was vetoed twice, in 1927 and 1928.Wheat averaged about $2.16 a bushel in 1919 and about $1.04 in 1929.
Coal and textilesBoth industries still employed large workforces and supplied basic needs, coal for heat and power and textiles for clothing.Coal lost customers to oil and natural gas and could produce more than buyers wanted. Textiles had more capacity than demand, and New England mills faced lower wage southern mills. Owners cut wages and jobs in both.Mine employment fell by roughly a quarter between 1923 and 1929, by contemporary estimates.
LaborEmployment was high and strikes were few, which could be read as labor peace.The quiet was weakness, not strength. The open shop drive kept workers from having to join unions, and welfare capitalism and company unions substituted employer controlled benefits and representation for independent bargaining.Union membership fell from about five million in 1920 to about three and a half million in 1929.
Income and household creditConsumer sales set records, and cars, radios, and furniture reached millions of homes.Income concentrated at the top while most wages rose slowly, so the sales were financed by installment contracts that committed future wages. Demand built on borrowing stops when income stops.A Brookings estimate put about thirty percent of 1929 personal income with the richest five percent.
The stock marketStock prices rose for years, and rising prices were taken as proof that the economy was sound.The rise ran on speculation and margin credit. Buyers paid part of the price and borrowed the rest against the stock itself, so a price fall forced immediate selling by buyers who could not meet demands for more cash.Brokers' loans were an estimated $8.5 billion by the fall of 1929.

The Crash in Five Lines (Key)

September 3, 1929. The Dow Jones Industrial Average reached its peak of 381.

October 24, 1929 (Black Thursday). Nearly thirteen million shares changed hands, the ticker ran hours behind, and a bankers' pool bought to steady prices, holding the market for two days.

October 28, 1929. Selling resumed and prices fell sharply again; the bankers' support did not hold.

October 29, 1929 (Black Tuesday). About 16.4 million shares were traded, the heaviest selling the exchange had recorded, and leading stocks lost roughly an eighth of their remaining value in one day.

Mid November 1929. The Dow Jones average stood near 199, about half its September peak.

The lesson in one sentence. Strong answers name at least two weak places the chart records and connect them to the crash. Farm prices never recovered and farm debts stayed high; coal, textiles, and organized labor weakened all decade; income concentrated at the top while consumption ran on installment debt; and stock prices rested on margin loans, so when prices fell, forced selling followed. The sentence should end at the crash, not the Depression: only a small minority of Americans owned stock, and the bridge to a decade of depression was built later.
