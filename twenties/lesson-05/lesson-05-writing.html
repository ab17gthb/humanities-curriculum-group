Writing Task: What the Law Built

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Why did Prohibition fail to stop drinking, and what did it build instead? A strong answer does more than say that people kept drinking. It explains why enforcement could not close the supply, and it names the institutions the illegal trade created in place of the saloon. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim. Your answer must also deal with the strongest evidence on the other side: drinking fell at first.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The Volstead Act of 1919 defined intoxicating liquor as any beverage containing 0.5 percent or more alcohol by volume, which banned beer and light wine along with distilled liquor. Its exceptions for medicine, sacramental wine, industrial alcohol, and home cider and juice making left legal channels open.

2. The Prohibition Bureau began with roughly 1,500 federal agents, an estimate, for more than 110 million people and thousands of miles of border and coastline. New York repealed its state enforcement act in 1923. The bureau dismissed hundreds of agents for corruption.

3. Drinking fell sharply in the first years of Prohibition, above all among working class drinkers who could not pay illegal prices, and deaths from cirrhosis of the liver dropped.

4. The saloon was replaced by the speakeasy, an illegal bar behind a locked door. Estimates for New York City alone ran to tens of thousands, and inside the speakeasy men and women drank together in public in a way the saloon had rarely allowed.

5. Supply ran as an organized business: Canadian whiskey across the Detroit River, liquor landed from ships offshore, and hidden stills inside the country. In Chicago, Capone's Outfit ran breweries and speakeasies and collected income from gambling and racketeering.

6. Illegal businesses could not use courts, so disputes were settled by violence, including the murder of seven men in a Chicago garage on February 14, 1929. The Wickersham Commission report of 1931 documented evasion and corruption, then split over the remedy, while Pauline Sabin's Women's Organization for National Prohibition Reform organized women for repeal from 1929.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the lesson question: why the law failed to stop drinking, and what it built instead. (Blueprint 7)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. The 0.5 percent definition and its exceptions support the claim that the law outran its own enforcement. The agent estimate, the coastline, and New York's 1923 repeal support the enforcement arithmetic claim. Speakeasies, the supply routes, and the Outfit support the built instead claim. The 1929 massacre and the corruption dismissals support the cost claim. (Blueprint 7)
  • An honest treatment of bank item 3. The essay must concede that drinking fell at first and cirrhosis deaths dropped, and must then show why that fact does not overturn the claim, for example because the fall was sharpest where illegal prices bit hardest and consumption climbed again as the supply organized. An essay that omits item 3 has not met the prompt. (Blueprint 7)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. A paragraph that lists three facts without saying why enforcement failed or what the trade built has assembled evidence without making the argument, and should earn partial credit.
  • An ending that names the replacement institutions: the speakeasy, the organized supply business, corrupted enforcement, and the repeal movement. (Blueprint 7)

Model opening for teacher reference. Prohibition failed to stop drinking because it banned a product that millions of people still intended to buy, and then defended the ban with a force that could not cover the ground. Roughly 1,500 federal agents at the start faced more than 110 million people, two coasts, the Canadian border, and a definition of contraband that reached down to ordinary beer. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the supply organization and the speakeasy, conceding and answering the early fall in drinking, and closing on the illegal industry rather than on drinking alone.

Common weak answers. People are stubborn (Americans simply refused to obey) names an attitude and misses the enforcement arithmetic, the exceptions, and the profit that organized the supply. Crime got worse (Prohibition caused gangsters) reverses the lesson's order: the law created the illegal market first, and the organizations grew because the market was profitable and unpoliced, not because criminals appeared from nowhere.
