The Noble Experiment

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A Law Inherited from the War Years

Prohibition arrived in the 1920s as a finished law, not a proposal. The Eighteenth Amendment, ratified in January 1919, declared that after one year the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquors for beverage purposes would be prohibited across the United States. In January 1920 the ban took effect. Congress supplied the enforcement machinery in the Volstead Act of 1919, passed over President Woodrow Wilson's veto.

The act defined intoxicating liquor as any beverage containing 0.5 percent or more alcohol by volume. That low line banned beer and light wine along with whiskey and gin, which many voters had not expected. The act allowed narrow exceptions, including liquor prescribed by a doctor, wine for religious services, and alcohol for industrial use. Each exception required permits, and each permit opened a channel that illegal sellers soon learned to use.

Too Much Ground, Too Few Agents

Enforcement fell to a small federal force. At the start, the Prohibition Bureau had roughly 1,500 agents, an estimate that grew only slowly, to police more than 110 million people. Agents guarded thousands of miles of border and coastline, inspected permit holders, and raided breweries and stills. State help varied widely. Some states funded their own enforcement laws. New York repealed its state act in 1923 and left the federal government to work alone.

The arithmetic favored the lawbreaker. One truck crossing from Canada, one boat waiting offshore, or one hidden still could supply hundreds of customers. Agents were poorly paid, and the work placed them beside people who could pay far more for cooperation than the government paid for honesty. The bureau dismissed hundreds of agents for corruption, and dismissals removed only the cases it could prove.

Drinking Moved. It Did Not Stop.

The legal saloon closed, and drinking changed its address. In its place stood the speakeasy, an illegal bar reached through a locked door or a back room. Estimates vary widely: New York City alone was commonly said to hold tens of thousands. Inside, men and women drank together in public in a way the old saloon, which had served mostly men, had rarely allowed. Drinking became a mark of fashion and defiance for many young city residents.

Households joined the supply. The act permitted families to make fermented cider and fruit juices at home, and stores sold grape concentrate with warnings that read as instructions. Doctors wrote medicinal prescriptions in large numbers. Drinking fell sharply in the first years, above all among working class drinkers who could not pay illegal prices, and deaths from cirrhosis of the liver dropped. By mid-decade, consumption was climbing again in the cities.

The Supply Business

Illegal liquor reached a glass by three main routes. Smugglers brought Canadian whiskey across the border and the Detroit River, and landed liquor from ships waiting offshore. Distillers inside the country ran hidden stills in barns, basements, and warehouses. Distributors moved the product by truck to speakeasies and buyers. At every step the price rose, and the profit paid for trucks, bribes, and protection.

In Chicago, Al Capone turned that trade into a corporation in all but name. Capone took control of the organization built by Johnny Torrio, kept its division of the city into territories, and enlarged it. His organization, often called the Outfit, ran breweries and stills, managed speakeasies, and collected income from gambling and from racketeering, forcing businesses and unions to pay for protection they had not chosen. Contemporary estimates credited Capone with income in the tens of millions of dollars a year, a figure that cannot be verified.

Violence and Corruption as Business Costs

Illegal businesses cannot use courts to settle disputes, so they settle them with threats and guns. Rival gangs fought over territory and killed competitors and witnesses. On February 14, 1929, gunmen murdered seven men connected to the rival North Side gang in a Chicago garage, in the crime remembered as the St. Valentine's Day Massacre. No one was convicted, and the public widely attributed the killings to Capone's organization. Violence was not a breakdown of the illegal market. It was one of its regular costs.

Corruption worked the same way. An officer who ignored a speakeasy, an agent who warned a brewer before a raid, or a politician who protected a distributor received a share of the profit the law had created. Citizens who considered themselves honest bought illegal liquor and served it to guests, and showed their children that a constitutional amendment could be treated as a suggestion.

A Divided Verdict and a Coalition for Repeal

In 1929 President Herbert Hoover appointed a commission chaired by George W. Wickersham to study the problem. Its 1931 report documented widespread evasion, corruption, and contempt for the law, and then declined to recommend repeal, calling instead for stronger enforcement. Members filed separate statements that disagreed with one another, and one member concluded that there was no alternative but repeal.

By then a repeal coalition had formed. City voters, many of them immigrants, had opposed the law from the start, and business leaders argued that legal liquor would restore tax revenue. Most striking of all, Pauline Sabin founded the Women's Organization for National Prohibition Reform in 1929. Women expected to defend the dry cause as home protection now argued that the law had produced hypocrisy and crime. Prohibition had reduced drinking at first. On its own stated terms, a permanent end to the liquor traffic, it had failed. In place of the saloon it had built an illegal industry with its own capital, workforce, and hold on public officials.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What did the Eighteenth Amendment prohibit, and how did the Volstead Act definition widen the ban? The amendment prohibited the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquors for beverage purposes throughout the United States, effective January 1920. The Volstead Act defined intoxicating liquor as any beverage containing 0.5 percent or more alcohol by volume, so beer and light wine were banned along with distilled liquor. Many voters had expected a ban aimed at hard liquor and saloons, not at beer and wine.

2. Explain the enforcement problem in numbers. The Prohibition Bureau began with roughly 1,500 agents for a population of more than 110 million, and the force grew only slowly. Those agents had to cover thousands of miles of border and coastline, including the Canadian border, the Detroit River crossing, and ships waiting offshore, while also inspecting permit holders and raiding stills and breweries. State help was uneven. New York repealed its state enforcement act in 1923, leaving federal agents to work there without a state law behind them. The reading presents the agent figure as an estimate, and students should keep that label.

3. Evidence for the early fall in drinking, and reasons the illegal trade grew stronger. Early fall: overall drinking dropped sharply in the first years, especially among working class drinkers priced out of the illegal market, and deaths from cirrhosis of the liver fell. Growth of the trade: smuggling routes from Canada and offshore ships became regular supply lines; hidden stills and illegal breweries operated at warehouse scale; legal exceptions for medicine, religious wine, and home cider and juice making gave the trade cover and raw material; and speakeasies gave sellers a stable retail network with passwords and protected premises. Accept any two accurate reasons for each half.

4. Why is violence a regular cost of the illegal market rather than a breakdown of it? An illegal business cannot sue, enforce a contract, or call the police when a rival takes its territory or its shipment, so disputes are settled by threats and killing. Violence therefore performs the work courts perform for legal business, and firms budget for it. The example in the reading is the St. Valentine's Day Massacre of February 14, 1929, in which seven men connected to Chicago's North Side gang were murdered in a garage and no one was convicted.

5. Why did Prohibition fail to stop drinking, and what did it build instead? Full credit answers argue that the law banned a product for which demand remained, then assigned enforcement to a force too small and too poorly paid to close the supply, while legal exceptions and uneven state cooperation left channels open. What it built was an organized illegal industry: smuggling and distilling supply chains, the speakeasy as a new social institution that mixed men and women drinkers, syndicate organizations such as Capone's Outfit with income from liquor, gambling, and racketeering, routine corruption of police and agents, and finally a repeal coalition of city voters, business leaders, and organized women that crossed the old dry and wet lines. Any three accurate facts from the reading, tied to the claim, earn full credit. Answers that say only that people kept drinking should earn partial credit, because they name the failure and miss the industry the lesson is about.
