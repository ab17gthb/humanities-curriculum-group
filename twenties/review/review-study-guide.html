Part 2: Review Questions

Lesson 1: The Business of America

1. Trace the chain from the moving assembly line at Highland Park to the price of a Model T. State the labor time for a chassis in the fall of 1913 and by the spring of 1914, and the touring car price in 1909, in 1916, and the runabout price in 1925.

2. The five-dollar day combined a base wage with a profit share. State both parts, name the department whose investigators visited workers at home, and give two business reasons Ford gained from the plan. Use the 1913 hiring figure in your answer.

3. Advertising, installment buying, and chain stores worked as one system. Explain the part each one played in creating a nation of buyers for standardized goods.

Lesson 2: Mass Media and the Celebrity Age

4. Station KDKA made the first commercial broadcast on November 2, 1920. Explain how the networks that followed, the National Broadcasting Company in 1926 and the Columbia Broadcasting System in 1927, turned local listening into a shared national culture, and state who paid for the programs.

5. The Jazz Singer opened on October 6, 1927, and most of the film was silent. Explain why it still ended the silent era within two years.

6. Babe Ruth, Jack Dempsey, and Charles Lindbergh each became a national celebrity in this decade. Give one dated fact for each man, then state whose voices the new media left out, using the most popular radio show of the period as your evidence.

Lesson 3: America on Wheels

7. Passenger car registrations rose from about 8 million in 1920 to about 23 million in 1929, roughly one car for every five people. Name two developments that had to happen before ordinary families could own cars on that scale.

8. Describe the road building system the federal government and the states created between 1916 and 1926. Cover the Federal Aid Road Act, the Federal Highway Act, the gasoline tax, and the numbering of the United States highways.

9. The car moved courtship from the parlor to the street and moved families to suburbs built for drivers. Explain both changes, then name two kinds of business or town that lost ground as drivers passed them by.

Lesson 4: The Politics of Prosperity

10. State what Secretary of the Interior Albert B. Fall did with the naval oil reserves at Teapot Dome and Elk Hills, what he received from Edward L. Doheny, who led the Senate investigation, and how Fall's story ended in 1929.

11. Secretary of the Treasury Andrew W. Mellon argued that the wartime top income tax rate of 73 percent collected less revenue than a lower rate would. State his argument, the rate Congress reached in 1926, and the years of the three tax cuts.

12. American foreign policy in this decade acted and refused at the same time. Describe the Washington Conference ratios and the Dawes Plan circle of payments, then name three things the United States refused to do abroad.

Lesson 5: The Noble Experiment

13. Enforcement was an arithmetic problem before it was anything else. State the size of the Prohibition Bureau force at the start and the population it policed, name the state that repealed its own enforcement act in 1923, and explain why poorly paid agents were part of the problem.

14. Prohibition built an illegal industry. Describe the speakeasy, the supply chains that fed it, and the organization Al Capone built in Chicago, and state what happened in Chicago on February 14, 1929.

15. Build the ledger the Wickersham Commission faced. State two things the law achieved, two things it cost, and two things it built, then explain why the repeal coalition joined voters who agreed on little else.

Lesson 6: The Culture Wars

16. Compare the Emergency Quota Act of 1921 with the National Origins Act of 1924: the percentage and the census year in each. Then use the annual quotas for Germany, Italy, and Greece under the 1924 law to show where the ranking of peoples happened.

17. The second Ku Klux Klan sold itself as a business as well as a cause. Describe its recruiting system and its public march on Washington on August 8, 1925, name the groups it declared un-American, and explain how scandal in Indiana broke the order.

18. The Scopes trial and the Sacco and Vanzetti case both turned on whose word a court would trust. State the law John Scopes was charged under, the concession William Jennings Bryan made on the stand, and the outcome of the Sacco and Vanzetti case on August 23, 1927.

Lesson 7: The Great Migration and the Harlem Renaissance

19. Separate the pushes from the pulls of the Great Migration. Give two of each, with one figure: the Black population of Chicago in 1910 and in 1930.

20. The Chicago Defender did more than report the migration. State who founded the paper and when, how copies reached the South, what the Great Northern Drive of May 15, 1917 was, and how southern towns answered the paper.

21. The Harlem Renaissance and the organizing of the same years made one claim in two forms. State the claim of the New Negro, name the four rivers in the Hughes poem of 1921, and describe what Marcus Garvey and the UNIA built and what the NAACP tried to pass in Congress.

Lesson 8: The Weak Spots and the Crash

22. Wheat averaged about $2.16 a bushel in 1919 and about $1.04 in 1929. Explain overproduction as the cause, state what the McNary-Haugen bills proposed, and state what President Coolidge did with the bills Congress passed in 1927 and 1928.

23. Union membership fell from about five million in 1920 to about three and a half million in 1929. Explain the open shop drive, welfare capitalism, and the company union as three parts of one employer campaign, and name the two sick industries of the decade.

24. Reconstruct October 1929. State the Dow Jones peak of September 3, 1929, the estimated total of brokers' loans that fall, what happened on Black Thursday and on Black Tuesday, and why the crash by itself did not create the Depression.

Part 3: The Arguments of the Era

Every lesson in this unit is one side of an argument the country was having with itself. The test asks you to argue these positions, not just name them. Read each pair below and be ready to state both sides with evidence.

The business government against farm relief. Presidents Harding and Coolidge held that the federal government should tax lightly, spend less than it collected, protect American producers with the Fordney-McCumber Tariff, and leave business decisions to business, and Secretary Mellon cut the top income tax rate from 73 percent to 25 percent on the argument that lower rates would free capital for investment. Farmers answered that the same government refused every tool that could help them: Coolidge twice vetoed the McNary-Haugen bills while a quarter of the country lived on farms in a decade long depression. Teapot Dome stood between the two positions, proof to critics that a government close to business could also be a government for sale.

Enforcement against repeal. Drys defended Prohibition as a law that had cut drinking and cirrhosis deaths in its first years and deserved a force large enough to work. Wets answered with the ledger the law actually produced: roughly 1,500 federal agents for more than 110 million people, speakeasies estimated in the tens of thousands in one city, corrupted police, and syndicate crime. By 1931 the Wickersham Commission reported enforcement a failure while its members divided on repeal, and in 1933 the Twenty-first Amendment repealed the Eighteenth, the only repeal of an amendment in the Constitution.

Restriction against admission. Restrictionists argued that the country could choose its future population by formula, and the National Origins Act of 1924 wrote the choice into arithmetic: 2 percent of the census of 1890, which ranked northern and western Europe high and southern and eastern Europe low, defended in Congress by eugenics testimony. The other side of the decade ran on the labor of the people being restricted and on the migrants already moving inside the country: factories hired immigrant and Black workers the quota system and Jim Crow undervalued, and the cities they built became the centers of the new national culture.

Literal faith against modern science. Fundamentalists defended a literal reading of the Bible and state laws, led by Tennessee's Butler Act of 1925, that barred public schools from teaching human descent from lower animals. Teachers and scientists answered that a state could not settle a scientific question by statute. At Dayton the argument turned on testimony: Bryan, defending the literal reading, conceded the days of creation could be periods, and newspapers carried the exchange to the whole country. Scopes was convicted, and the argument over whose knowledge counted as true outlasted the fine.

Exclusion against the New Negro. The order of exclusion had law and numbers behind it: Jim Crow in the South, restrictive covenants and bombings in northern housing, a revived Klan marching an estimated 30,000 strong in the capital in 1925, and a quota act that ranked peoples by ancestry. Black Americans answered by moving, publishing, and organizing: the Great Migration redrew northern cities, the Defender called people north and named the drive, Hughes and the Harlem Renaissance claimed a history as old as the rivers, Garvey built the UNIA into a mass movement, and the NAACP carried the anti-lynching fight to Congress. Achievement and danger ran together all decade, from Harlem's flowering to the destruction of Tulsa's Greenwood district in 1921.

Headline prosperity against the audit. The headline of the decade was the bull market: a Dow Jones peak of 381, brokers' loans estimated at $8.5 billion, and new goods in millions of homes. The audit told a different story in the same years: wheat near a dollar a bushel, coal and textiles sick, union membership down by about a third, a Brookings Institution estimate placing about thirty percent of 1929 personal income with the richest five percent, and most durable goods bought on installment plans. The test of the decade is whether a student can hold both accounts at once and say which one October 1929 proved incomplete.

Teacher Answer Key

Part 1: Key Terms (model definitions, as given in the guided notes)

Lesson 1. assembly line: a factory arrangement in which a product moves past workers who each add one part or perform one task until the product is finished mass production: the making of goods in very large quantities using standardized parts and machinery, so that each item costs less to produce Model T: the low priced automobile built by the Ford Motor Company from 1908 to 1927 and sold to millions of ordinary buyers productivity: the amount of goods or work produced for each hour of labor or for each unit of input installment buying: purchasing a good by paying part of the price at the time of purchase and the rest in regular payments over time, usually with interest or fees added advertising: paid public messages that present a product to potential buyers and seek to create a desire to buy it chain store: one of a group of retail stores owned by a single company, carrying the same name, goods, and prices across many locations

Lesson 2. mass media: forms of communication that reach very large audiences at the same time, including radio, film, and newspapers sold across the country broadcast: to send a program by radio waves to many listeners at once from a single station radio network: a group of stations linked by wire that carry the same programs at the same time under one company talkie: a motion picture with recorded dialogue and sound, which replaced silent film after 1927 tabloid: a newspaper printed in a smaller format that used large photographs and bold headlines to report crime, scandal, and celebrity news celebrity: a person who is widely known and followed by the public because the media cover his or her life and work fad: a craze that spreads quickly through the public and fades within months, such as a dance, a game, or a stunt

Lesson 3. suburb: a residential area on the edge of a city, outside the center, where people live and travel into the city for work commuter: a person who travels regularly between home in one place and work in another, usually each day paved highway: a main road between places with a hard, surfaced roadway of concrete, asphalt, or brick that can be used in wet weather filling station: a roadside business that sells gasoline and oil and provides air, water, and basic repairs for automobiles tourism: travel for pleasure, and the businesses that serve travelers with food, fuel, lodging, and entertainment motel: a lodging built for motorists, from motor and hotel, in which guests park their cars beside or near their rooms dating: a form of courtship in which a young man and a young woman go out together to public amusements, away from the family home and its supervision

Lesson 4. Teapot Dome: the scandal in which Secretary of the Interior Albert B. Fall secretly leased federal naval oil reserves in Wyoming and California to private oil companies and accepted money from the oilmen in return; Fall was convicted and imprisoned tax cut: a reduction in the rates at which a tax is charged, here the cuts in federal income tax rates during the 1920s tariff: a tax charged on goods brought into a country from abroad isolationism: a policy of avoiding political commitments and alliances with other countries while keeping a country free to act on its own Washington Conference: the conference held in Washington, D.C., from November 1921 to February 1922, where the major naval powers agreed to limit the size of their battle fleets war debt: money one government owes another for wartime loans, here the debts the Allied governments owed the United States from the First World War Kellogg-Briand Pact: the agreement signed in Paris in 1928 in which the signing nations renounced war as an instrument of national policy

Lesson 5. Prohibition: the nationwide ban on the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquor for beverage purposes, in force under the Eighteenth Amendment from 1920 to 1933 Volstead Act: the National Prohibition Act of 1919, the federal law that enforced the Eighteenth Amendment and defined intoxicating liquor as any beverage containing 0.5 percent or more alcohol by volume bootlegger: a person who illegally made, transported, or sold liquor during Prohibition speakeasy: an illegal bar or club that sold liquor during Prohibition, often hidden behind a locked door or a legitimate business organized crime: criminal activity carried out by a structured group that operates like a business, with leaders, ranks, territory, and regular sources of income racketeering: the use of threats, violence, or a front business to extract money from businesses or workers, including forcing them to pay for protection or services they did not choose repeal: the cancellation of a law or constitutional amendment; the Twenty-first Amendment repealed the Eighteenth in 1933

Lesson 6. quota system: a method of limiting immigration by setting a fixed maximum number of immigrants that may be admitted from each country in a year National Origins Act: the federal immigration law of 1924 that set each country quota at 2 percent of its people counted in the census of 1890 and barred nearly all immigration from Asia eugenics: a movement that claimed ability and character were inherited and could be ranked by ancestry, and that argued government should favor the groups it ranked high and restrict the groups it ranked low Ku Klux Klan: the organization revived in 1915 that claimed to defend white Protestant America and used intimidation and violence against Black Americans, Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and others it declared un-American fundamentalism: a Protestant movement that defended a literal reading of the Bible and opposed public school teaching that conflicted with it evolution: the scientific theory that species change over generations and that present species, including human beings, developed from earlier forms of life anarchism: a political belief that government and all ruling authority should be abolished and society organized without a state

Lesson 7. Great Migration: the movement of hundreds of thousands of Black southerners to northern cities for jobs and greater rights during and after the war years New Negro: a Black American of the 1920s who claimed dignity and equal rights, took pride in Black history and culture, and refused a second class place Harlem Renaissance: the flowering of Black writing, music, and art in the 1920s, centered in Harlem in New York City jazz: music formed in Black communities of New Orleans from ragtime, blues, and brass band traditions, built on syncopation and improvisation blues: a Black musical form built on a repeating pattern of chords, with verses that name hardship, work, and endurance in plain language UNIA: the Universal Negro Improvement Association, Marcus Garvey's mass organization, founded to unite Black people worldwide and to build Black owned businesses and political power Chicago Defender: the Black newspaper founded in Chicago in 1905 by Robert S. Abbott, which urged Black southerners to move north and printed the news of lynching and discrimination that white papers ignored

Lesson 8. overproduction: production of more of a good than buyers will purchase at prices that cover the cost of producing it open shop: a workplace in which workers are not required to join a union in order to hold their jobs welfare capitalism: an employer's policy of providing benefits such as pensions, paid vacations, and recreation in order to keep workers satisfied and to keep unions out company union: an organization for discussing workers' grievances that is set up and financed by the employer rather than by an independent union speculation: buying an asset in the hope of selling it soon at a higher price, rather than for the income the asset produces buying on margin: buying stock by paying only part of the price and borrowing the rest from a broker, with the stock pledged as security for the loan Black Tuesday: October 29, 1929, the day of the heaviest selling in the stock market crash of 1929, when about 16.4 million shares were traded

Part 2: Review Questions

1. The line at Highland Park. On the moving line each worker performed one task on every chassis that passed, and interchangeable parts needed no fitting. Building a chassis fell from about twelve and a half hours of labor in the fall of 1913 to about an hour and a half by the spring of 1914. Ford passed the saving to buyers: the touring car fell from about $850 in 1909 to about $360 in 1916, and the runabout reached $260 in 1925. Output per worker rose, cost per car fell, price fell, and sales rose into the millions.

2. Five dollars a day. The plan announced January 5, 1914 paid about $2.34 as a base wage and about $2.66 as a share of company profits, and the shift fell from nine hours to eight. The profit share was conditional: investigators of the Sociological Department visited workers at home and could withhold it for drinking, gambling, taking in boarders, or an unclean house. Ford gained because turnover collapsed, in a plant that had hired about 53,000 men in 1913 to keep about 14,000 jobs filled, and because better paid workers could buy the cars they built while applicants lined up for Ford jobs.

3. One system of selling. Advertising created the desire, presenting goods families had never owned as goods a modern family needed, and it sold status and a way of life along with the product. Installment buying removed the price barrier: the buyer paid part at once, took the goods home, and paid the rest in regular payments with interest or fees added, and the seller could take the goods back if payments stopped. Chain stores supplied the same goods at the same low prices in town after town, under one name, and pressed local merchants who could not match their buying power.

4. One audience. KDKA in Pittsburgh broadcast the Harding and Cox election returns on November 2, 1920. A network linked stations by telephone wire so one program played in many cities at the same moment: NBC formed in 1926 and CBS followed in 1927. A family in Ohio and a family in Georgia heard the same songs, jokes, scores, and news at the same time, and schedules taught the country to keep the same hours. Advertisers and sponsors paid for the programs, so listening was free and the audience was the product sold to the sponsor.

5. The talkie. The Jazz Singer was mostly a silent film told with title cards, but its song scenes and one spoken scene proved audiences wanted sound. Theater owners who wired their houses for sound drew crowds that silent houses could not match, and within two years the major studios had converted to sound production. One part of one film changed the industry because owners and audiences acted on it at once, in every state, from the same print.

6. Celebrity and its limits. Jack Dempsey defended the heavyweight title against Georges Carpentier on July 2, 1921, in a fight whose gate passed one million dollars and whose radio account carried it past the ring. Babe Ruth hit 60 home runs in the 1927 season. Charles Lindbergh flew alone from New York to Paris in May 1927 and became the decade's hero on his return. The limits: the most popular network show, Amos and Andy, first broadcast in 1928, presented two Black characters played by white actors, so the best known Black voices on national radio were not Black voices, and Black and immigrant audiences built media of their own, including the Black press.

7. From 8 million to 23 million. Any two, fully stated: mass production cut the price of a car until a Model T sold for under $300 by the middle of the decade; installment credit let families buy before they had saved the full price; paved highways and gasoline tax road building made a car useful beyond city streets; rising factory wages and electrified production put steady pay in workers' hands. The fall in people per car, from about thirteen in 1920 to about five in 1929, records a car becoming a normal expectation of family life rather than a rich man's toy.

8. Roads as a system. The Federal Aid Road Act of 1916 offered matching federal money for state highway building. The Federal Highway Act of 1921 concentrated aid on a connected network of main routes instead of scattering it. States paid their share in part with taxes on gasoline, first adopted by Oregon in 1919 and levied by every state by 1929, so drivers paid for the roads as they used them. In 1926 the main routes received one numbering system, the United States highways, and the paved highway replaced the mud road mile by mile.

9. The car redraws daily life. Suburbs: earlier suburbs grew within walking distance of a streetcar or railroad station; a family with a car could live on cheap land beyond those lines, with a garage beside the house, and the head of the household became a commuter driving in each day. Courtship: dating replaced calling in the parlor under family supervision; a couple went out to a movie, a dance, or a drive, away from the house, and parents, ministers, and judges protested in print. Losers, any two: streetcar companies, which lost riders and cut or ended service; railroads, which lost short trip passengers; small town stores on bypassed main streets, whose customers drove to larger towns.

10. Teapot Dome. The Navy held oil reserves at Teapot Dome, Wyoming, and Elk Hills, California. In 1921 control passed to Secretary of the Interior Albert B. Fall, who leased both reserves in secret, without the competitive bidding the law expected, Teapot Dome to Harry F. Sinclair and Elk Hills to Edward L. Doheny. Doheny paid him $100,000 in cash, and Sinclair gave large loans and gifts. Senator Thomas J. Walsh of Montana led the Senate investigation that exposed the leases. Fall resigned, and in 1929 he became the first cabinet officer imprisoned for a crime committed in office.

11. Mellon's argument. The wartime top rate reached 73 percent. Mellon argued that a rate that high drove wealthy men to shelter income in tax exempt state and city bonds, where the money built nothing and paid no federal tax. Cut the rate, and the same capital would move into railroads, factories, and mines, earn taxable profits, and set men to work. Congress cut rates in 1921, 1924, and 1926, and the top rate fell to 25 percent. Accept the counterpoint for full credit when a student adds it: the cuts left the federal surplus to debt payment rather than to farm relief or new programs.

12. Action and refusal abroad. At the Washington Conference, November 1921 to February 1922, the major naval powers accepted battleship ratios of 5 to 5 to 3 for the United States, Britain, and Japan, and ten year limits on capital ship building. War debts ran in a circle held together by American credit: American banks lent to Germany under the Dawes Plan of 1924, Germany paid reparations to the Allies, and the Allies paid their war debts to the United States. Refusals, any three: membership in the League of Nations; membership in the World Court; any enforcement pledge behind the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928, in which the signing nations renounced war as an instrument of national policy; cancellation of the war debts; a lower tariff so debtors could earn dollars by trade. The Fordney-McCumber Tariff of 1922 raised duties while the government demanded repayment, and students should name that tension.

13. Too much ground, too few agents. The Prohibition Bureau began with roughly 1,500 agents for more than 110 million people, guarding borders and coastline, inspecting permit holders, and raiding breweries and stills. New York repealed its state enforcement act in 1923 and left the federal government to work alone. Agents were poorly paid beside men who could offer bribes, and the bureau dismissed hundreds of agents for corruption during the decade. One truck from Canada, one boat offshore, or one hidden still could supply hundreds of customers, so the arithmetic favored the lawbreaker from the start.

14. What the law built. The legal saloon closed and the speakeasy took its place, an illegal bar behind a locked door or a legitimate front, where men and women drank together in public as the old saloon had rarely allowed. Supply ran through organized chains: Canadian whiskey across the Detroit River, liquor landed from ships waiting offshore, and home stills. In Chicago, Johnny Torrio and then Al Capone organized supply and territory into the Outfit, a syndicate run like a business, and racketeering turned threats and protection into regular income estimated in the tens of millions of dollars. On February 14, 1929, gunmen murdered seven men in the St. Valentine's Day Massacre, the decade's clearest display of violence as a business cost.

15. The Wickersham ledger. Achieved, any two: drinking fell sharply in the first years; deaths from cirrhosis of the liver dropped; the legal liquor trade was closed. Cost, any two: gang violence; routine bribery of police and agents; a vast illegal market the law could not tax or inspect. Built, any two: the speakeasy network, estimated in the tens of thousands in New York City alone; syndicate organized crime; the repeal movement itself. The repeal coalition joined wet city voters and immigrants, business leaders who argued that legal liquor would restore tax revenue, and women of the Women's Organization for National Prohibition Reform, founded in 1929 by Pauline Sabin, who had supported Prohibition when it was adopted and now argued the law had produced hypocrisy and crime. The Wickersham Commission reported in 1931 that enforcement had failed while its members split on repeal, which is the divided verdict the lesson teaches.

16. Ranking by formula. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 allowed each nationality 3 percent of its foreign born counted in the census of 1910. The National Origins Act of 1924 cut the rate to 2 percent and moved the census year back to 1890, before the large immigration from southern and eastern Europe. Under the 1924 law Germany received 51,227 places a year, Italy 3,845, and Greece the minimum of 100, so about thirteen Germans could enter for each Italian. The ranking happened in the choice of the base year, before any person applied: the table names no race or religion, and still sorts peoples by ancestry. The same law barred nearly all immigration from Asia by excluding aliens ineligible for citizenship, and supporters defended the ranking with eugenics, presented by Harry Laughlin of the Eugenics Record Office as expert testimony to the House committee.

17. The second Klan as a business. William J. Simmons refounded the order in 1915 on Stone Mountain, Georgia. Recruiters called kleagles kept part of each ten dollar initiation fee, and members bought robes from a Klan supplier, so growth paid the men who produced it. The order declared itself the defender of white Protestant America against Black Americans, Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and bootleggers. On August 8, 1925, an estimated 30,000 members paraded down Pennsylvania Avenue in Washington in daylight, in robes, before crowds of spectators. The collapse followed in Indiana, where Grand Dragon D. C. Stephenson was convicted of murder in the death of Madge Oberholtzer and, from prison, exposed the officials he had corrupted.

18. Two trials of trust. Tennessee's Butler Act of March 1925 made it a crime to teach that human beings had descended from a lower order of animals, and John Scopes was charged under it at Dayton. Clarence Darrow called William Jennings Bryan as a Bible expert, and Bryan conceded that the days of creation in Genesis could be periods rather than twenty four hour days, a move away from the literal reading the law rested on. Scopes was convicted and fined $100, and the fine was later set aside on a technical point. Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti, Italian anarchists convicted of murder in 1921 on disputed identification and firearms evidence, were reviewed by the trial judge, the state supreme court, and Governor Fuller's committee under A. Lawrence Lowell, which called the trial fair. Both men were executed on August 23, 1927, after protests in cities across the United States, Europe, and Latin America.

19. Push and pull. Push, any two: Jim Crow law and disfranchisement by poll taxes, literacy tests, and fraud; sharecropping accounts kept by landlords, with lynching enforcing the order; the boll weevil ruining cotton harvests; the Mississippi flood of 1927 and the guarded levee camps. Pull, any two: northern factory, stockyard, and railroad wages several times farm pay, with employers short of workers after war production and the cutoff of European immigration; the vote in most northern states; schools the law did not split by race. Chicago counted about 44,000 Black residents in 1910 and about 234,000 in 1930.

20. The Defender as an organizer. Robert S. Abbott founded the Chicago Defender in 1905. Pullman porters carried bundles of the paper south on the trains, past towns where its sale was forbidden. The paper printed train schedules, job notices, and migrants' letters, and it named the movement itself, setting May 15, 1917 as the Great Northern Drive, a day to leave together. In Emmett J. Scott's count its circulation rose from about 50,000 to about 125,000 in 1918. Southern towns answered by forbidding the paper's sale, which is evidence the paper was moving people and not only reporting a movement.

21. One claim, two forms. The New Negro claimed dignity and equal rights, took pride in Black history and culture, and refused a second class place. In art: Langston Hughes published The Negro Speaks of Rivers in The Crisis in 1921; its speaker claims the Euphrates, the Congo, the Nile, and the Mississippi in that order, a history running from the first dawns to Lincoln and the sunset, as his own. In organization: Marcus Garvey's UNIA, the Universal Negro Improvement Association, built a mass membership, Black owned businesses including the Black Star Line, and Liberty Hall in New York as its center, while the NAACP pressed Congress for the Dyer anti-lynching bill, which passed the House and died under Senate filibuster. Tulsa belongs in a full answer: the same decade destroyed the Greenwood district in 1921.

22. The farm problem. Wartime prices led farmers to borrow for land and machinery. When European farms recovered after the war, demand fell and American production stayed high: overproduction, more grown than buyers would purchase at prices covering costs, so each farmer planting more to cover fixed debts pushed prices lower for all. Wheat fell from about $2.16 a bushel in 1919 to about $1.03 in 1921 and about $1.04 in 1929, while debts stayed at wartime levels. The McNary-Haugen bills proposed federal support for crop prices at home, with surplus crops sold abroad at world prices. Congress passed the bill in 1927 and again in 1928, and President Coolidge vetoed it both times. Roughly a quarter of the population still lived on farms, so low farm income weakened demand for factory goods across the whole economy.

23. Labor in decline. The open shop drive, which employers called the American Plan, pressed the rule that no worker need join a union to hold a job, and courts backed employers with injunctions. Welfare capitalism offered pensions, paid vacations, and recreation from the employer, so a worker would see no need for a union. The company union let workers discuss grievances in a body the employer set up and financed, so it could not bargain from an independent position. Membership fell from about five million in 1920 to about three and a half million in 1929. The sick industries were coal, losing heating customers to oil and natural gas with mine employment down by roughly a quarter between 1923 and 1929 on contemporary estimates, and textiles, where New England mills faced newer southern mills paying lower wages and the industry could weave more cloth than customers wanted.

24. October 1929. The Dow Jones average peaked at 381 on September 3, 1929. Speculators bought on margin, paying part of the price and borrowing the rest from brokers with the stock as security, and brokers' loans reached an estimated $8.5 billion that fall. The break began on Black Thursday, October 24, when nearly thirteen million shares changed hands; a bankers' pool steadied prices for two days. On Black Tuesday, October 29, about 16.4 million shares were traded and leading stocks lost roughly an eighth of their remaining value in one day; by mid November the average stood near 199, about half the peak. The distinction: only a small minority of Americans owned stock, farms and sick industries had been weak all decade, and consumption ran on installment debt under concentrated income. The crash broke the market. Turning that break into a decade of depression required the bank failures, credit collapse, and policy choices of the years after 1929, which the next unit teaches.

Part 3: The Arguments of the Era

The summary is the answer material for Part 3. On the test, credit either side of each pair when the student states it accurately and supports it with one era fact from the lessons.
