The Business of America

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

The Line at Highland Park

In 1913 the Ford Motor Company began moving automobile chassis past its workers on a powered line at its Highland Park plant outside Detroit. Before the moving line, a crew had gathered around one chassis and built it where it stood. On the line, the work came to the worker instead. Each man stood in one place, added the same part or made the same motion on every chassis that passed, and let the line carry the car onward. Because every Model T was built to one design with interchangeable parts, no fitting was needed at any station.

The gain in productivity was immediate. Building a chassis had taken about twelve and a half hours of labor in the fall of 1913. By the spring of 1914 the line had cut the time to about an hour and a half, and Ford passed much of the saving to buyers. A touring car that sold for about $850 in 1909 sold for about $360 in 1916, and the runabout, the cheapest model, reached $260 in 1925. Ford built more than a million cars a year by the early 1920s. Mass production, the making of goods in very large quantities to a single standard design, became the normal way American factories worked.

Five Dollars a Day

The line solved a production problem and created a labor problem. The work was fast, fixed in pace, and endlessly repeated, and workers quit in large numbers. In 1913 Ford hired about 53,000 men to keep about 14,000 jobs filled. On January 5, 1914, the company announced the five-dollar day. Pay roughly doubled, the shift fell from nine hours to eight, and a worker could earn in about two months what the cheapest car cost.

The five dollars was not a simple wage. A worker kept a base wage of about $2.34 a day, and the rest came as a share of company profits, paid only to workers who met company conditions. Most women were excluded at first, as were men under twenty-two and new hires. Ford created a Sociological Department whose investigators visited workers at home. A man could lose his profit share for drinking, gambling, a dirty house, taking in boarders, or failing to save. The plan cut turnover sharply, and it made the company an authority inside the worker's home.

Electricity in the Factory and the Home

Electric power carried the new system beyond the automobile plant. In factories, individual electric motors replaced central steam engines and overhead shafts, so each machine ran on its own power. In the home, the share of dwellings wired for electricity rose from about one in six in 1912 to about half by the middle of the 1920s. Wiring created a market for a flood of appliances: washing machines, vacuum cleaners, electric irons, toasters, and refrigerators that cooled themselves and ended the daily visit of the iceman. Each appliance was a standardized factory product sold under a national brand name, and each raised the standard of what a normal home was expected to contain.

Selling the Goods

Factories that could produce goods by the million needed buyers by the million, and advertising became the tool for finding and teaching them. National brands advertised in magazines, on billboards, and in newspapers across the country. The copy rarely stopped at describing a product. Advertisements sold cleanliness, health, status, and modern life itself, and presented the family without the product as behind the times. Advertising turned goods most families had never owned, such as a refrigerator, into goods that families felt they needed.

Buying on Time

Installment buying closed the remaining gap between desire and price. A buyer paid part of the price down, took the goods home, and paid the rest in weekly or monthly installments, with interest or a carrying charge added. In the 1920s the plan spread to automobiles, radios, appliances, furniture, and clothing. Contemporary estimates held that a large share of new cars, by some reckonings three quarters, were sold on installments by the middle of the decade. Buying on time raised sales at once, since a family no longer had to save the full price first. It also committed future wages before they were earned, and repossession waited for the buyer who fell behind.

Chain Stores and the Local Merchant

The goods reached buyers through stores reorganized on factory principles. A chain store was one of many outlets owned by a single company and run to one plan, with the same name and prices in every town. The Great Atlantic and Pacific Tea Company, the A and P, grew from about 4,300 stores in 1920 to about 15,000 by the end of the decade. Chains bought in carload lots, sold for cash, and offered no delivery and little credit, so their prices undercut the independent merchant, who bought in small lots and carried his customers on credit. Shoppers followed the lower prices, and independent merchants organized in protest.

One System

Radio tied the system together in the home. Commercial broadcasting began in 1920, and within the decade millions of families owned sets. Sponsored programs carried advertising into the parlor and taught the same brand names to listeners in every region. Production and consumption were not two separate stories. Standardized goods were cheap because factories made them in enormous quantities, and factories could sell enormous quantities because advertising taught desire, installment plans supplied credit, chain stores cut distribution costs, and higher wages put cash in some workers' hands. Each part fed the others, and together they remade American economic life.

Teacher Answer Key

1. How did the moving assembly line change the work of building a car, and what did it do to the time required? Before the line, a crew gathered around a stationary chassis and built it where it stood. On the moving line the chassis traveled past workers who each stayed in one place and repeated one task, and interchangeable parts removed the need for fitting. Chassis building time fell from about twelve and a half hours of labor in the fall of 1913 to about an hour and a half by the spring of 1914.

2. Why is describing the five-dollar day as a raise incomplete? Only about $2.34 a day was base wage. The remainder was a share of profits paid only to workers who met company conditions. Most women, men under twenty-two, and new hires were excluded at first, and investigators of the Sociological Department visited workers' homes and could remove the profit share for drinking, gambling, a dirty house, taking in boarders, or failing to save. The pay was therefore conditional, and the company used it to supervise private life as well as factory work.

3. Name two ways installment buying raised sales, and the risk it created for the buyer. It let a family take goods home after paying only part of the price, so the family no longer had to save the full price before buying. It spread the cost across weekly or monthly payments that fit a wage earner's income, which brought cars, radios, and appliances within reach of buyers who could never have paid cash. The risk was debt and repossession. The buyer committed future wages before earning them, paid interest or a carrying charge on top of the price, and lost the goods if payments fell behind.

4. Why could a chain store charge lower prices than an independent merchant? Any three, accurately stated. Chains bought in carload lots from company warehouses and paid wholesale prices an independent buying in small lots could not match. They sold for cash and offered no delivery and little credit, which removed the cost of those services. Each store was small, standardized, and cheap to run. The same name, stock, and prices in every town let the company spread its costs across thousands of outlets.

5. How did mass production and mass consumption remake American economic life in the 1920s? Full credit answers argue that the two sides formed one system. The assembly line and interchangeable parts cut costs and prices, as the Model T price series shows. Higher output then required mass buying, which advertising created by teaching desire, installment buying financed by committing future wages, chain stores delivered at lower distribution cost, and radio promoted nationally. Wages such as the five-dollar day put cash in some workers' hands and fed the buying back into the factories. Any three accurate facts from the reading, tied to the system claim, earn full credit. Answers that describe production only, or shopping only, earn partial credit.
