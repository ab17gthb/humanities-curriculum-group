The Business of America

The Twenties · Lesson 1 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: The Twenties, Lesson 1 of 8 — the opening lesson of the unit. It builds the economic system the rest of the unit lives inside: mass production joined to mass consumption. Teach it first, before the lessons on consumer culture, the automobile age, and the politics and foreign policy of the decade, and well before the lessons that expose the prosperity's weaknesses and the crash.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “The Business of America” and the guided notes, which follow the deck's order exactly — the line at Highland Park, five dollars a day, electricity and appliances, selling the goods, buying on time, chain stores, and radio and one system, closing with The System at a Glance table. The activity is “Consumer Culture Stations: the falling price, five dollars a day, No More Ice, buying on time, the chain store.” The notebook page asks students to rebuild the system at a glance in their own words. The quick check is “The Line and the Advertisement: the line, the wage, and the Frigidaire advertisement.” The writing task is “One System: the lesson question as an essay.”

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: No prior unit is required beyond a general sense that factories made goods before the 1920s. Everything else is taught in order: the moving line and interchangeable parts, the chassis-time and Model T price figures, the five-dollar day and its conditions, wired homes and appliances, advertising, installment buying, chain stores, and radio.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.14 (analyze multiple and complex causes and effects — production, wages, advertising, credit, and distribution weighed together as one system), D2.His.13 (continuity and change — a factory and buying system built just before the decade remaking daily life inside it), D2.Econ.13 (explain how incentives and exchange shaped behavior — the five-dollar day, installment credit, and chain-store prices); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from the line to one system), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task: answer the lesson question as a system, not a list).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain how the moving assembly line cut chassis time and Model T prices, why the five-dollar day was not a simple raise, how electricity created a market for appliances, how advertising taught desire, how installment buying supplied credit and created risk, why chain stores charged less than independents, and how production and consumption fed each other as one system.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening, question, and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; the line at Highland Park (slides 4–6) 7 minutes; five dollars a day (slides 7–9) 7 minutes; electricity and appliances (slides 10–12) 6 minutes; selling the goods (slides 13–14) 5 minutes; buying on time (slides 15–17) 6 minutes; chain stores (slides 18–20) 6 minutes; radio, one system, and vocabulary (slides 21–25) 6 minutes; the turn to work (slide 26) 2 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. Warn students early that the guided notes end with The System at a Glance table, which they will rebuild from memory.

Key vocabulary

assembly line · mass production · Model T · productivity · installment buying · advertising · chain store

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: the title photograph filling the slide (the moving assembly line at Highland Park, 1913); the unit and lesson line “The Twenties · Lesson 1”; the title “The Business of America”; the standfirst — “How making goods by the million created buying by the million.”

  • Picture: The moving assembly line inside the Ford Motor Company plant at Highland Park, outside Detroit, in 1913. Its date needs saying plainly at the start: the picture belongs to 1913, before the decade this unit studies, because the system described was built just before the Twenties and then filled the Twenties. Chassis stand at successive stages of completion while the workers stay in one place.
  • Open the unit here. The decade is often remembered for music and parties, but its deepest change was economic.
  • A new way of making goods joined a new way of selling and buying them, and together they remade daily life.
  • This lesson follows that joining part by part: the line, wages, electricity, advertising, credit, chain stores, and radio, in the order of the guided notes.
  • Students should leave able to answer the lesson question as one system, with prices and figures, not as two separate lists.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the question — “How did mass production and mass consumption remake American economic life in the 1920s?”; below it, the unit question: “How did the prosperity of the 1920s change American life, and why was that prosperity weaker than it looked?”

  • [Read the lesson question aloud,] then the unit question beneath it as the frame for the whole Twenties unit.
  • Notice the last clause of that unit question, about prosperity being weaker than it looked. Keep it in reserve today.
  • This lesson builds the strong side of the story first: output rose, prices fell, wages at Ford doubled on paper, and goods spread into ordinary homes.
  • The weaknesses are already visible inside the success, and students should be asked to spot them as they appear. The five dollars came with conditions and home inspections. Installment buying committed wages before they were earned. Chain store prices rested on squeezing out the independent merchant.
  • Later lessons take up the farm economy, uneven income, and the crash.
  • For today, hold students to the system standard at the exit ticket: an answer about factories alone, or about shopping alone, earns only partial credit, because the reading's claim is that the two sides fed each other.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title “Making and buying become one system”; seven rows — A, The line at Highland Park (a moving line, one task per worker, 1913); B, Five dollars a day (pay roughly doubled, with conditions, 1914); C, Electricity and appliances (motors in the factory, wiring in the home); D, Selling the goods (advertising teaches desire, nationally); E, Buying on time (part down, the rest in weekly or monthly payments); F, Chain stores (one company, one plan, thousands of outlets); G, Radio and one system (sponsored programs carry brands into the parlor).

  • This map follows the guided notes section by section, so students can use the letters on screen to find their place in their notes all hour.
  • Sections A and B cover production and the wage that production made necessary: the moving line at Highland Park and the five-dollar day that followed it by a few months.
  • Section C follows electric power out of the factory and into the home, where wiring created a market for appliances.
  • Sections D, E, and F cover the selling side in order: advertising that taught desire, installment buying that supplied credit, and chain stores that cut the cost of getting goods to buyers.
  • Section G closes with radio and with the reading's final claim, that production and consumption were one system in which each part fed the others.
  • Tell students the table at the end of their guided notes, The System at a Glance, asks them to rebuild exactly this map from memory.

Slide 4 · Section A: The Line at Highland Park (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (the Highland Park plant, 1913); the section label “Section A”; the title “The Line at Highland Park”; the lines — “The work came to the worker. The car moved. The man stayed.”

  • Picture: The Library of Congress picture of the Highland Park plant in 1913, the same image as the title slide in a tighter crop. Its date deserves one sentence: it was taken in 1913, before the Twenties, in the first year of the moving line itself.
  • Look at what the frame actually shows. Chassis stand at successive stages of completion, each one further built than the last, inside a plant built around the line's path.
  • Nothing in the picture is decorative. The building, the spacing, and the men's positions all serve one arrangement, in which a partly built car travels and a worker repeats a single task as it passes.
  • Section A covers that arrangement and its two measurable results: the collapse in the time needed to build a chassis, and the fall in the price of the finished car.
  • Both results are in the guided notes as blanks, so students should have pencils ready for numbers from here on.

Slide 5 · One design, no fitting

On the slide: the photograph at right (an early Model T touring car, 1908); five bullets — In 1913, Ford began moving chassis on a powered line at Highland Park. Before the line, a crew built one chassis where it stood. On the line, each worker stayed in one place and performed one task on every car. Every Model T was built to one design, with interchangeable parts. No fitting was needed at any station.

  • Picture: An early Model T touring car on display, from a Library of Congress glass negative dated 1908 in the catalog listing. The early date is the point: the car predates the moving line by five years. Ford first fixed the product, then rebuilt the factory around it.
  • In the guided notes definition, the Model T is the low priced automobile built by the Ford Motor Company from 1908 to 1927 and sold to millions of ordinary buyers.
  • Because every car was built to one design with interchangeable parts, no fitting was needed at any station.
  • An assembly line, in the guided notes definition, is a factory arrangement in which a product moves past workers who each add one part or perform one task until the product is finished.
  • Before the line, a crew gathered around one chassis and built it where it stood. On the line, the work came to the worker instead, and the same motion repeated on every chassis that passed.

Slide 6 · $260: the cheapest Model T in 1925

On the slide: the big figure “$260”; the label — “the price of a Ford runabout in 1925, the cheapest Model T”; the note — “Touring car: about $850 in 1909, about $360 in 1916 · Chassis time: twelve and a half hours of labor down to an hour and a half.”

  • $260 — the price of a Ford runabout in 1925, the cheapest Model T
  • Touring car: about $850 in 1909, about $360 in 1916
  • Chassis time: twelve and a half hours of labor down to an hour and a half
  • Dwell on the price series, all of it from the reading, because it converts a factory arrangement into something a family could feel.
  • Behind the prices sits the time figure, and students should connect the two aloud. Building a chassis had taken about twelve and a half hours of labor in the fall of 1913. By the spring of 1914 the line had cut the time to about an hour and a half.
  • That gain is productivity, in the guided notes sense of output for each hour of labor, and Ford passed much of the saving to buyers instead of keeping it all as profit.
  • The result was scale: Ford built more than a million cars a year by the early 1920s, and mass production became the normal way American factories worked. Cheapness and volume were not two facts. Each one caused the other.

Slide 7 · Section B: Five Dollars a Day (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section B”; the title “Five Dollars a Day”; the line — “The line solved a production problem and created a labor problem.”

  • [Set up the puzzle of this section before any figures.] The line made each car far cheaper to build, but it did so by making the work fast, fixed in pace, and endlessly repeated.
  • A worker performed the same motion on every chassis, at the line's speed, all shift long. Men who had built whole cars now tightened the same part hundreds of times a day, and they responded in the simplest way available to them: they quit.
  • Turnover on that scale threatened the whole gain, because a line staffed by constant beginners cannot hold its pace or its quality.
  • Ford's answer, announced at the start of 1914, became the most famous wage in American industry: five dollars a day.
  • This section gives the announcement, the arithmetic behind it, and then the fine print, because the fine print is where the argument lives. The exit ticket's first question turns entirely on the difference between the headline and the fine print.

Slide 8 · The line drove workers away

On the slide: the photograph at right (Ford assembly line workers, 1913); five bullets — The work was fast, fixed in pace, and endlessly repeated. Workers quit in large numbers. In 1913, about 53,000 men were hired to keep about 14,000 jobs filled. On January 5, 1914, Ford announced the five-dollar day. Pay roughly doubled, and the shift fell from nine hours to eight.

  • Picture: Ford assembly line workers in 1913, the very year of the turnover figures in the reading. Its date should be stated: 1913, before the Twenties, in the year Ford had to hire about 53,000 men to keep about 14,000 jobs filled.
  • Do that arithmetic aloud. The company hired nearly four men for every job it kept filled, which means most new hires left within months. Training replacements cost money, and a line missing experienced hands slows or stops.
  • On January 5, 1914, the company announced the five-dollar day. Pay roughly doubled, the shift fell from nine hours to eight, and a worker could earn in about two months what the cheapest car cost.
  • That last comparison is the one Ford wanted printed, and it was true as far as it went: the men who built the cars could now plausibly buy one.
  • The plan cut turnover sharply. What the five dollars actually was, and who could actually get it, is the next slide.

Slide 9 · Not a simple raise

On the slide: five bullets — The base wage was about $2.34 a day. The rest was a share of company profits, paid only under company conditions. Most women, men under twenty-two, and new hires were excluded at first. The Sociological Department visited workers at home. The profit share could be lost for drinking, gambling, a dirty house, taking in boarders, or failing to save.

  • This slide is the fine print, and the exit ticket tests it directly, so walk through it slowly. The five dollars was not a simple wage.
  • A worker kept a base wage of about $2.34 a day, and the rest came as a share of company profits, paid only to workers who met company conditions.
  • Most women were excluded at first, as were men under twenty-two and new hires, so the famous wage did not cover the whole workforce even inside Ford.
  • Ford created a Sociological Department whose investigators visited workers at home. A man could lose his profit share for drinking, gambling, a dirty house, taking in boarders, or failing to save.
  • Name the exchange plainly. In return for higher pay, the company claimed authority inside the worker's home, over his habits, his housekeeping, and his savings.
  • The plan worked on its own terms: turnover fell sharply, and the line kept its experienced men. Whether it was generosity, labor policy, or social control is a judgment students can defend either way, with these facts.

Slide 10 · Section C: Electricity and Appliances (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section C”; the title “Electricity and Appliances”; the line — “Electric power carried the new system beyond the automobile plant.”

  • The automobile plant is where the lesson starts, but the system did not stay there, and this short section is the bridge from production to the home.
  • Electric power did two kinds of work in the 1920s. Inside factories, it changed how machines were driven, freeing each machine from the central steam engine and the overhead shafts that had dictated the layout of the older plant.
  • Inside homes, wiring spread from a minority of dwellings to about half of them, and every newly wired house became a market for goods that had not existed a generation earlier.
  • Keep the direction of causation in front of students. The appliances were not a separate story about comfort. Each one was a standardized factory product, made by mass production, sold under a national brand name, and bought, very often, on installments.
  • Section C therefore sets up everything after it: advertising, credit, and chain stores all presuppose a wired home full of branded goods to sell.

Slide 11 · Power to each machine

On the slide: four bullets — Individual electric motors replaced central steam engines and overhead shafts. Each machine ran on its own power. About one in six homes was wired for electricity in 1912. By the middle of the 1920s, about half of dwellings were wired.

  • Two changes sit on this slide, one in the factory and one in the home, and the reading treats them as one development.
  • In factories, individual electric motors replaced central steam engines and overhead shafts, so each machine ran on its own power. A plant no longer had to arrange every machine along a line of shafts, and a single machine could run without turning the whole system.
  • In the home, the share of dwellings wired for electricity rose from about one in six in 1912 to about half by the middle of the 1920s.
  • Students should register how fast that is: within roughly a dozen years, electric light and power went from a minority convenience to the condition of a normal house.
  • Wiring by itself sold nothing except current. What it created was a market, a house by house demand for machines that ran on the new power. The next slide names those machines, and the guided notes ask students to name three of them from memory.

Slide 12 · The wired home

On the slide: five bullets — The new appliances included washing machines, vacuum cleaners, and electric irons. Toasters and refrigerators that cooled themselves followed. The refrigerator ended the daily visit of the iceman. Each appliance was a standardized factory product sold under a national brand name. Each appliance raised the standard of what a normal home was expected to contain.

  • Read the appliance list slowly, because the guided notes ask for any three of these five: washing machines, vacuum cleaners, electric irons, toasters, and refrigerators that cooled themselves and ended the daily visit of the iceman.
  • Then make the analytical point, which is the sentence students most often miss. Each appliance was a standardized factory product sold under a national brand name.
  • The same system that built the Model T built the toaster: one design, interchangeable parts, enormous quantities, a falling price.
  • And each appliance raised the standard of what a normal home was expected to contain. That rising standard is the hinge between this section and the next.
  • A refrigerator could only become something a family felt it needed if somebody taught the family to feel the lack of one. Teaching that feeling, at national scale, was the work of advertising, and it is the subject of section D.
  • Ask: Who decided what a normal home contained, the family or the advertiser? (Hold that question into the next section.)

Slide 13 · Section D: Selling the Goods (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (the Frigidaire advertisement, 1922); the section label “Section D”; the title “Selling the Goods”; the lines — “Factories making millions of goods needed buyers by the million.”

  • Picture: A full newspaper advertisement for the Frigidaire electric refrigerator from 1922, placed by its distributors Cox and Dance. Students will work with this same advertisement in the activity.
  • Read its two headline claims aloud before explaining anything. The top line says a home is no longer modern without Frigidaire. The large headline says No More Ice.
  • Neither line describes how the machine works in any detail. Both lines sell a standard of life: modernity, cleanliness, and freedom from the iceman's dirt and uncertainty.
  • That is the whole argument of section D in one artifact. Factories that could produce goods by the million needed buyers by the million, and advertising became the tool for finding and teaching them.
  • This advertisement is dated 1922, squarely inside the decade, and it shows the teaching in progress: a machine most families had never owned is presented as something a modern home could no longer be without.

Slide 14 · Sold more than the product

On the slide: five bullets — National brands advertised in magazines, on billboards, and in newspapers. The copy rarely stopped at describing a product. Advertisements sold cleanliness, health, status, and modern life itself. The family without the product was presented as behind the times. A refrigerator changed from a good never owned to a good felt as needed.

  • In the guided notes definition, advertising is paid public messages that present a product to potential buyers and seek to create a desire to buy it, and the creating of desire is the load bearing phrase.
  • National brands advertised in magazines, on billboards, and in newspapers across the country, so the same message reached every region at once.
  • The copy rarely stopped at describing a product. Advertisements sold cleanliness, health, status, and modern life itself, and presented the family without the product as behind the times.
  • The guided notes ask students to name two of those four things besides the goods themselves, so give them a moment to fix the list: cleanliness, health, status, modern life itself.
  • The result, in the reading's summary, is that advertising turned goods most families had never owned, such as a refrigerator, into goods that families felt they needed.
  • Desire, however, does not pay a bill. The gap between wanting a refrigerator and having its price in hand is the subject of the next section.

Slide 15 · Section E: Buying on Time (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section E”; the title “Buying on Time”; the line — “Installment buying closed the gap between desire and price.”

  • Advertising could teach a family to want a car, a radio, or a refrigerator, but wanting did not produce the purchase price, and most of the new goods cost weeks or months of wages.
  • This section covers the financial tool that closed that gap. Installment buying was not invented in the 1920s, but in this decade it spread from a few trades to the center of the consumer economy, covering automobiles, radios, appliances, furniture, and clothing.
  • Treat it even handedly, because the reading does. On one side, it genuinely widened access: families used goods while paying for them, instead of saving for years first, and sales rose at once, which kept factories running at the volume their prices depended on.
  • On the other side, it committed future wages before they were earned and put repossession behind every missed payment.
  • Students who see both sides now will understand, in a later lesson, why a downturn in 1929 turned so quickly into lost cars, lost furniture, and lost homes.

Slide 16 · Part down, the rest on time

On the slide: five bullets — The buyer paid part of the price down and took the goods home at once. The rest was paid in weekly or monthly installments. Interest or a carrying charge was added to the price. The plan spread to automobiles, radios, appliances, furniture, and clothing. By the middle of the decade, about three quarters of new cars were sold on installments, by some reckonings.

  • State the mechanism first, in the guided notes definition: installment buying is purchasing a good by paying part of the price at the time of purchase and the rest in regular payments over time, usually with interest or fees added.
  • In the language of the reading, the buyer paid part of the price down, took the goods home, and paid the rest in weekly or monthly installments, with interest or a carrying charge added.
  • In the 1920s the plan spread to automobiles, radios, appliances, furniture, and clothing.
  • Its scale in the largest market is the figure to remember: contemporary estimates held that a large share of new cars, by some reckonings three quarters, were sold on installments by the middle of the decade.
  • Teach the caution in that sentence as well. These were contemporary estimates, not a census, and the reading says so; the honest claim is that installment selling had become the normal way to buy a car, not that the fraction is known exactly.

Slide 17 · Future wages, spent early

On the slide: four bullets — A family no longer had to save the full price first. Sales rose at once, and factories kept their volume. The buyer committed future wages before they were earned. Falling behind meant repossession of the goods.

  • This slide weighs the tool, and students should be able to state both sides in the reading's own terms.
  • Buying on time raised sales at once, since a family no longer had to save the full price first. Payments spread across weeks or months fit a wage earner's income, which brought cars, radios, and appliances within reach of buyers who could never have paid cash.
  • That was not a small thing: it is part of how the goods of the new economy reached beyond the wealthy in this decade.
  • The risk ran alongside the gain from the first payment. The buyer committed future wages before they were earned, paid interest or a carrying charge on top of the price, and faced repossession if payments fell behind: the seller could take the goods back, and the payments already made went with them.
  • Connect this forward, briefly. A system that sells against next year's wages works while wages keep coming. Students should store that sentence for the lessons on 1929, when the wages stopped and the commitments remained.

Slide 18 · Section F: Chain Stores (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (the Piggly Wiggly interior, 1918); the section label “Section F”; the title “Chain Stores”; the lines — “Stores reorganized on factory principles. One company, one plan, every town.”

  • Picture: The interior of the original Piggly Wiggly store in Memphis, Tennessee, from 1918, before the 1920s. It is used because it shows the self service chain store form that the 1920s adopted at scale: customers walking the aisles, selecting packaged goods themselves, in a store run to one company's plan. Piggly Wiggly had opened in 1916 as the first self service grocery store.
  • The reading's subject in this section is broader than one company. The goods of the new factories reached buyers through stores reorganized on factory principles, standardized, centrally supplied, and repeated town after town under the same name.
  • The example the guided notes test is the Great Atlantic and Pacific Tea Company, the A and P, whose growth across the decade is the next slide's subject after the reasons for its prices.
  • Shoppers, merchants, and students should all be able to answer one question by the end of the section: why was the chain cheaper?

Slide 19 · Why the chain charged less

On the slide: five bullets — Chains bought in carload lots at wholesale prices. They sold for cash, and offered no delivery and little credit. Each store was small, standardized, and cheap to run. The independent merchant bought in small lots and carried customers on credit. Shoppers followed the lower prices, and independents organized in protest.

  • In the guided notes definition, a chain store is one of a group of retail stores owned by a single company, carrying the same name, goods, and prices across many locations.
  • The guided notes ask for two practices that let a chain charge less, and the reading supplies a full list, so give students the whole of it.
  • Chains bought in carload lots from company warehouses and paid wholesale prices an independent buying in small lots could not match. They sold for cash, and offered no delivery and little credit, which removed the cost of those services from the price. Each store was small, standardized, and cheap to run.
  • The independent merchant stood on the other side of every point: he bought in small lots, delivered, and carried his customers on credit, and each service raised his costs.
  • Shoppers followed the lower prices, which is why the chain won, and independent merchants organized in protest, which is the first organized resistance this unit meets. Neither side of that fight was imaginary.

Slide 20 · 15,000 A and P stores

On the slide: the big figure “15,000”; the label — “A and P stores by the end of the 1920s”; the note — “About 4,300 stores in 1920 · The Great Atlantic and Pacific Tea Company, the A and P.”

  • 15,000 — A and P stores by the end of the 1920s
  • About 4,300 stores in 1920
  • Put the two figures together and do the arithmetic aloud. The Great Atlantic and Pacific Tea Company, the A and P, grew from about 4,300 stores in 1920 to about 15,000 by the end of the decade.
  • The chain more than tripled in ten years, opening, on average, close to three new stores a day, every day, for a decade.
  • Numbers on that scale change what a store is. The corner grocer was a neighbor who knew his customers, extended them credit, and answered for his goods personally. The chain store was one outlet of a distant company, run to a plan written elsewhere, competing on price alone.
  • Both descriptions are accurate, and contemporaries argued about which mattered more, which is why the protests of independent merchants belong in this lesson and not only in a later one.
  • For the guided notes, the two blanks are the two numbers on this slide: about 4,300 stores in 1920, about 15,000 by the end of the decade. Students should leave this slide able to pair the growth with the price practices from the slide before it.

Slide 21 · Section G: Radio and One System (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (a family listening to radio, 1921); the section label “Section G”; the title “Radio and One System”; the lines — “Commercial broadcasting began in 1920. Within the decade, millions of families owned sets.”

  • Picture: A family gathered around a radio receiver with a horn speaker, photographed by Harris and Ewing in 1921, while one boy listens through headphones. Its date falls inside the decade and at the very start of broadcasting: commercial broadcasting had begun only in 1920, and the horn and headphones in the picture are what home listening looked like in its first years.
  • Radio is the right place to end, because it carried the same content everywhere at once.
  • Sponsored programs carried advertising into the parlor and taught the same brand names to listeners in every region, so the selling system of sections D, E, and F gained a voice inside the house itself.
  • Section G therefore has two jobs: radio as a product of the system, sold on installments and advertised nationally, and radio as the place where the reading states its whole argument in one paragraph.

Slide 22 · One system, not two stories

On the slide: five bullets — Standardized goods were cheap because factories made them in enormous quantities. Factories could sell enormous quantities because the selling system moved them. Advertising taught desire, and installment plans supplied credit. Chain stores cut distribution costs, and higher wages put cash in some hands. Each part fed the others.

  • Close the content where the reading closes, with its central claim stated as a loop, and ask students to trace the loop around the slide once before anyone writes.
  • Production and consumption were not two separate stories. Standardized goods were cheap because factories made them in enormous quantities, and factories could sell enormous quantities because advertising taught desire, installment plans supplied credit, chain stores cut distribution costs, and higher wages put cash in some workers' hands.
  • Radio then carried the advertising into the home and taught the same brand names in every region. Each part fed the others, and together they remade American economic life.
  • Test the loop by removing parts, as a question: take away installment buying, and who can pay even $260 for a car out of weekly wages? Take away advertising, and why would a family want a refrigerator it had lived without for generations?
  • A system is exactly this: a set of parts each of which makes the others work. That sentence is the standard for the exit ticket's final answer.

Slide 23 · The system at a glance

On the slide: the title “Four sides, one system”; four rows — Production (the line, interchangeable parts, falling prices; cheap goods in enormous quantities to sell); Wages (five dollars a day, doubled pay with conditions; cash in some workers' hands for cars and goods); Consumption (advertising, installment buying, radio; desire taught, credit supplied, brands nationwide); Distribution (chain stores buying by the carload; lower prices that independents could not match).

  • This slide mirrors the final table in the guided notes, The System at a Glance, and students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • The four rows are the system's sides. Production covers the line at Highland Park, interchangeable parts, and the falling price series, and what it fed the other side was cheap goods in enormous quantities.
  • Wages covers the five-dollar day, doubled pay carrying conditions and inspections, and what it fed was cash in some workers' hands, enough in about two months to cover the cheapest car.
  • Consumption covers advertising, installment buying, and radio: desire taught nationally, credit supplied against future wages, and brand names carried into the parlor.
  • Distribution covers the chain stores, buying by the carload and selling for cash, which fed consumption lower prices and fed production a national outlet.
  • Grade the table generously on wording and strictly on logic: each entry must say what changed and what it fed on the other side, in the student's own words.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for the factory

On the slide: four terms with their definitions — assembly line, mass production, Model T, productivity. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Say: “assembly line: a factory arrangement in which a product moves past workers who each add one part or perform one task until the product is finished”
  • Say: “mass production: the making of goods in very large quantities using standardized parts and machinery, so that each item costs less to produce”
  • Say: “Model T: the low priced automobile built by the Ford Motor Company from 1908 to 1927 and sold to millions of ordinary buyers”
  • Say: “productivity: the amount of goods or work produced for each hour of labor or for each unit of input”
  • The moving product is the assembly line definition's heart; the workers stay, the work travels. The mass production definition ends with the result, cheapness per item, not just with size.
  • Ordinary buyers is the phrase to underline in the Model T definition; earlier cars had been rich men's goods. The chassis figures, twelve and a half hours down to an hour and a half, are the productivity definition measured.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for the marketplace

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — installment buying, advertising, chain store. These definitions match the guided notes word for word.

  • Say: “installment buying: purchasing a good by paying part of the price at the time of purchase and the rest in regular payments over time, usually with interest or fees added”
  • Say: “advertising: paid public messages that present a product to potential buyers and seek to create a desire to buy it”
  • Say: “chain store: one of a group of retail stores owned by a single company, carrying the same name, goods, and prices across many locations”
  • Students often forget the last clause of installment buying, and it is the one that made the tool profitable to sellers and risky to buyers.
  • Paid and create are the two words to stress in advertising: the messages cost the seller money, and their purpose was not to inform but to create a desire that had not existed.
  • Sameness is the chain store definition's point. The same name, goods, and prices in every town is what let one company spread its costs across thousands of outlets and undercut the merchant who stood alone.
