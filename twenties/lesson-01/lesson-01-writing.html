Writing Task: One System

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: How did mass production and mass consumption remake American economic life in the 1920s? A strong answer does more than list new products. It explains how the way goods were made and the way goods were sold depended on each other: cheap standardized goods required millions of buyers, and advertising, installment credit, chain stores, radio, and wages organized those buyers. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. Ford introduced the moving assembly line at Highland Park in 1913. Chassis labor fell from about twelve and a half hours to about an hour and a half by the spring of 1914. The Model T touring car fell from about $850 in 1909 to about $360 in 1916, and the runabout reached $260 in 1925.

2. Ford announced the five-dollar day on January 5, 1914, after hiring about 53,000 men in 1913 to keep about 14,000 jobs filled. About $2.34 a day was base wage. The rest was a profit share paid only to workers who met company conditions, supervised through home visits by the Sociological Department.

3. The share of homes wired for electricity rose from about one in six in 1912 to about half by the middle of the 1920s. Wiring created a national market for standardized appliances such as washing machines, vacuum cleaners, and electric refrigerators.

4. National advertising sold cleanliness, health, status, and modern life along with the product. A 1922 Frigidaire advertisement carried the line A Home Is No Longer Modern Without Frigidaire, presenting a family without the machine as behind the times.

5. Installment buying let a family take goods home after paying part of the price down, with the rest in weekly or monthly payments and interest or a carrying charge added. Contemporary estimates held that about three quarters of new cars were sold on installments by the middle of the decade, by some reckonings.

6. Chain stores bought in carload lots, sold for cash, and offered no delivery and little credit. The A and P grew from about 4,300 stores in 1920 to about 15,000 by the end of the decade. Commercial radio, begun in 1920, carried sponsored advertising into the home.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the lesson question directly and treats production and consumption as one system, not as two separate lists of changes. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank items 1 and 2 support the production and wage side: the line, the price fall, the five-dollar day, and its conditions. (Blueprint 1) Bank items 3 through 6 support the consumption side: electrification and appliances, advertising, installment credit, chain stores, and radio. (Blueprint 2) A full credit essay draws on both sides of the bank.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. A paragraph that lists three facts without saying how each one fed the other side of the system has assembled evidence without making the argument, and should earn partial credit.
  • An ending that identifies what the system changed at the level of national economic life, such as the expectation that ordinary families would buy new manufactured goods as a normal part of living.

Model opening for teacher reference. Mass production and mass consumption remade American economic life because each side created the conditions the other required. When Ford's moving line cut chassis labor from about twelve and a half hours to about an hour and a half, the price of a touring car fell from about $850 in 1909 to about $360 in 1916, and goods once built for the few were suddenly priced for the many. Factories producing at that scale could not sell to a local market. They needed a nation of buyers, and the consumer economy supplied one: advertising taught families to want goods they had never owned, installment plans let them take the goods home before saving the full price, and chain stores put the same branded products on sale in every town at the same low price. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the wage evidence or the radio evidence, and closing on the system rather than on any single product.

Common weak answers. Production only (the assembly line made goods cheap) names half the system and cannot explain how the goods were sold. Shopping only (people bought more things) cannot explain where the cheap goods or the desire for them came from. Product lists (cars, radios, and refrigerators were invented) name objects without the economic relationships the lesson teaches.
