Teacher Answer Key

MediumWhat it put in commonHow it reached the whole country at onceWhat it left out
Network radioOne evening of music, comedy, news, and sports scores, heard on the same schedule in every region. Amos and Andy, from 1928, held a national audience night after night.NBC, formed in 1926, and CBS, formed in 1927, linked stations by telephone wire so one program played in dozens of cities at the same moment. Sponsors paid, so listening was free.Black performers stayed at the margins. The leading Black characters on network radio were played by white actors in dialect. Local and foreign language programs survived outside the networks.
Movies and the talkiesThe same films, stars, and, after 1927, the same recorded voices and lines. Weekly attendance was estimated at about fifty million admissions in the middle of the decade.Studios distributed the same prints nationally, and movie palaces such as the Roxy and Grauman's Chinese, both opened in 1927, gave the films a standard setting in city after city.Sound ended local variation, the house musician and the adapted title card. Silent actors with unsuitable voices lost their careers, and women and minority performers held few decision jobs in the studios.
Tabloids and wire photographsThe same front page photographs, headlines, trials, scandals, and famous faces. The Daily News, founded in 1919, and the Daily Mirror, founded in 1924, set the form.Wire services moved photographs across the country, so readers in many cities saw the same image on the same morning. A flight that ended in Paris on Saturday evening was a photographed front page on Sunday.The papers chose which faces counted as news. Fame followed editorial choice and advertising value, not importance alone, and communities the advertisers did not seek appeared mainly as sensation.
Sports coverageA daily national drama with shared figures. Dempsey and Carpentier drew a gate over one million dollars in 1921 with a radio account. Ruth hit sixty home runs in 1927 and the Yankees swept the World Series.Newspaper sports pages, newsreels, and radio accounts carried the same games and the same record chase to fans who never attended, on the same days, all season.Coverage centered on the stars the media had already built and on events with promoters and sponsors behind them. Sports and athletes outside that economy received little national attention.
The hero storyOne figure the whole country could celebrate at once. Lindbergh flew alone from New York to Paris on May 20 to 21, 1927, and received a ticker tape parade in New York on June 13.Weeks of prize race coverage prepared the audience. Radio bulletins, special editions, wire photographs, and a national tour then put the same hero in front of every region within days.The story framed the flight as solitary achievement and left out the backers, mechanics, and the media organization that produced the fame. Other 1927 achievements without a single photogenic hero received a fraction of the attention.

The shared culture in one sentence. Strong answers name simultaneity in at least two media. Networks, talkies, and wire photographs let people in different states hear, see, and discuss the same program, film, game, and hero in the same week, which no earlier combination of local papers, local stages, and traveling performers could do.
