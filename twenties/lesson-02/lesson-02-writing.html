Writing Task: A Culture Heard at the Same Hour

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: How did radio, movies, and the popular press give Americans a shared national culture for the first time? A strong answer does more than say that the new media were popular. It explains how the media made experience simultaneous across regions, who paid for that system and what the payer selected for, and where the limits of the shared culture fell. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. KDKA in Pittsburgh made the first commercial broadcast on November 2, 1920, the returns of the Harding and Cox election. NBC, formed in 1926, and CBS, formed in 1927, linked stations by wire so one sponsored program played in dozens of cities at the same time.

2. Movie palaces such as the Roxy and Grauman's Chinese Theatre opened in 1927. Contemporary estimates put weekly attendance at about fifty million admissions in the middle of the decade. The Jazz Singer, released October 6, 1927, brought recorded dialogue, and within two years the studios had converted to the talkie, so every audience heard the same performance.

3. The tabloids, led by the New York Daily News, founded in 1919, built front pages around photographs. Wire services moved the same photographs across the country, so the same image and the same heroes greeted readers in many cities on the same morning.

4. Sport supplied the daily serial. Dempsey and Carpentier drew a gate over one million dollars in 1921 with a radio account of the fight. Ruth hit his sixtieth home run on September 30, 1927, and the Yankees swept the World Series, followed daily in papers, newsreels, and radio accounts.

5. Lindbergh flew alone from New York to Paris on May 20 to 21, 1927, after weeks of prize race coverage. A ticker tape parade in New York on June 13 and a national tour fixed a single hero in the public mind within weeks.

6. Sponsors and advertisers paid for radio programs, newspaper space, and the publicity around films. The leading Black characters on network radio, in Amos and Andy from 1928, were played by white actors, and Black and immigrant Americans sustained media of their own, such as the Chicago Defender and the foreign language press.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the lesson question directly and names simultaneity, the same program, film, image, and story reaching different regions at the same time, as the mechanism. [Blueprint 3]
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. KDKA and the networks support the radio claim. The palaces and The Jazz Singer support the film claim. The tabloids and wire photographs support the press claim. Dempsey, Ruth, and Lindbergh support the celebrity claim. [Blueprint 3]
  • An account of the payer. Sponsors and advertisers paid for the system, and the essay states what that selected for: the largest reachable audience and the figures who could hold it. [Blueprint 3]
  • A limit, supported by evidence. Amos and Andy, the Defender, and the foreign language press each show a group outside the center of the shared culture. An essay without a limit describes the system as its advertisers presented it and should earn partial credit. [Blueprint 3]
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. A paragraph that lists three facts without saying what each one made simultaneous has assembled evidence without making the argument.

Model opening for teacher reference. Radio, movies, and the popular press gave Americans a shared national culture because they made attention simultaneous. Before 1920, a town read about an event days after it happened and heard its music from local players. After NBC linked its stations by wire in 1926, a family in Ohio and a family in Georgia heard the same sponsored program at the same hour, and the same week they could watch the same talkie performance and open papers carrying the same wire photograph of the same hero. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the sports serial and the Lindbergh coverage, then closing on the limit: the culture was national, and it was built by advertisers for the audience they wanted, which is why the most popular Black voices on network radio belonged to white actors while Black readers built the Defender.

Common weak answers. Popularity alone (everyone liked radio and the movies) names the size of the audience and misses simultaneity, the payer, and the limit. Invention lists (radio, film sound, and photography were all invented) reverse the argument of the lesson, which is that distribution, networks, wire services, national prints, and sponsors, turned inventions into a common culture.
