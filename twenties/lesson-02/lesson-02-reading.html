Mass Media and the Celebrity Age

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A Voice in the Air

On November 2, 1920, station KDKA in Pittsburgh sent out the first commercial radio broadcast in the United States, the returns of the presidential election between Warren G. Harding and James M. Cox. The station grew out of the experimental work of Frank Conrad, an engineer for Westinghouse, which sold radio sets and needed programs to make the sets worth buying. Anyone with a receiver heard the same words at the same moment.

Stations multiplied quickly. Manufacturers sold sets by the millions, first crystal receivers used with headphones, then cabinet sets with speakers a whole room could hear. By the middle of the decade hundreds of stations were on the air, and listening was a normal evening activity.

Networks and the Sponsored Hour

A single station reached one city. A network reached the country. The National Broadcasting Company, formed in 1926, linked stations by telephone wire so one program could play in dozens of cities at once. The Columbia Broadcasting System followed in 1927. Advertisers paid for the programs, so a sponsor could speak to millions of listeners in a single evening. Radio was free to the listener because the listener was the product sold to the sponsor.

Network schedules taught the country to keep the same hours. Families planned supper around a favorite program. Amos and Andy, first broadcast from Chicago in 1928, drew listeners in every region. Its two Black characters were played by white actors using dialect, so the most popular Black voices on network radio were not Black voices at all.

The Movie Palace and the Talkies

Film had been national before radio, but in the 1920s it became grand. Studios built movie palaces that seated thousands, with marble lobbies and uniformed ushers. The Roxy in New York and Grauman's Chinese Theatre in Hollywood both opened in 1927. Contemporary estimates put weekly attendance at about fifty million admissions in the middle of the decade. For a quarter, a mill worker and a bank president watched the same picture.

Sound completed the change. On October 6, 1927, Warner Bros. released The Jazz Singer, a mostly silent film with several scenes of recorded dialogue and song by Al Jolson. Audiences crowded in to hear a picture speak. Within two years the major studios had converted to sound, silent stars with unsuitable voices lost their careers, and the talkie became the standard film. The same actor now said the same line in Georgia and in Ohio in the same week.

The Tabloid and the Photograph

Print changed with the rest. The New York Daily News, founded in 1919, proved that a smaller page built around photographs could outsell the older papers. The Daily Mirror followed in 1924. These tabloids printed large pictures, short stories, and bold headlines, and covered crime, scandal, and sport closely. Wire services moved photographs across the country, so the same front page image greeted readers in many cities on the same morning. The tabloids also manufactured fame, choosing which faces readers would see daily.

Sports as National Theater

Sport gave the new media its most reliable drama. On July 2, 1921, Jack Dempsey defended his heavyweight title against Georges Carpentier in Jersey City before about eighty thousand people. The gate passed one million dollars, and a radio account carried the fight far beyond the ring. Dempsey won by a knockout in the fourth round.

Baseball supplied the decade its largest sports figure. Babe Ruth of the New York Yankees hit home runs at a rate no player had approached, and newspapers, newsreels, and radio made his daily performance a national routine. On September 30, 1927, Ruth hit his sixtieth home run of the season, and the Yankees swept the Pittsburgh Pirates in the World Series.

The Hero of 1927

In May 1927 a young airmail pilot named Charles Lindbergh took off alone from Roosevelt Field on Long Island in the Spirit of St. Louis and flew to Paris in thirty-three and a half hours. He won the twenty-five thousand dollar Orteig Prize for the first nonstop flight between New York and Paris. Newspapers had followed the contest for weeks, and radio and newsreels carried his arrival to a public prepared to celebrate him. New York gave him a ticker tape parade on June 13. He had flown alone, but his fame was a joint product of his skill and of a media system that could build a hero in a weekend.

Fads, Advertising, and the Voices Left Out

A shared culture also meant shared crazes. Mah jongg sets sold out in the early decade. Crossword puzzles became a book publishing boom in 1924. Al Shipwreck Kelly sat on a flagpole in Atlantic City for thirteen days in 1924, and imitators followed, each stunt reported as news. The media that reported a fad also created it.

Advertising paid for nearly all of this system. Sponsors bought radio programs and newspaper space, and studios sold films and the products placed around them. The shared national culture was also a shared national market, addressed to the Americans advertisers most wanted to reach. That address left people out. Black Americans built media of their own, including the Chicago Defender and other Black newspapers, while network radio kept Black performers at its margins. Immigrant communities sustained foreign language papers and local radio hours the networks ignored. Women filled screens and photographs and held few of the jobs that decided what the country would see and hear. The new media gave Americans a common culture for the first time. It did not give every American the same place inside it.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What did KDKA broadcast on November 2, 1920, and why did Westinghouse have a commercial reason to put programs on the air? KDKA broadcast the returns of the presidential election between Harding and Cox. Westinghouse sold radio sets. Regular programs gave people a reason to buy a set, so broadcasting created the market for the company's own product. The station grew out of Frank Conrads experimental station.

2. How did a radio network change what a sponsor could buy, and what did the reading mean by saying the listener was the product being sold? A network such as NBC, formed in 1926, linked stations by wire so one sponsored program played in dozens of cities at once. A sponsor was no longer buying time in one market. It was buying simultaneous access to a national audience. Listeners paid nothing for programs, so what the network delivered to the sponsor was the audience itself, gathered at a predictable hour in predictable numbers.

3. What changed for audiences and for actors after The Jazz Singer in October 1927? For audiences, film gained a voice. A viewer in Georgia and a viewer in Ohio heard the same actor deliver the same line in the same week, which made the movie experience more nearly identical across the country. For actors, the change was a filter. The major studios converted to sound within about two years, and silent stars whose voices recorded poorly or whose acts depended on silence lost their careers, while performers who could speak and sing gained work.

4. Compare Ruth and Lindbergh as products of the media system. What did each man do himself, and what did the media add? Ruth supplied a repeatable daily performance, home runs at a record rate, ending with sixty in the 1927 season. Lindbergh supplied a single feat, the first solo nonstop flight from New York to Paris on May 20 to 21, 1927. In both cases the media supplied the scale. Newspapers ran the daily serial, radio carried results and arrivals as they happened, and newsreels put the same images before audiences nationwide. Ruth's fame was renewed every afternoon. Lindbergh's was built in advance by coverage of the Orteig Prize contest and then fixed by the parade and tour. Neither man could have become a household figure in towns he never visited without that system.

5. How did radio, movies, and the popular press give Americans a shared national culture for the first time? Full credit answers argue simultaneity and sameness. Broadcasts reached listeners while events were still happening, networks put one program into dozens of cities at the same hour, movie palaces in every region showed the same talkies, and wire photographs put the same front page images in many cities on the same morning. A family in Ohio and a family in Georgia could now discuss the same fight, the same film, and the same hero in the same week. The limit is required for full credit. The shared culture was built and paid for by advertisers, it presented Black characters through white performers in Amos and Andy, and it left Black, immigrant, and women producers at the margins, building media of their own. Answers that describe only popularity or speed earn partial credit.
