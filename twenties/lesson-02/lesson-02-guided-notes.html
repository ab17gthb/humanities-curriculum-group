Guided Notes: Mass Media and the Celebrity Age

Lesson question: How did radio, movies, and the popular press give Americans a shared national culture for the first time?

Unit question: How did the prosperity of the 1920s change American life, and why was that prosperity weaker than it looked?

Key Terms

mass media: forms of communication that reach very large audiences at the same time, including radio, film, and newspapers sold across the country

broadcast: to send a program by radio waves to many listeners at once from a single station

radio network: a group of stations linked by wire that carry the same programs at the same time under one company

talkie: a motion picture with recorded dialogue and sound, which replaced silent film after 1927

tabloid: a newspaper printed in a smaller format that used large photographs and bold headlines to report crime, scandal, and celebrity news

celebrity: a person who is widely known and followed by the public because the media cover his or her life and work

fad: a craze that spreads quickly through the public and fades within months, such as a dance, a game, or a stunt

The System at a Glance, radio row. Shared: one program in dozens of cities at the same hour, including news, music, comedy, and sports accounts. Paid and left out: sponsors paid and addressed the audience they wanted to reach. Black performers stayed at the margins, and the leading Black characters were played by white actors.

The System at a Glance, movies row. Shared: the same films in palaces and theaters in every region, and after 1927 the same recorded voices and lines. Paid and left out: studios and advertisers sold films and products together. Women held few decision jobs, and Black and immigrant audiences were rarely the audience addressed.

The System at a Glance, tabloids row. Shared: the same wire photographs, headlines, trials, and famous faces in many cities on the same morning. Paid and left out: advertisers and street sales paid. The papers chose the faces the country would see, and fame followed their choices.

The System at a Glance, sports row. Shared: the same fights and games followed daily by radio, newsreel, and newspaper, with Ruth and Dempsey as national figures. Paid and left out: promoters, sponsors, and papers profited from the drama. Coverage centered on the stars the media had already built.

Exit ticket 1. A stronger station still reached one area at a time. A network put the same program into dozens of cities at the same moment, which created one simultaneous national audience a sponsor could buy.

Exit ticket 2. Any two, with the deed stated. Babe Ruth hit his sixtieth home run on September 30, 1927, and the Yankees swept the World Series. Charles Lindbergh flew alone from New York to Paris on May 20 to 21, 1927. Al Jolson spoke and sang in The Jazz Singer, released October 6, 1927.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that ties the shared culture to simultaneity in at least two media and names one margin. The strongest answers note that networks, talkies, and wire photographs let a family in Ohio and a family in Georgia hear, see, and discuss the same program, film, fight, and hero in the same week, while Black Americans, immigrant communities, and women producers were left to build or use media of their own.
