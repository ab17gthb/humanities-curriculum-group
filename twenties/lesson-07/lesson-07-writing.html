Writing Task: What They Built

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: What did Black Americans build in the cities of the North, and how did that building answer the country that had excluded them? A strong answer covers more than new addresses. It explains what migrants made, in neighborhoods, in culture, and in organizations, and it states plainly what that building said to a country that had assigned Black Americans a lower place. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. Chicago's Black population grew from about 44,000 in 1910 to about 234,000 in 1930. Migrants filled neighborhoods, the Black Belt, Black Bottom, Central Avenue, and Harlem, with churches, businesses, newspapers, and Urban League offices.

2. The Chicago Defender, carried south by Pullman porters, printed train schedules, job notices, and migrants' letters, and set May 15, 1917, as a Great Northern Drive. Its circulation reached about 125,000 in 1918.

3. The housing fight: white owners used restrictive covenants to close districts, Chicago saw dozens of bombings of Black homes between 1917 and 1921, and in Detroit Dr. Ossian Sweet was tried for murder in 1925 and acquitted after his new house was attacked.

4. The Harlem Renaissance: Hughes published The Negro Speaks of Rivers in 1921 and The Weary Blues in 1926. Hurston wrote from Black southern speech and folklore. Ellington's orchestra broadcast from the Cotton Club from 1927, and Bessie Smith's blues records sold by the hundreds of thousands.

5. Garvey's UNIA marched in uniformed ranks through Harlem in 1920 and ran Harlem businesses. Its Black Star Line, financed by five dollar shares sold to Black buyers, failed, and Garvey was convicted of mail fraud in 1923, imprisoned in 1925, and deported in 1927.

6. The NAACP documented lynching and pressed for the Dyer anti-lynching bill, which passed the House in January 1922 and died by Senate filibuster that December. In Tulsa in 1921 a white mob burned about thirty five blocks of Greenwood, and no one was punished.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the lesson question: what was built, and what the building said to the country that had excluded its builders. (Blueprint 10, Blueprint 11)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, covering at least two kinds of building: neighborhoods and institutions (bank 1, 2, 3), culture (bank 4), or organizations (bank 5, 6). An essay drawn from one kind only has described one part of the answer. (Blueprint 10, Blueprint 11)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. A paragraph that lists a population figure, a poem, and a shipping line without saying what each one asserted has assembled evidence without making the argument, and should earn partial credit.
  • The limit included and used: the Dyer bill's death by filibuster, the bombings and covenants of the housing fight, the Black Star Line's collapse, or the destruction of Greenwood, placed so the essay's claim is tested against it rather than decorated by it. (Blueprint 10, Blueprint 11)

Model opening for teacher reference. Black Americans in the northern cities built the standing the country had refused them. A people who had been counted as a problem built neighborhoods that ran themselves: Chicago's Black population grew from about 44,000 in 1910 to about 234,000 in 1930, and the Black Belt filled with churches, businesses, and the Urban League offices that met the next train. They built a culture that claimed the national stage, in Hughes's poems, Hurston's stories, and Ellington's broadcasts from the Cotton Club. And they built organizations, Garvey's UNIA and the NAACP's anti-lynching campaign, that stated the claim in the open: citizens and creators, organizing for themselves. A full model continues in this pattern, then tests the claim against the limit the student chose, such as the Dyer bill's failure or the burning of Greenwood, before closing.

Common weak answers. A list of famous names (Hughes, Ellington, Garvey) without what each one built or claimed names celebrities and misses the lesson. A hardship list (covenants, bombings, Tulsa) without the building reverses the question: the lesson asks what the building answered, and hardship is the condition it answered, not the answer itself. Treating Garvey's deportation or the Dyer bill's failure as proof that nothing was built confuses a limit with a verdict.
