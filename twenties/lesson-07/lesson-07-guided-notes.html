Guided Notes: The Great Migration and the Harlem Renaissance

Lesson question: What did Black Americans build in the cities of the North, and how did that building answer the country that had excluded them?

Unit question: How did the prosperity of the 1920s change American life, and why was that prosperity weaker than it looked?

Key Terms

Great Migration: the movement of hundreds of thousands of Black southerners to northern cities for jobs and greater rights during and after the war years

New Negro: a Black American of the 1920s who claimed dignity and equal rights, took pride in Black history and culture, and refused a second class place

Harlem Renaissance: the flowering of Black writing, music, and art in the 1920s, centered in Harlem in New York City

jazz: music formed in Black communities of New Orleans from ragtime, blues, and brass band traditions, built on syncopation and improvisation

blues: a Black musical form built on a repeating pattern of chords, with verses that name hardship, work, and endurance in plain language

UNIA: the Universal Negro Improvement Association, Marcus Garvey's mass organization, founded to unite Black people worldwide and to build Black owned businesses and political power

Chicago Defender: the Black newspaper founded in Chicago in 1905 by Robert S. Abbott, which urged Black southerners to move north and printed the news of lynching and discrimination that white papers ignored

The Account at a Glance. Push: Jim Crow law and disfranchisement, landlord controlled sharecropping, lynching, the boll weevil, the 1927 flood. Pull: industrial jobs at several times farm pay, the vote, schools not split by race in law, news and help from the Defender and from letters. Built: the Black Belt, Black Bottom and Paradise Valley, Central Avenue, and Harlem; churches, businesses, newspapers, and Urban League offices; the Harlem Renaissance in writing and music; the UNIA and the NAACP campaign. Answer: the building stated that Black Americans were citizens, creators, and organizers who would hold ground, in housing, in art, and in law, rather than accept the place assigned to them.

Exit ticket 1. Any push and pull from the notes, each with its fact. Example: lynching enforced the southern order, and northern mills and auto plants paid wages several times farm pay.

Exit ticket 2. The Dyer bill would have made lynching a federal crime where states failed to act. It passed the House in January 1922, and southern senators filibustered it in the Senate until its supporters gave it up that December.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that names at least two things built and ties them to exclusion. The strongest answers join the neighborhoods and institutions, the Renaissance claim that Black life was a subject for art, and the organizing of the UNIA and the NAACP, and note that the answer was made while the Dyer bill failed and Greenwood burned.
