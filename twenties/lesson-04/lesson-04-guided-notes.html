Guided Notes: The Politics of Prosperity

Lesson question: What did the Republican governments of the 1920s do at home and abroad, and what did they deliberately leave undone?

Unit question: How did the prosperity of the 1920s change American life, and why was that prosperity weaker than it looked?

Key Terms

Teapot Dome: the scandal in which Secretary of the Interior Albert B. Fall secretly leased federal naval oil reserves in Wyoming and California to private oil companies and accepted money from the oilmen in return; Fall was convicted and imprisoned

tax cut: a reduction in the rates at which a tax is charged, here the cuts in federal income tax rates during the 1920s

tariff: a tax charged on goods brought into a country from abroad

isolationism: a policy of avoiding political commitments and alliances with other countries while keeping a country free to act on its own

Washington Conference: the conference held in Washington, D.C., from November 1921 to February 1922, where the major naval powers agreed to limit the size of their battle fleets

war debt: money one government owes another for wartime loans, here the debts the Allied governments owed the United States from the First World War

Kellogg-Briand Pact: the agreement signed in Paris in 1928 in which the signing nations renounced war as an instrument of national policy

The Record at a Glance. Taxes and spending: cut the top income tax rate from 73 percent to 25 percent and ran budget surpluses; left undone, any use of the surplus for farm relief or new federal programs. Business and farms: raised the tariff and kept regulation light; refused farm price support, vetoed twice by Coolidge. Foreign agreements: led the Washington Conference and signed the Kellogg-Briand Pact; refused League of Nations membership, World Court membership, and any enforcement behind the pact. Money owed across the Atlantic: insisted the war debts be paid and accepted the Dawes Plan circle; refused to cancel the debts or to lower the tariff so debtors could earn dollars by trade.

Exit ticket 1. The leases were corrupt because Fall made them in secret, without the competitive bidding the law expected, and took money from the very oilmen who received the leases: $100,000 in cash from Doheny and large loans and gifts from Sinclair.

Exit ticket 2. Did abroad, any two: led the Washington Conference and accepted naval limits; insisted on repayment of the war debts and the Dawes Plan; signed the Kellogg-Briand Pact. Refused, any two: League of Nations membership; World Court membership; enforcement of the peace pact; cancellation of the war debts; an advance promise to act in a future crisis.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that names action on one side and refusal on the other, with two facts. The strongest answers pair the tax cuts and tariff with the farm vetoes at home, and the Washington treaties and the pact with the refusal of the League, the World Court, and enforcement abroad.
