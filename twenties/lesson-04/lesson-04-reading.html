The Politics of Prosperity

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

Normalcy Takes Office

Warren G. Harding won the election of 1920 promising a return to normalcy after war and reform. In office from March 1921, he cut wartime spending and made two kinds of appointments. Some were excellent: Charles Evans Hughes at State, Andrew W. Mellon at the Treasury, Herbert Hoover at Commerce. Others were friends from Ohio whose loyalty he valued over honesty. These friends, the Ohio Gang, treated public office as a route to private profit. Harding was never shown to have taken a bribe. He never asked hard questions of the men he trusted.

Teapot Dome

The largest scandal grew out of oil. The Navy held oil bearing land at Teapot Dome, Wyoming, and Elk Hills, California, as a reserve for emergencies. In 1921 the reserves passed to Secretary of the Interior Albert B. Fall, a Harding friend. Fall secretly leased Teapot Dome to Harry F. Sinclair's company and Elk Hills to Edward L. Doheny's, without the competitive bidding the law expected. In return he took $100,000 in cash from Doheny and large loans and gifts from Sinclair. A Senate investigation led by Thomas J. Walsh of Montana exposed the leases in 1923 and 1924. Fall resigned, and in 1929 became the first cabinet officer imprisoned for a crime committed in office. Harding died in August 1923; the scandal attached to his memory and his party.

Coolidge and the Business Doctrine

Vice President Calvin Coolidge succeeded Harding in 1923 and won a term of his own in 1924. Coolidge held that the federal government should do a short list of things well: tax lightly, spend less than it collected, pay down the war debt, and leave business decisions to business. Regulation stayed light, and Coolidge twice vetoed bills that would have committed the government to supporting farm prices. For Coolidge, rising prosperity was the test of the policy. If business was expanding and budgets were balanced, government was doing its work by staying out of the way.

Mellon and the Tax Cuts

Mellon directed tax policy under all three Republican presidents. Wartime law had taken as much as 73 percent of income at the highest bracket. Mellon argued that rates that high pushed wealthy investors to shelter money in tax exempt bonds instead of putting it into factories and railroads, so the Treasury collected less, not more. Congress cut rates in 1921, 1924, and 1926, and by mid decade the top rate stood at 25 percent. Budgets ran surpluses. Critics answered that the cuts gave most of their benefit to the wealthy, while farmers and wage earners saw little change.

The Tariff

The same governments that cut taxes at home raised them at the border. The Fordney-McCumber Tariff of 1922 raised duties on farm products and manufactured goods to some of the highest levels in American history, shielding American producers from foreign competition. The tariff pleased the industries it protected. It also made it harder for European governments to earn, by selling goods in the United States, the dollars they needed to repay their war debts, a contradiction the administration never resolved.

The Washington Conference

Abroad, Republican policy is often called isolationism, but the label fits only in part. The United States refused to join the League of Nations, yet it led the decade's largest diplomatic gathering. In November 1921, Secretary Hughes opened the Washington Conference by proposing that the naval powers stop building battleships. The Five-Power Treaty of 1922 fixed the battle fleets of the United States, Britain, and Japan in a ratio of 5 to 5 to 3, paused new battleship construction for ten years, and ordered warships scrapped. Companion treaties pledged respect for Pacific possessions and open trade with China. The Senate approved the agreements because they limited arms without joining the League.

War Debts and the Circle of Loans

Money tied the United States to Europe even where politics did not. The Allies owed the United States about ten billion dollars borrowed during the war, and Washington insisted the debts be paid. The Allies answered that they could pay only what Germany paid them in reparations, and Germany could not pay at that scale. The Dawes Plan of 1924 loosened the knot. American banks lent money to Germany. Germany paid reparations to the Allies. The Allies paid their debts to the United States. The circle turned while American credit flowed. If American lending stopped, every payment in the circle stood at risk.

A Pact Against War, and Doors Left Closed

In August 1928, fifteen nations signed the Kellogg-Briand Pact in Paris, renouncing war as an instrument of national policy. The pact created no army, no court, and no penalty for breaking it. The Senate approved it 85 to 1 after stating that it did not limit self-defense and did not require the United States to enforce it. The same caution kept the United States out of the World Court, which senators would join only on reservations no president accepted. The pattern held: sign agreements, lend money, and sell goods abroad, but promise nothing in advance.

Hoover and the Election of 1928

Secretary of Commerce Herbert Hoover had spent the decade encouraging trade associations to set standards and share information under light guidance, a method called associational cooperation. Nominated in 1928, he faced Governor Al Smith of New York, the first Roman Catholic nominated for president by a major party. Smith opposed Prohibition and spoke for the immigrant city. Hoover won in a landslide, carrying even states of the normally Democratic South where many Protestant voters refused a Catholic candidate. Low taxes, high tariffs, and limited government had won a third straight national victory.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Who were the Ohio Gang, and what did Harding's handling of his administration make possible? The Ohio Gang were Harding's friends from Ohio appointed to federal office who treated public office as a route to private profit. Harding appointed some able men, including Hughes, Mellon, and Hoover, but he did not ask hard questions of the friends he trusted, and that inattention made corruption such as the Teapot Dome leases possible. Harding was never shown to have taken a bribe himself.

2. State Mellon's argument for cutting the highest income tax rates, and the main criticism made against the cuts. Mellon argued that a top rate of 73 percent drove wealthy investors to shelter money in tax exempt bonds rather than invest it in productive business, so lower rates would put capital to work and still supply the Treasury. Congress cut rates in 1921, 1924, and 1926, bringing the top rate to 25 percent, and the budget ran surpluses. The criticism was that most of the benefit went to the wealthy while farmers and wage earners saw little change.

3. Explain the contradiction between the Fordney-McCumber Tariff and the American war debt policy. The United States demanded that European governments repay about ten billion dollars in war debts, but the Fordney-McCumber Tariff of 1922 raised duties so high that Europeans could not easily earn dollars by selling goods in the American market. The tariff protected American producers while cutting off the trade income the debtors needed to pay.

4. What did the Five-Power Treaty do, and why could the Senate approve the Washington agreements while refusing to join the League of Nations? The treaty fixed the battle fleets of the United States, Britain, and Japan in a 5 to 5 to 3 ratio, paused new battleship construction for ten years, and ordered dozens of warships scrapped. Companion treaties covered Pacific possessions and open trade with China. Senators could approve these agreements because they were limited, specific treaties negotiated by the United States itself. They carried no standing obligation to act, unlike League membership, which senators feared would commit the country in advance.

5. What did the Republican governments of the 1920s do at home and abroad, and what did they deliberately leave undone? Full credit answers cover both halves. At home the administrations cut income taxes from a 73 percent top rate to 25 percent, raised the tariff in 1922, kept regulation light, and ran budget surpluses, while Harding's inattention allowed the Teapot Dome corruption. Abroad they led the Washington Conference and its naval limits, insisted on full payment of war debts through the Dawes Plan circle of loans, and signed the Kellogg-Briand Pact. Deliberately undone: farm price support, vetoed twice by Coolidge; membership in the League of Nations and the World Court; any enforcement behind the peace pact; and any advance promise to act in a future crisis. Any four accurate facts, divided between action and refusal, earn full credit.
