Writing Task: The Car and the Shape of a Life

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: How did the automobile change where Americans lived, how they spent their time, and what they expected from life? A strong answer covers all three parts of the question, and it weighs the change honestly: name at least one cost of the car alongside its gains. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. About 8 million passenger cars were registered in the United States in 1920 and about 23 million in 1929, roughly one car for every five people. A Model T fell from $850 in 1908 to under $300 by the middle of the 1920s, and most cars were bought on installment credit.

2. The Federal Aid Road Act of 1916 and the Federal Highway Act of 1921 put federal money into a connected network of main routes. States paid in part with gasoline taxes, and in 1926 the main routes became the numbered United States highways.

3. Builders laid out subdivisions on cheap land beyond the streetcar lines, with a garage beside the house as a standard feature. The head of the household became a commuter, driving between the suburb and the city each day.

4. Dating replaced parlor calling. A young man with access to a car took a young woman to a movie, a dance, or a drive, away from the family home and its supervision, and parents, ministers, and judges objected in print throughout the decade.

5. More than a thousand towns ran municipal tourist camps by the middle of the decade. The Tin Can Tourists organized in Florida in 1919, tourist cabins followed, and the word motel was coined in 1925 for lodging in which guests parked beside their rooms. Filling stations sold gasoline, oil, and repairs along the same roads.

6. Streetcar companies and railroads lost riders to private cars. Farmers could drive past small local stores to larger towns with lower prices, and main streets in bypassed towns thinned out. Motor vehicle deaths rose from about 12,000 in 1920 to about 30,000 in 1929.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers all three parts of the lesson question: where Americans lived, how they spent their time, and what they expected from life. (Blueprint 4)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Registrations and price support the spread of ownership. Federal acts, gasoline taxes, and the 1926 numbering support the road building claim. Subdivisions and commuting support the housing claim. Dating supports the private life claim. Tourist camps, the Tin Can Tourists, the motel, and filling stations support the leisure and roadside business claims. (Blueprint 4)
  • Explanation that connects each fact to the part of the question it proves. A paragraph that lists facts without assigning them to housing, time, or expectation has assembled evidence without making the argument, and should earn partial credit. (Blueprint 4)
  • At least one cost named with its evidence: the rise in deaths to about 30,000 by 1929, installment debt, or the loss of riders and trade suffered by streetcars, railroads, and bypassed towns. (Blueprint 4)
  • An ending that states the new expectation plainly, such as distance on demand, a home beyond the transit lines, or a private social life in motion.

Model opening for teacher reference. The automobile changed American life by moving the center of it from the rail line to the road. In 1920 about 8 million passenger cars were registered in the United States; by 1929 there were about 23 million, bought cheap because mass production had cut the price of a Model T to under $300 and bought on credit because installment plans let a family pay a little at a time. Where a family lived no longer had to be decided by a streetcar line. Builders sold houses in subdivisions beyond the transit routes, each with a garage, and the head of the household drove to work in the city and home again at night. A full model continues in this pattern, taking up time next through dating and automobile tourism, then expectation, and it closes by weighing one cost, such as the 30,000 deaths of 1929, against the gains.

Common weak answers. Speed alone (cars were faster than horses or trains) names a transport gain and misses the housing, courtship, and business changes the lesson teaches. Gain only answers (the car gave everyone freedom) ignore the prompt's instruction to weigh a cost and usually ignore the losers in bank item 6. (Blueprint 4)
