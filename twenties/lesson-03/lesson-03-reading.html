America on Wheels

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

From Luxury to Expectation

In 1900 an automobile was a rich man's toy, expensive and unreliable on roads built for horses. By 1929 it was a normal expectation of family life. Registrations tell the scale. About 8 million passenger cars were registered in 1920. By 1929 the figure was about 23 million, roughly one car for every five people. Mass production drove the change, as Lesson 1 showed: a Model T that cost $850 in 1908 sold for under $300 by the middle of the 1920s, and most cars were bought on installment credit. Ownership spread from the wealthy to clerks, skilled workers, and farm families. The car pulled other industries behind it, consuming most of the nation's rubber and much of its steel, glass, and oil, and advertisers sold it as freedom and status, not transport alone.

Good Roads and the Federal Highway

Cars were only as useful as the roads under them, and in 1920 most rural roads were dirt: dust in summer, mud after rain. Drivers and farmers organized in a Good Roads movement demanding surfaced roads paid for as a public good. Government answered. The Federal Aid Road Act of 1916 offered matching federal money for state highway building. The Federal Highway Act of 1921 concentrated that money on a connected network of main routes. States paid their share in part with gasoline taxes, first adopted by Oregon in 1919 and levied by every state by 1929. In 1926 the main routes received one numbering system, the United States highways. Mile by mile, the paved highway replaced the mud road, and a long drive became a plan rather than an adventure.

The Suburb and the Commuter

The car redrew the map of where people lived. Earlier suburbs had grown along streetcar and railroad lines, within walking distance of a station. A family with a car could live anywhere a road reached. Builders laid out subdivisions on cheap land beyond the streetcar lines, with a garage beside the house as a standard feature. The head of the household became a commuter, driving into the city each morning and back each evening. Home and work separated more completely than the streetcar had separated them: home was a house and a yard, work was downtown, miles away, and the road between them belonged to the car.

What the Car Weakened

Every gain in reach was a loss somewhere else. Streetcar companies, charging a fixed five cent fare while costs rose, lost riders to private cars, and many lines cut service or failed. Railroads lost short distance passengers to the new highways. Small towns faced the sharpest problem. A farmer who once traded at the nearest store could now drive past it to a larger town with lower prices and more choice. Main streets in bypassed towns thinned out, and some railroad towns began a long decline. The car did not only connect places. It ranked them, rewarding the places on the paved routes at the expense of the places off them.

Courtship Leaves the Parlor

The car changed private life as plainly as it changed the map. In the older pattern of courtship, a young man called on a young woman at her home, in the parlor, under the eyes of her family. Dating replaced calling. A young man with access to a car took a young woman out, to a movie, a dance, or a drive, away from the house and from adult supervision. Parents, ministers, and judges objected in print throughout the decade, and their alarm is evidence of how complete the change was. A car gave young people privacy and a social life set by their own choices. What a family once supervised in its own front room now happened miles down a paved road.

Tourism, Camps, and the Motel

The car also invented a new kind of travel. Tourism by automobile let a family see country that railroad tourists had seen only from a train window, and stop where it chose. Early motorists camped beside the road or in municipal tourist camps, which more than a thousand towns provided by the middle of the decade, some free and some for a small fee. Campers organized, most famously the Tin Can Tourists, founded in Florida in 1919. Business followed. Owners built rows of tourist cabins, then combined lodging and parking in one business: the motel, a word coined in 1925 from motor and hotel, with guests parking beside their rooms. Along the same roads, the filling station sold gasoline, oil, and repairs, and diners and billboards competed for the driver's money. A roadside economy had appeared that had no reason to exist before the car.

The Costs of the Car

The bill arrived with the benefits. Motor vehicle deaths rose from about 12,000 in 1920 to about 30,000 in 1929, a toll no earlier transport had imposed on ordinary families. Millions of cars were bought on credit, so a lost job could mean a lost car and a debt that remained. Farmers used cars to reach town and markets, yet the same roads carried their trade to larger competitors. The automobile had remade where Americans lived, how they spent their time, and what they expected from life: distance on demand, privacy in motion, and a house beyond the city with a machine in the garage. The debts and the deaths came with it.
