Teacher Answer Key

Area of lifeBefore the carWith the carCost or loser
HousingHomes clustered near streetcar and railroad lines, within walking distance of a station. Most families lived close to work, shops, and relatives.Builders laid out subdivisions on cheap land beyond the streetcar lines, with a garage as a standard feature. About 23 million cars were registered by 1929.Streetcar suburbs and the transit lines that served them lost riders and value as families moved beyond their reach.
CourtshipA young man called at the young woman's home and sat in the parlor under the supervision of her family.Dating replaced calling. A couple went out by car to a movie, a dance, or a drive, away from the house and from adult supervision.Parents, ministers, and judges objected in print. Families lost the supervision the parlor had given them over courtship.
WorkMost workers walked, rode a streetcar, or took a train to work, and lived within the reach of those lines.The head of the household became a commuter, driving daily between a suburb and a city job. Highway building, filling stations, and roadside businesses created new work along the roads.Streetcar companies and railroads lost riders and revenue. Workers who could not afford a car had fewer choices about where to live and work.
LeisureTravel for pleasure usually meant a train trip, planned around timetables and stations, and most free time was spent near home.Automobile tourism let families stop where they chose. More than a thousand towns ran tourist camps, the Tin Can Tourists organized in 1919, and the motel appeared in 1925.Motor vehicle deaths rose from about 12,000 in 1920 to about 30,000 in 1929. Travel on credit added to household debt.
Town businessFarmers traded at the nearest store, and towns on railroad lines held the trade of their surrounding countryside.Drivers passed small stores for larger towns with lower prices and more choice. Filling stations, diners, tourist cabins, and billboards formed a new roadside economy on the paved routes.Bypassed main streets thinned out, small merchants lost their captive trade, and some railroad towns began a long decline.

The car in one sentence. Strong answers name reach as the deep change. The car let a family choose where to live, where to shop, where to court, and where to travel, independent of rail and streetcar lines, and it charged for that freedom in deaths, debt, and the decline of the places and businesses the old lines had sustained.
