Guided Notes: America on Wheels

Lesson question: How did the automobile change where Americans lived, how they spent their time, and what they expected from life?

Unit question: How did the prosperity of the 1920s change American life, and why was that prosperity weaker than it looked?

Key Terms

suburb: a residential area on the edge of a city, outside the center, where people live and travel into the city for work

commuter: a person who travels regularly between home in one place and work in another, usually each day

paved highway: a main road between places with a hard, surfaced roadway of concrete, asphalt, or brick that can be used in wet weather

filling station: a roadside business that sells gasoline and oil and provides air, water, and basic repairs for automobiles

tourism: travel for pleasure, and the businesses that serve travelers with food, fuel, lodging, and entertainment

motel: a lodging built for motorists, from motor and hotel, in which guests park their cars beside or near their rooms

dating: a form of courtship in which a young man and a young woman go out together to public amusements, away from the family home and its supervision

Summary organizer. Housing: subdivisions beyond the streetcar lines with a garage as a standard feature; the suburb was no longer tied to a station. Courtship: dating by car replaced parlor calling, moving young couples away from family supervision. Work: the head of the household commuted daily by car between suburb and city; roadside and highway work also grew. Leisure: automobile tourism, tourist camps, tourist cabins, and the motel made family travel a normal use of free time. Town business: trade moved toward larger towns on paved routes, while bypassed main streets, streetcar companies, and railroads lost customers. Accept any accurate fact from the reading in the third column.

Exit ticket 1. Paved highways made land beyond the streetcar lines reachable in all weather at any hour, so builders could sell houses there and families could depend on the daily drive; the road came first and the subdivision followed it.

Exit ticket 2. Any two, with reasons. Streetcar companies lost riders to private cars while charging a fixed five cent fare. Railroads lost short distance passengers to the highways. Small town stores lost farm trade to larger towns that drivers could now reach. Rail towns lost the through traffic that had sustained them.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that covers at least two of the three parts of the lesson question with facts, and names one cost. The strongest answers connect the parts: the car moved housing into road based suburbs, moved leisure into tourism and courtship on the road, and taught families to expect distance, privacy, and mobility on demand, at the price of about 30,000 deaths a year by 1929, installment debt, and weakened towns and transit.
