## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- Why did the colonies break with Britain, and what did Americans build in the place of the empire they rejected?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's core plan is 18 instructional days. The unit opens with the imperial crisis of 1763 to 1765, where the Colonial America unit closed, and runs through the resistance campaigns, the tea crisis and the first shots, the argument and declaration of 1776, the war on its battlefields and its home fronts, the Confederation's troubles and achievements, the Constitutional Convention, the ratification contests, and the Bill of Rights. It closes at 1791 with the new government constituted. The Early Republic unit, next in the course, opens with Washington's administration and the politics of the 1790s, so this unit does not teach the Farewell Address or the Alien and Sedition Acts; they are named at most as a closing pointer in the final lesson. The Revolution is taught as a war with more peoples in it than two armies: enslaved and free Black Americans who read the era's promises against their own condition and acted on both sides, women who ran the home front and in some cases the guns, and Native nations who chose their sides for reasons of their own and paid the war's highest territorial price. Slavery stands inside the founding story where the record puts it: in Dunmore's Proclamation and the Black Loyalist exodus, in the Declaration's deleted passage, in the Convention's compromises, and in the first northern emancipations. Period texts are public domain, but the edition still matters: every quotation names its transcription or facsimile edition, and modernized texts are used only where the bank's audited record carries them, with the modernization the bank record itself discloses.

## Lesson 1: The Imperial Crisis: Taxation and Resistance

**Lesson question:** Why did Britain tax the colonies after 1763, and why did the colonists resist taxes they had never been asked to pay before?

**Reading scope:** The bill first, picked up exactly where the Colonial unit left it: Britain's national debt had nearly doubled in the French and Indian War, its ministers resolved that the colonies should pay for their own defense, and a standing army of some ten thousand soldiers in North America was the charge they meant the colonies to carry, Grenville's ministry designing the revenue program in 1764 and 1765. The Stamp Act of March 1765 stated as the break it was: the first tax laid directly on the colonies themselves rather than on trade, falling on printed paper, newspapers, pamphlets, legal documents, licenses, and playing cards, collected through stamped paper sold by royal distributors, and so falling hardest on the colonies' printers, lawyers, and merchants, the very people who ran its newspapers and assemblies. The colonial answer in its three registers: argument, in the Virginia Resolves of May 1765, Patrick Henry's resolves claiming that only the colonists' own assemblies could tax them, and the Stamp Act Congress of October 1765, nine colonies meeting at New York to declare their rights and grievances in one voice; combination, in the boycotts of British goods that merchants organized and households, above all women spinning and weaving homespun, made real; and crowd action, Sons of Liberty organizing protests that hung tax men in effigy, wrecked the Massachusetts lieutenant governor's house, and drove every stamp distributor in America to resign before the tax took effect on November 1, a day that came and went with no stamps sold. Repeal and its sting together: British merchants, hurt by the boycott, petitioned for relief, Franklin testified before Parliament in February 1766 that the colonies would never submit to the stamp tax, and Parliament repealed the act in March 1766 while passing the Declaratory Act the same day, claiming power to legislate for the colonies in all cases whatsoever, a claim the colonists celebrated past and would meet again. The second round: the Townshend Acts of 1767, duties on imported glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea, framed as external taxes of the kind colonists had seemed to accept, with the revenue assigned to pay royal governors' and judges' salaries, which struck at the assemblies' power of the purse the Colonial unit taught; Dickinson's Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania answering that a tax for revenue was a tax whatever its form, and the Massachusetts Circular Letter of 1768 carrying the argument to the other assemblies. Troops and blood: two regiments sent to Boston in 1768 to back the customs men, two years of street friction, and the night of March 5, 1770, when soldiers surrounded by a taunting, icy crowd fired and killed five men, among them Crispus Attucks; Revere's engraving, copied from Pelham and printed within weeks, fixing the event in the colonies' memory as a massacre of the unarmed, the soldiers' own accounts, Captain Preston's and the sentry testimony, telling a cornered garrison's story instead; the trial that followed, John Adams defending the soldiers, two convicted of manslaughter and branded on the thumb, six acquitted, as the record of a resistance movement that still claimed British justice. The reading closes with the repeal of the Townshend duties in 1770, all but the tea tax, kept to hold the principle, and the quiet years that followed, in which the committees of correspondence of 1772 and 1773 quietly wired the colonies together for the next crisis.

**Activity concept:** The tax file. Students work five records, the Stamp Act Congress's Declaration of Rights and Grievances of 1765, the Declaratory Act of 1766, Dickinson's Farmer's Letters in excerpt, Captain Preston's account of March 5, 1770 set against Samuel Drowne's testimony, and Revere's engraving of the Massacre as the bank describes it, and for each one state the claim it makes about who may tax and who may judge, then judge which document best explains why repeal in 1766 did not end the quarrel.

**Vocabulary:** Stamp Act, repeal, Declaratory Act, Townshend Acts, boycott, virtual representation, Boston Massacre

## Lesson 2: From Protest to Revolution: 1773 to 1776

**Lesson question:** How did a protest over tea become a war?

**Reading scope:** The Tea Act of May 1773 as the trap it was: the East India Company, drowning in unsold tea and near bankruptcy, was licensed to sell directly in the colonies through its own agents, cutting out colonial merchants, at a price lower than before even with the Townshend duty still on it, so that every cup drunk cheap would concede Parliament's right to tax; the colonies saw the bargain whole, and Charleston let the tea rot in a warehouse, New York and Philadelphia turned the ships back, and Boston's governor Hutchinson refused to let the Dartmouth leave without unloading. The night of December 16, 1773: after a mass meeting at Old South voted that the tea should not be landed, some one hundred men, thinly disguised as Mohawks, boarded the three ships and in about three hours emptied 342 chests into the harbor, harming nothing else, sweeping the decks after, an act of destruction aimed at property and law rather than at persons, whose discipline was itself an argument. Parliament's answer, the acts of the spring of 1774 that colonists named Intolerable: the Boston Port Act closing the harbor to trade until the tea was paid for, the Massachusetts Government Act rewriting the colony's charter to put its council and town meetings under royal control, the Administration of Justice Act letting accused royal officials be tried outside the colony, and a new Quartering Act, with the Quebec Act, tolerating Catholicism and extending Quebec to the Ohio, read in the colonies as a fifth blow; General Gage arriving as governor with regiments behind him. The colonies answering together: supplies voted to blockaded Boston from as far as South Carolina, and the First Continental Congress at Philadelphia in September and October 1774, fifty six delegates from twelve colonies, Georgia absent, which issued a Declaration and Resolves restating the colonial case, and adopted the Continental Association, a boycott of British imports, then exports, enforced by elected committees in every county and town, which in enforcing the boycott quietly became a second government; the Suffolk Resolves from Massachusetts urging the people to arm and drill. The winter of powder: both sides seizing magazines and cannon through the fall and winter, Massachusetts minutemen drilling on the greens, and on the night of April 18, 1775, Gage sending some seven hundred regulars to seize the stores at Concord, Paul Revere and William Dawes riding to warn the country, the militia under Captain Parker standing on Lexington green at dawn. April 19 told from both records the lesson carries: Lieutenant Barker's diary, a British officer's account of a shot he could not assign and a volley his men fired without orders, and the Massachusetts militiamen's sworn testimony taken a week later, depositions blaming the regulars for firing first on men already dispersing; eight Americans dead at Lexington, then the North Bridge at Concord, then the long ride back to Boston under fire from behind walls and trees, some 273 British casualties against about 95 American, figures stated as the standard counts; by nightfall thousands of militia had Boston besieged. The war's first year, stated in its sequence: the Second Continental Congress creating the Continental Army and choosing the Virginian George Washington to command it, so the South would own New England's war; the Battle of Bunker Hill of June 17, 1775, the militia twice throwing back assaults on Breed's Hill before ammunition failed, a British victory that cost over a thousand casualties and taught both sides the war's price; the Olive Branch Petition of July 1775, Congress professing loyalty and begging the king to interpose, answered before it arrived by George III's Proclamation of Rebellion of August 1775 and by Parliament's Prohibitory Act, which put the colonies outside the king's protection and seized their shipping as an enemy's; and Dunmore's Proclamation in Virginia that November, held for Lesson 5. The reading closes on the strange shape of the turn of 1776: royal governors had fled or been arrested in colony after colony, congresses and committees governed in fact, an army stood before Boston, and yet independence had not been declared and many who fought still called themselves Britons; the next lesson takes up the argument that closed that gap.

**Activity concept:** From tea to war. Students work five records, a Boston Tea Party account (George Hewes's recollection, labeled with its 1834 date and read as memory), the Boston Port Act, the Declaration and Resolves of the First Continental Congress, Barker's diary of Lexington, and the militiamen's sworn testimony, and for each one state what its makers wanted the record to prove, then judge which single step between December 1773 and April 1775 did most to make reconciliation impossible, and defend the judgment against the strongest rival step.

**Vocabulary:** Tea Act, Boston Tea Party, Intolerable Acts, First Continental Congress, minutemen, Loyalist, Patriot

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Bostonians Paying the Excise-Man visual record, Barker diary, minutemen testimony, and Olive Branch Petition, audited records in the founding era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**. ## Lesson 3: Declaring Independence

**Lesson question:** What did the Declaration argue, and why did Americans need an argument at all?

**Reading scope:** The stalled middle first: through 1775 the resistance still framed its quarrel as a defense of British liberties against Parliament and the king's ministers, and independence was widely called ruinous and treasonous in the same newspapers that cheered the militia; what changed the target was the king himself, who had declared the colonies in rebellion, hired some thirty thousand German soldiers to fight them (the figure stated as the standard count of auxiliaries hired across the war), and whose Prohibitory Act had placed American ships and goods outside his protection, acts colonists read as the king making war on them and so dissolving the bond from his side. Paine's intervention: Common Sense, published at Philadelphia in January 1776, a pamphlet of fewer than fifty pages in its first edition that argued the case had been miscast, that the villain was not this tax or that minister but monarchy and hereditary succession themselves, corrupt in Scripture and absurd in nature, an island presuming to govern a continent; its plain biblical and tavern language carrying republican theory to readers who had never opened Locke, selling, by the estimates historians work from, well over one hundred thousand copies within months in a country of two and a half million, the estimate labeled as such. The colonies turning: through the spring of 1776 colony after colony instructed its delegates to vote for independence, new state constitutions were drafted as the royal governments collapsed, and on June 7 Richard Henry Lee moved in Congress that the United Colonies are, and of right ought to be, free and independent States. The drafting: a committee of five, Adams, Franklin, Sherman, Livingston, and Jefferson, who did the writing, Jefferson drafting at a portable desk in a rented room and Congress then cutting about a quarter of his text, tightening the argument and striking, among other passages, his charge that the king had waged cruel war against human nature itself in the slave trade and had blocked every colonial attempt to end it, a passage removed, the record shows, at the insistence of South Carolina and Georgia and with northern delegates whose ports profited from the trade unwilling to press it; Jefferson's rough draft, with the changes visible, carried in the audited bank as this lesson's central artifact. The argument itself, read closely in its own order: a preamble of self evident truths, that all men are created equal and hold rights no government gives and none may rightly take, life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness among them, that governments are instituted to secure those rights and draw their just powers from the consent of the governed, and that a people may alter or abolish a government destructive of those ends; then the evidence, a long train of abuses charged not against Parliament but against the king alone, because it was the king's bond that had to be dissolved: refusing assent to laws, dissolving assemblies, obstructing justice and immigration, keeping standing armies, cutting off trade, imposing taxes without consent, depriving colonists of jury trials, transporting them overseas for trial, plundering coasts and burning towns, hiring foreign mercenaries, and two closing charges students must read with the whole record open, that he had excited domestic insurrections among the colonists, meaning Dunmore's freedom offer to the enslaved, and had brought on the inhabitants of the frontiers the merciless Indian Savages, a phrase the Declaration's universal language stands against inside the same document; and the conclusion, that these colonies are absolved from all allegiance and are free and independent states. The adoption and the risk: Congress voted independence on July 2 and adopted the text on July 4, printed it that night as the Dunlap broadside, read it to Washington's army on July 9 as New Yorkers pulled down the king's statue to melt into bullets, the signers, most of whom signed on August 2, pledging their lives, their fortunes, and their sacred honor to a document that was, in British law, a confession of treason. The reading closes with what the Declaration did and did not do: it stated a standard, government by consent for the protection of equal rights, in words capacious enough that the people it left out could and did quote them back, Abigail Adams writing to her husband in March 1776 to remember the ladies and warning that women would not hold themselves bound by laws in which they had no voice, and receiving a laughing answer; it freed no one by itself, settled nothing about who the people were, and left the building of governments to the states and to the lessons ahead.

**Activity concept:** The argument of 1776. Students work five records, Common Sense in excerpt, Jefferson's rough draft with the deleted passage on the slave trade, the Declaration as adopted, the king's Proclamation of Rebellion as the other side's argument, and the Olive Branch Petition as the road not taken, and for each one state its claim about where legitimate government comes from, then judge which of the five would most persuade a wavering colonist in June 1776, and say what it would have to overcome in him.

**Vocabulary:** Common Sense, natural rights, unalienable rights, consent of the governed, social contract, republic, Declaration of Independence

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. ## Lesson 4: The War for Independence

**Lesson question:** How did the United States survive a war against the world's strongest empire, and win it?

**Reading scope:** The odds, stated as the war's frame: Britain held the world's strongest navy, a funded treasury, a professional army, and hired German auxiliaries, against a Congress that could not tax, an army enlisted by the year and often unpaid, militia who served near home and went home, and a currency already sliding; Washington's strategy followed from the arithmetic, that the Americans could lose every city and still win by keeping an army in being, while Britain had to destroy that army or hold a continent, and could do neither cheaply. The disasters of 1776: the British taking New York after the Battle of Long Island in August, Washington escaping across the East River by night and then losing the city, the fall of Fort Washington with its garrison, the long retreat across New Jersey as enlistments ran out and the cause, in Paine's new words that December, tried men's souls. The counterstroke: on Christmas night 1776 Washington recrossing the ice choked Delaware with some 2,400 men and falling on the Hessian garrison at Trenton at dawn, then slipping around Cornwallis to win again at Princeton on January 3, 1777, two small victories that arrived exactly when the Revolution was within weeks of dissolving, and that restocked it with recruits, supplies, and belief. The turning year 1777: the British plan to cut New England off by taking the Hudson, Burgoyne marching south from Canada while Howe, instead of meeting him, sailed to take Philadelphia, the capital falling and the victory changing nothing; Burgoyne, his supply line stretched and his army cut up at Bennington and Freeman's Farm, surrendering some 5,800 men at Saratoga on October 17, 1777 (the figure stated as the standard count), the victory that did the war's diplomatic work, because it proved to France that the Americans could beat a British army in the field. The alliance and the endurance: the Treaty of Alliance of February 6, 1778, France recognizing the United States and joining the war with money, ships, and soldiers, Spain following against Britain in 1779, the war widening to the Caribbean, Gibraltar, and India; and at home the army wintering at Valley Forge in 1777 and 1778, where some two thousand men died of disease, hunger, and exposure (stated as an estimate), while Baron von Steuben, a Prussian drillmaster, taught it to maneuver and fight as one body, so the army that marched out in June and fought the British to a standstill at Monmouth was a different instrument from the one that had marched in. The war's other fronts in brief: the frontier war of raids and counter raids, held for Lesson 5, George Rogers Clark's campaigns in the Illinois country, John Paul Jones raiding Britain's own coasts, and the British, after 1778, shifting their weight to the South, where they counted on Loyalist strength. The southern war: Savannah taken in 1778, Charleston falling in May 1780 with an entire American army, the worst American defeat of the war, Camden that August destroying a second army; then the backcountry answering, militia destroying a Loyalist force at King's Mountain in October 1780, Daniel Morgan wrecking Tarleton's legion at Cowpens in January 1781, and Nathanael Greene, taking command of the southern army, wearing Cornwallis down through Guilford Courthouse and a campaign of retreat that cost the British men they could not replace, until Cornwallis abandoned the Carolinas and marched into Virginia to refit at Yorktown. The trap: Washington and Rochambeau marching their armies from New York to Virginia in the summer of 1781 while the French fleet under de Grasse sealed the Chesapeake against rescue, some seventeen thousand American and French troops closing around Cornwallis's eight thousand (the figures stated as the standard counts), the bombardment beginning October 9, and Cornwallis surrendering his army on October 19, 1781, his band, in the famous story students should hear labeled as tradition rather than record, playing The World Turned Upside Down. The reading closes with the peace that took two more years: fighting guttering out after Yorktown, the preliminary articles of November 1782, and the Treaty of Paris of September 3, 1783, by which Britain recognized the United States as free, sovereign, and independent, granted a boundary at the Mississippi and the Great Lakes, fishing rights off Newfoundland, and terms on debts and Loyalist property that the states would honor unevenly, which is where the next two lessons pick the story up.

**Activity concept:** The war in five records. Students work five records, the Address of the Congress to the Inhabitants of the United States of May 1778, the Treaty of Alliance in excerpt, a record of Valley Forge (a surgeon's or officer's account from a named edition, or a teaching summary labeled on its face), a record of the southern campaign (Greene's correspondence or a King's Mountain account from a named edition, or a labeled summary), and the Treaty of Paris in excerpt, and for each one state what it contributed to the final result, then rank the five contributions and defend the ranking against the claim that any one of them won the war alone.

**Vocabulary:** Continental Army, militia, George Washington, Valley Forge, Battle of Saratoga, Treaty of Alliance, Battle of Yorktown

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. ## Lesson 5: Winning and Losing: The War's Home Fronts and Its Peoples

**Lesson question:** Who else fought, paid, and waited through the war, and what did its promise mean to them?

**Reading scope:** A war run by civilians first: Congress printing paper money it could not redeem until a dollar in coin took hundreds in paper and the phrase not worth a Continental entered the language, states requisitioning food and wagons from farms already stripped by both armies, prices fixed by law and evaded in practice, and the war carried at the household level, women running farms and shops with husbands, sons, and laborers gone, boycotting British cloth and spinning homespun as political acts, raising money door to door, as the women of Philadelphia did in 1780 to fund shirts for the army. The armies' own households: the camp followers, women and children numbering in the thousands with both armies, who cooked, washed, nursed, sewed, and carried water and cartridges under fire for rations and little or no pay, without whom the armies could not have moved or fed themselves; and the women who fought outright, Margaret Corbin, wounded manning a cannon at Fort Washington in 1776 and pensioned by Congress, Deborah Sampson, who enlisted in 1782 as Robert Shurtliff, served over a year in the light infantry, was wounded, and was honorably discharged when a fever's treatment discovered her sex; and Mary Ludwig Hays at Monmouth in 1778, carrying water to the guns, whose story hardened over the following century into the legend of Molly Pitcher, taught here with the seam between the record and the legend showing. Women thinking politically: the Edenton Tea Party of October 1774, fifty one women in North Carolina signing a public boycott resolve under Penelope Barker's lead, mocked in a London cartoon and recorded as among the first organized political acts by American women; Abigail Adams's letter of March 31, 1776, asking her husband to remember the ladies in the new code of laws and warning that women would foment a rebellion of their own if they had no voice, and John Adams's laughing refusal, the exchange carried entire in the audited bank. Black Americans' war, the lesson's center: Virginia's royal governor Dunmore, driven from Williamsburg to a ship, proclaiming freedom in November 1775 to enslaved people and indentured servants held by rebels who would bear arms for the king, some hundreds reaching his lines and serving in an Ethiopian Regiment whose uniform reportedly bore the words Liberty to Slaves, most dying of smallpox and fever in the camps; the British offer echoing south and north for the rest of the war, so that across the conflict thousands of enslaved people ran to British lines wherever the armies passed, the largest slave rebellion of the era conducted as flight to the enemy; against it the American record, Black men barred from the Continental Army at first on the protests of slaveholders, then enlisted as manpower ran short, some five thousand serving in all (stated as the standard estimate), the First Rhode Island Regiment of 1778 raised substantially from enslaved men who earned freedom by enlisting, and Black sailors in both navies. Native nations' war: the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, whose Great Law had kept its internal peace since before Columbus, breaking on the question of the war, the Oneida and Tuscarora siding with the Americans, the Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, and most Onondaga with the British under leaders including the Mohawk Joseph Brant, Thayendanegea, who had been educated in Connecticut and presented at court in London and who fought to use the British alliance as a shield for his people's land; the war in the west and on the frontiers a war of raids and burnings by all parties, answered in 1779 by Washington's Sullivan expedition, some four thousand men sent to lay waste the Seneca and Cayuga country, which burned about forty towns and their stored corn and drove thousands into a starving winter at Niagara; the Cherokee, whose towns the southern states burned in 1776 after Cherokee attacks on the frontier settlements, and whose land cessions began before the war ended. The Loyalists' war: perhaps a fifth of the white population (stated as the conventional estimate), from great officeholders to small farmers who wanted the king's order left standing, subjected through the war to test oaths, tarring and feathering, confiscation of estates under state laws, and banishment, many serving in Loyalist regiments that fought some of the war's cruelest actions against their neighbors, the Revolution in the South being in part a civil war; and at the peace, the evacuation, some sixty thousand or more Loyalists leaving the new United States for Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Quebec, Britain, Florida, and the Caribbean (stated as an estimate), among them some three thousand Black Loyalists whose names, ages, and former owners the British recorded in the Book of Negroes at New York in 1783 so their freedom would hold, many of them moving on in 1792 to Sierra Leone. The reading closes with the reckoning the promises demanded: freedom petitions from the enslaved in Massachusetts and New Hampshire quoting the era's own language back at it, Vermont's constitution of 1777 barring adult slavery, Pennsylvania's gradual emancipation act of 1780, the Massachusetts courts ending slavery in 1783 in the cases of Elizabeth Freeman and Quock Walker, and Washington himself, writing to John Francis Mercer in 1786 that it was his first wish to see a plan for abolishing slavery adopted, the letter carried entire in the next lesson, while the man who wrote it continued to hold people in slavery at Mount Vernon until his death.

**Activity concept:** Whose war. Students work five records, Dunmore's Proclamation, Abigail Adams's letter, a freedom petition from the enslaved (the Massachusetts petition of 1777, built from a named transcription, or a labeled summary), a record of Deborah Sampson's service (labeled teaching summary from the public record), and a record of the Sullivan expedition and the Haudenosaunee decision (labeled summary, or Joseph Brant material from a named edition), and for each one state what the war offered its makers and what it cost them, then judge for whom the Revolution most changed what freedom meant, and for whom it changed least, arguing both from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** Dunmore's Proclamation, inflation, camp followers, Deborah Sampson, Joseph Brant, Sullivan expedition, gradual emancipation

## Lesson 6: The Confederation and the Road to Philadelphia

**Lesson question:** Why did the government the Revolution built first fail to hold the union together?

**Reading scope:** The Articles on their own terms first, as a design with reasons: drafted by Congress in 1777 and ratified in 1781, held up four years by Maryland's refusal until the landed states surrendered their western claims, because a generation that had just fought a distant legislature's taxes built a government that could not tax, a league of sovereign states in a firm league of friendship, one vote per state, a Congress that could declare war, make treaties, coin money, and run a post office, but could neither levy a tax nor regulate commerce nor compel a state, every act of consequence needing nine states and every amendment needing all thirteen. The hamstrung record: revenue by requisition, the states paying what they pleased, which by the mid 1780s was a small fraction of what Congress asked (stated as the record supports, without an invented figure); the army unpaid and its officers' pensions unfunded, a discontent that came near mutiny at Newburgh in 1783 before Washington talked his officers down; the peace treaty unenforced, Britain holding the frontier posts at Niagara, Detroit, and Michilimackinac on the ground that the states were violating the treaty's terms on debts and Loyalist property, and Congress unable to make them comply; Spain closing the Mississippi at New Orleans in 1784 to American trade, and Congress too weak to answer; American merchants shut out of the British West Indies while British goods flooded in and the states laid competing duties on one another's trade, New York taxing New Jersey's firewood and Connecticut's produce. The achievements, given their full weight because the crisis story alone miseducates: the Confederation Congress won and administered the peace, negotiated the land cessions that created a national domain, and in the Land Ordinance of 1785 set the survey of townships and sections that still grids the Midwest and reserved a section of every township for schools, and in the Northwest Ordinance of July 13, 1787, built the machine by which territories became states on equal footing with the original thirteen, guaranteed their inhabitants' civil liberties, and barred slavery from the whole territory north of the Ohio, the ordinance standing as the Confederation's monument and its contradiction in one document, freedom's territory surveyed across land whose Native owners had never sold it. The other founding text of the decade: Virginia's Statute for Religious Freedom, written by Jefferson in 1777 and passed in 1786 under Madison's management, disestablishing the church and declaring that no person should be compelled to support any worship or suffer for religious opinion, carried entire in the audited bank and read in this lesson as the Confederation era legislating like a founding age even where Congress could not. The economy's squeeze in the states: war debts held by speculators who had bought soldiers' certificates for pennies, state taxes levied in hard money to pay them, farmers in the western counties facing foreclosure and debtors' prison, legislatures answering with paper money and stay laws in some states, Rhode Island's paper money fiasco the conservatives' favorite horror, and Massachusetts choosing the hard road, heavy taxes in specie to pay the debt in full. Shays's Rebellion as the squeeze made visible: from August 1786, crowds of indebted farmers in western Massachusetts, many of them Continental veterans, under leaders including Daniel Shays, a captain who had fought at Bunker Hill and Saratoga, closing the courts at Northampton, Worcester, and Springfield to stop foreclosure judgments, the governor answering with a militia financed by private subscription among Boston merchants, which scattered the rebels at the Springfield arsenal in January 1787 where four men died, and broke the movement by February, the legislature then easing the taxes and the voters turning the governor out, the rebellion put down and its grievances partly answered by the state itself. The fear it lit among the propertied: Washington writing from Mount Vernon that the disorders left him wondering what a triumph for the enemies of republican government the news would be in Europe (the sentiment quoted as the record carries it, from his letters of that fall, with the edition named wherever the words are quoted), and the movement already under way given its mandate: the Mount Vernon Conference of 1785 settling a Potomac dispute between Virginia and Maryland and proposing a wider meeting, the Annapolis Convention of September 1786, five states attending, issuing under Hamilton's draft a call for a convention at Philadelphia in May 1787 to render the federal government adequate to the exigencies of the union, and Congress in February 1787 endorsing a convention for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation. The reading closes with the summer's cast on its way to Philadelphia: fifty five delegates from twelve states, Rhode Island sending none, a company of the Revolution's own officers, lawyers, merchants, and planters, younger on average than the Congress of 1776 and bound by instructions several of their states would have been surprised to read by September.

## Lesson 7: The Constitution and the Great Compromises

**Lesson question:** How did fifty five men in one room design a government strong enough to act and limited enough to trust?

**Reading scope:** The setting and the stakes: the State House at Philadelphia from May 25 to September 17, 1787, Washington elected to preside and sitting silent through the debates, the windows shut against eavesdroppers under a secrecy rule that let men change their minds in safety, James Madison arriving eleven days early with a plan already drafted and taking, every night, the notes that make this the best documented founding of the age, those notes themselves carried in the audited bank at their final day. The mandate exceeded in the first week: Congress had authorized a convention for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles, and the Virginia delegation instead proposed, through Edmund Randolph, a wholly new national government, the Virginia Plan, drafted chiefly by Madison: a legislature of two houses apportioned by population or wealth, choosing an executive and a judiciary, empowered to legislate wherever the states were incompetent and to strike down state laws that crossed the national ones, a plan whose numbers logic made it a big state plan and told the small states their equal votes, their one inheritance from the Articles, were the price. The small states' answer: the New Jersey Plan of June 15, keeping a single house with equal votes and patching the Articles with powers to tax and to regulate trade, and the weeks of deadlock that followed, the Convention voting on July 2 to a tie on equal representation in the second house and nearing dissolution. The Great Compromise: the Connecticut delegation's settlement, a House of Representatives apportioned by population and elected by the people for two years, originating all money bills, and a Senate of two from every state, chosen by the state legislatures for six, adopted July 16 by a single vote's margin in a five to four division of states, the decision that made the rest of the document possible, and that fixed the union's double nature, a people in one house and states in the other, into the government's frame. The question the compromise could not be settled without: how to count the enslaved, who could not vote and whom the southern states claimed as property when taxes were counted and as people when seats were counted; the settlement reached back to a Confederation tax formula of 1783, counting each enslaved person as three fifths of a person for both representation and direct taxation, so that slavery, unnamed anywhere in the document, was built into the House's apportionment and, through it, into the electoral college and the presidency itself; and beside it the two further protections the document gave the institution, the clause barring Congress from ending the Atlantic slave trade before 1808, and the clause requiring the return of persons held to service escaping into free states, with Madison's notes recording the debate's own discomfort in the delegates' words, and Madison himself writing later that the Convention had not confronted the thing by its right name. The design assembled from the surviving pieces: federalism, power divided between the nation and the states, each government acting directly on the people within its sphere, the states keeping the whole field of ordinary life, health, property, crime, and family, against the Articles' league of governments acting on governments; separation of powers among a Congress of two houses, an executive chosen by electors rather than by Congress or direct vote (the electoral college stated with its tangled reasons, distrust of a national popular vote across a continent of slow news, fear of cabal, and the advantage it quietly gave the slave states through the three fifths count), and a judiciary appointed for life; checks and balances, the veto and its override, the Senate's advice and consent, impeachment, judicial review nowhere written and everywhere implied by a written constitution; enumerated powers with the necessary and proper clause stretching toward the unforeseen; an amendment process of two thirds and three fourths that made the document at once flexible and hard to move; and a presidency stronger than the Anti-Federalists' nightmares and weaker than the king, with Washington's known occupancy of the office doing much of the design's persuading. What was left out: on September 12 George Mason moved a bill of rights to be prepared by committee, and the Convention, tired and holding that the states' own declarations of rights sufficed, refused without a single state in favor, an omission Mason and Elbridge Gerry and Edmund Randolph would carry into the ratification fight as the refusal that cost the document their signatures; and the Preamble, written last by the Committee of Style in Gouverneur Morris's hand, beginning not with the states' names, as the Articles had, but with We the People of the United States, the claim that the new government rested on the people themselves, carried entire in the audited bank. The close: Franklin's speech of September 17, read aloud for the aged Franklin by Wilson, confessing that there were parts of the Constitution he did not approve and parts he expected to outlive his doubts, and asking every member to doubt a little of his own infallibility and sign, pointing to the sun carved on the back of Washington's chair, which he had wondered through the summer whether it was rising or setting, and now knew to be rising; thirty nine delegates signing, three refusing, the document going out to Congress and then to the states, needing nine of thirteen to take effect among the states that ratified it.

**Activity concept:** Philadelphia in five records. ## Lesson 8: Ratification and the Bill of Rights

**Lesson question:** How was the Constitution sold to a skeptical people, and what did the skeptics win?

**Reading scope:** The contest's terms: the Constitution went to conventions elected in each state for that single purpose, bypassing the state legislatures whose powers it cut, and nine ratifications of thirteen would put it in force among the ratifying states, leaving any holdout outside the union it had belonged to the day before; through the fall of 1787 and the year following, the argument ran in the newspapers as the first great national debate conducted in print, Federalists against Anti-Federalists, and the unit reads it through its two best arguments rather than its slogans. The Federalist case at its strongest: the essays of Publius, eighty five in all by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay, written at newspaper speed for New York's contest and collected as The Federalist, of which No. 10 is read here as the argument's engine: that faction, a group of citizens united by an interest or passion harmful to others or to the whole, cannot be removed without removing liberty itself, which feeds it as air feeds fire; that its causes being incurable, its effects must be controlled; that a small republic falls to its factions because a majority can form and rule at once, while an extended republic multiplies interests until an unjust majority can rarely find itself, representation refining the public voice through chosen citizens; a defense of bigness itself as liberty's protection, the opposite of everything republican theory had taught since Montesquieu. The design defended as machinery in No. 51: that the structure itself must supply the defect of better motives, ambition made to counteract ambition, each branch given the means and the motive to resist the others, so that a government controlling the governed might be obliged in the next place to control itself; the essay's closing judgment that justice is the end of government quoted as the bank carries it. The Anti-Federalist case at its strongest: the essays of Brutus, read here in No. 1, answering that history and Montesquieu agreed a free republic could survive only in a small territory of shared manners, that a government of thirteen million people across a continent, armed with the necessary and proper clause and a supremacy clause that overrode state law, holding a standing army and a power to tax every article of life, would consolidate by slow degrees into the very thing the Revolution had been fought against, and that its distance from the people would make it a government of strangers; and with the argument about size, the objection that outlasted all the others, that the new frame carried no bill of rights, no written fence around speech, press, religion, juries, and arms, at the moment it created the strongest government Americans had ever lived under. The states deciding, in the order the news arrived: Delaware first and unanimous on December 7, 1787, Pennsylvania within the week over minority protests printed for the nation to read, New Jersey and Georgia unanimous, Connecticut in January; then Massachusetts, where the Convention of February 1788 invented the settlement that saved the document, ratifying unconditionally while recommending amendments the first Congress should take up, the Massachusetts compromise, carried 187 to 168 with John Hancock presiding and Samuel Adams's support bought by the promise; Maryland and South Carolina following, New Hampshire in June becoming the ninth and decisive state; then the two prizes still out, Virginia, where the June debates at Richmond set Patrick Henry's eloquence against Madison's brief for a month and ended 89 to 79 for ratification with amendments recommended, and New York, where the Federalist essays had been written to win and the convention at Poughkeepsie, with an Anti-Federalist majority at the start, ratified 30 to 27 in July only after news that New Hampshire and Virginia had already put the Constitution beyond recall, and only with a declaration that ratification implied no surrender of the right to call a second convention; North Carolina and Rhode Island waiting outside, Rhode Island not ratifying until May 1790 and then by two votes and under the pressure of a Congress preparing to treat it as a foreign nation for customs purposes; the Looking Glass cartoon of 1787, Connecticut's ratification argument in a single engraved image, standing in this lesson for the whole contest as the colonies' publics saw it pictured. The launching, kept short because the next unit owns the government at work: elections in the winter of 1788 and 1789, Washington chosen president by every elector, the new government opening at New York in April 1789 with the inauguration on April 30, Congress organizing the executive departments and, in the Judiciary Act, the federal courts, and, first among the promises to redeem, the amendments Massachusetts had asked for. The amendments made: Madison, who had called a bill of rights unnecessary during ratification, taking the promise as his own in the First Congress, introducing amendments on June 8, 1789, steering seventeen through the House and twelve through the Senate out to the states, drawn from the state declarations of rights and the ratifying conventions' recommendations; ten ratified by December 15, 1791, when Virginia's approval made them part of the Constitution, the two left behind, on congressional pay and apportionment, waiting in the record (one of them would be ratified two centuries later as the Twenty Seventh Amendment, a fact the reading may state in one clause). The Bill of Rights read as a document rather than a list: its first words a limit on Congress, its protections for speech, press, religion, and assembly, arms, quartering, searches, juries, and bail, and its closing pair, the Ninth, that the naming of some rights shall not deny others the people retain, and the Tenth, that powers not delegated are reserved to the states or the people, the pair standing as the Federalists' answer to the fear that a list would itself become a trap, and the whole as the Anti-Federalists' defeat converted into their monument. The reading closes the unit at the hinge of 1791: the government the quarrel of 1763 had made necessary stood constituted, elected, and amended, and every question that had built it, how large a republic, how strong a union, whose rights and who counts as the people, stood open as the working questions of the republic the next unit watches learn to argue with itself.

**Activity concept:** The ratification debate. Students work five records, Federalist No. 10, Brutus No. 1, Federalist No. 51, the Looking Glass for 1787 as the bank describes it, and the Bill of Rights, and for each one state the fear it answers or the fear it raises, then judge who won the argument of 1787 and 1788, the Federalists who got their Constitution or the Anti-Federalists who got its first ten amendments, arguing the verdict from what the final settlement contains.

**Vocabulary:** ratification, Federalists, Anti-Federalists, Federalist Papers, faction, Bill of Rights, amendment

## Changes from the working map

- The unit opens with the revenue program of 1764 and 1765 rather than with the French and Indian War's settlement, because the Colonial unit closes at the Treaty of Paris, Pontiac's Rebellion, and the Proclamation of 1763, and its final judgment, that victory changed every term of the partnership at once, is this unit's first premise; restating the war would spend a lesson re-teaching the hinge instead of walking through the door.
- The war gets two lessons divided by kind rather than by year: Lesson 4 teaches the campaigns, and Lesson 5 teaches the war as it was lived by the peoples who fought, paid for, fled, and resisted it, because the peoples' war is not a sidebar to the campaigns and cannot carry its full weight inside a battles narrative; the division follows the course's settled practice of giving enslavement and the war's hardest subjects their own room, as the Colonial unit did with its Lesson 6.
- The Declaration gets a full lesson of its own (Lesson 3), with Paine before it and the drafting edits inside it, because the unit's essential question is double, why they broke and what they built, and the Declaration is the hinge between its halves: the argument that justified the break is the same argument the builders of 1787 claimed to be institutionalizing, and Lesson 8's ratification fight cannot be weighed without it.
- The Confederation is taught as a government with achievements as well as failures (Lesson 6), the land ordinances and the Virginia Statute given their full weight before Shays, because a unit that taught only the crisis would make the Constitution look inevitable, and the record shows a decade that legislated seriously with weak tools; the Constitution then reads as a choice among live alternatives, which is how its makers and its opponents both experienced it.
- Slavery is placed at the center of the founding documents rather than in a single lesson's sidebar: Dunmore's Proclamation and the Black Loyalist exodus in Lesson 5, the deleted passage in Lesson 3, the freedom petitions and first emancipations closing Lesson 5, the Mercer letter in Lesson 6, and the three fifths and slave trade clauses in Lesson 7, because that is where the founding generation itself put the argument, and a unit that moved it to the margin would be misdescribing the record its own documents carry.
