A Progressive Era Lesson Plan Built on the Muckrakers

If you need a Progressive Era lesson plan that does more than list reforms in the order they passed, it is worth starting with the writers, because before there were laws there were magazine articles, and the articles worked because they named names and printed numbers that readers could check. This is the lesson we run second in the Progressive Era unit. Students work through how exposure produced legislation, and then they try the same method themselves on a problem in their own school or town.

Start with the insult

In April 1906, President Theodore Roosevelt gave a speech criticizing journalists who, in his view, did nothing but hunt for filth, and he compared them to a character in Pilgrim's Progress who raked up muck from the floor and never looked up at anything higher. Roosevelt meant the comparison to sting, but the writers adopted the name as a badge of honor, because it described their method fairly. They dug into the dirt of American life and piled what they found where the reading public could not avoid seeing it, working from a simple calculation about how reform happens. Reform laws needed votes, votes needed readers who were angry about something specific, and the muckrakers supplied that anger one documented fact at a time.

Why the exposés landed

Exposure needed a delivery system, and the new mass-circulation magazines provided it. During the 1890s, magazines like McClure's cut their price to ten or fifteen cents and paid their bills with national advertising instead of subscriptions alone, and as circulation climbed into the hundreds of thousands, a magazine earning money from half a million readers could afford to pay a writer to spend months on a single investigation. The result arrived in November 1902, when one run of McClure's carried Ida Tarbell on Standard Oil, Lincoln Steffens on corruption in St. Louis, and Ray Stannard Baker on the railroads, all at the same time. Readers who worked through that issue could see that a monopoly in one industry and bribery in one city were parts of the same pattern rather than separate scandals.

The results were specific enough to trace. Tarbell's account of Rockefeller's rebates, espionage, and coerced buyouts ran in McClure's from 1902 to 1904, and in 1911 the Supreme Court ordered Standard Oil broken into separate companies. Upton Sinclair spent seven weeks in Chicago's Packingtown and wrote The Jungle, a novel about an immigrant family destroyed by the meat industry, intending to win readers over to the workers' side. Readers fixated on the filth going into their food instead, and Sinclair later said he had aimed at the public's heart and hit its stomach. Within months of the novel's publication, Congress passed the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act, both in 1906. David Graham Phillips named individual senators and documented their corporate ties in his 1906 series "The Treason of the Senate," and the campaign that series fed produced the Seventeenth Amendment in 1913, which gave voters the direct election of senators.

The muckrakers also had a generation before them to learn from, since Jacob Riis had already shown in How the Other Half Lives, back in 1890, what a photograph could do that an argument on its own could not. His tenement photographs put readers inside rooms they would never have entered, and Jane Addams wrote from inside the problem in a different way, reporting on Chicago's poor from Hull House, the settlement house she ran. What runs through all of their work is a single method, which is to stay close to the harm, document it carefully, and publish what you find.

The lesson in one period

  1. Five minutes. The bellringer is Roosevelt's insult, and the question is who would take that name as a compliment and why, which students answer in two or three sentences before any of the content has been introduced.
  2. Fifteen minutes. The Muckrakers reading, taken with guided notes. The reading covers the magazine business that paid for the investigations, the five major exposés and what each one produced, and the limits of the strategy, including how readers eventually tired of outrage and how some writers drifted into exaggeration and gave their targets an excuse to dismiss the rest.
  3. Twenty-five minutes. Be a Muckraker, the writing activity. Each student picks a real problem in the school, the town, or an industry they know something about, and the target has to pass three tests before any writing starts. Someone is hurt by the problem, someone is responsible for it or profits from it, and evidence exists that can be documented rather than merely asserted. Students then gather three pieces of evidence, write out the other side's defense and answer it honestly, and compose the exposé in the muckraker structure, leading with the harm, supporting every claim with the evidence they gathered, and ending with one specific demand addressed to whoever has the power to grant it.
  4. Five minutes. The exit ticket asks each student to state their demand in a single sentence, and those sentences are the fastest read in the whole unit on what students actually understood.

The writing step is the reason this lesson sits second in the unit rather than later in it. Students who have just worked through Tarbell's patience with court records tend to write tighter evidence paragraphs than students who are handed a generic persuasive prompt with no model attached, because they have spent the first half of the period watching the method work before anyone asks them to use it.
