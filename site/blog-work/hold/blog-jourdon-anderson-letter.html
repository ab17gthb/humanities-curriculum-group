Jourdon Anderson's Letter to His Former Enslaver, 1865

This post is a source spotlight from The Primary Source Project, our sister site, and spotlights appear here about one post in three. In August 1865, Jourdon Anderson was living in Dayton, Ohio, free since 1864 and earning $25 a month, when his former enslaver, Colonel P. H. Anderson of Tennessee, wrote to ask him to come back and work for wages. Anderson dictated a reply, and the Cincinnati Commercial printed it. The reply treats the invitation the way a free man treats a business offer from someone he has no reason to trust. Anderson asks what the wages would be, describes the life he has already built in Ohio, and then presents the colonel with a bill for the work that he and his wife had already done.

The situation, in his words

Anderson opens with courtesies that carry a sting in nearly every line. He is glad the colonel survived the war, although he had worried about him, since he thought the Yankees would have hanged the colonel for sheltering Confederates who were found at his house. Then he adds this, in the same even tone.

Although you shot at me twice before I left you, I did not want to hear of your being hurt, and am glad you are still living.

He asks what the job pays, and he describes the life he already has, with a comfortable home for his wife Mandy, whom people in Dayton call Mrs. Anderson, and with the children in school and Sunday school while he draws a wage of $25 a month with food and clothing provided. Then he turns to the colonel's suggestion that freedom itself is part of what is being offered.

As to my freedom, which you say I can have, there is nothing to be gained on that score, as I got my free papers in 1864 from the Provost-Marshal-General of the Department of Nashville.

The bill

The center of the letter is a calculation. Anderson and his wife have decided to test the colonel's sincerity, and the test is whether he will send the wages owed for the years they served him, in which case they will forgive the old scores and rely on his promises for the future. Anderson does the arithmetic at the wage he now earns in Ohio.

I served you faithfully for thirty-two years, and Mandy twenty years. At twenty-five dollars a month for me, and two dollars a week for Mandy, our earnings would amount to $11,680. Add to this the interest for the time our wages have been kept back, and deduct what you paid for our clothing, and three doctor's visits to me, and pulling a tooth for Mandy, and the balance will show what we are in justice entitled to. Please send the money by Adams's Express, in care of V. Winters, Esq., Dayton, Ohio. If you fail to pay us for faithful labors in the past, we can have little faith in your promises in the future.

Students usually laugh at the tooth, and then they stop laughing once they work through what the deduction list is doing. Anderson grants every small credit the colonel could possibly claim, down to three doctor's visits and a pulled tooth, and the balance still stands at five figures. The politeness is doing argumentative work, because a letter written in anger could be waved away as temper, while an itemized invoice, complete with interest and with the deductions already taken, leaves the colonel with nothing to answer except payment or silence.

His conditions for returning go beyond money. He asks whether his grown daughters Milly and Jane would be safe in that neighborhood, referring to what happened to Matilda and Catherine without describing it, and he asks whether any schools have opened for Black children nearby, calling his children's education the great desire of his life. The letter then closes with a message for a neighbor that brings the whole performance back to earth.

Say howdy to George Carter, and thank him for taking the pistol from you when you were shooting at me.

The lesson in one period

  1. Five minutes. Set the scene with two facts, which are that the war ended four months earlier and that the colonel is asking a man he once owned to return as an employee, and ask students what a fair reply would even look like under those circumstances.
  2. Fifteen minutes. Read the letter together and pause at the bill, where students check Anderson's arithmetic against his stated wage and his years of service. The numbers hold up, and finding that out changes how the class reads everything that follows.
  3. Fifteen minutes. Close reading in pairs. Students find the sentence where Anderson answers the freedom offer, the sentence where the invoice becomes a test of the colonel's good faith, and the two questions he asks at the end, and for each one they name what it demands from the colonel.
  4. Ten minutes. One paragraph of writing on what Anderson's reply says freedom has to include before a man can call himself free. Wages, safety, and schooling are all in the letter, and students have to rank them and defend the ranking they chose.
