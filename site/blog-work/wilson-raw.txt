Document 1, Wilson. 1) The president, speaking on November 11, 1919, the first anniversary of the armistice. He asks the country to spend the day in reflection on the war and its meaning. 2) "Solemn pride in the heroism of those who died in the country's service" and "gratitude for the victory." Pride, because Americans died heroically; gratitude, both for what the victory freed the world from and for the chance it gave America to stand for peace and justice. 3) Everything in the address looks back at one war: "our enemies laid down their arms," the "associates in Europe," the single victory. No other war is mentioned or imagined. The day honored the dead and the victory of the First World War only. Document 2, McCrae. 1) A Canadian army surgeon wrote it in May 1915, at the front near Ypres, the day after he buried his friend Lieutenant Alexis Helmer, who had been killed in the fighting. It was published in Punch that December. 2) The speakers are the dead soldiers. They ask the living to "take up our quarrel with the foe" and hold the torch high. If the living break faith, the dead warn, "we shall not sleep." 3) Accept reasoned answers grounded in the poem. The poem names no country, no enemy, and no cause, only the dead, the living, and an obligation. Any allied country could read its own soldiers into it, which helped it travel. Document 3, Michael. 1) She is answering McCrae's poem, which she read on November 9, 1918. Her promise, made in the first stanza, is that the living have caught the torch and will "keep the Faith with All who died." 2) The Torch and the Poppy Red. In her poem the red stands for the blood of heroes: "It seems to signal to the skies / That blood of heroes never dies." 3) McCrae's dead demand that the living continue the fight. Michael, writing two days before the armistice, changes the debt. The living owe the dead memory and teaching instead: "We'll teach the lesson that ye wrought." Keeping faith becomes remembrance, symbolized by wearing the poppy, rather than more fighting. Document 4, Owen. 1) Owen was a serving officer writing for people at home, including poets who presented the war as a glorious adventure. Because his readers had not seen the front, he shows them the gas attack in unflinching detail, saying in effect that no one who had watched it could repeat the slogans. 2) The poem has shown a man dying of gas in front of his friends: stumbling and floundering "like a man in fire or lime," drowning under a green sea, his blood gargling from "froth-corrupted lungs," and the sight returning in the speaker's dreams. Nothing in that death is sweet or fitting, which is why Owen calls the Latin claim a lie told to children. 3) Strong answers will argue from Owen's words and notice the tension. Owen attacks telling children with "high zest" that death for one's country is sweet.
