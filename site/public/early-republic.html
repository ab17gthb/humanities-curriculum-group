About this unit

The unit opens with a government invented while it governed. The Constitution named offices and powers but not the machinery, so Washington's cabinet and the federal courts were built new in 1789. Hamilton's program gave it a design: fund the debt, assume the states' war debts, charter a national bank, and lay an excise on whiskey, all on a reading of the Constitution. Jefferson and Madison answered with strict construction, and the argument hardened into the first parties. When western farmers resisted the excise in 1794, Washington marched a militia army west; the rebellion dissolved, and the government's laws stood enforced. The same years gave slavery an engine in the cotton gin, and closed with the Farewell Address, warning against permanent alliances and party spirit.

The republic then tested its promises. In the war scare of 1798, Federalists passed the Alien and Sedition Acts and prosecuted its critics, among them a congressman reelected from his jail cell. Kentucky and Virginia answered that the Constitution was a compact of states, Kentucky naming the remedy nullification. The election of 1800 deadlocked the House through thirty-five ballots, and seated Jefferson without a soldier in the street: a party that had jailed its opponents surrendered power on the strength of ballots, and the Twelfth Amendment kept the tie from recurring. Jefferson unwound much of it, but two inheritances held: the midnight judges, whose undelivered commission produced Marbury v. Madison and judicial review, and Napoleon's offer of Louisiana, which doubled the nation for fifteen million dollars.

Expansion sent the republic to meet the peoples whose country it had bought on paper. The Corps of Discovery ascended the Missouri in 1804, wintered among the Mandan and Hidatsa, crossed the mountains because Sacagawea's kinship opened the Shoshone country, and recorded York and Sacagawea voting in a nation where neither could. The West made a war: impressment, the failed embargo, Tecumseh's confederacy, and the War Hawks carried the republic into war with Britain in 1812. Canada held and Washington burned; Ghent restored the boundaries, Hartford's timing ruined the Federalists, and New Orleans, fought after the peace, made the draw feel like a second independence.

Peace let the national government build: the Second Bank, a protective tariff, Clay's American System, and a nationalist Marshall Court, while the Panic of 1819 taught the West to hear bank as an enemy's name. Monroe closed the hemisphere in 1823; Missouri drew slavery's line at thirty-six thirty to Jefferson's fire bell; and the 1840s, naming the taking Manifest Destiny, annexed Texas, divided Oregon, and took half of Mexico, closing at Guadalupe Hidalgo in February 1848 with the Wilmot Proviso unanswered. The unit is organized around the essential question, “How did the United States turn its Constitution into a working government, and what did continental expansion do to the republic that government served?” A review day gives students a study guide and crossword before the 50-point unit test. Every test item is tagged in the key to a published 15-entry blueprint, so teachers can see what the test asks and where it was taught. The planner turns those choices into a calendar and checks the work against the blueprint, naming any gap while there is time to close it.
