About this unit

The Gilded Age was the era when the United States became an industrial nation. Between the completion of the transcontinental railroad in 1869 and the election of 1896, factories, mines, railroads, and cities grew at a pace that remade daily life. Steel output multiplied, oil became an industry, and corporations learned to organize production on a national scale. The growth was real, and so was the bill. Wealth pooled at the top, the work was dangerous and cheap, and the new order reached into everything from the price of wheat to the water in a tenement tap.

The lessons follow the transformation from several sides. Railroads and heavy industry built one national market, and big business learned to dominate it through trusts and integration, defended by laissez-faire and Social Darwinism and attacked by critics who pushed the Sherman Antitrust Act. Workers answered with unions and strikes, from the Knights of Labor to Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman, and mostly lost to employers backed by courts and troops. Immigrants from southern and eastern Europe and from Asia remade the cities even as nativism wrote exclusion into law. In the West, miners, ranchers, and farmers worked land taken from Native nations, while federal policy pressed assimilation through reservations, boarding schools, and the Dawes Act, and the period closed at Wounded Knee. In the South, Jim Crow hardened into law while Ida B. Wells and Booker T. Washington argued over how to fight it. Farmers in debt built the Populist movement, and its free silver campaign carried William Jennings Bryan into the election of 1896.

The unit is organized around the essential question, “How did industrial growth transform American life, and who gained, who paid, and who resisted?” Students return to it in every lesson, weighing what industrial growth built against the people who paid for it and the movements that resisted it. The eight lessons follow the same working pattern as the sample unit. Each has a reading, guided notes, slides with full speaker notes, an activity, a notebook page, a quick check, and a writing task. A review day gives students a study guide and crossword before the 50-point unit test. Every test item is tagged in the key to a published 15-entry blueprint, so students and teachers can see what the test asks and where that material was taught.

The planner turns those choices into a calendar. A teacher picks the materials that fit a class, sets the start and test dates, and checks the selected work against the blueprint and six era topics. A shorter path through the unit can still be sound if it teaches what the test assesses, and the check shows where a selected set leaves a gap while there is still time to add a reading, activity, or review task. The unit ends in 1896 with the arguments the Progressive Era took up, so it also works as the first half of a two-unit run into the free sample unit.
