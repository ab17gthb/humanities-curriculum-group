About this unit

The unit opens in 1935, with a country determined not to be pulled into another European war. Congress wrote that determination into the Neutrality Acts, barring arms sales and loans to nations at war, while Americans watched Japan invade China, Italy take Ethiopia, and Germany absorb Austria and Czechoslovakia and invade Poland. Inside that story the unit follows Franklin Roosevelt arguing with his own policy: the Quarantine speech of 1937 and its cold reception, the fall of France, destroyers for bases, the first peacetime draft, cash-and-carry giving way to Lend-Lease, and an undeclared naval war in the Atlantic, closing on a country arming an ally and shooting at sea while its laws still said neutral.

Pearl Harbor ended the argument in a day. The second lesson takes up what the attack settled and what it left open, a two-ocean war against three enemies, answered by the Grand Alliance and the Germany first strategy. Mobilization follows: the draft, converted factories, rationing and price controls, war bonds, and the six million women and the Black, Mexican American, and Native American workers who redrew the map of American work. Then the war, in two theaters. In Europe, North Africa, Sicily and Italy, the bombing campaign, D-Day, the drive across France, the Bulge, and the meeting at the Elbe. In the Pacific, Midway and Guadalcanal, island hopping, the submarine blockade, the firebombing of Japan, Iwo Jima, and Okinawa, each step closer to the home islands costing more than the last.

A war fought in democracy's name gets a lesson of its own. Students work the contradiction directly: Executive Order 8802 and the fight for war work, the Double V campaign, service in a segregated military, and, in the same years, Executive Order 9066, the removal of 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry to camps, and a Supreme Court that upheld the exclusion in Korematsu. The Holocaust follows as its own lesson, taught from the record: the Nuremberg Laws, Kristallnacht, the refugees the world refused, the ghettos, the Final Solution, and six million Jews murdered, then liberation, the documentation Eisenhower ordered, and the answer the Allies chose at Nuremberg.

The final lesson runs from Yalta and Potsdam to the atomic decision, from the documents of July and August 1945, and closes with the reckoning: about 405,000 American dead, tens of millions dead worldwide, and a transformed country of demobilized soldiers, the GI Bill, and two powers facing each other over a divided Europe. The unit is organized around the essential question, “How did the Second World War transform the United States, and how far did the country live up to the democracy it claimed to defend?” Each lesson has a reading, guided notes, slides with full speaker notes, an activity, a notebook page, a quick check, and a writing task. A review day gives students a study guide and crossword before the 50-point unit test. Every test item is tagged in the key to a published 15-entry blueprint, so students and teachers can see what the test asks and where that material was taught. The planner turns those choices into a calendar and checks the selected work against the blueprint, naming any gap while there is still time to close it.

Review Day and Unit Test

The test is 50 points: matching, multiple choice, and a source-based section built on President Franklin D. Roosevelt's address to Congress asking for a declaration of war against Japan (December 8, 1941), Executive Order 9066 (February 19, 1942), from the Federal Register, Volume 7, and Robert F. Sargent's photograph "Into the Jaws of Death," Normandy, June 6, 1944, National Archives.
