About this unit

The unit opens in the spring of 1865 with the war won and the peace unwritten. Andrew Johnson offered the South restoration on easy terms: pardons, provisional governors, conventions that repealed secession and ratified the Thirteenth Amendment, and readmission with no congressional conditions. The Freedmen's Bureau, a wartime agency of rations, contracts, and schools, stood between four million freedpeople and the planters who had owned them. Freedom arrived unevenly, proclaimed at Galveston in June 1865, and the restored governments answered it with Black Codes: Mississippi's Vagrant Law rebuilt labor control in statute, fining the unemployed freedman and hiring him out to whoever paid the fine. Freedpeople answered in their own hand, the Edisto Island petitioners claiming the land their labor had made valuable, and Jourdon Anderson pricing thirty-two years of stolen wages.

Congress refused that settlement and built another. The Thirteenth Amendment, proclaimed ratified in December 1865, gave Congress power to enforce freedom, and when Johnson vetoed the Civil Rights Act of 1866, Congress overrode him. The Fourteenth Amendment wrote citizenship into the Constitution itself, birthright citizenship, due process, and equal protection; when ten states refused it, the Reconstruction Acts set those states aside in five military districts and made ratification, new constitutions, and Black suffrage the price of return. The collision ended in impeachment and acquittal by one vote. Registration enrolled seven hundred thousand Black voters; conventions wrote the South's most democratic constitutions; and Black officeholders, Revels in Jefferson Davis's Senate seat and Pinchback governing Louisiana, staffed the new governments. The Fifteenth Amendment closed the trilogy in 1870, barring denial of the vote by race, its silences leaving literacy tests and poll taxes standing.

Freedom on the ground was built without the land settlement that might have secured it. Sherman's field order reserve went back to its owners on Johnson's order, confiscation was defeated, and sharecropping under the crop lien bent free labor toward a yearly debt. What freedpeople built, they built themselves: schools taxed out of poverty wages, a quarter million students by 1870; churches doubling as schoolhouses and league halls; a savings bank lost in 1874. Against the building stood the Ku Klux Klan, its night riding aimed at the Republican electorate; Abram Colby's testimony tells what was done to him for refusing to sell his politics. The Enforcement Acts and the habeas corpus suspension in nine South Carolina counties broke the first Klan by 1872. Then the retreat: amnesty, the Slaughter-House narrowing, the Panic of 1873, the White League by daylight, a Civil Rights Act left unenforced, and a disputed election settled by bargain in 1877, the last troops withdrawn, the amendments standing and their enforcement standing down.

The unit is organized around the essential question, “What did Reconstruction settle about freedom, citizenship, and the federal Union, and what did it leave open?” A review day gives students a study guide and crossword before the 50-point unit test. Every test item is tagged in the key to a published 15-entry blueprint, so teachers can see what the test asks and where it was taught. The planner turns those choices into a calendar and checks the work against the blueprint, naming any gap while there is time to close it.
