About this unit

In 1920 the United States turned away from war and reform crusades and toward making money, and the decade that followed delivered the goods. Radios, movies, automobiles, and electric appliances reached ordinary homes at prices working families could manage, often on installment credit. National advertising taught a nation of local shoppers to want the same products, and network broadcasts and tabloid newspapers gave them the same heroes, Ruth and Lindbergh above all. The prosperity was real, and so were the signs that it rested on less than it seemed.

The first half of the unit follows the new economy and the culture it built. Students trace how Ford's assembly line made the automobile cheap, and how cheap cars then remade the map of daily life: families pulled into suburbs, rail towns stranded, courtship moved out of the parlor, and roadside America built along the new highways. Mass media gets its own lesson, because radio networks, movie palaces, and the popular press put the same voices and faces in front of the whole country at the same hour, while Black and immigrant audiences built media of their own alongside them.

The second half turns to government and to the fights over who belonged. Republican administrations cut taxes, raised the tariff, and kept the state small on purpose, while abroad they pursued disarmament and war-debt settlements without joining the League. Prohibition failed at enforcement and built organized crime instead, and its ledger of gains and costs is the decade's clearest lesson in what government can and cannot do. Against that backdrop the country argued about itself: quota laws ranked immigrants by national origin, a marketed Ku Klux Klan marched on Washington, fundamentalists and scientists fought in a Tennessee courtroom, and two anarchists went to the electric chair while the world judged the trial.

Black Americans answer the decade in the seventh lesson, which follows the Great Migration into northern cities and the Harlem Renaissance it produced, from the Chicago Defender's call to move north to Garvey's mass movement and the NAACP's campaign against lynching. The final lesson audits the boom sector by sector: farms in a depression that ran all decade, labor in retreat, income pooled at the top, debt at the bottom, and speculation on margin, ending with the crash of October 1929 and the distinction the test enforces, that the crash began the Depression without explaining all of it. The unit is organized around the essential question, “How did the prosperity of the 1920s change American life, and why was that prosperity weaker than it looked?” The eight lessons follow the same working pattern as the other units in the course. Each has a reading, guided notes, slides with full speaker notes, an activity, a notebook page, a quick check, and a writing task. A review day gives students a study guide and crossword before the 50-point unit test. Every test item is tagged in the key to a published 15-entry blueprint, so students and teachers can see what the test asks and where that material was taught. The planner turns those choices into a calendar and checks the selected work against the blueprint, naming any gap while there is still time to close it.
