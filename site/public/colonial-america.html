About this unit

The unit opens in 1491, a year before Columbus sailed, with the Americas and Europe as they then stood. The hemisphere held perhaps fifty million people: Tenochtitlan on its lake, larger than London, Cahokia's mounds on the Mississippi, and the Haudenosaunee league, five nations under the Great Law. Across the Atlantic stood rival kingdoms, a Spain newly unified and fresh from the Reconquista, and Portugal pushing down Africa's coast. The meeting ran on the Columbian Exchange and above all on disease: smallpox killed on a scale no army could match, in many regions nine in ten within a century. Colonies rose in a shattered hemisphere, whose nations rebuilt, adapted, and resisted.

Spain built first, an empire of conquest, mines, and missions worked through the encomienda and answered by Bartolomé de las Casas, whose Brief Account of 1552 put the dying on the record. France built on fur instead, bound to Indigenous allies; its sugar islands ran on enslaved labor under the Code Noir. England came late and by company: the Virginia Company planted Jamestown in 1607, lost most of its settlers in the starving time, and was remade by tobacco, hungry for land and labor. 1619 brought both the House of Burgesses and the first Africans sold in Virginia, and after a generation of indentured servants and Bacon's Rebellion in 1676, planters turned to enslaved African labor, bound for life.

New England built covenant towns around a meetinghouse, where belonging was examined and dissent punished: Williams banished, Hutchinson tried, the discipline ending at Salem in 1692. The middle colonies gathered many peoples: Dutch New York, Penn's Pennsylvania, Maryland's uneasy toleration, inside an Atlantic economy of mercantilism and Navigation Acts: grain south, sugar north, manufactures from Britain, enslaved Africans from the Guinea coast. Slavery stands at the system's center in a lesson of its own: the Middle Passage told by Equiano, Virginia's statutes making status follow the mother, the lowcountry's rice plantations, and resistance running to Stono in 1739. Colonial society closes the survey: assemblies taking the purse under salutary neglect, a jury acquitting Zenger, the Awakening teaching people to judge authority for themselves.

The unit closes with the French and Indian War, fought over an Ohio Valley three peoples claimed: Washington's opening, Braddock's defeat, the rejected Plan of Union, Pitt's money and navy, Quebec in 1759, and the Treaty of Paris in 1763 putting France off the mainland, with bills for everyone: a doubled debt, Pontiac's rising, the Proclamation line. The unit is organized around the essential question, “How did the meeting of Europe, Africa, and the Americas remake all three worlds, and what kind of society grew up in England's colonies?” Each lesson has a reading, guided notes, slides with full speaker notes, an activity, a notebook page, a quick check, and a writing task. A review day gives students a study guide and crossword before the 50-point unit test. Every test item is tagged in the key to a published 15-entry blueprint, so teachers can see what the test asks and where it was taught. The planner turns those choices into a calendar and checks the selected work against the blueprint, naming any gap while there is still time to close it.

Review Day and Unit Test

The test is 50 points: matching, multiple choice, a source-based section, and an essay, built on Columbus's letter reporting his first voyage (1493, Gilder Lehrman Institute text), Equiano's Middle Passage account from The Interesting Narrative (1789), and Franklin's Join, or Die cartoon (1754, Library of Congress), each quoted or described from the audited TPSP records with every cut marked.
