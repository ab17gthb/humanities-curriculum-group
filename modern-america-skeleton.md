# Modern America: Assignment Skeleton

_Tenth and final built unit for the Knoweth curriculum and planner site, sitting in course order after The Civil Rights Movement. Modeled on the Progressive Era pilot, the Gilded Age, Imperialism and World War I, the Twenties, the Depression and New Deal, the World War II, the Cold War, and the Civil Rights units. Every lesson has the same working plate so teachers can pick pieces without losing the line of the unit._

## How to read this

Status marks:

- **HAVE**: an audited source or image already exists in the TPSP bank
- **BUILD**: create for this unit
- **LINK**: use the TPSP bank rather than rebuilding it

## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- How did the United States remake itself after 1980, in its economy, its population, and its place in the world, and what did the remaking settle and what did it leave open?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's core plan is 18 instructional days. The unit opens where its two predecessors set down their threads. The Civil Rights unit closes through Bakke in 1978, with the argument over remedy still running. The Cold War unit owns foreign policy through the dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991, and this unit takes the same years for their domestic record: the stagflation and tax revolt of the late 1970s, the Reagan program, the Cold War's end as it landed at home, and the sole superpower decade. From there the unit runs to the present in the order the changes arrived: globalization and the new economy, the digital transformation of work and news, a population remade by the immigration the 1965 act had opened, the September 11 attacks and the wars and security state that followed, the financial collapse of 2008 and its long aftermath, and the polarized present, closed honestly rather than settled. Recent and contested events are treated the way the whole course treats everything. The materials rest on documents, statutes, court opinions, government data, and speeches from more than one side; claims are attributed to their makers; and no editorial verdict is written into a reading, a key, or a deck. The test and the readings present evidence and let students argue. The copyright discipline is heavier here than in any earlier unit, because so much of the record is recent. Presidential addresses, statutes, court opinions, commission reports, and census and labor data are public documents and carry the unit. Memoirs, journalism, editorial cartoons, and convention speeches by private citizens are quoted only at the short lengths the audited TPSP bank records themselves carry, never beyond 90 words from any one work, and every use is counted and disclosed in the manifest.

**The plate in this build**

1. **Reading**: 850 to 900 words with five questions and a teacher key
2. **Guided notes**: keyed to the reading and lesson, with the vocabulary set verbatim and a teacher key
3. **Activity**: stations, jigsaw, map work, simulation, debate, or structured chart work in the locked print design
4. **Notebook page**: a single organizer students keep
5. **Quick check**: a short formative tied to the blueprint
6. **Writing task**: a paragraph or short essay with a scaffold and a teacher key or rubric

TPSP source analyses and photo sets are linked where the audited bank already carries them. They are not rebuilt merely to fill a folder.

**Six state-neutral era topics**

1. The conservative turn: small government and the market ascendancy
2. A new economy: globalization, technology, and the service age
3. A changing people: immigration, region, and the diversifying nation
4. America in the world after the Cold War: sole superpower to war on terror
5. Crisis and response: terror, financial collapse, and the tests of government
6. The unsettled present: polarization, rights, and the argument over the country's direction

## Lesson 1: The Conservative Turn: Stagflation and the Tax Revolt

**Lesson question:** Why did the postwar economic settlement break down in the 1970s, and who organized the politics that replaced it?

**Reading scope:** The settlement first, stated in the course's own terms: the generation after 1945 had expected rising wages, cheap energy, and a federal government that managed the economy, and by the middle 1970s all three expectations failed at once. The breakdown in numbers: inflation rising through the decade while growth stalled and unemployment stayed high, the combination named stagflation, which the standard economics of the day said should not happen together; oil as the accelerant, the Arab oil embargo of 1973 and the Iranian revolution of 1979 sending prices up and gasoline lines around the block, with the country's dependence on imported oil stated as a fact of the era. The political wreckage around the economics: Watergate and the pardon behind the decade (Nixon's one sentence resignation and Ford's Proclamation 4311, both bank records, named as the trust crisis the decade opened with), the fall of Saigon closing the Vietnam account the Cold War unit taught, and a crime and urban fiscal crisis, New York City near bankruptcy in 1975, that made liberal government look exhausted. The diagnoses on offer by 1979: President Carter's address of July 15, 1979, the Crisis of Confidence speech carried in the bank, blaming a national crisis of confidence and a turn toward consumption, answered from the right by the claim that government itself had become the problem. The organization of the answer: the tax revolt, opened by California's Proposition 13 in June 1978, which cut property taxes by more than half and capped them, and spread as limits on taxes and spending across the states; the New Right's machinery of direct mail, single issue groups, and think tanks; the Moral Majority founded in 1979 to carry evangelical voters into politics; and the coalition's southern and western geography, the southern strategy's voters now joined by suburban taxpayers, stated as the electoral arithmetic of 1980. The reading closes with Bakke decided the same month as Proposition 13, the courts already refereeing the argument over remedy, and the election of 1980 standing as the hinge the next lesson takes up.

**Activity concept:** The diagnosis file. Students work through five records, a Crisis of Confidence passage, the Proposition 13 text, a Ford pardon passage, a statistical record of the decade's inflation and unemployment drawn from Bureau of Labor Statistics data, and an ERA passage as the era's other organized politics, and for each one name the diagnosis it carries of what had gone wrong, then judge which diagnosis the election of 1980 rewarded and defend the judgment from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** stagflation, energy crisis, tax revolt, Proposition 13, New Right, Moral Majority, southern strategy

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Crisis of Confidence address, Ford's Proclamation 4311, Nixon's resignation letter, the Equal Rights Amendment text, and the Herblock "Mugging" cartoon record (described, not reproduced), audited records in the Modern era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**. The Proposition 13 text is a public record of the California Constitution, Article XIII A, built for this unit with the edition named. The southern strategy definition is carried verbatim from the Civil Rights unit, as recorded in this unit's build spec.

## Lesson 2: The Reagan Revolution

**Lesson question:** What did the Reagan administration change about what the federal government did, and what did it leave in place?

**Reading scope:** The mandate as its maker stated it: Reagan's first inaugural of January 20, 1981, carried in the bank, declaring that government is not the solution to the problem, government is the problem, and promising to curb the size and influence of the federal establishment. The economic program: supply-side economics as its theorists stated it, the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 cutting income tax rates by a quarter over three years and cutting the top rate from seventy to fifty percent, spending cuts aimed at domestic programs, deregulation continued and extended in banking, transport, and communications, and Paul Volcker's Federal Reserve breaking inflation with interest rates above nineteen percent at the cost of the deep recession of 1981 and 1982, unemployment reaching 10.8 percent. The labor settlement rewritten in one act: the PATCO strike of August 1981, the president firing more than eleven thousand striking air traffic controllers and barring them from federal service, read by unions and employers alike as the era's terms. The coalition held and widened: Reagan Democrats crossing over, the recovery of 1983 and 1984 and the forty nine state reelection of 1984 under the Morning in America claim. The revolution's limits stated in the same record: the federal deficit growing from about nine hundred billion dollars of national debt in 1981 toward three trillion by 1989, the entitlement core of Social Security and Medicare untouched and the tax increase of 1982 quietly reclaiming part of the 1981 cut, and the courts becoming the revolution's longest instrument through judicial appointments. The breach inside the administration: the Iran-Contra affair of 1985 to 1987, arms sold to Iran and proceeds diverted to the Nicaraguan Contras against a congressional ban, investigated by the Tower Commission and congressional committees, stated as the era's largest scandal and the price of a foreign policy run off the books. The era's other domestic wars carried in brief: the War on Drugs and the crack epidemic's mandatory sentences, and the AIDS epidemic, named as the crisis the administration was charged with answering slowly, with claims attributed to their makers on both charges. The reading closes with the Challenger address of January 28, 1986, carried in the bank, as the presidency's other register, the consoler's, and with the question of what the revolution had actually reversed of the New Deal order it set out to undo.

**Activity concept:** The revolution ledger. Students work five records, a First Inaugural passage, an Economic Recovery Tax Act passage, a PATCO record from the public record, a Tower Commission passage, and a Challenger address passage, and enter for each the change it made, the instrument used, tax law, executive order, appointment, or speech, and what it cost or left standing, then judge which single act changed the government's work most durably and defend the judgment against the strongest rival entry.

**Vocabulary:** supply-side economics, deregulation, Reagan Democrat, PATCO strike, federal deficit, Iran-Contra affair, War on Drugs

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Reagan first inaugural and Reagan Challenger address, audited records in the Modern era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**. The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 and the Tower Commission report are public documents, built for this unit with editions named.

## Lesson 3: After the Cold War: Globalization and the New Economy

**Lesson question:** What did the end of the Cold War make possible at home, and who gained and who paid in the economy that followed?

**Reading scope:** The hinge at home first: the Cold War unit owns the diplomacy of 1989 to 1991; this lesson takes the same years as an American domestic event, the Persian Gulf War of January and February 1991 as the sole superpower's first war, brief and televised, and the peace dividend debate, whether the money the rivalry had consumed should now go to deficits, taxes, or needs at home. The rights settlement of the same hinge years: the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, whose findings and signing statement are bank records, extending the era's civil rights method to disability, signed by President George H. W. Bush as the domestic face of the new world order. The economy rebuilt around trade: globalization named and measured, imports and exports rising as a share of the economy, production spread across borders, and the North American Free Trade Agreement, fought through Congress in 1993 against the opposition of organized labor and Ross Perot's warning of jobs moving south, in force from January 1, 1994, with the World Trade Organization following in 1995 and permanent normal trade relations with China in 2000, each step attributed to its makers and its critics. The factory economy's long answer: deindustrialization continuing through the boom, the Rust Belt's emptied mills, the service economy replacing goods jobs with health care, retail, finance, and food service work, and productivity rising while median wages rose slowly, the distributional argument stated with its numbers attributed to the Bureau of Labor Statistics and the Census Bureau. The boom itself: the long expansion of the 1990s, unemployment under four percent by 2000, the federal budget in surplus from 1998 to 2001 for the first time in a generation, welfare law rewritten in 1996 with work requirements and time limits, signed by President Clinton over his own party's left. The shadows inside the boom: the savings and loan cleanup's cost at the decade's open, the first World Trade Center bombing of 1993 named as a warning little heard, and the anti-globalization protests at Seattle in 1999. The reading closes with the question the next era argued: whether the new economy's gains were a tide that lifted all boats or a sorting that lifted some, stated as the question the numbers could frame but not settle.

**Activity concept:** The ledger of the boom. Students work five records, an ADA findings passage, a NAFTA passage, a Persian Gulf War address record from the public record, a trade and manufacturing data record from federal statistics, and a 1996 welfare law passage, and sort each under what the post-Cold War settlement built, who it paid, and who it cost, then judge whether the peace dividend was ever really paid, from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** peace dividend, Persian Gulf War, globalization, North American Free Trade Agreement, outsourcing, Rust Belt, service economy

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Americans with Disabilities Act findings and the Bush ADA signing statement, audited records in the Modern era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**. The NAFTA text and the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 are public documents, built for this unit with editions named; President George H. W. Bush's address of January 16, 1991 is a public presidential document.

## Lesson 4: The Digital Transformation

**Lesson question:** How did the computer and the Internet remake American work, commerce, and news between 1980 and 2010?

**Reading scope:** The machine first: the personal computer moving computing from institutions to desks and homes between the late 1970s and the early 1990s, software becoming an industry of its own, and the workplace rewritten task by task, typing pools and drafting tables giving way to screens. The network: the Internet growing out of government and university systems, opened to the public and to commerce in the middle 1990s, the browser making it usable by anyone, and household access rising from almost none in 1990 to a majority of homes by 2001, the Census Bureau's numbers carrying the claim. The law that framed it: the Telecommunications Act of 1996, the first rewriting of communications law since 1934, loosening ownership rules across broadcasting and cable, and the liability shield in its Section 230 that the platforms later grew behind, quoted from the statute. The boom and the bust: e-commerce remaking retail from almost nothing, the dot-com bubble inflating Internet stocks to a peak in March 2000 and then erasing trillions in paper wealth by 2002, with the railroads' older boom named as the comparison contemporaries reached for. The news world fractured: cable news running around the clock from CNN's founding in 1980, talk radio national after the Fairness Doctrine's repeal in 1987, the newspaper's classified and advertising base draining toward free online listings, and by the 2000s a citizen able to live inside a news world of one tendency, the fragmentation stated as a measured fact of audience data rather than an accusation. The second transformation: social media from the middle 2000s putting publication in every pocket, the smartphone after 2007 joining the network to the person, and the digital divide running along income, education, age, and rural ground, with the Census and NTIA surveys carrying the numbers. The reading closes by handing the consequences forward: a country that shopped, worked, argued, and read differently than it had a generation before, with the political uses of the new machinery taken up in the unit's last lesson.

**Activity concept:** The transformation file. Students work five records, a Telecommunications Act passage including Section 230, a Census Bureau computer and Internet use record, a record of the dot-com peak and crash from federal statistics, a cable news audience record, and a digital divide record from the NTIA surveys, and for each one name the work, commerce, or news practice it changed and the evidence it gives, then judge which change reached furthest into ordinary life by 2010 and defend the judgment.

**Vocabulary:** personal computer, Internet, e-commerce, dot-com bubble, cable news, social media, digital divide

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. The Telecommunications Act of 1996 and the Census and NTIA survey records are public documents, built for this unit with editions named. **BUILD**. No TPSP bank record carries this lesson's texts; the bank's Modern era file holds no computing record, and the lesson is built from federal law and federal statistics, which the lesson's source notes state plainly.

## Lesson 5: A Changing America: Immigration, Cities, and the Sun Belt

**Lesson question:** How did the immigration opened in 1965 and the movement of Americans themselves remake the country's population and map by the twenty first century?

**Reading scope:** The door reopened first: the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, the Hart-Celler Act, ending the national origins quotas of the 1920s and building the new system on family reunification and needed skills, its makers expecting a modest change and producing a transformation, as immigration's sources shifted from Europe toward Latin America and Asia across the following half century. The numbers carrying the claim: the foreign born share of the population rising from under five percent in 1970 to about thirteen percent by the 2010s, the largest immigrant population in the nation's history in absolute terms, from the Census Bureau. The settlement of 1986: the Immigration Reform and Control Act, carried in the bank, joining legal status for about three million people already in the country to the first federal ban on knowingly hiring unauthorized workers, and the argument since over whether its two halves were each carried out. The reckoning inside the same decades: the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, Congress apologizing and paying redress to Japanese Americans interned in the war the World War II unit taught, resting on the Personal Justice Denied report carried in the bank, as the country revised who counted as wronged and owed. The internal migration: the Sun Belt's rise on air conditioning, defense and technology work, and lower costs, the Frost Belt's cities losing people and the South and West gaining them, congressional seats and electoral votes following the moving vans in each census reapportionment. The cities' two stories in the same decades: deindustrialization and white flight hollowing the older industrial cities, Detroit's population falling by more than half from its peak, and from the 1990s a partial urban revival and gentrification raising prices and pushing poorer residents out of neighborhoods in the strongest cities, both stories told with their numbers and neither written as the other's excuse. The reading closes with the demographic fact the century opened on: a country more various in origin, language, and faith than any before it, the argument over what that variety required of its laws and schools carried into the unit's remaining lessons.

**Activity concept:** The changing map file. Students work five records, an Immigration and Nationality Act passage, an Immigration Reform and Control Act passage, a Personal Justice Denied passage, a census data record on the foreign born and on regional growth, and a record of Detroit and Sun Belt population change from census figures, and enter for each the change it documents, its cause as the documents give it, and its size, then judge which single document best explains the country's changed map in 2020 and answer the strongest rival.

**Vocabulary:** Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986, amnesty, Sun Belt, deindustrialization, gentrification, white flight

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Immigration Reform and Control Act and Personal Justice Denied, audited records in the Modern era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 text and the census records are public documents, built for this unit with editions named.

## Lesson 6: September 11 and the War on Terror

**Lesson question:** How did the attacks of September 11, 2001 change what the American government did abroad and at home, and what did Americans argue about the changes?

**Reading scope:** The day first, stated in the Commission's terms: four airliners hijacked by nineteen men of the al Qaeda network, two flown into the World Trade Center, one into the Pentagon, the fourth brought down in Pennsylvania by its passengers, 2,977 people killed, the worst attack on American soil in the nation's history, from the 9/11 Commission Report whose executive summary is carried in the bank. The president's answer that night: President George W. Bush's address of September 11, 2001, carried in the bank, naming the enemy as terrorists and the states that harbor them, and the joint session address of September 20 setting the doctrine beside it. The legal architecture built in weeks: the Authorization for Use of Military Force of September 18, 2001, carried in the bank, passed by all but one member of Congress, and the USA PATRIOT Act of October 2001 widening surveillance, search, and detention powers, its Section 102, carried in the bank, itself condemning discrimination against Arab and Muslim Americans in the same statute that widened the powers. The wars in sequence: Afghanistan from October 2001 against the Taliban government that sheltered al Qaeda, the quick fall of the regime and the long war that followed; Iraq from March 2003, justified to the public and to the United Nations chiefly by the claim of weapons of mass destruction, no stockpiles found, the insurgency and the long occupation, the two wars' combined American dead above seven thousand and their cost in the trillions by the accounting the documents use, with each figure attributed. The state built at home: the Department of Homeland Security of 2002 merging twenty two agencies, airport security federalized, surveillance programs revealed in 2013 by Edward Snowden's disclosures argued over as necessity and as overreach, with the claims on each side attributed. The Commission's judgment and the argument that ran through it all: the report's finding of failures of imagination, policy, capabilities, and management, and the standing argument between the government that said the new powers kept the country safe and the critics who said the wars and the powers cost more than they bought, stated as an argument with two sides documented, not settled by the reading. The reading closes with the wars still running when the financial crisis arrived, handing the exhausted country to the next lesson.

**Activity concept:** The powers file. Students work five records, a 9/11 Commission executive summary passage, a Bush September 11 address passage, the AUMF text, a PATRIOT Act Section 102 passage, and a record of the Iraq weapons claim and its outcome drawn from the public record of the Duelfer Report, and for each one name the power claimed or the finding made, the evidence offered for it, and the check placed on it if any, then judge which document shows the era's machinery at its strongest and which at its most questionable, and defend both judgments.

**Vocabulary:** September 11 attacks, al Qaeda, War on Terror, Authorization for Use of Military Force, USA PATRIOT Act, Department of Homeland Security, weapons of mass destruction

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP 9/11 Commission Report executive summary, Bush's address of September 11, 2001, the Authorization for Use of Military Force, and the PATRIOT Act Section 102, audited records in the Modern era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**. The Duelfer Report of 2004 is a public government document, built for this unit with the edition named. The bank's audio record of the September 20, 2001 address is not quoted as text; the address is named in the reading, following the Cold War unit's handling of audio records.

## Lesson 7: The Great Recession and Its Long Aftermath

**Lesson question:** How did the financial system nearly fail in 2008, what did the government do, and why did the recovery leave so many Americans unconvinced?

**Reading scope:** The boom that built the collapse: house prices rising for a decade on easy credit, subprime mortgages issued to borrowers with weak credit and bundled into securities sold around the world, the rating and leverage machinery described at the level the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission's public report describes it, and the assumption, stated as the era's own, that house prices would not fall nationwide. The failure in sequence: prices falling from 2006, the securities souring, Lehman Brothers failing on September 15, 2008, money market funds and credit markets freezing, and the judgment of the officials who answered, stated in their own record, that the system itself was days from collapse. The rescue and its terms: the Troubled Asset Relief Program of October 2008, seven hundred billion dollars authorized to steady the banks, the Federal Reserve's lending and near zero interest rates, the automobile industry carried through managed bankruptcies, and the stimulus of February 2009, the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act at about eight hundred billion dollars of spending and tax cuts, each measure's size and stated purpose from the public record. The bill as households paid it: unemployment reaching ten percent in October 2009, about nine million jobs lost, millions of homes foreclosed, household wealth down by trillions, the numbers from the Bureau of Labor Statistics and the Federal Reserve. The first fruits of the era gathered in: the Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act of 2009, carried in the bank, the first bill the new president signed, as the recovery government's opening statement about whose losses counted. The long argument the aftermath became: the Affordable Care Act of 2010, the largest extension of health coverage since the 1960s, passed without a vote from the other party and argued over in every election after; the Tea Party rising in 2009 against the bailouts, the stimulus, and the health law as one government grown too large, and Occupy Wall Street in 2011 naming the one percent and the ninety nine, the two answers facing each other across the same wreckage, each quoted from its own documents where documents exist and summarized where they do not. The reading closes with the recovery's arithmetic: output and jobs returned while wealth and trust did not return on the same schedule, the gap the final lesson inherits.

**Activity concept:** The rescue file. Students work five records, a Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission passage, a TARP statute passage, a Ledbetter Act passage, an Affordable Care Act passage, and a pair of records from the Tea Party and Occupy movements, one a posted set of demands and one a founding declaration, labeled as movement documents, and for each one name the failure it answers or the claim it makes and the evidence it rests on, then judge which of the era's two protest answers the following decade's politics rewarded, from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** subprime mortgage, Great Recession, Troubled Asset Relief Program, stimulus, Affordable Care Act, Tea Party movement, Occupy Wall Street

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act, audited record in the Modern era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Report of 2011, the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008, and the Affordable Care Act are public documents, built for this unit with editions named.

## Lesson 8: A Polarized Nation: America in the Twenty-First Century

**Lesson question:** What divided Americans most deeply in the first decades of the twenty first century, and which of the course's oldest arguments were they still having?

**Reading scope:** The sorting first, stated as measured fact: the parties pulling apart in Congress and in the electorate from the 1990s onward, measured by roll call records and survey series the lesson names, landslides at the county level growing, split ticket voting shrinking, and the close election of 2000, settled by the Supreme Court in Bush v. Gore, carried in the bank, as the era's opening lesson in how narrow the country's margins had become. The courts as the era's referees, four decisions carrying the argument: District of Columbia v. Heller in 2008, carried in the bank, holding the Second Amendment protects an individual right to a handgun in the home; Citizens United in 2010, freeing corporate and union independent spending and building the super PAC era; Obergefell v. Hodges in 2015, carried in the bank, requiring every state to license same sex marriage; and the affirmative action argument the Civil Rights unit left at Bakke, closed for college admissions in 2023 when the Court ended race conscious admissions in Students for Fair Admissions, the forty five year argument stated whole from Powell's opinion to the 2023 majority. The demographic and geographic divide beneath the partisan one: the changing population of Lesson 5, the urban and rural economies of Lessons 3 and 4 parting, and the new media machinery of Lesson 4 giving each camp its own account of the same events. The pandemic as the system's stress test: COVID-19 reaching the United States in 2020, more than a million American deaths, schools and businesses closed by state order, a federal rescue of more than two trillion dollars in the CARES Act, vaccines developed in under a year by a government backed crash program, and the argument over mandates and closures dividing along the lines the lesson has already drawn, each claim attributed. The stress on the machinery itself: the election of 2020, its result certified after the attack on the Capitol of January 6, 2021, stated from the public record of the count and the prosecutions, and the argument over the election's legitimacy stated as a documented division, with the claims of each side attributed to their makers and the official record stated as the record. The reading closes the course by returning to its beginning: the arguments of the present, over the size of government, the reach of rights, the terms of membership in the nation, and the country's place in the world, named as the same arguments the course met in 1776, 1861, 1933, 1954, and 1968, still unsettled, which is the honest ending the evidence supports.

**Activity concept:** The continuity file. Students work five records, a Bush v. Gore passage, a Heller passage, an Obergefell passage, a Students for Fair Admissions passage, and a CARES Act or pandemic data record, and for each one name the older argument of the course it continues, in the founding, the Civil War era, the New Deal, the movement years, or the conservative turn, with the evidence for the pairing, then write the course's closing verdict: the strongest single claim the whole year's documents support about what changed in America and what did not.

**Vocabulary:** political polarization, Bush v. Gore, Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, District of Columbia v. Heller, Obergefell v. Hodges, affirmative action, COVID-19 pandemic

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Bush v. Gore, District of Columbia v. Heller, and Obergefell v. Hodges, audited records in the Modern era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**. Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission (558 U.S. 310), Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (600 U.S. 181), and the CARES Act are public documents, built for this unit with editions named. The affirmative action definition is carried verbatim from the Civil Rights unit, as recorded in this unit's build spec. The writing task names TPSP premium DBQ number 23, "Evaluate the extent to which Americans' debate since 1968 over what the country should be has been about its moral condition rather than its policies," as the course's linked closing extension; it is named, not reproduced.

## Changes from the working map

- The unit opens in the late 1970s rather than in 1980, because the conservative turn was a reaction before it was a government: the tax revolt, the New Right's organization, and the diagnosis fight of 1979 all precede the inauguration, and a unit that began at the inaugural would teach the revolution without its cause. The Civil Rights unit's close at Bakke in June 1978 and this unit's open at Proposition 13 the same month are the same hinge seen from the two sides of the course's last boundary.
- The Cold War's end is taught here a second time, deliberately and only in its domestic register: the Gulf War as the sole superpower's first war, the peace dividend argument, and the ADA as the hinge years' rights settlement. The diplomacy, summits, and dissolution belong to the Cold War unit, and this unit names them in a paragraph rather than reteaching them, to keep the boundary the Cold War skeleton set.
- Vietnam and Watergate appear only as the inherited wreckage of Lesson 1, in the bank records of the pardon and the resignation and in one summarizing paragraph, because the Cold War and Civil Rights units own those arcs; this unit's subject is what the wreckage authorized.
- The digital transformation stands as its own lesson rather than a section of the globalization lesson, because the course's own evidence standard divides them: the trade argument is an argument about policy that governments chose, while the network's remaking of work and news was a change in the machinery of daily life, and Lesson 8's account of polarization cannot be built without the media transformation standing on its own first.
- The unit closes with the present argued from documents rather than summarized from headlines. No event after 2023 is assigned a settled meaning in any reading or key; contested claims, including the claims around the election of 2020, are attributed to their makers, and the official record is stated as the record. The final lesson weighs continuities with the course's earlier units because the course, not the news cycle, is the unit's frame.
