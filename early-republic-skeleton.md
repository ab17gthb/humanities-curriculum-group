## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- How did the United States turn its Constitution into a working government, and what did continental expansion do to the republic that government served?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's core plan is 18 instructional days. The unit opens with Washington's inauguration in 1789, exactly where the Founding unit closed (the government constituted, the Bill of Rights ratified in December 1791, with the Farewell Address and the Alien and Sedition Acts named in that unit only as a closing pointer and owned here). It runs through Hamilton's financial program and the first parties, the Whiskey Rebellion and Washington's foreign policy, the Alien and Sedition Acts and the Revolution of 1800, Jefferson's republic and the Louisiana Purchase, Marbury and the Marshall Court's beginnings, Lewis and Clark among the peoples of the West, the road to the War of 1812 and the war itself, the American System and the nationalist Court, the Monroe Doctrine and the Missouri Compromise, and it closes at the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in February 1848, with the question of slavery in the new territory forced onto the table and deliberately left open there: the Jackson and Reform unit opens with the election of 1828, the Missouri Compromise belongs here as the early republic's settlement and warning, and the Compromise of 1850 belongs to the Sectional Crisis unit, not here. The republic is taught with its peoples in the frame throughout: Native nations negotiating, resisting, and being dispossessed on the record; enslaved Americans, including York, whose labor and presence the expansion story usually walks past; women such as Sacagawea acting on the record for reasons of their own; and immigrants and editors in the 1790s learning what the new government's promises were worth to them. Period texts are public domain, but the edition still matters: every quotation names its transcription or facsimile edition, and modernized texts are used only where the bank's audited record carries them, with the modernization the bank record itself discloses.

## Lesson 1: Washington's Republic: Hamilton, Parties, and the Farewell

**Lesson question:** What did Washington's government have to invent, and what did it warn against as it left?

**Reading scope:** The government as an invention problem: the Constitution named offices and powers but not the machinery, and Washington's first administration built the machinery while the ink dried, the cabinet formed out of the departments Congress created in 1789, the federal courts organized, the president received as head of state, and every act a precedent because everything was done for the first time. Hamilton's program as one design with four moving parts: fund the national debt at full value so the government's credit would stand; assume the states' war debts so the creditors of the union would look to the union; charter a national bank to hold the government's money, issue a uniform currency, and make credit move; and lay an excise tax on distilled whiskey to help pay for it all, each part resting on a broad reading of the Constitution's powers, the necessary and proper clause above all. The opposition it manufactured: Jefferson and Madison reading the Constitution strictly, seeing a bank the document never named, funding that enriched speculators who had bought soldiers' certificates for pennies, and assumption that rewarded states which had borrowed wildly; the argument moving out of the cabinet into newspapers, and the first parties forming around it, Federalists and Democratic-Republicans, before anyone had planned a party system at all. The excise answered on the frontier: western farmers for whom whiskey was the only form in which their corn could reach a market, the tax falling on them as the stamp tax had fallen on the colonies, and in 1794 open resistance in western Pennsylvania, tax men tarred, mail robbed, a federal marshal's house attacked; Washington's answer, a militia army of some thirteen thousand called from four states and led part of the way west by the president himself, the rebellion dissolving before it, two men convicted of treason and pardoned, the point made that the new government's laws would be enforced by the government itself rather than watched helplessly as under the Articles. The cotton gin entering the story in the same years, patented by Eli Whitney in 1794, a small machine that made short-staple cotton profitable across the inland South, so that slavery, which many of the founding generation had expected to wither, instead acquired a new economic engine under the new government, a fact the reading states plainly because the whole unit turns on it. The foreign storm: revolution in France turning to war with Britain, American opinion splitting along the party line, Washington's Neutrality Proclamation of 1793 holding the country out of Europe's war against Jefferson's sympathy for France; the Jay Treaty of 1794 settling the frontier posts and trade at the price of popular fury, and Pinckney's Treaty with Spain opening the Mississippi. The Farewell: Washington retiring after two terms in 1796, the precedent itself among his largest gifts, and his published address warning against permanent alliances with foreign nations and against the spirit of party he had watched form inside his own cabinet, the address read as the era's closing document and the next unit's question in the first president's own words.

**Activity concept:** Building the republic in five records. Students work five records, Hamilton's Opinion on the Constitutionality of the Bank in excerpt, the Neutrality Proclamation, the Jay Treaty in excerpt, Whitney's cotton gin patent, and the Farewell Address in excerpt, all audited bank records, and for each one state what problem of the new government it was solving and what new problem it created, then judge which of Hamilton's four measures did the most to make the federal government real, and defend the judgment from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** cabinet, assumption, excise tax, Bank of the United States, Whiskey Rebellion, cotton gin, Farewell Address

## Lesson 2: The Alien and Sedition Acts and the Revolution of 1800

**Lesson question:** When the government called its critics traitors, who answered, and what did the election of 1800 prove?

**Reading scope:** The war scare that made the laws: the French Revolution's wars arriving in American politics as a party question, the XYZ Affair of 1798 publishing the dispatches in which French agents demanded a bribe before they would even negotiate, an undeclared naval war with France in the Caribbean, Federalist majorities preparing for a war they half expected to come ashore, and an immigrant population, Irish and French above all, that voted overwhelmingly for Jefferson's party. The four laws of 1798 read as one program: the Naturalization Act stretching the wait for citizenship from five years to fourteen; the Alien Friends Act and the Alien Enemies Act arming the president against immigrants by decree; and the Sedition Act making it a crime to write or publish false, scandalous, or malicious matter against the government, Congress, or the president, with truth allowed as a defense and juries given the law and the fact together, safeguards its defenders cited and its prosecutions ignored. The prosecutions on the record: about twenty five arrested and some ten convicted, among them a congressman, Matthew Lyon of Vermont, jailed for criticizing President Adams and reelected from his cell, and the Republican editors whose papers were the opposition's only national voice; the act expiring by its own terms on March 3, 1801, the day before Jefferson took office, and the Federalists' last use of the season, the Judiciary Act of 1801 creating new judgeships that Adams filled to the last night, the appointments Lesson 3 inherits. The answer from the states: Jefferson's Kentucky Resolutions and Madison's Virginia Resolutions of 1798 and 1799, arguing that the Constitution was a compact among the states, that the federal government held only delegated powers, and that the states retained the right to judge when those powers had been exceeded, Kentucky's second set going further and naming the remedy nullification, a word and a doctrine that would outgrow their authors and return in later units carrying far heavier loads; ten states answered back rejecting the resolutions, and no state joined them, a limit the reading states, because the doctrine's weakness in 1799 is part of its history. The election of 1800 as the crisis the laws had been built to prevent: Jefferson and Burr tied at seventy three electoral votes because every Republican elector voted for both, the Federalist House of Representatives deadlocked through thirty five ballots in February 1801 with some Federalists scheming to install Burr, Hamilton arguing for Jefferson as the lesser danger, and on the thirty sixth ballot the transfer secured; Adams leaving Washington before dawn on inauguration day and Jefferson walking to his swearing in, and in the First Inaugural the sentence the unit reads whole, that every difference of opinion is not a difference of principle, the claim that the republic could hold its enemies inside the law rather than jail them. The settlement made permanent: the Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, separating the ballots for president and vice president so the tie of 1800 could not happen again, and the era's largest precedent, larger than any single law: a party that had jailed its opponents surrendered power to them on the strength of ballots, and the government changed hands without a soldier in the street.

**Activity concept:** The sedition file. Students work five records, the Alien and Sedition Acts in excerpt, Jefferson's draft of the Kentucky Resolutions, Madison's Virginia Resolutions, a record of Matthew Lyon's prosecution and reelection (labeled teaching summary built from the public record, its labeling on its face), and Jefferson's First Inaugural Address, and for each one state whether it defends the government's power or the citizen's speech and what it claims the Constitution says, then judge whether the Sedition Act was a wartime necessity or a party weapon, and defend the verdict from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** XYZ Affair, Alien Acts, Sedition Act, Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, nullification, Twelfth Amendment, Revolution of 1800

## Lesson 3: Jefferson's Republic and the Louisiana Purchase

**Lesson question:** How did a president elected to shrink the government double the nation's size?

**Reading scope:** Jefferson's program as a deliberate unwinding: the Sedition Act left to expire and its prisoners pardoned, the naturalization wait restored to five years, the whiskey excise repealed, the army and navy cut, the national debt attacked as a moral burden, Gallatin at the Treasury running the government on customs and land sales while paying down what Hamilton had funded, the whole resting on a strict reading of the Constitution that Jefferson had wielded against the Bank and now had to govern with. The inheritance from Adams he could not unwind: the Judiciary Act of 1801 and its midnight judges, commissions signed and sealed and one of them, William Marbury's, left undelivered in the secretary of state's office; Marbury's suit, and Chief Justice Marshall, Adams's own secretary of state and a Federalist in a robe for life, facing a trap in which ordering Madison to deliver the commission would invite refusal and weaken the Court, while surrendering would weaken it further. Marbury v. Madison, February 1803, read as a maneuver and as a doctrine: the Court holding that Marbury deserved his commission, that the law's grant of original jurisdiction to the Court was itself unconstitutional, and that the commission therefore could not be ordered, surrendering the case and claiming in the same opinion the power of judicial review, the authority to declare acts of Congress void, which no president dared attack because the decision had given Jefferson the only outcome he wanted. The Mediterranean test: the Barbary states' tribute system, Jefferson refusing new payments to Tripoli, a small navy sent to the Mediterranean, the Philadelphia captured and burned in Tripoli's harbor, a war of blockade and bombardment closing in 1805, the republic defending its commerce with frigates instead of bribes. Then the offer no ideology survived: Napoleon, his American empire broken in Haiti and war with Britain resuming, offering not New Orleans alone but the whole of Louisiana; Jefferson's constitutional scruple stated honestly, that the Constitution gave no express power to acquire territory or govern it, his draft amendment contemplated and abandoned as the Senate pressed for speed, the treaty signed in April 1803 for fifteen million dollars, roughly doubling the national territory at a stroke; the Federalists who had preached broad construction now discovering strictness, and Jefferson, who had built a career on strictness, signing anyway, the reading holding both facts up without rescuing either party from them. The transfer and the first governings: the flag raised at New Orleans in December 1803, a territory of French and Spanish law and some fifty thousand people, many of them enslaved, governed by appointment from Washington while its people petitioned for the rights of citizens, and the question the purchase opened rather than closed, whether the republic was an empire of liberty expanding or a union dissolving across distance its own theorists had warned against, the question the next lesson walks into the country itself to examine.

**Activity concept:** The purchase file. Students work five records, the Louisiana Purchase Treaty in excerpt, Marbury v. **Vocabulary:** strict construction, implied powers, Louisiana Purchase, midnight judges, Marbury v. Madison, judicial review, Barbary Wars

## Lesson 4: Lewis and Clark and the Peoples of the West

**Lesson question:** What did the Corps of Discovery actually find in the West, and whose country was it?

**Reading scope:** The expedition as an errand of the purchase: Jefferson's instructions to Lewis of June 1803 naming the objects plainly, a water communication across the continent for commerce, knowledge of the country and its peoples, and relations with the Native nations whose land it all was, the president writing of those nations as sovereigns to be treated with, in the same years his government measured their country for sale; the Corps of Discovery setting out from St. Louis in May 1804, some forty men, soldiers and interpreters and Clark's enslaved man York, ascending the Missouri against the current. The river country as inhabited country: the Mandan and Hidatsa villages where the corps wintered in 1804 and 1805, towns larger than St. Louis, farmers and traders whose hospitality the expedition's survival depended on; the Teton Sioux confrontation on the river, diplomacy at the edge of violence; the journals kept as an instrument of possession, every river named, every nation counted, every plant and animal described for a government that meant to use the record. The winter's hire that changed the expedition: Toussaint Charbonneau, a French Canadian trader, engaged largely because his young wife Sacagawea, a Shoshone woman taken captive by the Hidatsa as a girl, could interpret and because a party traveling with a woman and an infant, her son Jean Baptiste born that February, read to the nations they met as a party of peace rather than war. The mountains and the kinship: the Great Falls portage consuming a month, the corps starving toward the divide, and the meeting with the Shoshone in August 1805 in which Sacagawea, interpreting, recognized the chief Cameahwait as her brother, the expedition's horses and its passage west obtained in the space of that recognition; the crossing of the Bitterroots in snow, the Nez Perce feeding and guiding the strangers, the descent of the Columbia, the winter at Fort Clatsop in the rain, the vote on the winter camp in which York and Sacagawea both voted, a ballot recorded in the journals without comment in a nation where neither could vote. The return and the uses of the record: the corps back at St. Louis in September 1806 having lost one man to illness and killed two Blackfeet in a single violent encounter on the way home, its maps redrawing the continent's geography for the government that had bought a claim on paper, its journals the opening of a fur trade that would reach the Mandan villages a generation before smallpox destroyed them. The treaty system behind the whole story: the Treaty of Greenville of 1795, in which the Western Confederacy, defeated at Fallen Timbers, ceded most of present Ohio, standing in this lesson for the instrument by which the United States had been taking the West for a decade before the corps set out, purchase and pressure and cession lines, so that the expedition reads not as a walk into emptiness but as a survey of other peoples' countries by a republic that had already decided what the survey was for. The reading closes on the two facts the journals hold together: the expedition was a triumph of endurance and record keeping, and it was the first page of a dispossession, and both are in the documents the students have just read.

**Activity concept:** The expedition file. Students work five records, the Treaty of Greenville in excerpt, Jefferson's instructions to Lewis (Library of Congress transcription), Clark's journal entry for May 14, 1804, a record of the Shoshone meeting and Sacagawea's recognition of her brother (built from the journals in a named edition), and a record of York's service and his refused petition for freedom (labeled teaching summary built from the journals and Clark's letters, its labeling on its face), and for each one state whose purposes the expedition served at that moment, then judge whether the Corps of Discovery should be remembered chiefly as explorers, diplomats, or surveyors for settlement, and defend the judgment from the documents.

## Lesson 5: The War of 1812

**Lesson question:** Why did the United States go to war with Britain again, and what did the war actually settle?

**Reading scope:** The grievances as the war's makers stated them: a Europe at war since 1803, Britain ruling the seas and France the continent, the United States caught between Orders in Council and imperial decrees, its ships seized by both sides and its sailors taken by one, impressment, the Royal Navy's practice of stopping American vessels and carrying off seamen it claimed as British subjects, some of them American citizens, thousands taken across the years. The peaceful weapon tried first and its failure: the Embargo Act of 1807, Jefferson's wager that closing every American port would starve Britain and France into respecting neutral rights without a shot, the exports of the United States collapsing by four fifths, New England's harbors idle and its smugglers busy, the law enforced with a coercion at home that sat strangely on a republic of limited government, repealed in 1809 with the grievances untouched. The West's war inside the war: settlers pressing into Indiana and Ohio against treaties the Native nations read as frauds, Tecumseh building a confederacy on the claim that no single nation could sell land held by all in common, his brother Tenskwatawa, the Prophet, preaching renewal at Prophetstown, and Governor William Henry Harrison marching on the town in Tecumseh's absence and fighting the Battle of Tippecanoe in November 1811, a victory claimed and a town burned, after which Tecumseh's confederacy turned openly to the British in Canada, so that on the frontier the war of 1812 was also a war over whether the Old Northwest would be Native country or American states. The declaration: the War Hawks, young congressmen led by Henry Clay of Kentucky and John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, arguing that Canada could be taken by marching and that national honor was itself a cause; Madison's war message of June 1, 1812, laying impressment, the Orders in Council, and the blockades before Congress in the order the administration ranked them; the vote, the closest war vote in the nation's history, every Federalist and the commercial Northeast against, so that the republic went to war divided in a way the reading refuses to hide. The war's humiliations and its one clean arc: the invasions of Canada failing year after year, York burned and Washington burned in answer, Dolley Madison saving the Lansdowne portrait of Washington from the President's House ahead of the fire, the rockets over Fort McHenry and Francis Scott Key's verses in September 1814; the war at sea and on the lakes fought better than the war on land, and the Creek War inside it in the South, Jackson at Horseshoe Bend in 1814 taking twenty three million acres of Creek land in the treaty that followed, the war's largest single land transfer falling on a Native nation, some of whose people had fought beside him. The opposition's last stand and its timing: New England's governors refusing militia, its merchants trading with the enemy, its Federalists meeting at Hartford through December 1814, the convention's moderates carrying amendments instead of secession, and its report traveling south exactly as the news traveled north: the Treaty of Ghent, signed December 24, 1814, restoring the boundaries of 1811 and saying nothing about impressment or neutral rights because Napoleon was beaten and the grievances had died with his wars, and Andrew Jackson, not knowing of the peace, destroying a British army below New Orleans on January 8, 1815, in the war's most lopsided battle. The settlement the reading leaves students with: the war settled none of its stated causes on paper and nearly everything in fact, Canada confirmed unconquerable, the Native confederacies of the West broken for good, the Federalist Party ruined by its own report's timing, and a generation of officers and voters persuaded the republic had fought Britain to a draw and won its independence a second time.

**Activity concept:** The war file. Students work five records, the Embargo Act in excerpt, Madison's War Message of June 1, 1812, Tecumseh's address to Governor Harrison of August 1810 (named edition), the Hartford Convention Resolutions, and Key's Star-Spangled Banner in its four stanzas, and for each one state which of the war's causes or costs it speaks for, then judge whether the United States won, lost, or merely survived the War of 1812, and defend the verdict from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** impressment, embargo, War Hawks, Tecumseh, Tippecanoe, Hartford Convention, Treaty of Ghent

## Lesson 6: The Era of Good Feelings: The American System and the Marshall Court

**Lesson question:** After the war, how far would Americans let their national government build?

**Reading scope:** The peace dividend and the mood it bought: the Federalists gone as a national party, Monroe's tours of 1817 received in Boston as the opening of an Era of Good Feelings, one party holding the field and the old arguments of 1798 seemingly buried, while underneath the surface the war had taught lessons the republic now acted on, that a government without a bank, a tariff, or roads had nearly lost a winnable war. The nationalist program of 1816 read as a sequel to Hamilton by Hamilton's opponents' heirs: the Second Bank of the United States chartered for twenty years, the Tariff of 1816, the first tariff laid avowedly for protection, Madison signing both, the Republican president enacting the Federalist program a generation late and calling it experience. Clay's American System as the program's full statement: a national bank to give the currency order, a tariff to shelter manufactures until they could stand, and the tariff's revenue spent on internal improvements, roads and canals binding the sections into one market, the West's food feeding the East's mill towns, the South's cotton clothing both, each section prospering by the others' demand, an economic union offered as the answer to the political union's fragility. The constitutional brakes applied by the program's own friends: Madison's veto of the Bonus Bill on his last day in office in 1817, holding that the power to build roads and canals was nowhere granted and would require an amendment for all his agreement that the roads were needed; Monroe's veto of 1822 in the same line; and the states building what the federal government would not, New York breaking ground on the Erie Canal in 1817 with the state treasury's money, the era's greatest improvement arriving by state action while the constitutional argument stood unresolved. The Panic of 1819 as the era's first fracture: land bought on credit at prices cotton's boom had floated, the Second Bank under new management calling in loans to save itself, state banks failing, western farms foreclosed by the national bank its debtors now cursed, unemployment in the cities and relief laws in the states, and a generation of westerners learning to hear the word bank as an enemy's name, the lesson Jackson's unit will cash in. The Marshall Court building the Constitution's nationalist reading into law across the same years: McCulloch v. Maryland in 1819, Maryland's tax on the Bank's Baltimore branch struck down, Marshall's opinion holding the Bank constitutional under powers implied from the great ends of the document, the phrase the opinion is remembered by, that the power to tax involves the power to destroy, and its rule for reading the Constitution itself, that it was a constitution intended to endure for ages to come and be adapted to the various crises of human affairs; Dartmouth College protecting charters as contracts; Gibbons v. Ogden in 1824 reading commerce among the states to reach a steamboat monopoly on New York's waters, the decision striking the monopoly and announcing that commerce included navigation and intercourse, not traffic alone. The reading closes on the Era of Good Feelings read against its own record: the feelings were real at the top of politics and the machinery built in those years was permanent, but the bank fight, the panic's resentments, and the Court's broad Constitution were all gathering the forces that would end the calm, and one question above all had merely been waiting for a territory to ask it again, which Lesson 7 takes up.

**Vocabulary:** American System, Second Bank of the United States, protective tariff, internal improvements, Panic of 1819, McCulloch v. Maryland, Gibbons v. Ogden

## Lesson 7: The Monroe Doctrine and the Missouri Compromise

**Lesson question:** What did the settlements of 1820 settle, and what did they only postpone?

**Reading scope:** Two settlements in one season, the second Monroe administration opening with the republic facing outward and inward at once. The outward settlement's materials: Spain's American empire dissolving in revolution, new republics from Mexico to Chile declaring themselves through the 1810s and 1820s, the United States recognizing them in 1822; the Adams-Onís Treaty of 1819 already having taken Florida and drawn a line of American and Spanish claims to the Pacific; Russia's ukase of 1821 claiming the Pacific coast down to the 51st parallel; Britain, whose trade wanted the new republics open and the old empires shut out, proposing through Canning a joint declaration with the United States, and John Quincy Adams arguing in cabinet that his country should declare its principles itself rather than come in, in his phrase, as a cockboat in the wake of the British man of war. The Doctrine as Monroe delivered it in the annual message of December 2, 1823, in the passages the unit reads whole: the American continents, by the free and independent condition they had assumed, no longer subjects for future colonization by any European power; any attempt to extend the European system to this hemisphere dangerous to the peace and safety of the United States; and, in the other direction, no American interference with Europe's existing colonies or its internal concerns. The doctrine's real weight stated honestly: a declaration the United States could not have enforced alone in 1823, floating on the British navy whose interest ran the same way, addressed as much to American voters and to the new republics as to Europe's chancelleries, and durable because later generations kept choosing to stand on it, Roosevelt's corollary in a later unit being only the Doctrine's most famous rereading. The inward settlement's opening: Missouri applying for statehood in 1819 as a slave state, the balance in the Senate standing at eleven free and eleven slave, Representative Tallmadge of New York proposing that Missouri enter with no more enslaved people brought in and those born there freed at twenty five, the amendment passing the House on sectional lines and dying in the Senate, and the Congress of the Era of Good Feelings discovering that the question it had compromised in 1787 and 1794 had been growing with every cotton bale the gin had made profitable. The settlement of 1820 in its three parts: Maine, waiting as a district of Massachusetts, entering as a free state; Missouri entering as a slave state with its constitution's bar on free Black citizens noted and fudged rather than settled; and the line, Thomas's amendment drawing 36 degrees 30 minutes across the Louisiana Purchase lands, slavery barred north of it, permitted south, the line holding for a generation because it gave both sections a map they could stop arguing over. The warning the settlement came with: Jefferson in retirement at Monticello writing to John Holmes in April 1820 that the momentous question, like a fire bell in the night, awakened and filled him with terror, that he considered it the knell of the Union, the old man who had bought the territory reading the line drawn across it as a geographical line, coinciding with a marked principle, moral and political, that once conceived would never be obliterated and would deepen with every new irritation. The reading closes holding the two settlements together: in the same years the republic told Europe a hemisphere was closed and told itself a line would hold, and both declarations were acts of confidence by a government that had learned to settle by declaring, with the fire bell already rung in at least one house in Virginia for anyone who wanted to hear it.

## Lesson 8: Manifest Destiny and the War with Mexico

**Lesson question:** What did Americans tell themselves expansion was, and what did the war with Mexico leave unsettled?

**Reading scope:** The lull and the acceleration: the line of 1820 holding through the 1820s while settlement filled the lands east of the Mississippi, the republic's population doubling and doubling again, steamboats and canals moving the filling west, and by the 1840s the question no longer whether the continent would be taken but on what story the taking would be told. Texas as the story's first test: American settlers invited into Mexican Texas in the 1820s on conditions Mexico set and the settlers increasingly ignored, slavery among them, Texas revolting in 1836 and winning its independence at San Jacinto, and then the Republic of Texas standing outside the Union for nine years, recognized, courted by Britain and France, and unadmitted because every president from Jackson to Tyler could count: a Texas admitted was a slave state admitted, and the Senate's balance and the northern House stood in the way, until annexation arrived in 1845 by joint resolution, a simple majority of both houses doing what a treaty needing two thirds of the Senate could not, and doing it in the last days of Tyler's presidency with Polk elected on expansion. The name the expansion took: John O'Sullivan writing in 1845 of the nation's manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence, the phrase giving the movement its theology, the continent as a gift already given and American institutions as the proof of the gift, and the reading stating plainly what the phrase was built to make unseeable, that the continent was inhabited, governed, and claimed, by Native nations whose country the survey lines crossed and by a Mexican republic whose northern provinces were thinly held but sovereign. Oregon as the expansion that ended in division: the country jointly occupied with Britain since 1818, the campaign cry of fifty-four forty or fight, and Polk, with a war opening against Mexico, settling at the 49th parallel in 1846, the same president taking half of Mexico by war while halving his claim against Britain by treaty, the difference between the two negotiations being part of the lesson. The war with Mexico as Polk conducted it: American troops ordered into the ground between the Nueces and the Rio Grande that both nations claimed, the Thornton affair's bloodshed on the Rio Grande in April 1846, Polk's message that Mexico had shed American blood on American soil, a young congressman named Abraham Lincoln answering later with his spot resolutions demanding the president name the exact spot; the war itself, Taylor in the north, Scott from Veracruz to Mexico City, Kearny and Frémont in New Mexico and California where the Bear Flag revolt had already raised an American flag, the United States winning every campaign against a nation divided against itself; and the opposition the war drew at home, Whigs who had voted the supplies while doubting the cause, abolitionists naming it a slave power war for new slave states, Thoreau in a Massachusetts jail for refusing its taxes. The settlement and the question it forced: the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo of February 2, 1848, the Rio Grande confirmed as Texas's border, California, New Mexico, and the country between ceded for fifteen million dollars and the assumption of claims, some five hundred thousand square miles added in a signature, the treaty arriving in Washington nearly unopposed because opposing it meant opposing the peace. And the proviso that had already failed and would not go away: David Wilmot's amendment of 1846, offered while the war still ran, barring slavery in any territory taken from Mexico, passing the House again and again on sectional votes and dying in the Senate, so that the question the Missouri line had answered for the Louisiana lands now stood unanswered for the Mexican cession: the unit closing where the treaty left it, with the republic doubled again in a lifetime, its government proved workable, its armies victorious, its doctrines declared, and the one question every settlement since 1787 had deferred standing alone on the table of the enlarged house, in the words of the men who had failed to settle it.

## Changes from the working map

- The unit opens with Washington's administration rather than with a recap of ratification, because the Founding unit closes at December 1791 with the government constituted and names the Farewell Address and the Alien and Sedition Acts only as a pointer; this unit picks up both documents as its own ground, the Farewell in Lesson 1 and the acts in Lesson 2.
- The embargo stands in Lesson 5 as the road to war rather than in Lesson 3 with Jefferson's presidency, because the embargo's meaning is the failure of peaceful coercion that made 1812 arguable, and Jefferson's lesson carries the purchase and Marbury at full weight without it; the reading in Lesson 3 closes on the Mediterranean and the purchase, and Lesson 5 opens the embargo's account whole.
- Marbury is taught inside Jefferson's lesson rather than Adams's, because the case arose from Adams's appointments but was decided in 1803 against Jefferson's administration, and its pairing with the purchase, a strict constructionist claiming judicial review's ground and signing an unconstitutional looking treaty in the same season, is the lesson's argument.
- The Missouri Compromise is taught here rather than in the Jackson unit, as the early republic's settlement and warning, because its materials are the Louisiana lands, the cotton gin's economics, and Jefferson's fire bell, all of this unit's ground; the Jackson unit opens with the election of 1828 and inherits the doctrine of nullification this unit's Lesson 2 defines, which the Jackson build must carry character for character from this unit's spec.
- Texas stands at the boundary: the republic's decade of independence is summarized inside Lesson 8 rather than given its own lesson, because the annexation of 1845 and the war it caused are this unit's close and the Compromise of 1850, which the cession made necessary, opens the Sectional Crisis unit; the unit therefore ends in February 1848 with slavery's expansion forced onto the table and left there, deliberately unsettled, as the record left it.
