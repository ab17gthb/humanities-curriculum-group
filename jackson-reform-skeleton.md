## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- What did democracy mean in Jackson's America, who was it for, and what did the era's reformers claim it still owed?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's core plan is 18 instructional days. The unit opens with the election of 1828, the first presidential election decided by a mass white electorate, ten years before the Early Republic unit's closing date: that unit closes at the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in February 1848 with the Wilmot Proviso's question left open, and this unit treats Texas annexation and the war with Mexico as prior knowledge, taught there, and does not reteach them. It runs through Jackson's presidency and the democracy that bore his name, the removal of the southeastern Native nations, the nullification crisis, the Bank War and the panic that followed it, the market revolution's second phase of factories, canals, railroads, and the telegraph, the Second Great Awakening and the reform movements it powered, abolition's turn from gradualism to immediatism, and it closes at the end of 1848 with the Seneca Falls Convention, the moment the era's reform logic was turned on the legal condition of women. Two boundaries hold. The Missouri Compromise belongs to the Early Republic unit as settlement and warning; it is prior knowledge here, named in prose and nowhere retaught as core. The Compromise of 1850 opens the Sectional Crisis and Civil War unit; it is not taught here, and the unit's closing lesson stops at December 1848 with the reform movements' agenda complete and the sectional question standing where the Early Republic unit left it, deliberately unanswered. The era is taught with its peoples in the frame throughout: Cherokee constitutionalists and signers of a minority treaty, mill women writing for their own newspaper, enslaved rebels and the free Black writers who addressed them, revival converts organizing societies, and the women who had run abolition's local work for a decade before they demanded its logic for themselves. Period texts are public domain, but the edition still matters: every quotation names its transcription or facsimile edition, and modernized texts are used only where the bank's audited record carries them, with the modernization the bank record itself discloses.

## Lesson 1: The Election of 1828 and Jacksonian Democracy

**Lesson question:** What changed when the common white man got the vote, and who was still left outside it?

**Reading scope:** The electorate remade before the candidate: state after state in the 1810s and 1820s striking the property qualifications that had fenced the vote since the founding, until by 1828 nearly every white man could vote and turnout had become the currency of politics, while the same constitutions that widened the white vote narrowed others, women excluded everywhere but New Jersey's closed door of 1807, free Black men stripped of the vote in states that had let them cast it, Native nations outside the polity entirely. The grievance that made the candidate: the election of 1824, four candidates, Jackson first in popular and electoral votes and short of a majority, the House choosing John Quincy Adams, Henry Clay named secretary of state, and the cry of corrupt bargain that Jackson's men carried through four years of organizing, the charge unprovable and unforgettable, and truer to the new politics than to the facts. The campaign of 1828 as the new politics in the open: committees and newspapers and barbecues, mudslinging of a depth the founders would not have survived, Rachel Jackson attacked in print and dead between the election and the inauguration, Jackson arriving in Washington as a widower at the head of a movement that believed the government had been stolen from the people and returned. The inauguration as the movement's portrait: the crowd in the White House, muddy boots on the furniture in the hostile accounts and the people's possession in the friendly ones, Margaret Bayard Smith's letter read as evidence by both sides since. The doctrine of the administration stated in Jackson's first annual message: offices as public trusts no man had more right to than another, rotation as the democratic answer to a permanent officeholding class, and the removals that followed, modest beside the legend, some nine hundred of nine thousand civil officers by the usual count, but establishing the principle the opponents named from the Senate floor, that to the victor belong the spoils. The limits stated in the same lesson and never let go of afterward: a democracy widened by race and sex at once, its expansion for white men built alongside the exclusion of everyone else, its hero a slaveholder and a cotton planter whose portrait of the people's rule the rest of the unit tests against removal, the bank, and the tariff.

**Activity concept:** The people's president file. ## Lesson 2: Indian Removal

**Lesson question:** How did the United States take the Southeast, and what did the Cherokee do to stop it?

**Reading scope:** The ground of the question: some 125,000 Native people living east of the Mississippi in 1828 by the usual estimates, and in the Southeast the five nations white Americans called civilized for their written constitutions, schools, farms, mills, and printing, the Cherokee with a syllabary, a bilingual newspaper, and a constitution modeled on the republic dispossessing them, their country coveted because cotton land was wealth and because Georgia gold was found on Cherokee land in 1828. The pressure and the answer before Jackson: Georgia demanding the federal government honor its 1802 compact to extinguish Native title, extending its laws over Cherokee country in 1829 and 1830, the Cherokee answering not with war but with law, a memorial campaign to Congress and a case carried to the nation's own court. The law that made removal national policy: the Indian Removal Act of May 1830, passed the House by five votes, authorizing the president to exchange eastern lands for country west of the Mississippi and appropriating half a million dollars, and Jackson's annual message of December 1830 defending it in the language of benevolence, the father removing his children from contact with a civilization he claimed was destroying them, the message read here in the bank's audited text. The court's answer and its fate: Cherokee Nation v. Georgia in 1831, the nation held a domestic dependent nation, and Worcester v. Georgia in 1832, Marshall holding Georgia's laws without force in Cherokee country, the judgment defied by Georgia and unenforced by the president, the missionary Worcester freed in 1833 only when Georgia let the case die, the lesson the Cherokee drew being that the republic's law ran where the republic chose to run it. The instrument and the march: the Treaty of New Echota of December 1835, signed by Major Ridge, John Ridge, Elias Boudinot, and their party without the authority of Ross's government, protested by a petition carrying the names of nearly the whole nation, ratified by the Senate by one vote, and in 1838 General Scott's troops rounding up some sixteen thousand Cherokee into camps and detachments, the march west through the winter of 1838 and 1839, roughly a quarter of the people dying on the road by the standard estimate, Ross's wife Quatie among them. The others and the ones who stayed: the Choctaw removed first and in disorder, the Creek in chains after a war of resistance, the Chickasaw on better terms they negotiated, the Seminole fighting the Second Seminole War, the costliest Indian war the United States ever fought, and never wholly removed, a remnant holding in Florida; and the Eastern Band of Cherokee remaining in North Carolina on land bought back by William Holland Thomas. The reading closes on the two statements of the lesson held together: removal was carried out under law at every step, act, treaty, appropriation, and bayonet, and the law's forms were used by men who had been told by the nation's own court that the ground was not theirs to take.

**Vocabulary:** Indian Removal Act, Cherokee Nation, Worcester v. Georgia, Five Civilized Tribes, Treaty of New Echota, Trail of Tears, John Ross

## Lesson 3: The Nullification Crisis

**Lesson question:** Could a state veto the Union, and what made South Carolina back down?

**Reading scope:** The tariff as the South's bill for the market revolution: the duties of 1816 through 1828 laid to shelter northern manufactures, raising the prices the planting states paid for everything they bought and inviting retaliation against the cotton that paid for it all, South Carolina's economy stagnant while its representatives did the arithmetic of a permanent minority. The doctrine built to answer a minority's arithmetic: Calhoun, the vice president writing in secret, the South Carolina Exposition and Protest of December 1828 arguing from the compact of 1798 that a state could interpose its sovereignty and nullify a federal law within its borders until three fourths of the states amended the Constitution over its head, the doctrine carried verbatim in this unit from the Early Republic build, whose Kentucky Resolutions are its seed. The doctrine aired and answered in the Senate: the Webster-Hayne debate of January 1830, begun over western land sales and risen to the nature of the Union itself, Hayne for the compact and Webster for the people, his second reply closing with the defense of liberty and union as one and inseparable that schoolchildren would recite for a century. The doctrine made personal: the Jefferson Day dinner of April 1830, Jackson's toast rising glass in hand, our federal Union, it must be preserved, and Calhoun's answer, the Union, next to our liberty most dear, the table understanding that the president and his vice president had just declared positions, and the breach completed by the Eaton affair and the Van Buren succession, Calhoun resigning the vice presidency in December 1832, the only man to hold the office under two presidents and surrender it for a Senate seat and a doctrine. The crisis itself: the Tariff of 1832 lowering duties and nullifying nothing, South Carolina's convention answering in November 1832 with the Ordinance of Nullification, the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 declared void, collection forbidden after February 1, 1833, secession threatened if the duties were collected by force, the ordinance read here in the bank's audited text. The president's answer, Jackson's proclamation of December 10, 1832, telling South Carolina that the Union was a government and not a league, that nullification was treason's argument dressed in law, and asking the state, in the proclamation's hardest passage, whether it would persist in disunion against the country its own sons had bled for. The settlement and its double edge: no other state joining South Carolina, the Force Bill of March 1833 arming the president to collect the duties, and Clay's compromise tariff of the same week lowering the duties by stages to 1816 levels by 1842, South Carolina repealing its ordinance and nullifying the Force Bill in empty triumph, each side claiming it had won. The reading closes on what the crisis rehearsed: a state had written the legal brief for leaving the Union twenty eight years before the brief was used, and every man in the argument of 1833 knew the question under the tariff was already becoming the question of slavery, Calhoun himself saying within the decade that the tariff was only the occasion, not the cause, of the quarrel, the cause being slavery itself.

## Lesson 4: The Bank War and the Panic

**Lesson question:** Was the Bank a public good or a private monster, and who paid when it fell?

**Reading scope:** The institution the era argued about: the Second Bank of the United States as the Early Republic unit left it, chartered in 1816, by 1832 holding the government's deposits, issuing the paper that passed for national currency, regulating state banks by returning their notes for specie, and lending to congressmen, editors, and businesses in every section, Biddle running it as a central bank before the country had the word, its usefulness real and its accountability to voters exactly none. The trap and the veto: Clay and Biddle forcing the recharter in 1832, four years early, so that Jackson must sign and alienate the West or veto and alienate the moneyed East in an election year, and Jackson vetoing, in a message that spoke past Congress to the voters, arguing that the Bank's charter made the rich richer by act of law and that the president owed his own constitutional judgment to the people who had elected him, the veto read here in the bank's audited text. The war after the vote: Jackson reelected on the veto's verdict and reading the election as permission, the deposits removed in 1833 over the refusal and removal of two Treasury secretaries, Taney signing at last, the government's money scattered among the pet banks, Biddle answering by contracting the Bank's credit to prove the country could not do without it, and proving instead that a single private man could squeeze the nation's economy at will, the episode converting many who had defended him. The figure the war made: the King Andrew cartoon of 1833, the president in a king's robes trampling the Constitution and the Bank's charter together, read in this lesson as the opposition's whole argument in one image, and the Whig party coalescing from 1834 out of everyone the argument persuaded. The money let loose and the bill arriving: state banks multiplying, paper flooding, federal land sales paid in notes of banks that might not exist, the Specie Circular of 1836 demanding hard money for public land, British credit tightening as the Bank of England raised its rates, and in May 1837 the banks of New York suspending specie payment, the panic general by the week, cotton and land values collapsing, the depression that followed lasting into the 1840s. The settlement that was not one: Van Buren, Jackson's heir, inheriting the wreck and refusing both a new national bank and relief by the government, the independent treasury of 1840 divorcing the federal money from banks altogether, the Whigs repealing it in 1841 and failing twice on their own bank bills against President Tyler's vetoes, so that the question of who should govern the currency stood exactly unsettled when the lesson closes, with the reading stating the war's honest ledger: a genuinely dangerous concentration of unaccountable power destroyed by methods that enlarged the presidency, and an economic catastrophe its victors and its victims each laid wholly at the other's door.

## Lesson 5: The Market Revolution: Factories, Canals, and Wires

**Lesson question:** What did the factory and the telegraph do to the way Americans lived and worked?

**Reading scope:** The second phase of a transformation the Early Republic unit began: the Erie Canal's lesson generalized, turnpikes and steamboats joined after 1830 by the railroad, some three thousand miles of track in 1840 and nine thousand by 1850, and by the telegraph after 1844, so that the cost of moving a ton of goods fell by as much as ninety five percent on the improved routes and news, which had traveled at the speed of a horse since the world began, traveled instantly, the two facts together reordering where Americans lived, what they made, and what their time was worth. The factory as the transformation's sharpest edge: power looms and the putting out system's death, the Waltham and Lowell mills joining spinning and weaving under one roof on water power, the factory system defined against the artisan's shop and the household's loom, and interchangeable parts, perfected in the federal armories, teaching American mechanics to make every part to a pattern so that repair meant replacement. The labor the system invented: the Lowell system as its owners designed it, young women recruited from New England farms, housed in corporation boardinghouses under rules and curfews, working twelve to fourteen hour days at the looms, paid wages of their own, and expected to leave after a few years, the owners advertising the arrangement as proof that American factories need not breed an English mill town's misery. The women's own record: the Lowell Offering, a magazine written by the operatives themselves, read here in the bank's audited mill girls record, its literary societies and its defenders of the system, and the same women's other record, the turn outs of 1834 and 1836 against wage cuts and raised board, the Female Labor Reform Association of 1844 petitioning Massachusetts for the ten hour day and Sarah Bagley testifying before a legislative committee that the hours broke health, the first time working women addressed a state government as a body. The unions and their ceiling: journeymen organizing trade unions through the 1830s in the cities, the National Trades' Union of 1834, strikes for the ten hour day winning in the building trades, and the whole structure collapsing in the panic of 1837, which taught the era's workers what the market revolution's booms cost the people inside them. The household remade from both ends: farmers producing for markets instead of subsistence and buying cloth, clocks, and stoves they once made, clocks themselves falling from artisan prices to a dollar's reach under the same methods, and the middle class home reimagined around a wage earned outside it and a domesticity kept inside it, the ideology the reform lessons will meet again. The reading closes on the connection the unit keeps making: the same fall in the price of moving things that carried Lowell cloth to western farms carried the South's cotton to Lowell and to Liverpool, and the market revolution bound the sections' economies tighter in the very years their politics were coming apart.

## Lesson 6: Revival and Reform: The Second Great Awakening

**Lesson question:** Why did revival produce reformers, and what did they set out to fix first?

**Reading scope:** The faith that made the reformers: the Second Great Awakening as a generation long campaign of revivals, Methodists and Baptists carrying it west by circuit rider and camp meeting, Charles Grandison Finney professionalizing it in the 1820s and 1830s with new measures, the anxious bench, protracted meetings, and prayer for sinners by name, and Rochester in 1830 and 1831 as the model campaign, a whole city's churches, lawyers, and merchants converted in six months. The theology with consequences: against the older Calvinism of the Colonial unit's Great Awakening, Finney preaching that sin was a voluntary act and conversion a choice the sinner could make, perfectionism following in some of his converts' hands, the claim that a Christian could be freed from sin in this life, and the social form of the same doctrine, that a society, like a soul, could choose to stop sinning, drunkenness, slavery, and cruelty to the helpless standing as sins a converted nation could simply resolve to end. The geography of the fire: the burned over district of western New York, crossed and recrossed by revival until preachers said no fuel was left unburned, the Erie Canal's corridor of new money, new towns, and displaced certainties, producing in one generation abolitionism, women's rights, Mormonism, Millerism, and utopian communities, Oberlin College carrying Finney's theology into a school that admitted women and Black students and trained the movement's organizers. The first object, drink: American consumption of alcohol standing at levels later generations would find incredible, the American Temperance Society of 1826 organizing the pledge, the movement moving from moderation to total abstinence to the demand for law, Neal Dow's Maine Law of 1851 standing just past the unit's close as the movement's first statutory victory, and women carrying temperance as the defense of households whose wages and safety drink consumed. The second object, the school: Horace Mann at the Massachusetts board of education from 1837, his annual reports arguing that common schools, tax supported, professionally taught, open to all, were the republic's balance wheel, the Twelfth Report of 1848 read here in excerpt, and the system's exclusions stated as plainly as its ambitions, schooling that served the North's towns while the South's enslaved children were forbidden letters by law. The third object, the helpless: Dorothea Dix investigating Massachusetts jails and almshouses in the 1840s and finding the insane confined in cages, closets, and cellars, her Memorial of 1843 laying named cases before the legislature in the plain style of an inspector's report, read here in excerpt, and the asylum building her memorials produced across the states, reform by documentation, the era's characteristic method, shared with the school reports and the abolitionist fact book alike. The reading closes on the movement's method and its limit: the reformers believed a fact, plainly printed and widely read, would move a Christian republic to act, and built the voluntary society, the petition, the report, and the convention into the era's instruments of change, the instruments the next two lessons watch turned on the era's largest fact.

## Lesson 7: Abolition Turns Immediate

**Lesson question:** What changed when abolitionists stopped asking gradually, and how did the country answer?

**Reading scope:** The older antislavery and its ceiling: the founding generation's gradual emancipation in the North, the colonization movement's answer of removal to Liberia, and the arithmetic that outran both, cotton's expansion after the gin carrying slavery from some seven hundred thousand souls in 1790 to more than two million by 1830, so that gradualism's timetable stretched past any living person's sight. The Black voice first: David Walker's Appeal of 1829, written by a free Black clothing dealer of Boston, printed in three editions, sewn into the linings of sailors' clothes and carried into southern ports, calling on the enslaved to resist and on white Christians to answer for a republic of liberty holding slaves, the pamphlet read here in the bank's audited text, Georgia answering it with a price on Walker's head and the South's ports with laws against incendiary print. The revolt and the closing South: Nat Turner, an enslaved preacher in Southampton County, Virginia, reading signs in the sky, and in August 1831 leading the deadliest slave rebellion in the nation's history, about sixty white people killed before the militia ended it, more than a hundred Black people killed in the answering terror, Turner's Confessions taken down by Thomas Gray and read here with their mediation stated on the page, Virginia's legislature debating emancipation in the winter after and choosing instead to tighten the codes, literacy banned, assembly banned, preaching licensed, the last open southern debate on ending slavery closing in 1832. The white movement remade: Garrison's Liberator of January 1831 opening with an apology retracted, gradualism renounced, and immediatism declared, his "To the Public" read here in the bank's audited text, the American Anti-Slavery Society of 1833 organizing the doctrine nationally, its agents and its postal campaign of 1835 flooding the South with print until Charleston's post office was broken into and the mail burned, President Jackson asking Congress to censor it and Congress, in time, tabling the petitions instead. The answer in the streets and in the House: mob violence as the era's enforcement, Garrison dragged through Boston with a rope in 1835, Elijah Lovejoy murdered at Alton in 1837 defending his press, Pennsylvania Hall burned in 1838 three days after it opened, and in Congress the gag rule of 1836 tabling every antislavery petition unread, old John Quincy Adams fighting it for eight years as the right of petition's own question, until its repeal in 1844. ## Lesson 8: Women's Rights and Seneca Falls

**Lesson question:** Where did the women of 1848 get their argument, and what did they ask for first?

**Reading scope:** The legal ground the argument had to move: coverture, the common law doctrine under which a wife's legal existence was covered by her husband's, her property his, her wages his, her children his in law's eye, her signature void, the doctrine stated in the language of Blackstone's commentaries as the era's lawyers learned it, and the first breach in it, New York's Married Women's Property Act of April 1848, passed after a decade of petitioning led by Ernestine Rose and Paulina Wright Davis, letting a married woman hold property apart from her husband's debts, three months before Seneca Falls met in the same state. The schooling that made the speakers: the female seminaries of the Early Republic's republican motherhood grown into something their founders had not planned, Troy and Mount Holyoke teaching women the full curriculum and sending them out as teachers, the largest educated female population in the world by the era's own boasts, trained to read, argue, and organize, and confined by law and custom to the schoolroom and the home. The abolitionist apprenticeship: women doing the movement's local work through the 1830s, its petitioning above all, Angelina Grimké presenting antislavery petitions to the Massachusetts legislature in 1838, the first American woman to address a legislative body, the Grimké sisters' lecture tour of 1837 drawing the Congregational clergy's pastoral letter against women speaking to mixed audiences, and Sarah Grimké's Letters on the Equality of the Sexes of 1837 and 1838 answering from scripture and natural rights together, read here in excerpt, the movement's own logic, that a soul's equality before God admitted no intermediary, turned on the movement's own practice. The insult that organized them: London, June 1840, the World's Anti-Slavery Convention refusing to seat the American women delegates, Mott and Stanton meeting in the curtained gallery where the women were made to sit, and resolving there to hold a convention on the rights of women when they returned home, the resolution taking eight years and a move to Seneca Falls to keep. The convention itself: the call placed in the Seneca County Courier on July 14, 1848, five days' notice, some three hundred attending the Wesleyan Chapel on July 19 and 20, James Mott presiding, Stanton reading the declaration she had drafted at the M'Clintock table, and Frederick Douglass in the room, the one public man whose name carried weight with the press, standing to defend the suffrage resolution when it faltered. The document read whole in this lesson from the bank's audited text: the Declaration of Sentiments, the Declaration of Independence's architecture kept beam for beam, all men and women are created equal, its grievances a bill of particulars against coverture, exclusion, and the double standard, signed by one hundred of those present, sixty eight women and thirty two men, and the convention's own report, the bank's second audited record, carrying its resolutions, its debates, and the ridicule the newspapers answered with, ridicule the report prints and the movement learned to use. The closing of the unit: the suffrage resolution itself, the only one not carried unanimously, Mott fearing it would make the movement ridiculous, Stanton and Douglass holding it through, the demand that would take seventy two years to win stated first at the smallest of the era's conventions; and the unit ending where 1848 ends, with reform having widened the meaning of the era's democracy in every direction except the one the Early Republic unit left open, the question of slavery in the republic, which the conventions of 1848 had argued around, under, and through, and which the next unit takes up with the settlements failing one by one.
