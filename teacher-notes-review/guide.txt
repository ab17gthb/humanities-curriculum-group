Post-Civil War

and communication which also became cheaper
      • Lots of investment into the railroads; helped the country develop

• Mass industrialization

     • Long hours
     • Dangerous work conditions
     • Inability to support family with meager wages

Slide 7
Taylorism
Note: Taylorism is a management system that was pioneered by Frederick Taylor; it began his
theory in the 1880s within manufacturing industries

• Scientific management that made production much more efficient
• Tasks subdivided
     • Example: instead of 30 mechanics making 30 products, 30 laborers do 30 distinct
         tasks to create a product
• Resulted in mass production

Note: Many Taylorism ideas are still in use today in industrial engineering and management; for
example: division of tasks, analysis, synthesis, logic, work ethic, elimination of waste,
standardization of best practices, transformation of specialist production into mass production,
transfer of knowledge between workers, ...

Industrialization and Mass Production
• The use of interchangeable parts progressed during the Civil War
      • Evidence of the use of interchangeable parts goes back centuries in China and Europe
      • Eli Whitney progressed the use of interchangeable parts in the early 1800s
      • Civil War progressed the use even more and would influence the Gilded Age
• Many popular products were able to be produced very efficiently with advanced production
  management systems (e.g., Taylorism) and the use of interchange parts
      • McCormick Grain Reaper
            • For decades, hired many different specialists to make one (e.g., blacksmiths,
                wood workers, machinists, etc.)
                  • 1880 hired production who manufactured Colt Firearms and had
                      experience with interchangeable parts
                         • 1880 – 20,000 reapers produced
                         • 1885 – 40,000 reapers produced
                         • 1889 – 100,000 reapers produced
      • Singer Sewing Machines
      • Duke cigarette roller
      • Chicago Packers’ “disassembly” lines (for slaughtering and preparing meat)
• In the early 1900s, Henry Ford intruded the assembly line
      • Ford was able to produce automobiles quicker for a cheaper price

• 1860s – U.S. behind Britain, Germany, France
• 1900 – U.S. was the leading manufacturing nation
• 1913 – U.S. produced 1/3 of industrial output (more than Britain, Germany, and France
  combined)

Corporations (Post Civil War)
      • A corporation is a company that acts like a single entity that is given certain legal
        protections
      • Some forms of corporations had been around since Ancient Rome and the Maurya

      • Some of the early English charters that came to the New World were similar to

     • Corporations able to obtain mass capital with limited liabilities for shareholders

Slide 12
Cartoon Analysis
Instructions: Students work in pairs and answer the questions below. Then prepare to have
a class discussion. (If students want to view the image online so they can zoom in, share this
link: The Tournament of Today)
Questions:
1. What does this cartoon tell you about the Gilded Age?
2. What symbols can you find in this cartoon? What do they symbolize?
3. Find some similarities and differences between the Gilded Age and today.

Possible Answers (answers will vary; encourage historical thinking skills):
1. It shows how a small percentage of society controlled most of the wealth. The rich were
   very rich while the poor were very poor. These circumstances would likely result in some
   sort of conflict and reform.
2. Gold horse represents wealth a power through monopolies, while the emaciated donkey
   represents poverty and hopelessness for the laborers. The train represents industry and
   the wealth it brought to the elite class, while the laborers have simple tools (e.g., hammer).
   The long plume on the knight’s helmet represents “arrogance,” the shield represents
   “corruption and legislature,” and the lance represents “subsidized press,” all these are
   literally written on the items. It suggests the wealthy class are arrogant and have control
   over the legislative branch of the government and the media. The hammer represents the
   tools of the labor class: striking and unionizing.
3. Income inequality has increased over the last 50 years in the United States. Like in the
   Gilded Age, the rich of today are incredibly rich while many live below the poverty line (34
   million according to the 2019 census). Titans of industry like Andrew Carnegie, J.D.
   Rockefeller, J.P. Morgan can be compared to people like Jeff Bezos, Mark Zuckerberg, and
   Elon Musk. The poverty in the Gilded Age was much worse though. And today there are
   more regulations to protect and help those in need (e.g., food stamps, minimum wage,
   eight-hour workdays, laws against child labor, etc.).

Possible Class Discussion Questions:
1. Jeff Bezos has created a products and services that have helped society. So, it doesn’t
   matter that he has billions of dollars. He makes everybody’s life better and deserves the
   money. Him being rich doesn’t make my life worse, it makes it better. Agree or disagree?
2. Jeff Bezos is worth almost $200 billion dollars while 34 million Americans live beneath the
   poverty line. Many children go to bed hungry every night. There should be regulations to
   decrease inequality in the United States. Agree or disagree?

Slide 15
Primary Source Activity
Teacher Instructions: Print the primary source titled “Gospel of Wealth” by Andrew
Carnegie. If you don’t want to print the primary source, you can project the primary source
on your classroom screen and read it together as a class or upload it to your Learning
Management System (LMS) and students can use their devices. After reading the primary
source, students answer the questions below. The questions below are also found in the
Student Study Guide Notes.

If you want the Google Drive version of this primary source, copy click here: “Gospel of
Wealth”

Questions:
1. According to Andrew Carnegie, how had life revolutionized over the prior two hundred
   years from when he wrote this article?
2. Carnegie says the change from the past (i.e., increase in wealth inequality) is “not to be
   deplored.” Explain his reasoning using a quote from the text.
3. According to Carnegie, why is the “law of competition” important?
4. According to Carnegie, what responsibility do the wealthiest in society bear?

Possible Answers (answers will vary; encourage historical thinking skills):
1. There was little difference between the richest and the poorest two hundred years prior;
   Carnegie wrote: “In former days there was little difference between the dwelling, dress,
   food, and environment of the chief and those of his retainers. . . . The contrast between the
   palace of the millionaire and the cottage of the laborer with us today measures the change
   which has come with civilization”
2. Carnegie argues that is better for society that some people are rich, than nobody. This is
   exemplified in the following quote: “[it is] essential for the progress of the race, that the
   houses of some should be homes for all that is highest and best in literature and the arts,
   and for all the refinements of civilization, rather than that none should be so.”
3. The “law of competition” brings “wonderful material development” and “improved [living]
   conditions” for society
4. Carnegie expresses that the wealthiest citizens in society bear the responsibility of using
   their surplus capital properly as to help poorer individuals and families. Carnegie writes,
   “the millionaire will be but a trustee for the poor; entrusted for a season with a part of the
   increased wealth of the community, but administering it for the community far better than
   it did, or would have done, of itself.”
Slide 16
Darwinism
• 1859 – British naturalist Charles Darwin wrote On the Origin of Species
• 1870s – Darwin’s theories gained traction
     • Biologists, naturalists, and scientists started to accept Darwin’s theories
• Survival of the fittest
     • Phrase used by Darwin describing natural selection
     • Idea that organisms that could adjust to their environment are fit for survival and
        reproduction

Slide 17
Social Darwinism
• British sociologist Herbert Spencer applied Darwin’s theory of survival of the fittest to society
• Idea where the fittest have the most economic success
• State welfare and charity leads to social degeneration (“survival of the weakest”)
• Social Darwinism spread among the rich; some felt inequality of outcome should be celebrated
     • J.D. Rockefeller and Thomas Edison were among those who followed Spencer
• Many people (e.g., laborers) were strongly against the idea of Social Darwinism

Slide 18
Republican Domination - Gilded Age
• Came to power as anti-slavery party but was also supporters of American business
     • Abraham Lincoln was a lawyer that defended railroad businesses
           • During war (when he didn’t have opposition from Democrats), Lincoln pushed
               through pro-business agenda
• Pushed for high protective tariffs
     • To protect American business
     • Before the war, Democrats opposed the high tariffs; after the war, they didn’t have to
        power to oppose
• Republican Party dominated the Gilded Age and the first couple of decades of the 20th Century
     • Between the Civil War and the Great Depression, only four Democrats would become
        president

* With the Republican in charge, a new industrial order emerged with the justification that hard
work and talent could bring riches; minimalized government interference
Slide 19
Labor Movement
• The majority of industrial laborers didn’t support Social Darwinism
• Working conditions were difficult
      • 60 plus hour weeks
      • Below poverty line
      • Dangerous working conditions
      • Periods of unemployment
      • Wives and children forced to work
      • Slums and Cities overcrowded
• Strikes started erupting before the Civil War; during the Civil War and Reconstruction the
  labor movement was distracted
      • With the rise of big business, the Labor Movement gained momentum
      • Strikes would occur around the country
      • Unions would form

Slide 20
Great Railroad Strike of 1877
•   Started the era of labor conflict
•   Panic of 1873 would hurt workers for years to come
        • Railroad bubble busted
        • The effects would be felt for years
•   1877 – Baltimore and Ohio Railroad cut wages
        • This was the third time in a year
•   Strikers shutdown railroad traffic
        • The Baltimore to St. Louis line was completely shut down
        • Strikes popped up all across Pennsylvania
               • Railroads being shut down hurt the economy
•   State militias and federal troops called in to stop the strikes
•   100 people would die; $40 million in damages
•   Convinced workers the need to form unions
        • The country was galvanized
        • 50 years of labor conflicts would ensue

Slide 21
Knights of Labor
•   1880s – Union that united skilled and unskilled labor started
       • Welcomed all laborers (including women); did not include lawyers, bankers, or liquor
            dealers
•   By 1886, 700,000 members
•   Originally wanted to create a cooperative producer-centered society, but many felt this was too
    radical
•   Changed demands to be more practical (e.g., eight-hour workday)
Slide 22
Knights of Labor Strike
• 1886 – Jay Gould’s rail companies fired a Knights of Labor member for attending a union
  meeting
• Workers from all over the country went on strike
• Gould called in the Pinkerton Detective Agency to put down the strike
• After lots of property damage, the strike was put down, but strikers changed their
  demands to an eight-hour day
• May 1, 1886, around 400,000 strikers around the country
• Police killed one striker outside the McCormick Reaper Works
• Strikers called for a protest the next day at the Haymarket Square

Slide 23
Haymarket Riot
• Peaceful protest called for the day at Haymarket Square in Chicago the day after a worker
  was killed by police
• Somebody threw a bomb killing seven protestors and four police
• Sensational news made Americans view unionists as radicals
     • Eight anarchists were arrested; four hung; one committed suicide
• Membership in the Knight of Labor dramatically dropped
• The movement for an eight-hour day halted; it would have to wait for the future

Slide 24
American Federation of Labor (AFL)
• Conservative alternative to the Knights of Labor
     • Founded in 1886, the same year as the Haymarket Riot
     • Members were able to distance themselves from the Knights of Labor
• Union of skilled workers
• Advocated for higher wages, less working hours, and more safety in the workplace
• Samuel Gompers was elected as its president
• AFL would go on to be the largest union in the United States at the beginning of the 20th
  century

Slide 25
Pullman Strike
• A strike after workers at Pullman car factories had their wages cut
     • The workers lived in a “company town” near the factory; though wages were cut,
         rents were not cut
• American Railway Union (ARU) led by Eugene Debs refused to accept any Pullman cars on
  the rail lines
• National rail traffic stopped
• President Cleveland sent thousands of American soldiers to stop the strike
Slide 26
Homestead Strike
• 1892, Homestead, Pennsylvania – strike between the Amalgamated Association of Iron and
  Steel Workers and Carnegie Steel Company
• Strike turned into a bloody battle between union strikers and private security agents
• Major defeat for labor movement

Slide 28
The Populist Movement
• A coalition of agrarian reformers who were politically inclined; advocated for a wide range of
  economic and political legislation in the late 1800s
• Against Wall Street
• Farmers at the turn of the century were a majority of the population
• Farmers went into debt while bankers, railroad tycoons, and other businessmen got rich
• Industry changed agrarian society; farmers were once only dependent on the local markets,
  but in the late 1800s, they were connected to towns, cities, NY, Chicago, then London, then
  the rest of the world
• Hence, farmers at the turn of the century were subject to the national economy which did not
  have many regulations
• They wanted to change the system

Slide 29
Farmers’ Alliance
• Farmers didn’t want monopolies to get rich while the farmers fed the country
     • Giant corporations monopolized every sector; farmers power had decreased
     • The price of commodities decreased while farmers’ debts increased
• Organized the Farmers’ Alliance
     • Farmers could share machines, bargains from wholesalers, negotiate lower prices,
       and more
     • Farmers’ Alliance would later be called the People’s Party (or the Populist Party)

Slide 30
People’s (Populist) Party
•   The Farmers’ Alliance would later become the People’s Party
•   Ideas spread throughout the South, Midwest, and the Plains
•   “Cooperative commonwealth”
•   1.5 million members
•   Won 21 electoral votes in the Election of 1892
•   Earned more credit after the Panic of 1893 when the economy was destroyed
Slide 31
The “Omaha Platform” of the People’s Party
• Since the Democrats and Republicans did not meet the needs of the farmers, they started the
  People’s Party by created the “Omaha Platform”
• Their platform included a coherent political vision (that some felt was too extreme):
     • Counter power of monopolistic capitalism
     • Expansion of federal power
     • Nationalize railroad and telegraph
     • Subtreasuries that would extend loans to farmers
     • Monetize silver
            • By monetizing silver, they would be creating an inflationary monetary policy
     • Shift economy toward producing class

Slide 32
Colored Farmers’ Alliance
• The Farmers’ Alliance in the South would not allow Black farmers to join
• Black farmers in the South started the Colored Farmers’ Alliance
• Inspired by Booker T. Washington’s call for economic self-sufficiency
     • Washington was an author, educator, and orator; he was a leader of the African
         American economy

Slide 33
William Jennings Bryan & Politics of Gold
• Debates over monetary policy in the United States have been ongoing from the days of
  Alexander Hamilton to today
• From the days of George Washington to the Civil War, the U.S. was on a gold and silver
  standard
     • In other words, paper money was backed by a certain ratio of gold and silver
• During the Civil War, greenbacks were issued; greenbacks were fiat money (i.e., they were not
  backed by gold or silver; just confidence in the government)
• With the Coinage Act of 1873, the U.S. was back on the gold standard
• Many people argued that the United States should go back to bimetallism
     • William Jennings Bryan was one of the most famous advocates for going back on the
        silver standard (also known as “free silver”)

Slide 34
William Jennings Bryan
• One of the greatest speakers in American history
• Nebraska congressman
     • Democrat
• Three-time presidential candidate
• Secretary of State under President Woodrow Wilson
• Lawyer during Scopes Monkey Trial (1925)
Slide 35
Gold Standard
• Gold standard was attacked
• Promoted “free silver” saying it would benefit average citizens and make the United States
  prosperous
• 1896 – William Jennings Bryan gave his “Cross of Gold” speech at the Democratic National
  convention
     • Considered one of the greatest political speeches in American history
• William McKinley would defeat William Jennings Bryan in the Election of 1896
     • U.S. would remain on the Gold Standard

Slide 36
The Socialists
• Open to all races, genders, social classes, and religions
• Notable figures like Helen Keller, Upton Sinclair, Jack London and more were socialists
• Factory workers, miners, railroad builders, tenant farmers, small farmers were attracted to
  the Socialist movement

Slide 37
Socialist Party of America (SPA)
• Founded in 1901
• Some socialist congressmen and mayors
• 1912 – Eugene Debs was the SPA candidate
      • Received one million votes
• Unable to persuade Americans that socialism was compatible with American values
• Government censorship of Socialists during World War I would diminish the SPA
      • SPA was anti-war
• The Populists and Socialists had a great influence on American politics and culture whose
  effects can be felt to the present

Slide 38
Final Thoughts
The years after the American Civil War would see great changes throughout the country.
Before the Reconstruction Era ended, the U.S. would industrialize and see great developments.
With such great progress also came inequality and movements to counter the effects.
