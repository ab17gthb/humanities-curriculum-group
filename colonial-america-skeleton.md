## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- How did the meeting of Europe, Africa, and the Americas remake all three worlds, and what kind of society grew up in England's colonies?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's core plan is 18 instructional days. The unit opens before colonization, with the Americas and Europe on the eve of contact, and runs through English settlement, the regional colonial societies, the Atlantic system and enslavement, colonial government and religion, and the imperial wars, closing with the French and Indian War and its costs. The Founding unit, next in the course, owns the imperial crisis from 1763, so this unit does not open the Stamp Act crisis; it ends at the hinge where Britain's victory in 1763 changed the terms of empire. Indigenous peoples appear throughout as historical actors with their own politics, diplomacy, and strategies, not as backdrop: the Haudenosaunee league, Powhatan's paramount chiefdom, the Pequot and Wampanoag decisions of the 1630s and 1670s, the Ohio Valley nations who played France against Britain, and Pontiac's coalition all act on the record for reasons of their own. Enslavement is handled with the documentary sobriety the later units use for hard subjects: verified sources, exact captions, no sensationalism, and the Middle Passage taught from Equiano's own account as the bank carries it. Period texts are public domain, but the edition still matters: every quotation names its transcription or facsimile edition, and modernized texts are used only where the bank's audited record carries them, with the modernization the bank record itself discloses.

## Lesson 1: Two Worlds Before Contact

**Lesson question:** What did the Americas and Europe each look like in 1491, and what changed when they met?

**Reading scope:** The Americas first, on their own terms: a hemisphere of perhaps fifty million people or more (contemporary scholarly estimates, labeled as estimates), farming peoples and hunting peoples, cities and confederacies. Tenochtitlan on its lake, larger than London, with its chinampas, markets, and causeways; Cahokia on the Mississippi, its mounds and plazas raised centuries before Columbus; the Haudenosaunee league in the northeast woodlands, five nations joined under the Great Law to keep peace among themselves and deal jointly with outsiders, and the farming villages, towns, and trade routes that covered the Eastern Woodlands. Then Europe on the eve: a continent of rival kingdoms, a Spain newly unified and fresh from completing the Reconquista in January 1492, Portugal already pushing down Africa's coast in caravels, and the equipment of expansion, the compass, the lateen sail, gunpowder weapons, and printing to spread the news. The meeting itself: Columbus's four voyages for Spain, claiming Caribbean islands for Ferdinand and Isabella while believing he had reached Asia's edge, his 1493 letter printed across Europe within months. The exchange that followed, stated as the unit's master fact: crops moving both ways, maize, potatoes, and tomatoes east, wheat, horses, and cattle west; European diseases moving west into populations with no resistance, smallpox first among them, killing on a scale no army could match, in many regions nine people in ten over the following century, estimates labeled as estimates. The reading closes with the demographic catastrophe stated as the ground every later lesson stands on: empires and colonies were built in a hemisphere whose peoples had already been shattered by disease, and Indigenous nations rebuilt, adapted, and resisted from inside that loss.

**Activity concept:** Two worlds in 1491. Students work five records, a passage from Columbus's 1493 letter, a plan or view of Tenochtitlan as the period and the record carry it, a record of Cahokia and the Mississippian world, a record of the Haudenosaunee league, and a Columbian Exchange record sorting what moved in each direction, and for each one state what it shows about the world it came from, then judge which single change after 1492 did the most to remake the Americas, and defend the judgment from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** Columbian Exchange, Tenochtitlan, Cahokia, Iroquois Confederacy, caravel, Reconquista, epidemic

## Lesson 2: The Spanish and French Americas

**Lesson question:** How did Spain build an empire on conquest, and how did France build one on trade?

**Reading scope:** Spain first: the conquistadores, Cortés taking Tenochtitlan in 1521 with Indigenous allies who had their own quarrels with the Aztec empire and smallpox doing work no sword could, Pizarro in Peru, and an empire of mines, missions, and great estates running from Mexico to Potosí's silver mountain. The labor system at its base: the encomienda, granting a colonist the tribute and labor of Indigenous people in return for supposed protection and Christian teaching, defended at court and attacked by Bartolomé de las Casas, whose Brief Account of the Destruction of the Indies, published in 1552, put the dying on the record in numbers his readers could not forget, and whose advocacy helped push the Crown toward the New Laws and toward African enslavement as the labor answer, a turn Las Casas himself came to regret. The society the empire made: a ranked order of peninsulares, creoles, mestizos, Indigenous peoples, and enslaved Africans, Catholic missions remaking the borderlands, and silver flowing to Spain and onward to China. Then the northern empires: France on the St. Lawrence after 1608, a thin line of settlers living by the fur trade, dependent on Indigenous hunters, guides, and allies, binding the trade with diplomacy, intermarriage, and missions, and fighting the Haudenosaunee for two generations over the beaver country; the Dutch on the Hudson in the same trade; and France's other America in Louisiana and the sugar islands, where the Code Noir of 1685 wrote slavery into French law and Saint-Domingue's plantations ran on enslaved labor as brutally as any in the hemisphere. The reading closes with the comparison the unit keeps: Spain ruled millions and extracted; France traded and allied with peoples it could not rule; both empires rested in the end on land taken and labor coerced, and both had to reckon with Indigenous nations strong enough to set terms.

**Activity concept:** Two empires, one hemisphere. Students work five records, a Las Casas passage from the Brief Account, a Bernal Díaz passage on the entry into Tenochtitlan, a record of the encomienda and the New Laws, a fur trade record from the French and Indigenous exchange, and the Code Noir's opening articles, and for each one name the system it belongs to and whose labor or land it organized, then judge which empire's way of holding America proved stronger by 1700, and why.

**Vocabulary:** conquistador, encomienda, mestizo, New Spain, mission, fur trade, hacienda

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Las Casas Brief Account (colonial era file), Díaz entry into Tenochtitlan and Code Noir (early modern empire era file), audited records. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 3: Jamestown and the Chesapeake

**Lesson question:** How did a starving outpost become a tobacco colony, and what did tobacco demand?

**Reading scope:** The venture's form first: the Virginia Company of London, a joint-stock company chartered in 1606, investors risking money for profit where Spain's kings had spent their own. Jamestown planted in 1607 on a swampy James River peninsula chosen for defense against Spanish ships and bad for almost everything else; a gentleman-heavy company of settlers, disease, brackish water, and a supply system that failed, then the starving time of 1609 and 1610, when the colony of some five hundred ate horses, dogs, and in documented cases the dead, and fell to about sixty survivors. The Powhatan paramount chiefdom around it, some thirty peoples under Wahunsenacawh, trading corn that kept the English alive and weighing how to use or remove them. John Smith's discipline and his own published accounts, read as sources with an author selling himself; John Rolfe's tobacco, a milder West Indian strain planted from about 1612, which found England's market and remade the colony's economy and its hunger for land. The year 1619 stated as the hinge it was: the House of Burgesses, the first elected assembly in English America, meeting at Jamestown, and weeks later a Dutch ship selling some twenty Africans to the colony, the beginning of African slavery in England's mainland colonies, its first legal status there still argued by historians and stated here as such. The labor tobacco demanded: headright grants of fifty acres for every passage paid, indentured servants coming by the thousands on four to seven year terms, working a crop that wore out soil and men, dying in seasoning fevers at rates the records keep. The turn at the end of the century: Bacon's Rebellion of 1676, frontier settlers and former servants under Nathaniel Bacon attacking Indigenous peoples, burning Jamestown, and dying with their leader, after which Virginia's great planters shifted decisively toward enslaved African labor, harder to free, easier to mark, and bound for life.

**Activity concept:** The tobacco ledger. Students work five records, a John Smith passage from the General History, a record of the starving time from the colony's own accounts, the Virginia Company's headright and Burgesses records, a tobacco price and labor record, and Bacon's Declaration of 1676, and for each one state what problem it was answering and what it cost, then judge which single change between 1607 and 1680 mattered most to what Virginia became.

**Vocabulary:** joint-stock company, Jamestown, cash crop, headright system, indentured servant, House of Burgesses, Bacon's Rebellion

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Smith True Relation and General History, Smith's letter to Queen Anne of 1616, and Bacon's Declaration of 1676, audited records in the colonial era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 4: New England: Covenant and Community

**Lesson question:** What kind of community did New England's settlers mean to build, and who could belong to it?

**Reading scope:** Two foundings, kept distinct: Plymouth in 1620, Separatist Pilgrims crossing on the Mayflower, signing a compact aboard ship to govern themselves by consent because they had landed outside their patent, losing half their number the first winter, and surviving with the help of Tisquantum and the Wampanoag under Massasoit, whose own people the epidemic of 1616 to 1619 had already shattered; and Massachusetts Bay from 1630, Puritans who stayed inside the Church of England in name while building a Bible commonwealth in fact, John Winthrop's lay sermon A Modell of Christian Charitie, with its city upon a hill, preached as the fleet crossed. The community they built: tight farming towns laid out around a meetinghouse, congregational churches whose members examined one another, town meetings, a broad distribution of land to families, families larger and longer-lived than the Chesapeake's, schools ordered by law so children could read Scripture. The discipline inside it: a covenant people watched by their neighbors, ministers supported by taxes everyone paid, and dissent treated as danger. The dissenters on the record: Roger Williams, banished in 1636 for teaching that the king could not grant Indigenous land he did not own and that the state could not compel worship, founding Rhode Island on soul liberty; Anne Hutchinson, tried in 1637 for holding that grace, not works or ministers, stood between a believer and God, her trial examination surviving as the colony's own record of its fear, banished to Rhode Island and killed in New Netherland in 1643. The expansion and its violence: Connecticut's Fundamental Orders of 1639, often called the first written constitution in English America, the Pequot War of 1637 and the Mystic massacre stated plainly, and, a generation on, King Philip's War of 1675 and 1676, Metacom's coalition striking back at fifty years of land loss, the deadliest war per capita in the colonies' history. The reading closes at Salem in 1692, where a community built on watching for the devil found him in its own daughters' accusations, nineteen hanged and one man pressed to death, and the colony's leaders, Increase Mather among them, called the trials home.

**Activity concept:** The covenant file. Students work five records, the Mayflower Compact, a passage from Winthrop's Modell of Christian Charitie, Anne Hutchinson's trial examination, the Fundamental Orders of Connecticut, and the trial record of Sarah Good at Salem, and for each one state the rule of belonging it sets and who stands inside or outside it, then judge whether New England's discipline was the price of its survival or a separate choice, and answer the strongest counterclaim from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** Mayflower Compact, Puritans, covenant, Anne Hutchinson, Roger Williams, Fundamental Orders of Connecticut, Salem witch trials

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Mayflower Compact, Modell of Christian Charitie, Hutchinson trial examination, Fundamental Orders, Winthrop's 1634 Boston letter, and Sarah Good trial records, audited records in the colonial era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**.

## Lesson 5: The Middle Colonies and the Atlantic Economy

**Lesson question:** How did the middle colonies become the colonies of many peoples, and what held the Atlantic economy together?

**Reading scope:** The middle ground of the seaboard first: New Netherland planted by the Dutch West India Company for the fur trade, New Amsterdam a trading town of many languages, taken by an English fleet in 1664 and renamed New York, its Dutch settlers, patroons, and enslaved Africans staying on under new rulers; New Jersey split off beside it; Pennsylvania chartered to William Penn in 1681 as a debt paid to his father, a Quaker proprietor advertising his holy experiment across Europe in German and Dutch, drawing Quakers, Germans, Scots-Irish, and Welsh to rich wheat land, governing with an elected assembly, buying land from the Lenape under treaties Penn honored in his lifetime, and making Philadelphia by 1750 the largest city in British America. Delaware below it, Maryland beside it, where the Calverts' Catholic refuge had written the Toleration Act of 1649 to protect Christian worship and punish its disturbance in the same clauses. The Flushing Remonstrance of 1657, English Quaker sympathizers in a Dutch town petitioning Stuyvesant for the liberty he had denied, as the record of what conscience claimed against every proprietor. Then the frame that held all of it: mercantilism, the doctrine that colonies existed to enrich the mother country, exporting raw materials, importing finished goods, and piling up bullion; the Navigation Acts from 1651 onward, routing enumerated goods, tobacco and sugar first among them, through England in English or colonial ships; the customs houses, the smuggling the acts invited, and the colonial carrying trade that grew up inside the rules anyway, New England ships and middle colony grain feeding the sugar islands. The Atlantic drawn as one economy: grain, fish, and timber south and east; sugar and molasses north; manufactured goods from Britain; enslaved Africans from the Guinea coast, the whole pattern students will take apart in the next lesson. The reading closes with the colonies' growth stated in numbers: from some 250,000 people in 1700 to about 2.5 million by 1775, estimates labeled, a population doubling roughly every twenty five years by birth more than by immigration.

**Activity concept:** The Atlantic ledger. Students work five records, the Flushing Remonstrance, the Maryland Toleration Act, a Navigation Act passage, a record of Penn's invitation and Pennsylvania's settlement, and a trade record sorting the Atlantic's cargoes by direction, and for each one state whose interest it served and whose it bound, then judge whether mercantilism in practice helped the colonies grow or mainly held them in place, and defend the judgment from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** middle colonies, Quakers, William Penn, proprietary colony, mercantilism, Navigation Acts, triangular trade

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Flushing Remonstrance and Maryland Toleration Act, audited records in the colonial era file; the Navigation Acts are public statutes, built for this unit with the edition named. **LINK** / **BUILD**.

## Lesson 6: Slavery and the Slave Trade

**Lesson question:** How was the Atlantic slave trade organized, and what did enslaved people endure, build, and resist?

**Reading scope:** The trade's machinery first, stated plainly: European ships carrying guns, cloth, and metal to Africa's Guinea coast, African rulers and merchants selling war captives and the kidnapped to the traders, a fact the reading states without letting it soften what the buyers built; some 12.5 million Africans forced aboard between the 1500s and the 1800s, of whom about 10.7 million survived the crossing, figures from the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, labeled as its estimates. The Middle Passage from its best witness: Olaudah Equiano, kidnapped in Igboland at about eleven, carried to Barbados and Virginia, whose Interesting Narrative of 1789 describes the hold's heat, filth, and terror in the first person, quoted as the bank carries it. The law that made it permanent in England's colonies: Virginia's statutes of the 1660s making a child's status follow the mother's and declaring that baptism did not free, slavery rebuilt as hereditary chattel property, and the slave codes that followed in colony after colony, defining enslaved people as property, denying them courts, assembly, and arms, and punishing resistance with death. The regional worlds slavery made: the Chesapeake's tobacco quarters, where enslaved families put down roots and the enslaved population grew by birth from early in the 1700s; the South Carolina and Georgia lowcountry, where enslaved Africans' rice knowledge built the plantations' wealth, the task system ordered the day, a Black majority lived in something close to an African country, and the Gullah language and culture took form; the North's smaller but real enslavement, in farms, ports, and households, stated so no region stands outside the system. Resistance and survival together: day to day defiance, work slowed and tools broken, flight to swamps, Spanish Florida, and the woods, families held together against sale, African faiths and music carried into new forms. The Stono Rebellion of September 1739, enslaved men seizing guns near Charleston and marching for Florida's promised freedom under banners, put down in a day, answered by South Carolina's Negro Act of 1740 forbidding assembly, movement, and teaching the enslaved to write. The reading closes with Phillis Wheatley, kidnapped from West Africa as a child, enslaved in Boston, published in London in 1773 as the first African American poet in print, her poem On Being Brought from Africa to America holding gratitude and accusation in the same eight lines, assigned to students to read whole.

**Activity concept:** The system in five records. Students work five records, Equiano's Middle Passage account, a Virginia statute passage making slavery hereditary, a slave code passage, a record of the Stono Rebellion and the Negro Act, and Wheatley's poem read entire, and for each one state the part of the system it shows, capture, law, labor, resistance, or voice, then judge which record a reader must not be allowed to skip, and why the others cannot replace it.

**Vocabulary:** Middle Passage, Atlantic slave trade, chattel slavery, slave codes, task system, Stono Rebellion, Olaudah Equiano

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Equiano Middle Passage excerpt and Wheatley's On Being Brought from Africa to America, audited records in the colonial era file; Equiano's full narrative is also in the early modern empire file; the Virginia statutes and the Negro Act are public records built for this unit with editions named. **LINK** / **BUILD**. ## Lesson 7: Colonial Society: Religion, Family, and the Great Awakening

**Lesson question:** How did colonists govern themselves, raise their families, and argue about God?

**Reading scope:** Self-government first: by the early 1700s every colony had an elected assembly voting taxes and laws, the governor appointed from London or by a proprietor, the council and assembly colonial, and the assemblies spending the decades quietly taking powers, above all the power of the purse, while Britain, busy in Europe, let them: salutary neglect, Edmund Burke's later phrase for the policy nobody announced. The rights colonists claimed as Englishmen, stated through their test case: John Peter Zenger, a New York printer jailed in 1734 for his newspaper's attacks on the royal governor, tried in 1735, his lawyer Andrew Hamilton arguing that true statements could not be libel, the jury acquitting against the judge's instruction, the verdict standing as the colonies' landmark for a free press, qualified honestly as a jury's act, not a change in the law. The family order beneath the politics: households as the unit of work and discipline, fathers holding legal authority over wives, children, servants, and the enslaved, women's work running farms and shops while coverture erased them in law, children put to work young, apprenticeship and indenture moving them between households; regional contrasts, New England's long lived families and town schools against the Chesapeake's early deaths and scattered settlement, stated from the record. Then the earthquake inside the churches: the Great Awakening of the 1730s and 1740s, Jonathan Edwards at Northampton preaching Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God at Enfield in 1741, George Whitefield touring from Georgia to New England in 1739 and 1740, drawing crowds in the open air larger than any town's population, itinerants challenging settled ministers, congregations splitting into Old Lights and New Lights, Baptists and Methodists multiplying in the South. What the Awakening taught beyond religion: that ordinary people could judge their betters' messages for themselves, that print and preaching could make one celebrity of thirteen colonies, and that a shared experience could run the length of the seaboard at once. Beside it, the Enlightenment arriving in the same decades through books, colleges, and men like Benjamin Franklin, prizing reason and observation, the two currents stated as rivals and partners rather than a single tide. The reading closes with colonial society on the eve of the imperial wars: richer, healthier, more literate, and more self-governing in practice than Britain understood.

**Activity concept:** The conscience file. Students work five records, Andrew Hamilton's summation from the Zenger trial narrative, a passage from Edwards's Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God, a Whitefield record from his journals and the press he mastered, a record of colonial assemblies and the power of the purse, and a family and household record from a colonial will or inventory as the public record carries it, and for each one state the authority it claims and the authority it questions, then judge which force, self-government, print, or revival, did most to make colonists ready to judge authority for themselves.

**Vocabulary:** salutary neglect, Zenger trial, Great Awakening, Jonathan Edwards, George Whitefield, Enlightenment, revival

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Zenger trial narrative and Edwards Sinners sermon, audited records in the colonial era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**. ## Lesson 8: Empire and War: The French and Indian War

**Lesson question:** How did Britain win North America, and what did the victory cost everyone who fought it?

**Reading scope:** The stakes first: by 1750 the Ohio Valley was the ground three peoples claimed at once, Virginia speculators and settlers moving west, France binding its Canada to Louisiana with a chain of forts, and the Ohio nations, Delaware, Shawnee, and Mingo among them, playing the empires against each other to keep their country, the balance their diplomacy had maintained for two generations breaking as both empires crowded in. The war's opening: a twenty two year old Virginia colonel, George Washington, sent to order the French out in 1753, ambushed a French party in May 1754, lost his hastily built Fort Necessity in July, and signed a capitulation written in French he could not read; the Albany Congress that summer, where Benjamin Franklin's Plan of Union proposed one colonial government for defense and Indian affairs and every assembly rejected it, Franklin's Join, or Die snake running in his Gazette that May as the colonies' first famous cartoon. The disasters: Braddock's army destroyed on the Monongahela in 1755, Britain's frontier raided for two years, thousands of settlers killed or captured, the numbers stated as the estimates they are. The turn: William Pitt taking the war's direction in 1757, paying for it with borrowed millions, promoting officers by merit, and sending Britain's regulars and navy in force; Louisbourg and Quebec taken in 1758 and 1759, Wolfe and Montcalm dying on the Plains of Abraham, Montreal surrendering in 1760. The allies' divergent fates inside the victory: Indigenous nations who had backed France losing their counterweight, colonial soldiers who had served beside regulars learning both British competence and British contempt, colonial assemblies that had voted their own men and money for the cause. The settlement: the Treaty of Paris of February 1763, France off the mainland, Canada and the lands east of the Mississippi to Britain, Louisiana to Spain. The war's bills, presented to everyone at once: Britain's national debt nearly doubled and its ministers resolved that the colonies should pay for their own defense; the Ohio nations, shut out of the treaty's making, rising under Pontiac in the spring of 1763 and taking fort after fort; London answering with the Proclamation of October 1763, drawing a line along the Appalachians and forbidding colonial settlement west of it, a line settlers were already crossing. The reading closes the unit at the hinge: in 1763 Britain and its colonies had never been stronger together, and every term of their partnership, money, land, and authority, was about to be renegotiated. The next unit takes up the renegotiation.

**Activity concept:** The war in five records. Students work five records, Franklin's Join, or Die cartoon as the bank carries it, a passage from Washington's account of the war's opening or his 1754 capitulation record, a record of the Albany Plan and its rejection, a record of Pontiac's war and the nations' reasons, and the Proclamation of 1763 with a period map of the new North America, and for each one state what its maker wanted and what it cost, then judge whether 1763 was a triumph, a warning, or both, and defend the judgment from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** French and Indian War, Seven Years' War, Albany Plan of Union, William Pitt, Treaty of Paris (1763), Pontiac's Rebellion, Proclamation of 1763

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task. **BUILD**. TPSP Join, or Die cartoon record, audited, in the colonial era file. **LINK** / **HAVE**. Washington's journals, the Albany Plan, the Treaty of Paris, and the Proclamation of 1763 are public documents, built for this unit with editions named.

## Changes from the working map

- The unit opens in 1491 rather than 1492, with a full lesson on the Americas and Europe before contact, because the course's first judgment is that the hemisphere Columbus reached was already full of politics, farming, and cities, and the demographic catastrophe after contact cannot be measured without knowing what stood there before it.
- Slavery gets a lesson of its own (Lesson 6) between the regional surveys and colonial society, rather than living inside the Chesapeake and middle colony lessons as labor background, because the Atlantic system was the colonies' economic foundation and enslaved people were actors in its history, not a feature of other people's. The Chesapeake's turn to slavery is still taught in Lesson 3 where it happened; Lesson 6 teaches the trade and the system whole.
- Colonial government stands inside Lesson 7 with religion and family rather than as its own lesson, because the unit's frame is the society the colonists built, and self-government, the household, and the Awakening were the three schools where colonists learned to judge authority, the skill the Founding unit's crisis will demand of them.
- The unit ends at the Treaty of Paris and the Proclamation line of 1763 and does not teach the Stamp Act, because the Founding unit owns the imperial crisis, and this unit's closing judgment, that victory changed every term of the partnership at once, is the strongest door into it.
- Wheatley's poem is dated 1773, ten years past the unit's formal close, and is used in Lesson 6 anyway, with its date stated wherever it appears, because it is the bank's one first-person poetic record of the crossing by an African writer, and the lesson teaches the trade's whole era, 1500s to 1800s, not only its years before 1763.
