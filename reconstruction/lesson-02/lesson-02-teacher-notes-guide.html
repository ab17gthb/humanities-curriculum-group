The Thirteenth Amendment and the Meaning of Freedom

Reconstruction · Lesson 2 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Reconstruction, Lesson 2 of 8 — the lesson that follows Congress's return in December 1865. Lesson 1 closed with Johnson's restoration seated, the Black Codes passed, and freedpeople petitioning against both. This lesson follows the reply that came in law. Between December 1865 and April 1866 the country abolished slavery in its Constitution, heard freedpeople state freedom's terms in their own assemblies, and watched Congress write citizenship into a statute over a president's veto. It closes on the doubts that made that statute look frail and sent Congress back to the Constitution — the road Lesson 3 takes up, with citizenship written into an amendment and imposed as the price of readmission.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 1 (Johnson's restoration and the Black Codes are this lesson's starting condition; Congress's return is its opening date) and Lesson 3 (the Fourteenth Amendment answers the doubts this lesson ends on). Inside the lesson, the Thirteenth Amendment's full text and Iowa's ratification resolution pair against the labeled teaching summaries of the freedmen's conventions and of Johnson's veto message: the settlement as the law wrote it, and freedom's terms as freedpeople and the president argued them.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Lesson 1 complete — Johnson's restoration, the three acts, the Freedmen's Bureau, and the Black Codes; the Emancipation Proclamation and its limits (Sectional Crisis and Civil War unit). The amendment process itself — proposal by Congress, ratification by three fourths of the states — is taught on the slides as the count runs, and no other machinery is assumed. No image of violence is used anywhere in the lesson, in a document, the deck, or these notes, and no portrait of Andrew Johnson is used: the veto is taught through its labeled summary.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (an amendment, a state resolution, a statute, and two labeled summaries read as one contested settlement), D2.His.9 (a settlement built clause by clause across one winter and spring, and doubted by its own authors within the year), D2.His.16 (freedpeople as historical actors defining freedom in families, marriages, names, and conventions), D2.Civ.12 (Section 2's enforcement clause against the veto and the override — the constitutional contest the unit runs on); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.8, WHST.9-10.1 (what each record settled and what it left open, judged from the documents).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to work the ratification count (twenty seven of thirty six states, Georgia's assent on December 6, 1865, proclamation on December 18, eight assents from states lately in rebellion, Delaware and Kentucky refusing, and Iowa assenting after the count closed as an act of commitment); read the Thirteenth Amendment's two sections and its exception closely (a ban with a geography in Section 1, an enforcement commission in Section 2, and the punishment-for-crime exception carried from the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, through which convict leasing later grew); state freedpeople's terms in freedpeople's order (families, marriages, and names first, then the conventions' demands — land or fair wages, family protection, schools, repeal of the Black Codes, testimony and jury service, and the ballot); follow Trumbull's Civil Rights Act through Johnson's veto of March 27, 1866 to the override of April 6 and 9, the first override of a major measure in the country's history; and explain why the men who passed the act doubted their own statute — repeal, a narrow judicial reading of Section 2, and a hostile president's feeble enforcement — and reached for a constitutional amendment instead.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; ratification (slides 4–7) 7 minutes; the two clauses and the exception (slides 8–10) 7 minutes; freedom defined from below (slides 11–13) 6 minutes; the conventions (slides 14–15) 5 minutes; the statute, the veto, and the override (slides 16–19) 10 minutes; settled and not settled (slides 20–21) 4 minutes; glance, vocabulary, synthesis, and exit (slides 22–27) 6 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes, and Freedom's Terms, Drafted Twice (five stations). The notebook page charts the five definers of freedom; the quick check is "Citizenship and Its Rights"; the writing task is "In Law or In Life?"; the exit ticket has three parts — the amendment defined with its ratification dates, Section 2 and its statute named, and the lesson question answered in two or three sentences.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "What did the Thirteenth Amendment settle, and why did Congress decide a statute was not enough?"
  • Today's question has two halves, and the writing task will grade both.
  • The first half asks what the Thirteenth Amendment settled, and the answer is narrower and stronger than students expect: it settled abolition, everywhere, in the Constitution, and it armed Congress to enforce the ban. It did not define citizenship, grant land, or give the ballot.
  • The second half asks why Congress decided a statute was not enough, which sends students past the celebration of the April override to the doubts of December 1866 — a contested clause, a hostile president, and a court that might say no.
  • Preview the route without giving the ending away: a ratification fight, two clauses, an exception hiding inside the first clause, freedom defined from below, a statute, a veto, an override, and then the question the override could not answer. The unit question stands beneath today's question on the slide, and it stays open all unit.

Slide 4 · Section A: Ratification Was a Fight (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Proposing words is not law. Twenty seven of thirty six states had to assent."

  • Section A insists on a distinction students often miss: proposing an amendment and ratifying one are different acts, and only the second makes law.
  • Congress proposed these words in January 1865, while the war still ran. What happened through the autumn of 1865 was a contest state by state, run inside Johnson's restoration, with the president pressing the governments he had recognized to assent and needing their votes to reach three fourths.
  • That dependence is the section's quiet fact: abolition entered the Constitution with the assent of states lately in rebellion, counted because the president's program had seated their governments.
  • Keep the arithmetic in front of students — thirty six states, twenty seven required — because the next two slides run on it, and the activity's second station turns on a state whose vote the arithmetic did not need.

Slide 5 · The count, state by state

On the slide: five bullets — Congress passed the amendment on January 31, 1865; eight former Confederate states were counted in the total; Delaware rejected it, and Kentucky refused it; Georgia assented on December 6, 1865, as the twenty-seventh vote; Seward proclaimed adoption on December 18.

  • Work the ratification as a map and a count together. The amendment needed twenty seven of thirty six states.
  • Johnson's restored southern governments supplied eight of the assents, a fact worth pausing on: the president who would veto the Civil Rights Act helped complete abolition's count, because ratification was one of the three acts his restoration asked of the South.
  • The border slave states split the other way. Delaware rejected the amendment, and Kentucky's legislature refused it outright — reminders that slavery still had legal defenders in states that had never left the Union.
  • Georgia's assent on December 6, 1865, supplied the twenty seventh vote, and Secretary of State Seward proclaimed the amendment adopted on December 18. From that day slavery was not a war measure or a state custom. It was abolished by the Constitution itself.

Slide 6 · Iowa assents after the count closed

On the slide: four bullets — Georgia had already made the amendment law; Iowa ratified on January 24, 1866, as the thirty-first state; its resolution quoted the amendment in full; ratification stood as commitment, not only arithmetic.

  • Iowa is the lesson's small document with a large point, and it is Station 2 in the activity.
  • Georgia's vote had already made the amendment law when Iowa's legislature took the question up, and Iowa assented anyway on January 24, 1866, the thirty first state to do so. Its joint resolution quotes the amendment in full inside a Whereas clause and then records the state's ratification and assent in one sentence.
  • Ask: "Why does a legislature spend a formal act on a settled question?" The resolution's own form answers: a state enters its name on the settlement, in its session laws, beside the whole text of what it approved, so that its citizens and its courts can be held to the page.
  • Ratification, Iowa shows, was arithmetic and commitment at once, and the commitment half explains the ceremony states gave a vote that changed no count.

Slide 7 · 27 of 36 states

On the slide: the big figure "27"; the label — "of 36 states, the three fourths the Constitution required"; the italic note — Georgia's vote, December 6, 1865; proclaimed December 18; eight of the 27 were states lately in rebellion.

  • 27 of 36 states.
  • Hold this number up as the lesson's first measure. Twenty seven was not a round figure or a slogan; it was three fourths of thirty six, the Constitution's own threshold for changing itself, and the amendment stood one Georgia vote short of it until December 6, 1865.
  • Have students state the full sentence the number belongs to: twenty seven states, eight of them lately Confederate, two border slave states refusing, one secretary of state proclaiming adoption on December 18.
  • The count also explains Johnson's leverage and its limit. His restoration governments were indispensable to reaching twenty seven, which gave the president a claim on the settlement. But the count was Congress's to certify and the amendment was the country's once proclaimed, and Section 2, the next section's subject, would soon be read against the very president whose program had helped complete it.

Slide 8 · Section B: Two Clauses, Two Powers (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Section 1 declares a condition abolished. Section 2 arms Congress to enforce it."

  • Section B slows the class down to two sentences, because the whole lesson is an argument about what those sentences authorize.
  • Read Section 1 aloud first and note its grammar: it declares a condition abolished rather than ordering a government to stop, which is why it reaches a private enslaver as directly as a state.
  • Then read Section 2 and note what kind of clause it is. The Bill of Rights mostly tells Congress what it may not do. Section 2 tells Congress what it may do — enforce this article by appropriate legislation — and no amendment before it had spoken that way.
  • Tell students plainly that constitutional fights are often fights over one sentence's reach. The Civil Rights Act, the veto, and the override in Section F are all readings of Section 2, offered by men who agreed on the words and nothing else.

Slide 9 · Section 1: a ban with a geography

On the slide: four bullets — the text declared that neither slavery nor involuntary servitude shall exist; it reached the United States and any place under its jurisdiction; it named no race and no region; it did not define citizenship, land, or the vote.

  • Teach Section 1 as a ban with a geography, because its reach is its meaning. Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction.
  • No state is named, no race is named, and no exception is made for a territory, a household, or a private contract. A condition that had rested on state law and private ownership was ended by national command, in every place the flag governed.
  • Set that beside what the sentence does not do, since students should learn to read a text for its silences as carefully as for its commands. It does not define a citizen. It does not list rights a citizen holds. It does not mention land, wages, schools, or voting.
  • Everything the amendment leaves unnamed becomes the agenda of the rest of this lesson, and much of it becomes the agenda of the next two amendments.

Slide 10 · Section C: The Exception in the Text

On the slide: four bullets — the exception allowed involuntary servitude as a punishment for crime; it applied where the party shall have been duly convicted; the words were carried from the Northwest Ordinance of 1787; the doorway stood open through which convict leasing grew.

  • Section C reads the amendment against itself, and students should hear the exception in the amendment's own words before they hear its history: except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted.
  • The phrase was old, carried from the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, and it passed in 1865 with little debate, because punishing crime with labor seemed ordinary to the men who wrote it.
  • State the consequence plainly and briefly, as the reading does. Southern legislatures wrote broad vagrancy and contract offenses into their codes, men were convicted on minor charges, and their labor was hired out under convict leasing. One narrow clause, kept for an ordinary purpose, became a legal doorway.
  • Note: The teaching point is about texts: a settlement is only as strong as its exceptions are narrow, and later generations inherit the words, not the intentions.

Slide 11 · Section D: Freedom Defined From Below (divider)

On the slide: the Richmond sewing women print at right; the section label and title; the caption — "Freedwomen earning wages in Richmond, the former Confederate capital."

  • Picture: Freedwomen at sewing work in Richmond, the former Confederate capital — the same era print as the title slide, at portrait size.
  • Section D changes the lesson's vantage. So far freedom has been defined by texts written in Washington and in statehouses. Freedpeople defined it in acts, and their definitions came first in time and in logic, because they stated what the law would later be asked to protect.
  • Three acts carry the section. Families searched for kin whom sale had scattered. Couples married in law, making unions and children legitimate that slavery had refused to recognize. And men and women chose their own names for the public record.
  • None of these acts needed Congress's permission, and each one asserted the same claim: that freedom meant a household and an identity the law had to reckon with, not a status granted from above.

Slide 12 · Families, marriages, names

On the slide: the Richmond sewing women print at right; four bullets — families searched for kin sold away, by notice and letter; couples married in law, so their unions and children stood recognized; men and women chose surnames for the record; freedom's first test was a family made whole.

  • Picture: Freedwomen at sewing work in Richmond, the former Confederate capital — freedwomen earning wages, freedom practiced as work, household, and record.
  • Give each act its weight. The searches came first: notices in newspapers, letters carried by ministers and Bureau agents, parents asking after children sold away years before, because slavery's deepest wound had been the family's insecurity, and freedom's first test was whether a family could be made whole and stay whole.
  • The marriages answered the same wound in law. Under slavery a couple's union had no standing a court would honor; in 1865 and 1866 freed couples married in ceremonies and courthouse entries across the South, and the record itself was the right being claimed.
  • The names carry the smallest fact with the largest meaning: a surname chosen by the person who bore it, entered in a register, announced that the recorded identity of a free person was theirs to state. When students reach the conventions next, they should hear the petitions as these same claims, organized.

Slide 13 · Four million freedpeople

On the slide: the big figure "4,000,000"; the label — "freedpeople, the roughly four million the settlement concerned"; the italic note — stated in the lesson's vocabulary; their demands and institution building drove the agenda from below.

  • 4,000,000 freedpeople.
  • Four million is the scale under every sentence in this lesson, and students should sit with it briefly. The amendment, the conventions, and the statute all concern roughly four million men, women, and children — the freedpeople, whose labor had been property in law at the war's start and whose status the country now had to define in practice, county by county and contract by contract.
  • The number also measures what a clause can and cannot do. A sentence in the Constitution could abolish their enslavement in a day, and did. It could not by itself reunite a family, write a fair contract, open a school, or protect a voter, which is why this lesson keeps moving from text to act to assembly to statute.
  • When the conventions speak in the next section, they speak for a population of this size, organizing itself within months of emancipation, without money, offices, or arms.

Slide 14 · Section E: The Conventions State Their Terms (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Delegates elected, minutes kept, petitions sent. Most were received and left unacted on."

  • Section E introduces the era's first Black political assemblies, and their novelty deserves a plain statement.
  • Within months of emancipation, people whom the law had treated as property were electing delegates, choosing officers, keeping minutes, and addressing governments in the forms of American politics.
  • The freedmen's conventions met in state capitals and port cities in the autumn of 1865 and through 1866, and they were not protests in the street. They were conventions, with credentials and committees, and their product was paper: addresses and petitions that stated a program.
  • Teach students to read that program as a draft constitution for freedom, written by the people with the most at stake in its terms. The next slide lists the demands. The point to carry forward is who is drafting: Congress's statute, when it comes in Section F, will be the second draft of freedom's terms, not the first.

Slide 15 · The conventions' demands

On the slide: five bullets — they demanded land of their own, or fair wages on honest contracts; they demanded families protected against forced apprenticeship; they demanded schools for their children; they demanded repeal of the Black Codes, testimony, and jury service; they argued the ballot on service and taxes.

  • Read the demands as a list with an order hidden in it. Land or fair wages comes first in most addresses, because the conventions understood that legal freedom without a livelihood would be renegotiated every season on the planter's terms.
  • Family protection follows, aimed at the apprenticeship clauses in the Black Codes that bound Black children to former enslavers without parental consent. Schools follow, then the legal demands — repeal of the Black Codes, testimony in court, jury service, and property held on equal terms.
  • The ballot closes the list, argued on service and taxation: men who had borne arms for the Union and paid its taxes could not justly be governed without a voice.
  • Then give the reception its sentence. Most legislatures received the petitions and did nothing with them. The program stood complete and unenacted, which is the precise condition Congress faced when it took up Trumbull's bill.

Slide 16 · Section F: Congress Answers With a Statute (divider)

On the slide: the Stevens portrait at right; the section label and title; the caption — "Thaddeus Stevens of Pennsylvania, the House leader of the Radical Republicans."

  • Picture: Thaddeus Stevens of Pennsylvania, in the Brady studio portrait held by the Library of Congress.
  • Section F turns to Congress's answer, and Stevens frames it because he stated its furthest reach. As the House's leading Radical Republican he held that the seceded states were conquered provinces subject to Congress's terms, and he demanded land for freed families taken from the great planters' estates — a demand his own party would not enact.
  • Beside him stand the men who carried the actual statute: Charles Sumner pressing from the Senate's Radical side, and Lyman Trumbull, the moderate Judiciary chairman, writing the text the center could pass.
  • Teach the faction map before the bill, because the bill's limits are the map's product. What became law in April 1866 was the strongest measure two thirds of both houses could agree to repass over a veto, and its silences — no ballot and no land — are coalition arithmetic as much as conviction.
  • Say: "Radical Republicans: the faction of the Republican Party, led in the House by Thaddeus Stevens and in the Senate by Charles Sumner, which held that the war had earned a thorough reconstruction of the South on the basis of equal civil and political rights, and that Congress, not the president, held the power to set its terms."
  • Say: "Thaddeus Stevens: the Pennsylvania congressman, the House's most uncompromising Reconstruction leader, who argued that the seceded states were conquered provinces subject to Congress's terms and demanded confiscation of the great planters' estates to give freed families land of their own."

Slide 17 · Trumbull and the bill

On the slide: the Trumbull portrait at right; four bullets — Lyman Trumbull, the Judiciary chairman, was a moderate; the bill declared all persons born in the United States citizens; it guaranteed the same rights as white citizens — contract, suit, testimony, and property; it granted no ballot and no land, by the coalition's arithmetic.

  • Picture: Lyman Trumbull, in the Brady-Handy photograph held by the Library of Congress, dated on its file page to the decade after the war; it is not presented as an 1866 likeness.
  • Trumbull is the statute's author and the lesson's hinge: a moderate, chairman of the Judiciary Committee, who broke with Johnson over the Black Codes and carried the Republican center with him.
  • His bill did two things. It declared all persons born in the United States citizens, answering the old claim that no Black person could hold citizenship. And it guaranteed citizens of every race the same rights white citizens enjoyed — to contract, to sue and testify, to hold property, and to the law's equal protection.
  • Note what the guarantee is measured against: not an abstract list, but the rights of white citizens, which made the Black Codes illegal by comparison in a single sentence. The next slide gives the president's answer.
  • Say: "Lyman Trumbull: the Illinois senator who chaired the Judiciary Committee and wrote both the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and the Freedmen's Bureau bill Johnson vetoed, a moderate whose break with the president over the Black Codes carried the Republican center into congressional Reconstruction."

Slide 18 · Johnson's veto, March 27, 1866

On the slide: four bullets — he held that Congress could not confer citizenship, because the states governed it; eleven states stood unrepresented in Congress; he said the bill favored the colored race over the white; in his account, it was a stride toward centralization.

  • Johnson's veto message of March 27, 1866, is taught in this lesson as a labeled summary, in the activity's fifth station and in this telling, and students should know why the summary carries that label when they meet it.
  • Its arguments, as the public record gives them, ran in a set order. Congress, he held, could not confer citizenship on whole races born on American soil, because citizenship's terms belonged to the states. Eleven of the thirty six states stood unrepresented in Congress, and so grave a law should not pass in their absence.
  • The bill, in his account, discriminated in favor of the colored race against the white race. And beneath each point ran his principle: that the measure was a stride toward centralization, against all the country's experience.
  • His own cabinet had urged him to sign. The refusal, not any single argument, is what moved the center: Trumbull's moderates concluded the president would accept no congressional settlement at all.

Slide 19 · The override, April 1866

On the slide: the Stevens portrait at right; four bullets — the Senate repassed the bill on April 6, and the House on April 9; it was the first override of a major measure in the country's history; the Bureau bill fight ended the same way in July; Congress, not the president, now set the terms.

  • Picture: Thaddeus Stevens of Pennsylvania, in the Brady studio portrait held by the Library of Congress — the House leader of the faction that set Congress's terms.
  • The override is the section's climax and the unit's first transfer of power. The Senate repassed the Civil Rights bill over the veto on April 6, 1866, and the House followed on April 9, two thirds in both chambers — the first time Congress had enacted a major measure over a president's refusal.
  • The same season supplied the proof it was no accident: Johnson vetoed Trumbull's Freedmen's Bureau bill as well, and in July Congress repassed that too.
  • Teach the sequence as a constitutional fact, not a score. A president who could be overridden at will on the central question of the day had lost the settlement to Congress, and everyone in Washington understood the session that way.
  • Then turn the class toward the doubt that followed immediately, because the men who won in April spent the rest of 1866 asking whether a statute, however dramatically passed, could hold what they had written in it.

Slide 20 · Section G: Settled, and Not Settled (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "The override settled who could pass a law. It did not settle whether the law would stand."

  • Section G is the lesson's turn from victory to audit, and it should be taught as the reading teaches it, in the order of the doubts.
  • The override had settled who could pass a law in April 1866. It had not settled what the law rested on. The Civil Rights Act claimed its authority from one clause, Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment, and a clause can be read narrowly by a court that did not share Congress's purposes.
  • Nor could a statute command its own enforcement: the president who had vetoed it still directed the officers who would execute it, in states whose legislatures had passed the Black Codes and had not surrendered.
  • The section closes the lesson where December 1866 actually stood, with Congress already drafting the next instrument, a constitutional amendment, and with the question of ratification waiting in the next lesson.

Slide 21 · Why a statute was not enough

On the slide: four bullets — a court might read Section 2 narrowly and strike the act down; a hostile president would enforce it feebly; the Black Code legislatures had not surrendered; Congress's next answer was citizenship in the Constitution itself.

  • State the conclusion in the question's own terms. A statute was not enough for three reasons students should be able to separate.
  • It could be repealed by a later Congress, as any law can. It could be read down or struck down by a court that held Section 2 gave Congress power against slavery alone, not power to define citizenship against the states. And it depended for its daily force on a president who had already refused it once.
  • An amendment answers all three frailties at once: it cannot be repealed by ordinary majorities, it binds the courts as the text they interpret, and it binds presidents as the law they swear to execute.
  • That is why the party that had just won the greatest legislative victory of the century went back to the harder road of constitutional amendment within the year, and why this lesson's question is phrased from December, looking forward, rather than from April, looking back.

Slide 22 · Freedom's Terms at a Glance

On the slide: the title "What each record settled, and what it left open"; the four rows — the amendment (ratified December 1865; settled abolition, left citizenship open), the conventions (freedpeople, 1865 and 1866; stated the program, held no power to enact it), the Civil Rights Act (over a veto, April 1866; wrote citizenship in statute, authority contested), the veto fight (veto, then override; settled who governs, left the ground unsupplied).

  • This slide mirrors the summary table in the guided notes, Freedom's Terms at a Glance, and students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • Read the rows as the lesson's argument in miniature. The amendment settled abolition and armed Congress, and left citizenship's content open.
  • Freedpeople and their conventions stated the whole program — land, wages, family, schools, courts, and the ballot — and held no power to enact any of it. The Civil Rights Act wrote citizenship and equal civil rights into statute over a veto, and left its own authority contested and the ballot and land ungranted.
  • The veto fight settled that Congress would set the terms when it held two thirds, and left the constitutional ground unsupplied. Grade the table strictly on the open column: naming what each record left standing is the lesson's discipline.

Slide 23 · Vocabulary: the amendment and the statute

On the slide: two terms with their short forms — Thirteenth Amendment (ratified December 1865, the first Reconstruction amendment), Civil Rights Act of 1866 (passed over a veto, April 1866). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Say: "Thirteenth Amendment: the constitutional amendment, passed by Congress on January 31, 1865, and ratified that December, that abolished slavery and involuntary servitude everywhere in the United States, except as punishment for a crime, the first of the three Reconstruction amendments and the only one adopted while the war still ran."
  • Say: "Civil Rights Act of 1866: the law Congress passed over Johnson's veto in April 1866, the first statute enacted over a presidential veto on a major measure, which declared all persons born in the United States citizens and guaranteed citizens of every race the same rights to contract, sue, hold property, and enjoy the law's protection."
  • Two instruments of two different strengths stand here. The amendment binds every legislature and court in the country and cannot be repealed by an ordinary majority. The statute it authorized can be repealed or read down by a later Congress, and that frailty is where the lesson ends.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: the faction and its leader

On the slide: two terms with their short forms — Radical Republicans (Stevens in the House, Sumner in the Senate), Thaddeus Stevens (conquered provinces, and land for freed families). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: "Radical Republicans: the faction of the Republican Party, led in the House by Thaddeus Stevens and in the Senate by Charles Sumner, which held that the war had earned a thorough reconstruction of the South on the basis of equal civil and political rights, and that Congress, not the president, held the power to set its terms."
  • Say: "Thaddeus Stevens: the Pennsylvania congressman, the House's most uncompromising Reconstruction leader, who argued that the seceded states were conquered provinces subject to Congress's terms and demanded confiscation of the great planters' estates to give freed families land of their own."
  • The faction and its House leader belong on one slide: the men who held that Congress, and not the president, would set the settlement's terms, and that the settlement should reach land itself.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: the author, the assemblies, the people

On the slide: three terms with their short forms — Lyman Trumbull (the moderate who wrote the act of 1866), freedmen's conventions (the first political assemblies of the emancipated), freedpeople (roughly four million, freed by emancipation and the amendment). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: "Lyman Trumbull: the Illinois senator who chaired the Judiciary Committee and wrote both the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and the Freedmen's Bureau bill Johnson vetoed, a moderate whose break with the president over the Black Codes carried the Republican center into congressional Reconstruction."
  • Say: "freedmen's conventions: the state conventions freedpeople held across the South in 1865 and 1866, electing delegates, organizing communities, and petitioning for land, fair wages, family protection, schools, and the ballot, the first political assemblies of the emancipated."
  • Say: "freedpeople: the name historians use, following the usage of the time, for the roughly four million men, women, and children freed from slavery by emancipation and the Thirteenth Amendment, whose own demands and institution building drove Reconstruction's agenda from below."
  • Close the vocabulary where the lesson closes: the moderate who wrote the statute, the assemblies that drafted freedom's terms before Congress did, and the people whose name for themselves runs under every document in the lesson. The set of seven is complete with these.

Slide 26 · The settlement in one view

On the slide: the title "The settlement of 1866"; the four rows — Settled (abolition in the Constitution, Congress armed to enforce it), Stated (freedpeople's terms: land, wages, family, schools, the ballot), Written (citizenship and equal rights in statute, over a veto), Doubted (a contested clause, a hostile president, an unreconciled South).

  • Use this slide to close the argument before the work begins, and make students say the four rows in order, because the sequence is the lesson.
  • Settled: slavery abolished in the Constitution itself, by a count completed in December 1865, with Congress expressly armed to enforce the ban.
  • Stated: freedpeople defined freedom in families reunited, marriages recorded, names chosen, and conventions petitioning for land, wages, schools, and the ballot.
  • Written: Congress turned that definition toward law, declaring citizenship and equal civil rights in a statute passed over a president's veto for the first time on a major measure.
  • Doubted: the statute's own authors saw that a contested clause, a hostile president, and an unreconciled South could leave citizenship standing on paper, and they reached for the Constitution again. Law, life, statute, doubt, in that order, is the shape students should carry into the activity.

  • Close by returning to the lesson question and handing the lesson to the work.
  • The activity, Freedom's Terms, Drafted Twice, sets out five stations: the Thirteenth Amendment in full and Iowa's ratification resolution and Section 1 of the Civil Rights Act, all verbatim from the editions their Source lines name, beside two labeled teaching summaries, of the freedmen's conventions and of Johnson's veto message, and it ends with a table and a verdict on whether freedom existed more in law or in life by the end of 1866.
