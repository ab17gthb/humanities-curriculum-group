The Thirteenth Amendment and the Meaning of Freedom

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Ratification Was a Fight

Congress had passed the amendment on January 31, 1865, in the war's last winter, but passing only proposed words. Ratifying made them law, and ratification was a contest. Three fourths of the thirty six states had to assent, twenty seven in all. President Johnson, running his restoration program, pressed the southern governments to ratify, and eight former Confederate states were counted in the total. The border slave states split. Delaware rejected the amendment, and Kentucky's legislature refused it outright. Georgia's assent on December 6, 1865, supplied the twenty seventh vote, and Secretary of State William H. Seward proclaimed the amendment adopted on December 18. Slavery was abolished by the Constitution itself, by votes that included states lately in rebellion.

B. Two Clauses, Two Powers

Read the amendment as two working parts. Section 1 states the ban in one sentence: neither slavery nor involuntary servitude shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction. The sentence names no race and no region, and it reaches private action as well as state law, because it declares a condition abolished rather than forbidding a government to impose it. Section 2 then assigns the work: Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation. No earlier amendment had carried such a clause. The first ten amendments limited Congress. This one armed it. Whoever held Congress held an express constitutional commission to write freedom's terms into statutes, and the fight of 1866 ran on that sentence.

C. The Exception in the Text

One clause in Section 1 requires slow reading. The ban runs everywhere except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted. The words were carried from the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, and in 1865 they passed with little debate. Their meaning on the page is plain: a state may compel the labor of a person lawfully convicted of a crime. In the years after ratification, southern legislatures wrote broad vagrancy and contract offenses into law, and the exception became the legal doorway through which convict leasing grew, a system that seized Black men on minor charges and hired out their labor. The amendment abolished slavery and kept one narrow ground on which compelled labor remained lawful.

D. Freedom Defined From Below

Freedpeople stated what freedom meant in acts before Congress stated it in statutes. Families separated by sale began searching for one another, placing notices in newspapers, writing letters, and asking Bureau agents and ministers for word of children, parents, and spouses sold away. Couples who had lived as husband and wife under slavery, with no legal marriage, now married in law, in ceremonies the Freedmen's Bureau recorded, making their unions and their children legitimate in the eyes of the state. Men and women chose names for the record, taking surnames of their own choosing and dropping names that had marked an enslaver's ownership. Freedom was a family made whole and recognized, a name of one's own, wages for one's labor, and a school for one's children.

E. The Conventions State Their Terms

In the autumn of 1865 and through 1866, freedpeople organized the first political assemblies of the emancipated. State conventions met in Raleigh, Charleston, Richmond, New Orleans, and other cities, with delegates elected from counties and parishes. These freedmen's conventions issued addresses and petitions to legislatures, to Congress, and to the president. Their demands recur across state lines: land of their own or fair wages on others' land, protection of families against forced apprenticeship, schools for their children, repeal of the Black Codes, the right to testify and to serve on juries, and the ballot. Most petitions were received coldly or not at all. The conventions stand as freedpeople's own draft of freedom's terms, written before Congress produced its version.

F. Congress Answers With a Statute

Congress answered the Black Codes with a statute built on Section 2. Senator Lyman Trumbull of Illinois, chairman of the Judiciary Committee, wrote the Civil Rights bill. It declared all persons born in the United States citizens and guaranteed citizens of every race the same rights to contract, sue, testify, and hold property that white citizens enjoyed. The Radical Republicans wanted still more, including the ballot, but the center held on Trumbull's text. Johnson vetoed the bill on March 27, 1866. Congress repassed it over his veto, the Senate on April 6 and the House on April 9, the first override of a major measure in the country's history. A second fight the same season, over renewing the Freedmen's Bureau, ended the same way in July, veto and override together.

G. Settled, and Not Settled

The override settled who could pass a law. It did not settle whether this law would stand. Doubts ran in two directions. Lawyers asked whether Section 2 truly authorized Congress to define citizenship and strike down state law, and a court unsympathetic to the act might say it did not. A president hostile to the statute would enforce it feebly, and legislatures passing Black Codes had not surrendered. A statute resting on a contested reading of one amendment was a frail vessel for citizenship. By December 1866 Congress had already drafted the next answer, a new amendment that would write citizenship into the Constitution itself, beyond the reach of any veto or court reading. Whether that answer would be ratified, and on what terms, is the next lesson's subject.

5. What did the amendment settle, and why was a statute not enough? Full credit answers hold both halves. The amendment settled abolition itself: slavery and involuntary servitude ended everywhere in the United States by constitutional command, ratified in December 1865, with Congress expressly empowered to enforce the ban. Congress then wrote the Civil Rights Act of 1866 on Section 2, declaring birthright citizenship and equal civil rights, and repassed it over Johnson's veto on April 6 and April 9, 1866. A statute was not enough because its authority rested on a contested reading of Section 2 that a court might reject, because a hostile president would enforce it feebly, and because the legislatures that passed the Black Codes had not surrendered. Citizenship needed to stand in the Constitution itself. (Blueprint 3 and 4)
