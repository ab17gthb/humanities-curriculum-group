Freedom's Terms, Drafted Twice

Directions: Work through the five stations in order. For each record, state which of freedpeople's stated terms the record answered and which it left standing. Freedpeople's terms, from the conventions and the reading, are land, fair wages, family protection, schools, and the ballot, with equal rights in court. Stations 1, 2, and 3 are verbatim documents from the editions named in their Source lines. Stations 4 and 5 are teaching summaries, labeled as summaries on their faces. At the end, complete the table and deliver the verdict.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: The Thirteenth Amendment, in Full, 1865

Source: United States Congress, Thirteenth Amendment, 1865 (U.S. Constitution, Amendment XIII), as recorded in the audited TPSP bank record thirteenth-amendment-1865. Printed here in full.

Section 1. Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction. Section 2. Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.

State what Section 1 abolishes and the one exception it keeps, in the amendment's own terms. Which of freedpeople's stated terms does the ban itself answer, and which does it not name at all?

State what Section 2 adds beyond the ban. Why would a congressman in 1866 treat that sentence as the most important line in the amendment?

Station 2: Iowa's Ratification Resolution, 1866

Source: Iowa General Assembly, Joint Resolution No. 1: Ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment, 1866 (Acts and Joint Resolutions of the Eleventh General Assembly of Iowa, 1866), as recorded in the audited TPSP bank record iowa-ratification-thirteenth-amendment-1866. Printed here in full.

JOINT RESOLUTION ratifying the amendment to the Constitution of the United States abolishing Slavery.

Whereas, The Congress of the United States has proposed to the several States the following amendment to the Federal Constitution, viz:

"ARTICLE XIII.

"Section I. Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime, whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States or any place subject to their jurisdiction.

"Sec. II. Congress shall have power to enforce this Article by appropriate legislation."

Therefore,

Be it Resolved by the General Assembly of the State of Iowa, That the State of Iowa, by its Legislature, hereby ratifies and assents to said Amendment.

Approved January 24th, 1866.

Iowa's vote was not needed. Georgia's vote in December 1865 had already completed the count. State what this resolution shows a state legislature doing after the legal question was closed, and what that tells you ratification meant beyond arithmetic.

The resolution quotes the amendment inside its own text before assenting. State why a ratifying state would print the whole amendment rather than name it, using the document's own form as your evidence.

Station 3: The Civil Rights Act of 1866, Section 1

Source: U.S. Congress, Civil Rights Act of 1866, 1866 (Facing History & Ourselves; 14 Stat. 27-30), Section 1, as recorded in the audited TPSP bank record civil-rights-act-1866. The ellipsis at the end stands in the bank record and is preserved here.

An Act to protect all Persons in the United States in their Civil Rights, and furnish the Means of their Vindication.

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled, That all persons born in the United States and not subject to any foreign power, excluding Indians not taxed, are hereby declared to be citizens of the United States; and such citizens, of every race and color, without regard to any previous condition of slavery or involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall have the same right, in every State and Territory in the United States, to make and enforce contracts, to sue, be parties, and give evidence, to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, and convey real and personal property, and to full and equal benefit of all laws and proceedings for the security of person and property, as is enjoyed by white citizens, and shall be subject to like punishment, pains, and penalties, and to none other, any law, statute, ordinance, regulation, or custom, to the contrary notwithstanding [...]

State how the act defines a citizen, and list four rights it guarantees citizens of every race in the same terms white citizens enjoyed.

Set this station beside freedpeople's terms. State which two terms the act answers directly, and name two terms from the conventions that this section does not grant.

Station 4: The Freedmen's Conventions, 1865 and 1866 (Teaching Summary)

Source: Teaching summary, built for this lesson from the public record of the state conventions freedpeople held across the South in 1865 and 1866. This station is a summary, not a verbatim document.

Beginning in the autumn of 1865, freedpeople elected delegates and held state conventions in capitals and port cities across the South, among them Raleigh in North Carolina, Charleston in South Carolina, and meetings in Virginia, Georgia, Louisiana, and elsewhere. The conventions organized communities that slavery had forbidden to organize, chose officers, kept minutes, and adopted addresses and petitions directed to state legislatures, to Congress, and to President Johnson. Across state lines the demands run together. The conventions asked for land of their own or fair wages under honest contracts. They asked that families be protected, that Black children not be bound out as apprentices without their parents' consent, and that marriages be recognized in law. They asked for schools. They asked for repeal of the Black Codes, for the right to testify in court, to serve on juries, and to hold property on equal terms. And they asked for the ballot, arguing that men who had borne arms for the Union and paid taxes to its governments could not justly be governed without a voice. Most legislatures received the petitions and did nothing with them. The conventions are the record of freedpeople drafting freedom's terms for themselves, in public, within months of emancipation.

This station is a summary, and its Source line says so. State two demands in it that Station 3's statute grants, and two demands that no station so far has granted.

State the argument the conventions made for the ballot, as the summary gives it, and state what happened to most of the petitions.

Station 5: Johnson's Veto of the Civil Rights Bill (Teaching Summary)

Source: Teaching summary of President Andrew Johnson's veto message of March 27, 1866, built for this lesson from the public record of the message. This station is a summary, not a verbatim document.

Johnson returned the Civil Rights bill to the Senate with his objections on March 27, 1866. He argued first from citizenship. The bill, he held, made citizens of the United States by act of Congress of entire races born on its soil, including the freedpeople, and Congress held no such power over a subject the states had always governed, since the power to confer state citizenship belonged to the states as exclusively as federal citizenship belonged to Congress. He argued second from representation. Eleven of the thirty six states stood unrepresented in Congress, and so grave a change in the country's law should not be made by a Congress from which a third of the Union was absent. He argued third from the bill's operation. In his account the measure discriminated in favor of the colored race and against the white race, granting freedpeople protections no law gave others. And he argued throughout from first principles, that the bill was a stride toward centralization, concentrating legislative power in the national government against all the country's experience. Members of his own cabinet had urged him to sign. He refused, and Congress prepared to repass the bill without him.

State Johnson's representation argument in one sentence, and state the strongest answer to it that the reading supplies, about who had set the terms under which the eleven states stood unrepresented.

Johnson held that the bill discriminated in favor of the colored race. Set that claim beside Station 3's text, which guarantees the same rights as are enjoyed by white citizens. State what the statute's own wording does to his claim.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: The Thirteenth Amendment

What it abolishes, and the exception. Section 1 abolishes slavery and involuntary servitude everywhere within the United States and any place subject to their jurisdiction, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted. The ban answers the first term underneath all the others, that no person may be owned, and it names none of the rest: land, wages, schools, family protection, and the ballot appear nowhere in its text. (Blueprint 3)

What Section 2 adds. Section 2 gives Congress power to enforce the article by appropriate legislation. A congressman in 1866 could treat that sentence as the amendment's working half, because it converted a prohibition into a commission: Congress claimed the Civil Rights Act as exactly the appropriate legislation Section 2 authorized, against a president who denied the power existed. (Blueprint 3)

Station 2: Iowa's Ratification Resolution

A vote after the count closed. Iowa ratified on January 24, 1866, after Georgia's December vote had already made the amendment law, becoming the thirty first state to assent. The resolution shows a legislature placing its state on the record of abolition when nothing legal turned on its vote, which tells students ratification was also a public commitment, a state's name entered on the settlement its citizens would be held to. (Blueprint 3)

Why print the whole amendment? The resolution's Whereas clause quotes the amendment in full before the assent, so that the act of ratifying carries its object inside it. A reader of Iowa's session laws meets the ban, the exception, and the enforcement clause on the same page as Iowa's name, and no later reader can mistake what the state assented to. (Blueprint 3)

Station 3: The Civil Rights Act, Section 1

Citizenship and four rights. The act declares all persons born in the United States and not subject to any foreign power, excluding Indians not taxed, to be citizens of the United States. Its guaranteed rights include the same right to make and enforce contracts, to sue, be parties, and give evidence, to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, and convey real and personal property, and to full and equal benefit of all laws for the security of person and property, as is enjoyed by white citizens. Any four earn the point. (Blueprint 4)

Answered and ungranted. The act answers the conventions' demands for equal standing in court and for property and contract rights directly. It does not grant the ballot, and it does not grant land. Political rights and the material base of freedom stood outside the statute, which is one reason the conventions' program outran the law of 1866. (Blueprint 4)

Station 4: The Conventions (Teaching Summary)

Granted and ungranted. Station 3 grants the conventions' demands for testimony and court standing and for property and contract on equal terms. No station grants the ballot, and none grants land. Students should also credit family protection and schools as demands the act touches only in part, through equal benefit of the laws, without building either one. (Blueprint 4)

The ballot argument and the petitions. The conventions argued that men who had borne arms for the Union and paid taxes to its governments could not justly be governed without a voice. Most legislatures received the petitions and did nothing with them, which is why the conventions read in this unit as a program awaiting a government willing to enact it. (Blueprint 4)

Station 5: Johnson's Veto (Teaching Summary)

The representation argument and its answer. Johnson argued that so grave a law should not pass a Congress in which eleven of the thirty six states stood unrepresented. The reading's answer is that the absence was the settlement's own doing: the southern states stood outside Congress because the governments Johnson had recognized rested on his restoration program, and Congress had never accepted that those governments, elected under Black Codes, entitled their states to seats while their terms were unsettled. (Blueprint 4)

The discrimination claim against the text. The statute guarantees citizens of every race the same rights as are enjoyed by white citizens, and subjects them to like punishment and none other. Its standard is sameness, not preference. Johnson's claim requires reading a guarantee of equality as a grant of advantage, and Station 3's wording is the refutation students should quote. (Blueprint 4)

Part 2: The Table and the Verdict (Grading Guide)

Table, expected entries. The amendment answered ownership itself and armed Congress; it left citizenship's content, land, and the ballot unnamed. Iowa's resolution answered nothing new in law; it placed a free state's name on the settlement after the count closed, and it left every term to Congress and the states to work out. The Civil Rights Act answered citizenship, contract, testimony, and property; it left the ballot and land standing. The conventions answered nothing in law, because they held no power; they stated the whole program, and every item in it that required a legislature stood open. The veto answered none of freedpeople's terms; it contested Congress's power to answer any of them, and its defeat in the override is itself part of the record. The verdict earns full credit on either side when argued from the stations. The law side rests on the amendment's permanence, the act's citizenship clause, and the override's proof that Congress would act. The life side rests on the ungranted ballot and land, the petitions received and ignored, and the reading's closing doubts about enforcement and the courts. A full credit paragraph answers the other side's strongest point rather than ignoring it. (Blueprint 3 and 4)
