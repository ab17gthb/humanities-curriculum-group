Guided Notes: The Thirteenth Amendment and the Meaning of Freedom

Lesson question: What did the Thirteenth Amendment settle, and why did Congress decide a statute was not enough?

Unit question: What did Reconstruction settle about freedom, citizenship, and the federal Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Thirteenth Amendment: the constitutional amendment, passed by Congress on January 31, 1865, and ratified that December, that abolished slavery and involuntary servitude everywhere in the United States, except as punishment for a crime, the first of the three Reconstruction amendments and the only one adopted while the war still ran

Civil Rights Act of 1866: the law Congress passed over Johnson's veto in April 1866, the first statute enacted over a presidential veto on a major measure, which declared all persons born in the United States citizens and guaranteed citizens of every race the same rights to contract, sue, hold property, and enjoy the law's protection

Radical Republicans: the faction of the Republican Party, led in the House by Thaddeus Stevens and in the Senate by Charles Sumner, which held that the war had earned a thorough reconstruction of the South on the basis of equal civil and political rights, and that Congress, not the president, held the power to set its terms

Thaddeus Stevens: the Pennsylvania congressman, the House's most uncompromising Reconstruction leader, who argued that the seceded states were conquered provinces subject to Congress's terms and demanded confiscation of the great planters' estates to give freed families land of their own

Lyman Trumbull: the Illinois senator who chaired the Judiciary Committee and wrote both the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and the Freedmen's Bureau bill Johnson vetoed, a moderate whose break with the president over the Black Codes carried the Republican center into congressional Reconstruction

freedmen's conventions: the state conventions freedpeople held across the South in 1865 and 1866, electing delegates, organizing communities, and petitioning for land, fair wages, family protection, schools, and the ballot, the first political assemblies of the emancipated

freedpeople: the name historians use, following the usage of the time, for the roughly four million men, women, and children freed from slavery by emancipation and the Thirteenth Amendment, whose own demands and institution building drove Reconstruction's agenda from below

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher

Teach this lesson second in the Reconstruction unit, directly after Lesson 1 on presidential Reconstruction, the Freedmen's Bureau, and the Black Codes, whose statutes this lesson's Civil Rights Act answers. It pairs with the lesson activity, Freedom's Terms, Drafted Twice, whose five stations the writing task draws on, and its first three stations should be taught after sections A through C of the reading so students meet the amendment and the act in the documents themselves. Students need Lesson 1's account of Johnson's restoration at working level, the Emancipation Proclamation's standing as a wartime measure from the Sectional Crisis unit, and that unit's account of the amendment's passage by Congress on January 31, 1865, which this lesson treats as prior knowledge and does not reteach. Content: students will trace how abolition was ratified in December 1865, read the amendment's two sections and its exception clause closely, state freedom's terms as freedpeople stated them in families and conventions, and follow the Civil Rights Act of 1866 through veto to the first override of a major measure, closing on why Congress judged a statute a frail vessel for citizenship. Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies (cite textual evidence, determine central ideas, integrate and evaluate multiple sources, write arguments from evidence) and the C3 Framework (evaluate sources and use evidence, develop claims, communicate conclusions). Timing: one period of about 50 minutes for the reading and these notes; the activity takes a second period. Materials: the lesson reading, these notes, and the slide deck if projected.

Summary table. The Thirteenth Amendment: it settled abolition in the Constitution itself, ratified December 1865, and armed Congress in Section 2; it left open what citizenship and its rights would be, and it kept the punishment exception in its own text. Freedpeople and the conventions: they demanded land or fair wages, family protection, schools, testimony and jury rights, repeal of the Black Codes, and the ballot; most petitions were received coldly, and the ballot and land stood unanswered at the end of 1866. The Civil Rights Act of 1866: it settled birthright citizenship and equal civil rights in statute, over a veto; it left open whether a court would sustain it and whether a hostile president would enforce it. The veto fight: it settled that Congress, not the president, would set Reconstruction's terms when it held two thirds; it left open the constitutional ground the next lesson's amendment was drafted to supply.

Exit ticket 1. The Thirteenth Amendment is the constitutional amendment, passed by Congress on January 31, 1865, and ratified that December, that abolished slavery and involuntary servitude everywhere in the United States, except as punishment for a crime, the first of the three Reconstruction amendments and the only one adopted while the war still ran. Georgia completed ratification on December 6, 1865, and the amendment was proclaimed adopted on December 18, 1865.

Exit ticket 2. Section 2 gives Congress power to enforce the amendment by appropriate legislation. The statute built on it in 1866 is the Civil Rights Act of 1866, written by Senator Lyman Trumbull of Illinois, chairman of the Judiciary Committee.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that names the settlement, one freedperson's term, and the frailty. The amendment settled abolition as constitutional law and armed Congress to enforce it. Freedpeople stated freedom's terms themselves, in land, wages, family protection, schools, and the ballot. A statute was not enough because it rested on a contested reading of Section 2, faced a hostile president's feeble enforcement, and could be struck down or repealed, so citizenship had to be written into the Constitution itself.
