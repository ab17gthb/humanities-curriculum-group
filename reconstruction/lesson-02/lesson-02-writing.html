Writing Task: In Law or In Life?

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: What did the Thirteenth Amendment settle, and why did Congress decide a statute was not enough? A strong answer states what the amendment itself settled, states freedom's terms as freedpeople stated them, follows the Civil Rights Act from Trumbull's draft through Johnson's veto to the override, and explains the two doubts that made a statute a frail vessel for citizenship. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The amendment was ratified when Georgia assented on December 6, 1865, the twenty seventh of the thirty six states, and proclaimed adopted on December 18. Eight former Confederate states were counted in the total. Delaware rejected it and Kentucky refused it. Iowa assented afterward, on January 24, 1866, the thirty first state.

2. Section 1 abolished slavery and involuntary servitude everywhere in the United States, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, words carried from the Northwest Ordinance of 1787. Section 2 gave Congress power to enforce the article by appropriate legislation, a clause no earlier amendment had carried.

3. Freedpeople stated freedom's terms themselves: families searching for sold kin, marriages made legal, surnames chosen, and state conventions in 1865 and 1866 petitioning for land or fair wages, family protection, schools, repeal of the Black Codes, testimony and jury rights, and the ballot. Most petitions were received and left unacted on.

4. Trumbull's Civil Rights Act of 1866 declared all persons born in the United States citizens and guaranteed citizens of every race the same rights to contract, sue, testify, and hold property as white citizens enjoyed. It granted neither the ballot nor land.

5. Johnson vetoed the act on March 27, 1866, holding that Congress could not confer citizenship, that eleven states stood unrepresented, and that the bill discriminated in favor of the colored race. Congress repassed it, the Senate on April 6 and the House on April 9, the first override of a major measure in the country's history. The Freedmen's Bureau fight ended the same way in July.

6. The act's friends doubted it on two grounds: a court might hold that Section 2 gave Congress no power to define citizenship against state law, and a hostile president would enforce it feebly. By December 1866 Congress had drafted a new amendment to write citizenship into the Constitution itself.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the prompt: what the amendment settled, and why a statute was judged not enough. Either doubt may lead, contested authority or hostile enforcement, when it is argued from the bank. (Blueprint 3 and 4)
  • The settlement stated exactly. Abolition everywhere by constitutional command, ratified December 1865 with Georgia's vote, and Congress armed by Section 2: bank items 1 and 2 carry the dates, the count, the exception, and the enforcement clause. An essay that treats the amendment as a proclamation or a statute has missed the lesson's ground. (Blueprint 3)
  • Freedpeople's terms stated as their own program, not as background color: bank item 3 carries the searches, the marriages, the names, and the conventions' full list, and a full credit essay names at least the ballot or land as a term the law of 1866 left ungranted, from bank item 4. (Blueprint 3 and 4)
  • The veto fight followed in order: Trumbull's draft, Johnson's veto of March 27, 1866 with at least one of his stated grounds, and the override of April 6 and April 9, from bank item 5. (Blueprint 4)
  • The closing doubts named and the prepared answer stated: bank item 6. An essay that ends at the override has stopped one section early, because the lesson's question is asked from December 1866, not from April. (Blueprint 3 and 4)

Model opening for teacher reference. The Thirteenth Amendment settled the oldest question in the country and opened the next one in the same sentence. It abolished slavery everywhere in the United States, by votes counted in December 1865, and in Section 2 it handed Congress the power to enforce the ban, which Congress used within months to declare freedpeople citizens with equal civil rights, over a president's veto. Yet the men who passed that act doubted it as they passed it, because a statute built on one contested clause could fall to one unfriendly court, and the conventions' larger program, land and the ballot above all, stood outside it entirely. A full model continues in this pattern through the exception clause and the veto's stated grounds, and closes on the amendment Congress drafted to put citizenship beyond a statute's reach.

Common weak answers. The celebration answer that ends at ratification has stated the settlement and skipped the question, which begins where the settlement ends. The biography answer that makes the lesson a duel between Johnson and Congress loses freedpeople, whose program is the standard both men are measured against in this unit. An essay that gives the act the ballot, or gives the amendment a citizenship clause, has merged the two instruments the lesson exists to separate, and bank items 2 and 4 are the correction.
