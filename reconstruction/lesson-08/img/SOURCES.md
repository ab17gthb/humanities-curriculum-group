## Image discipline for this lesson, stated on the record

- A. B. Frost's "Of Course He Wants to Vote the Democratic Ticket" (Harper's Weekly, October 21, 1876) is NOT reproduced in any document or in the deck. The audited TPSP bank record presents this print by description, with its caption dialogue transcribed, and the lesson follows the bank's presentation at activity Station 4, in the quick check, and in the deck's Section B teaching. The print shows pistols held to a Black voter's head, and under this unit's binding image discipline an image of voter intimidation and violence is taught in text, not reproduced as a picture. The choice is named on the station's face. For the record: a public domain file of the print does exist on Wikimedia Commons (2461 by 1724 JPEG, license field "Public domain," verified through the Commons API during this build, at https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%27Of_Course_He_Wants_To_Vote_The_Democratic_Ticket%27_%28October_1876%29%2C_Harper%27s_Weekly.jpg), and the lesson's choice not to reproduce it is a presentation decision under the unit's discipline, not an availability failure.
- No image of violence is used as decoration anywhere in this lesson.

- Joint Resolution proposing the Fifteenth Amendment, activity Station 3: the audited TPSP bank record fifteenth-amendment-joint-resolution-1869 in reconstruction.json, whose sourceNote names "U.S. Congress, 'Joint Resolution Proposing the Fifteenth Amendment,' 1869-1870 (Hamilton Fish's certification of ratification, via the National Park Service)." Quoted verbatim from the bank record's text field, extracted programmatically, in full as the record carries it.
- Frost, "Of Course He Wants to Vote the Democratic Ticket," activity Station 4 and quick check Document B: the audited TPSP bank record frost-democratic-ticket-1876 in reconstruction.json, whose sourceNote names "A. B. Frost, 'Of Course He Wants to Vote the Democratic Ticket,' 1876 (Harper's Weekly, October 21, 1876; image described)." The bank record's text, its description of the print with the caption dialogue transcribed, is quoted verbatim, extracted programmatically.
- Hayes's First Inaugural Address, March 5, 1877, activity Station 5 and the reading's Section D: a labeled teaching summary, NOT a quotation. The builder attempted to fetch and verify the address from the American Presidency Project transcription during this build; the Project's page for the address could not be retrieved and read through the tools available to this build, so under the unit's edition discipline the address is taught through a summary labeled as a summary on its face in the activity, in the reading, and here. The summary's substance (pacification defined as complete protection of all citizens in their constitutional rights; a moral obligation to establish and protect the rights of the emancipated; honest and efficient local self government; the interests of white and colored people regarded both and equally; universal suffrage resting on universal education) follows the address as carried in the public record of the address consulted in secondary transcriptions during this build, and no sentence of the summary is presented as Hayes's words.
- The deliberate return of the three amendments in this lesson's activity is disclosed on the activity's face, as the skeleton directs: the closing lesson audits the settlement in its own texts.

## Gaps recorded honestly

- Hayes's First Inaugural Address is summarized, not quoted, for the reason stated above. If a later pass retrieves the American Presidency Project transcription and verifies it, the station can be upgraded to a verified quotation with that edition named.
- No portrait of Daniel Henry Chamberlain, Wade Hampton, or David M. Key is used; the lesson's portraits are Hayes and Tilden, and its scenes are the electoral count and the commission.
- The Tilden painting's Commons file is 750 by 750 pixels; the deck crop is enlarged from it and is softer than the Hayes photograph. No larger verified public domain portrait file for Tilden was located during this build; a small Brady photograph on Commons (188 by 300) was rejected as too small.
