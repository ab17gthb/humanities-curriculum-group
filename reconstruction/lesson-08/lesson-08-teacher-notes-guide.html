1876 and the End

Reconstruction · Lesson 8 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Reconstruction, Lesson 8 of 8 — teach it last in the unit, after The Retreat: Courts, Panic, and the Leagues (Lesson 7). Lessons 1 to 7 built the settlement and traced its retreat; this closing lesson runs the disputed election of 1876, the Electoral Commission, the bargain of 1877, and the withdrawal of the last federal troops in April 1877, then audits the whole settlement in two columns — what was settled in the constitutional text, and what its ending left open. The lesson question is the unit question narrowed to its final year, and the lesson ends with the audit students will carry into the unit test.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 7 (the retreat that left only three southern states still Republican, each resting on federal troops) and the unit review and test that follow. Inside the lesson, the activity, The Settlement Audited, works five records in order — the Thirteenth Amendment, the Fourteenth Amendment Section 1, the Joint Resolution proposing the Fifteenth Amendment, the description of Frost's 1876 cartoon, and a labeled teaching summary of Hayes's First Inaugural Address — each quoted or carried as the audited bank presents it. The writing task, Ended, or Only Stood Down, turns the lesson question into a defended judgment on whether 1877 ended Reconstruction or only its enforcement.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: the three Reconstruction amendments from Lessons 2 and 3, the Reconstruction governments from Lesson 4, the economic settlement that left land unredistributed from Lesson 5, and the retreat of 1872 to 1876 from Lesson 7 — amnesty, the narrowed amendment, the depression, and the leagues. Every figure in the lesson is taught as the documents state it — Tilden's popular majority of some quarter of a million votes, twenty disputed electoral votes, the commission's eight to seven rulings, the electoral count of 185 to 184, and the troop withdrawals of April 1877. No image of voter intimidation or violence is reproduced anywhere in this lesson, by design; Frost's cartoon is taught from the bank's description and transcribed caption dialogue, never as a picture.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (three amendments, a cartoon description, and a labeled summary of an inaugural address read as one record and weighed by kind), D2.His.16 (Hayes, Tilden, and the commission's members read as historical actors with stated reasons), D2.Civ.10 (a disputed count, a commission, and a bargain analyzed as the machinery by which a settlement stands or falls), D2.Civ.14 (rights read with the enforcement that made them effective, and with what happened when it was withdrawn); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.8, WHST.9-10.1 (the settlement audited in two columns and the judgment defended from the records).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to state who Hayes and Tilden were and what each man's record made possible; explain why Tilden's popular majority elected no one and why twenty disputed electoral votes decided the presidency; describe the Electoral Commission's design, the loss of Justice Davis, and its eight to seven rulings; state the three assurances of the Compromise of 1877 and read Hayes's inaugural address as the bargain's public face; recount the April 1877 withdrawals at Columbia and New Orleans and the Redeemer order they installed; and audit the settlement in two columns — the three amendments standing in the text, and land, protection, and enforcement left open.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1-3) 5 minutes; the election of 1876 (slides 4-7) 7 minutes; the disputed returns (slides 8-10) 6 minutes; the Electoral Commission (slides 11-13) 6 minutes; the bargain of 1877 (slides 14-16) 6 minutes; April 1877 (slides 17-19) 5 minutes; the audit, settled and open (slides 20-23) 7 minutes; vocabulary, synthesis, and exit (slides 24-28) 8 minutes. The activity takes a second period if the schedule allows. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes, and The Settlement Audited (five stations). The notebook page holds the count, the bargain, and both columns of the audit; the quick check is "Settled in Text: the Thirteenth Amendment read against Frost's voter"; the writing task is "Ended, or Only Stood Down: the claim of 1877 judged from the bank"; the exit ticket has three parts — the two results of 1876, the bargain's three assurances, and the lesson question answered from both columns.

Key vocabulary

Compromise of 1877 · Rutherford B. Hayes · Samuel J. Tilden · Electoral Commission · Redeemers · home rule · Redemption

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title "1876 and the End"; the standfirst — "What Reconstruction settled, and what its ending left open"; the caption — "Counting the electoral vote, 1877. Wood engraving. Library of Congress."

  • Picture: A wood engraving of the counting of the electoral vote in Congress in 1877, from the Library of Congress, showing the Tilden side's lawyer objecting to the vote of Florida.
  • [Open with the count rather than a candidate,] because this lesson is about how an election was settled and what the settling cost. The unit closes here, and the early survey of the course closes with it.
  • A popular vote majority produced no president. A commission produced one by a single electoral vote, a bargain made the result acceptable, and in April 1877 the last federal troops left the South.
  • The amendments stood in the Constitution. Their enforcement stood down. Hold both facts from the first slide.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "What did Reconstruction settle, and what did its ending leave open?"
  • The lesson question is the unit question narrowed to its final year. What did Reconstruction settle is a question the whole unit has been building evidence for, lesson by lesson: abolition in the Thirteenth Amendment, citizenship in the Fourteenth, the vote in the Fifteenth, governments, schools, and churches built on the ground.
  • What did its ending leave open asks students to read 1876 and 1877 against that evidence and name what the ending did not settle.
  • Keep the two halves separate all period. Students who merge them into a single verdict, it worked or it failed, will miss the audit the activity requires, in which the same settlement is settled in its text and open in its enforcement at the same time.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "An election, a count, a bargain, an audit"; the seven section rows, A-G.

  • The map follows the guided notes section by section, so students can find their place in their notes by letter all hour. Section A runs the election of 1876, its candidates and its popular vote.
  • Section B reads the disputed returns and the terror that stood behind them. Section C follows the Electoral Commission and its party line awards.
  • Section D states the bargain of 1877, its terms and its public face. Section E records April 1877, the withdrawal of the last troops and the fall of the last two Reconstruction governments.
  • Section F conducts the settled side of the audit, in constitutional text. Section G closes on the open side, and on the distance the whole unit has measured.

Slide 4 · Section A: The Election of 1876 (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Two reform governors, and a vote the South contested before it was counted."

  • [Open Section A with the personnel before the crisis,] because the crisis is unintelligible without the two men's records. The Republicans, holding the presidency since Lincoln, nominated an Ohio governor who had been a Union general and a reformer in his statehouse.
  • The Democrats, out of the presidency since Buchanan, nominated New York's governor, whose fame rested on prosecuting corruption in his own party's greatest city machine. Neither man campaigned in the modern manner.
  • Their parties did, and in the South the campaign was conducted on ground this unit has covered for seven lessons, where the electorate the Reconstruction Acts created was being driven from the polls by the methods of Lessons 6 and 7.
  • [Name both men's states and records now;] the crisis makes little sense without them.

Slide 5 · The governor and the general

On the slide: the Hayes portrait at right; three bullets — Rutherford B. Hayes came from Ohio; he had served as a Union general and as Ohio's governor three times; he was the Republican nominee in 1876.

  • Picture: Rutherford B. Hayes, photographed by Mathew Brady, held by the Library of Congress.
  • His definition belongs in this teaching in full. Say: "Rutherford B. Hayes: the Ohio Republican, Civil War general and three time governor, who lost the popular vote in 1876, won the presidency through the Electoral Commission's rulings and the Compromise of 1877, and in April 1877 ordered the last federal troops from the statehouses at Columbia and New Orleans."
  • [Teach the career as the bargain's precondition.] Hayes was a plausible president to both sections' negotiators in a way a more partisan Republican would not have been: a war record no one questioned, a reform record in Ohio, and a letter accepting his nomination that had already promised honest local self government in the South if the amendments were observed.
  • Students should register that the man who would order the troops out had announced the principle behind the order before a single vote was counted.

Slide 6 · The prosecutor of the Tweed Ring

On the slide: the Tilden portrait at right; three bullets — Samuel J. Tilden came from New York; he was the Democratic nominee in 1876; he won a majority of the popular vote.

  • Picture: Samuel J. Tilden, painted by Thomas Hicks, held by the National Portrait Gallery.
  • His definition belongs in this teaching in full. Say: "Samuel J. Tilden: the New York Democratic governor and corruption fighter who won the election of 1876's popular vote by some quarter of a million votes and claimed the presidency on the disputed returns, whose party in the end accepted the bargain that seated Hayes rather than risk a contested inauguration."
  • [Teach the popular vote as a fact with two edges.] Tilden's majority of some quarter of a million votes was real, and Democratic newspapers said so daily through the winter. But the electoral college, not the popular vote, elects the president, and Tilden stood one electoral vote short of its majority.
  • His claim rested on the same disputed southern returns his party's southern organizations had produced under the conditions Frost pictured, a weakness in his case his lawyers never escaped. Both edges belong in the lesson, because the commission's work sits between them.

Slide 7 · 250,000 votes, a majority that elected no one

On the slide: the big figure "250,000"; the label — "votes, Tilden's popular majority, by the usual figure"; the italic note — "The election of 1876: a majority that elected no one."

  • 250,000 votes, Tilden's popular majority, by the usual figure.
  • [Pause on the margin,] because it is the election's most misread figure. A quarter of a million votes is a decisive popular majority by the standards of the era, larger than most nineteenth century margins, and it elected no one.
  • The Constitution counts states, not citizens, for this office, and the disputed states held twenty electoral votes against Tilden's one vote shortage.
  • Ask: students to state the arithmetic back precisely: Tilden needed one of the twenty, Hayes needed all twenty. That asymmetry explains everything that followed, the commission's state by state hearings, the ruling pattern's importance, and why the Democratic side, holding the popular majority, was the side threatening to block the count rather than simply claiming victory. That is the count students should carry into the commission's hearings.

Slide 8 · Section B: The Disputed Returns (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Twenty electoral votes, from elections that had not been free."

  • [Open Section B behind the returns,] to the elections that produced them, because a disputed count is only as honest as the voting it counts. Three of the four disputed states still held Republican governments in 1876, and those governments stood behind federal bayonets at their capitals.
  • Their canvassing boards had the legal power to throw out returns from counties where fraud and intimidation were charged, and they used it, for Hayes.
  • The Democratic organizations that had spent years driving Black voters from the polls then cried fraud in their turn. Both charges contained truth, which is why the returns could not settle the election and a political body had to.
  • Keep the two sets of charges separate; students will need both when they build the audit.

Slide 9 · Frost's arithmetic, October 1876

On the slide: four bullets — Harper's Weekly published Frost's cartoon on October 21, 1876; a voter stood at a southern polling place; two men held pistols to his head and ordered him to say he was free; the print is taught by description, as the bank presents it.

  • [Teach the cartoon as evidence,] with the discipline this unit uses for images of the counterrevolution: the print is described in the activity, as the audited bank presents it, and is not reproduced on this screen or in the documents.
  • Its facts are these. A. B. Frost, Harper's Weekly, October 21, 1876, two weeks before the vote: two white men hold pistols to a Black voter's head at a southern polling place, and the man identified as a Democratic Reformer orders him to say he is free, on pain of death.
  • The irony is the lesson. Freedom at that poll existed as a sentence a man could be forced to pronounce.
  • Students meet the record at Station 4 of the activity.

Slide 10 · Two governments, two sets of returns

On the slide: four bullets — Florida, Louisiana, South Carolina, and Oregon sent disputed returns; Republican boards certified Hayes electors; Democratic claimants certified Tilden electors; twenty electoral votes stood in dispute.

  • [Work the mechanics plainly,] because students often imagine a single disputed count. Each disputed state produced two certificates.
  • The Republican canvassing boards of Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina rejected Democratic returns from parishes and counties where intimidation was documented, and certified Hayes electors. Democratic claimants certified Tilden electors from the full returns.
  • In Oregon the dispute was smaller and stranger, one elector who held a federal office and was therefore ineligible, replaced by the Republican governor.
  • Twenty electoral votes in all hung on which certificates Congress would count, and the Constitution did not say whose count governed. That silence, in a House held by one party and a Senate by the other, is what forced the commission. The twenty disputed votes, not the popular margin, are the number this section asks students to hold.

Slide 11 · Section C: The Electoral Commission (divider)

On the slide: the commission portrait at right; the section label and title; the caption — "Counting the electoral vote, 1877. Wood engraving. Library of Congress."

  • Picture: A portrait crop of the wood engraving of the electoral count in Congress, from the Library of Congress. The engraving students saw on the title slide returns here as the section's image.
  • Section C follows the body Congress built to escape its own deadlock. Its design was meant to be above party: equal numbers from each house of Congress, and five justices of the Supreme Court, with the fifteenth place expected to fall to David Davis, the justice both sides counted as independent.
  • The design failed before the hearings opened, when Illinois Democrats helped elect Davis to the United States Senate and he left the commission.
  • His replacement was a Republican justice, and every student in the room can now predict the voting pattern the next slide states.

Slide 12 · Fifteen members, one expected balance

On the slide: four bullets — five senators, five representatives, and five justices made fifteen members; Justice Davis was expected to hold the balance; Illinois sent Davis to the Senate; every ruling went eight to seven for Hayes.

  • The commission's definition belongs in this teaching in full. Say: "Electoral Commission: the fifteen member body Congress created in January 1877, five senators, five representatives, and five Supreme Court justices, which awarded every disputed electoral vote of 1876 to Hayes by votes of eight to seven, giving him the presidency by a single electoral vote, 185 to 184."
  • [Teach the hearings as a trial that behaved as a legislature.] The commission sat through February 1877, heard lawyers argue each state's returns, and voted on every contested question. The votes were eight to seven each time, and the eight were always the Republican members.
  • Democrats called the result a fraud and Hayes Rutherfraud for the rest of his term. Republicans answered that the Democratic certificates rested on terrorized elections.
  • Students do not have to acquit either side. They have to see that a body built to stand above the dispute decided it along the dispute's own line, which is why the count still needed a bargain behind it.

Slide 13 · 185 to 184, a majority of one vote

On the slide: the big figure "185 to 184"; the label — "the electoral count, a majority of one vote"; the italic note — "Every disputed vote awarded to Hayes, February and March 1877."

  • 185 to 184: the electoral count, a majority of one vote.
  • [Stay on the single vote,] because it is the narrowest margin by which the presidency has ever turned on a count. One hundred eighty five to one hundred eighty four means that any one disputed state, any one elector in Oregon, any one ruling reversed, changes the president.
  • The commission's awards gave Hayes every state he needed and no state to spare. Proclaimed in the first days of March, the count left two days for a transfer of power the losing side had threatened to prevent by delay.
  • That the inauguration happened on schedule is not a small fact. It is the fact the bargain of the next section was built to purchase, and its price is the subject of the rest of the lesson.

Slide 14 · Section D: The Bargain of 1877 (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "The count finished, at a price named in assurances."

  • [Open Section D by naming the transaction] the winter had been moving toward. Through February 1877, while the commission heard cases by day, Hayes's allies met southern Democratic leaders in Washington, at the Wormley House hotel among other places.
  • No single signed document records the whole understanding, and honest teaching says so. Its terms are known from the participants' accounts and from what each side then did: the count was allowed to finish, and the assurances given in return were performed within weeks of the inauguration.
  • The cabinet seat was filled at once. The troops were gone by the end of April. The internal improvements support came as promised.
  • Students should learn to read a bargain by its performance when its paper is thin.

Slide 15 · The Wormley House terms

On the slide: four bullets — the Democrats let the count finish; a cabinet seat went to a southerner; federal support was pledged for southern internal improvements; the last troops were withdrawn.

  • The bargain's definition belongs in this teaching in full. Say: "Compromise of 1877: the informal bargain that settled the disputed election of 1876, by which Democratic leaders accepted the Republican Rutherford B. Hayes as president in return for a cabinet seat for a southerner, support for southern internal improvements, and the withdrawal of the last federal troops protecting the Republican governments of South Carolina and Louisiana, ending federal Reconstruction."
  • [Set the three assurances in a row on the record.] A cabinet seat for a southerner: David M. Key of Tennessee became postmaster general. Support for southern internal improvements: federal aid the South's representatives wanted for its railroads and rivers. Withdrawal of the last federal troops: the soldiers still stationed at the statehouses of South Carolina and Louisiana, the force behind the last two Reconstruction governments.
  • Only the third assurance ended anything beyond a patronage question, and students should see why. The first two were the ordinary currency of politics.
  • The third dismantled the settlement's enforcement in the two states where it still stood.

Slide 16 · The public face, March 5, 1877

On the slide: the Hayes portrait at right; four bullets — the inaugural address was the bargain's public face; complete protection of all citizens was pledged; honest local self-government was promised; the troops were not named.

  • Picture: Rutherford B. Hayes, photographed by Mathew Brady, held by the Library of Congress — the same portrait as slide 5, returning for the bargain's public face.
  • The slide reads the bargain's public face: the First Inaugural Address of March 5, 1877. This lesson teaches the address through a labeled teaching summary, printed at Station 5 of the activity, and the summary's substance belongs in this teaching.
  • Hayes named the permanent pacification of the country the supreme subject and defined it as the complete protection of all citizens in their constitutional rights, a moral obligation of the government that had emancipated them. His remedy for the southern states was honest and efficient local self government, with the interests of white and colored people regarded both and equally, and universal suffrage resting on universal education.
  • [Note what the address does not name:] the troops.

Slide 17 · Section E: April 1877, the Troops Withdraw (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Two garrisons recalled, and the last governments fall."

  • Section E is the shortest section of the lesson and the one everything before it purchased. In April 1877 Hayes ordered the remaining federal troops in South Carolina and Louisiana back to their barracks.
  • The orders were administrative, a few lines each, and no battle marked them. What they ended was a decade in which the federal government had stationed soldiers behind southern governments whose electorates could not protect themselves.
  • [Have students hold the scale of the instrument against the scale of the effect:] not an act of Congress, not a constitutional amendment, not a court ruling, but a president's order moving two small garrisons, sufficient because everything else had already been decided in the winter's count and bargain.
  • Two orders, two capitals, one month: the section is deliberately brief.

Slide 18 · Columbia, then New Orleans

On the slide: four bullets — the troops at the Columbia statehouse were recalled; Chamberlain yielded to Hampton; the New Orleans troops were withdrawn days later; no Reconstruction government remained.

  • [Take the two capitals in order,] because the sequence shows the mechanism. In South Carolina, the troops at the Columbia statehouse had stood between two claimants to the governorship, the Republican Daniel Henry Chamberlain and the Democrat Wade Hampton, each inaugurated, each claiming the state.
  • When the soldiers withdrew in April 1877, Chamberlain yielded and Hampton governed.
  • In Louisiana the same arrangement ended the same way at New Orleans, where the Republican government of Stephen B. Packard gave way to the Democratic claimant.
  • With those two falls, no Reconstruction government remained anywhere in the South. The lesson's earlier governments, elected by the electorate of Lesson 4, ended state by state across the decade and here at last by order. The order of the falls follows the order of the bargain's own geography.

Slide 19 · Redemption and home rule

On the slide: three bullets — the Redeemers held every southern state; they governed for low taxes, cheap government, and retrenched schools; the vote remained in law, and its protection was gone.

  • The three names of the new order are taught here as vocabulary in the deck's closing slides, and their full definitions stand there. April's orders are the hinge of the lesson; this vocabulary is what the orders installed across the South.
  • [Teach the words as the victors' own language,] because that is what it is. Redemption is a theological word, rescue from sin, and its users meant it as one: the South rescued, in their telling, from corruption and misrule.
  • Home rule named the condition the bargain delivered, southern governments answerable to no federal supervision.
  • The governments that followed taxed lightly, spent little, cut the schools the Reconstruction constitutions had built, and governed for white supremacy as an open premise. Students should be able to translate all three terms into their plain meanings without adopting their claims.

Slide 20 · Section F: The Settled Account (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Unenforced is not unsettled: the text stood."

  • Section F conducts the audit the activity formalizes, and it begins by insisting on the settled column's reality, because the withdrawal's drama can swallow it.
  • Nothing in the winter of 1876 and 1877 amended the Constitution. No state repealed the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, or Fifteenth Amendments, and no Congress could have repealed them by statute.
  • The sentences Congress wrote between 1865 and 1870 stood in the spring of 1877 exactly as ratified, including the enforcement clauses that arm Congress to act on each of them.
  • A settlement can be unenforced without being unsettled, and the distinction between those two states is the intellectual work of this closing lesson and of the unit test's final blueprint line. [Make students say the distinction aloud] before the activity asks them to write it.

Slide 21 · Settled, in the text

On the slide: four bullets — slavery was abolished, and Congress was armed to enforce it; citizenship came by birth, with due process and equal protection; the vote was protected against racial denial; no settlement of 1877 repealed any of it.

  • [Read the settled account as the unit's own documents give it,] amendment by amendment. Slavery and involuntary servitude abolished everywhere in the United States, Section 2 arming Congress to enforce the abolition.
  • Citizenship defined by birth, and no state permitted to abridge privileges or immunities, deny due process, or deny the equal protection of the laws.
  • The vote protected against denial or abridgment on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude, Congress again armed to enforce it.
  • Beside the text stands the Union itself: the seceded states readmitted and represented, secession answered by war and never revived as a claim. These are not aspirations in the record. They are the law the era left standing when its enforcement ended.

Slide 22 · Section G: What Was Left Open (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Land, protection, and enforcement, left where 1877 left them."

  • Section G writes the audit's other column, and its discipline is the unit's boundary. The open accounts are stated inside the era: land never redistributed, protection withdrawn, institutions left under hostile rule.
  • The amendments' later history, their narrowing and their eventual revival as the law of a later century's movements, is named here in one framing sentence and taught nowhere in this unit, because the survey's first half ends in 1877 and the second half will meet that history in its own time.
  • What students carry out of the unit is the distance the closing documents measure: between a settlement written into the Constitution and a country whose will to enforce it had run out.
  • One framing sentence is the whole of what this lesson says about the century after 1877.

Slide 23 · The open account

On the slide: four bullets — land was never redistributed; protection was withdrawn with the troops; schools and governments passed under hostile rule; enforcement clauses stood, unused.

  • [Itemize the open account from the unit's evidence,] item by item, as the reading does. Land: the redistribution freedpeople demanded from 1865 onward never came, and the economic settlement of Lesson 5 stood untouched by 1877.
  • Protection: the marshals, supervisors, and troops of the enforcement years were withdrawn or stood down, and the voter of Frost's cartoon had no federal officer between himself and the men with pistols.
  • Institutions: the schools and churches freedpeople built, and the governments their votes raised, passed under rulers who campaigned against all three.
  • Enforcement itself: the amendments' clauses remained, awaiting a Congress willing to use them. These are the unit's unanswered claims, stated as plainly as its settlements. Each item answers to a lesson of the unit, and students should be able to name which.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary, words for the count

Slide 26 · Vocabulary, words for the new order

On the slide: three terms with their short forms — Redeemers (the leaders who retook the South), home rule (the slogan of the return), Redemption (the name they gave it). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • The lesson's last three terms, again matching the guided notes exactly, and their full definitions are carried here word for word.
  • Say: "Redeemers: the name the South's white Democratic leaders gave themselves as they retook the southern state governments across the 1870s, presenting their return as the rescue of the region from misrule and governing afterward for low taxes, cheap government, retrenched schools, and white supremacy."
  • Say: "home rule: the Redeemers' slogan for ending federal supervision of southern governments and elections, the restoration of the South to the rule of its white Democratic majorities, the condition the Compromise of 1877 delivered and the phrase under which Redemption governed."
  • Say: "Redemption: the name white southern Democrats gave their overthrow of the Reconstruction governments and their own return to power, accomplished state by state across the 1870s by violence, fraud, economic pressure, and northern weariness, and completed when the last federal troops withdrew in the spring of 1877."
  • Notice that all three are the victors' words for their own victory. Students who can define Redemption must also say who named it, and why the naming is itself evidence.
