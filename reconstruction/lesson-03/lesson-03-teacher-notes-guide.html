Congress Takes Charge

Reconstruction · Lesson 3 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Reconstruction, Lesson 3 of 8 — the lesson that follows the Thirteenth Amendment and the Civil Rights Act fight of Lesson 2. Teach it third in the unit, after the Thirteenth Amendment and the Civil Rights Act fight and before the new political order of Black voting and officeholding in Lesson 4. Between 1866 and 1868 Congress wrote citizenship into the Constitution, rebuilt ten southern governments on a registered electorate that included Black men, and fought the president who struck at that settlement through impeachment to acquittal by one vote.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 2 (the doubts about a statute that sent Congress back to the Constitution are this lesson's starting condition) and Lesson 4 (the voters the Reconstruction Acts registered open that lesson, governing). Inside the lesson, the activity, The Amendment and the Machinery, carries five stations: Section 1 of the Fourteenth Amendment in full, labeled teaching summaries of the Reconstruction Acts and of the 1866 attacks in Memphis and New Orleans, Article I of the impeachment charges quoted from the Avalon Project transcription, and Waud's The First Vote. The writing task, Conqueror, Constitution Maker, or Both?, turns the lesson question into a verdict argued from those stations.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Lessons 1 and 2 complete — presidential Reconstruction, the Black Codes, the Thirteenth Amendment, and the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and its override. The amendment process and the impeachment process are both taught on the slides as they are used, and no other machinery is assumed. No image of the Memphis or New Orleans violence, of any riot, or of any victim is used anywhere in this lesson, in a document, the deck, or these notes, following the unit's image discipline: the violence is taught in text only, stated plainly and briefly.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (an amendment, a statute, articles of impeachment, and two labeled summaries read as one contested settlement), D2.His.9 (a settlement built instrument by instrument from a committee's testimony to a Senate trial), D2.Civ.12 (citizenship defined in the Constitution against a state-by-state refusal), D2.Civ.14 (the two-thirds numbers that run the lesson — the veto override and the conviction vote); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.8, WHST.9-10.1 (a verdict on Congress's power judged from the amendment's text and the Acts' terms).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain how the Joint Committee on Reconstruction heard the South through the winter and spring of 1866 and reported that the seceded states had no claim to representation until Congress set the terms; read Section 1 of the Fourteenth Amendment closely (birthright citizenship, and the three bindings on the states, with the shift from citizens in the first clause to any person in the other two) and state the machinery of Sections 2, 3, and 4; explain why Tennessee returned and the other ten states refused, and how the Memphis and New Orleans attacks and the elections of 1866 returned a Congress able to govern over a veto; state the Reconstruction Acts' working terms in order (provisional governments, five military districts, registration first, and return priced at constitutions, ratification, and approval) and the scale of the electorate they created, 700,000 Black voters on the rolls; and follow the collision over Stanton from the Tenure of Office Act to the removal of February 21, 1868, the eleven articles, and acquittal by one vote.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; the committee hears the South (slides 4–6) 6 minutes; Section 1 and vocabulary (slides 7–10) 8 minutes; the machinery and the refusal (slides 11–12) 4 minutes; violence and the elections of 1866 (slides 13–15) 6 minutes; the Reconstruction Acts (slides 16–19) 8 minutes; the collision over Stanton (slides 20–22) 5 minutes; impeachment and acquittal (slides 23–26) 5 minutes; glance, answering, and exit (slides 27–29) 3 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes, and The Amendment and the Machinery (five stations). The notebook page charts the lesson in one sentence and a chart filled from the lesson; the quick check is "Citizenship Written, Governments Rebuilt"; the writing task is "Conqueror, Constitution Maker, or Both?"; the exit ticket has three parts — the amendment's definition whole, the Acts' three requirements for return in their order, and the conqueror or constitution maker judgment argued from both sides.

Key vocabulary

Fourteenth Amendment · Reconstruction Acts · military districts · Joint Committee on Reconstruction · Tenure of Office Act · Edwin M. Stanton · impeachment

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title "Congress Takes Charge"; the standfirst — "An amendment refused, ten governments rebuilt, and a president tried by one vote."

  • Picture: Andrew Johnson, photographed by the Brady studio in the 1860s, held by the Library of Congress in the Brady-Handy collection — the president this lesson puts on trial in its final section.
  • Open with the president this lesson puts on trial in its final section. Andrew Johnson inherited the presidency and the war's unfinished settlement in April 1865, and Lessons 1 and 2 traced what he built and what Congress answered.
  • This lesson is Congress's turn at full strength. A committee studies the South and reports an amendment. The states refuse it, violence instructs the North, and Congress answers with military districts, a registered electorate that includes Black men, and a statute guarding a single cabinet chair.
  • The lesson ends with the House impeaching the president and the Senate acquitting him by one vote, and students must judge what kind of power Congress was exercising.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "How did Congress rewrite the Constitution and the South's governments between 1866 and 1868, and at what cost?"
  • Read the lesson question aloud and divide it at its comma, because the writing task grades both halves.
  • The first half is double: a Constitution rewritten and governments rebuilt, the amendment of 1866 and the Reconstruction Acts of 1867, and students should keep the two instruments separate in their notes. The second half asks the cost, and the lesson keeps a plain ledger: ten governments set aside, five generals ruling districts, a cabinet officer removed in defiance of a statute, a president tried, and a settlement left resting on voters the federal government had registered and would have to protect.
  • Warn the class against treating impeachment as a separate drama. In this lesson it is the settlement's defense, fought over the War Department that enforced the Acts.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "A committee, an amendment, an army of paper, a trial"; the seven section rows, A–G.

  • Walk the map once, slowly, because it is the lesson's whole argument in seven lines.
  • A committee hears the South and concludes that the 1865 settlement cannot protect freedpeople. Congress writes citizenship into the Constitution, and the states refuse it. Two attacks in 1866 and a national election give Congress the votes to act over a veto.
  • The Acts rebuild the southern governments on a new electorate. The president strikes at the cabinet officer who guards those Acts, and the House and Senate fight the collision out to a single vote.
  • Students should copy the seven section titles into their notes now, because the guided notes follow this order exactly, the activity stations run in the same order, and the exit ticket assumes it throughout.

Slide 4 · Section A: The Committee Hears the South (divider)

On the slide: the Stevens portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Congress refuses the delegations, and builds a committee to hear the South itself."

  • Picture: Thaddeus Stevens of Pennsylvania, photographed by the Brady-Handy studio in the 1860s, held by the Library of Congress — a House leader of the congressional side who sat on the House side of this fight and pressed its hardest terms.
  • Section A is about evidence before it is about law. When Congress returned in December 1865 it refused to seat the delegations the restored states sent, because seating them would have accepted Johnson's settlement as finished.
  • Instead it built an instrument for knowing the South at first hand, a joint committee drawn from both houses, and sent for witnesses.
  • Teach the refusal and the committee together: a Congress that would not seat the South's members was already saying that representation was Congress's to grant, on conditions Congress would set after it had heard the record.

Slide 5 · Testimony and two conclusions

On the slide: the Stevens portrait at right; four bullets — Congress refused to seat the restored delegations; governors, generals, planters, and freedpeople testified; the old order survived in statute and in practice; the states had no claim to representation until Congress set the terms.

  • Picture: Thaddeus Stevens of Pennsylvania, in the Brady-Handy photograph held by the Library of Congress.
  • Give the committee its full definition in the notes, because the test treats it as the settlement's author.
  • Say: "Joint Committee on Reconstruction: the fifteen member committee of senators and representatives formed in December 1865, which took testimony on conditions across the South, drafted the Fourteenth Amendment, and reported that the seceded states had no claim to representation until Congress set the terms of their return."
  • Through the winter and spring of 1866 the committee heard governors, generals, planters, and freedpeople, and the testimony ran one way often enough to govern the report: the restored governments were rebuilding the old order in statute, Black southerners were being denied testimony, arms, and fair labor, and no local authority was protecting them.
  • The report's constitutional claim was as important as its facts. The seceded states, it held, had no right to representation until Congress set the terms, which moved the question of Reconstruction out of the president's hands and into Congress's for the rest of the lesson.

Slide 6 · 15 members of the Joint Committee

On the slide: the big figure "15"; the label — "members of the Joint Committee on Reconstruction"; the italic note — nine representatives and six senators, hearing the South through the winter and spring of 1866 and drafting the Fourteenth Amendment.

  • 15 members of the Joint Committee.
  • Linger on the number, because fifteen is the size of the room where the Fourteenth Amendment was drafted. Nine representatives and six senators heard hundreds of witnesses and read reports from across the South, and a small committee could work as a drafting body in a way two full houses could not.
  • The committee's size also explains the amendment's shape: it is a negotiated instrument, broad where the negotiators agreed, on citizenship and equal protection, and indirect where they did not, on suffrage, which Section 2 prices instead of commanding.
  • When students ask why the amendment does not simply grant Black men the vote, this slide is the honest beginning of the answer: its authors were fifteen men counting the votes of two houses and twenty five state legislatures.

Slide 7 · Section B: Section 1, Citizenship Defined (divider)

On the slide: the Stevens portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "June 1866: the committee's amendment goes to the states."

  • Picture: Thaddeus Stevens of Pennsylvania, in the Brady-Handy photograph held by the Library of Congress.
  • Introduce the amendment as the committee's answer to a legal problem students already know. The Civil Rights Act of 1866, from Lesson 2, was a statute, and a statute can be repealed by a later Congress or read down by a court, and it rested on a contested reading of the Thirteenth Amendment.
  • The Black Codes had shown what state law would do to freedpeople when Congress left the field. So Congress moved the settlement into the Constitution itself, where no legislature could repeal it and no president could veto its enforcement away.
  • Section B teaches the first section word by word, because those words are the ones later generations would litigate, and students meet them again at Station 1 of the activity in their full text.

Slide 8 · Born or naturalized

On the slide: four bullets — all persons born or naturalized in the United States are citizens; they were citizens of the nation and of their state at once; Dred Scott's exclusion was overturned in the text; citizenship was placed beyond any legislature's repeal.

  • Put the amendment's full definition on the board in its joined form, because the unit test quotes its structure.
  • Say: "Fourteenth Amendment: the amendment Congress proposed in June 1866 and the states ratified in 1868, which wrote birthright citizenship into the Constitution, forbade any state to abridge citizens' privileges or immunities, due process, or equal protection of the laws, cut the representation of states that disfranchised male voters, and barred leading former Confederates from office unless Congress removed the disability."
  • Walk the first sentence slowly. Citizenship is defined by birth or naturalization, national and state at once, and the definition is placed in the Constitution precisely so that Dred Scott's exclusion and the Black Codes' local definitions both fail against it.
  • Then mark what the sentence does not do: it says nothing about voting, nothing about office, and nothing about land. The amendment's authors settled belonging first and left the political rights to the machinery of the later sections and, when that machinery was refused, to the Acts of 1867. Students should hear that order as the lesson's plot in miniature.

Slide 9 · Three bindings on the states

On the slide: the Sumner portrait at right; four bullets — no state shall abridge citizens' privileges or immunities; no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process; no person shall be denied the equal protection of the laws; the first clause speaks to citizens, and the other two speak to any person.

  • Picture: Charles Sumner of Massachusetts, daguerreotyped by Southworth and Hawes about 1850 — the Senate's great antislavery voice, whose standard for the settlement was always the fullest one in the room. Sumner is named in this unit's prose only and is not redefined here.
  • Teach the three bindings as three different legal instruments sharing one sentence's grammar. Privileges or immunities belongs to citizens and names a national stock of rights the states may not abridge. Due process belongs to any person and governs how a state may act against life, liberty, or property. Equal protection also belongs to any person and governs the laws' coverage, not only their procedure.
  • The shift from citizens to person between the first clause and the other two is deliberate drafting, and the quick check grades it, so slow down until the class can state it unaided.

Slide 10 · Vocabulary: words for the amendment and its author

On the slide: three terms with their short forms — Fourteenth Amendment (Congress's 1866 amendment: birthright citizenship, and no state to abridge privileges or immunities, due process, or equal protection), Joint Committee on Reconstruction (the fifteen member committee of 1865 and 1866 whose testimony built the case for congressional terms), Reconstruction Acts (the March 1867 laws: ten governments set aside, five military districts, readmission on Congress's terms). The full definitions below match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Run this vocabulary slide as a first check on the lesson's two instruments and their author. Read the third term's full form aloud here, even though the Acts arrive in Section E, because students write better verdicts when the destination is named early.
  • Say: "Fourteenth Amendment: the amendment Congress proposed in June 1866 and the states ratified in 1868, which wrote birthright citizenship into the Constitution, forbade any state to abridge citizens' privileges or immunities, due process, or equal protection of the laws, cut the representation of states that disfranchised male voters, and barred leading former Confederates from office unless Congress removed the disability."
  • Say: "Joint Committee on Reconstruction: the fifteen member committee of senators and representatives formed in December 1865, which took testimony on conditions across the South, drafted the Fourteenth Amendment, and reported that the seceded states had no claim to representation until Congress set the terms of their return."
  • Say: "Reconstruction Acts: the laws of March 1867, passed over Johnson's veto, which set aside the existing governments of the ten unreconstructed states, divided them into five military districts, and required new constitutions guaranteeing Black male suffrage, ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment, and Congress's approval before readmission to representation."
  • Notice what the third definition's verbs do: set aside, divided, required. The Acts are the amendment enforced against refusal, and their price of return, ratification itself, is the hinge between the two halves of this lesson. A student who can already say why Congress made ratification a condition rather than a request has understood Section C before it is taught, and the class should be told so.

Slide 11 · Section C: The Amendment's Machinery and the Refusal (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Representation priced, leaders disabled, debts settled, and ten states say no."

  • Section C teaches the amendment's working parts, the sections students skip when they read only Section 1, and it teaches them as incentives and penalties rather than declarations.
  • Section 2 prices disfranchisement instead of forbidding it. Section 3 disables the rebellion's leadership instead of punishing the rank and file. Section 4 settles the war's debts in both directions, the Union's honored and the Confederacy's voided, including every claim for enslaved people treated as lost property.
  • Then the section turns to the states' answer, because machinery is only as strong as its reception. One state took the bargain at once. Ten refused it in a body, and their refusal is what converts this lesson from constitutional drafting into government building under military authority.

Slide 12 · Machinery, then refusal

On the slide: four bullets — Section 2 cut representation where male voters were denied the vote; Section 3 barred leading former Confederates from office; Section 4 honored the Union debt and voided Confederate debts; Tennessee ratified the amendment and returned, and the other ten states refused it.

  • Work the machinery in order and keep Tennessee's exception in view the whole time. Tennessee ratified in July 1866 and its delegation was seated, proof that the amendment's route back was real and open.
  • The other ten legislatures, most of them chosen under Johnson's restoration, rejected the amendment, several by unanimous or near unanimous votes, encouraged by Johnson's own opposition.
  • Their refusal rested on a calculation worth naming in class: they expected the elections of 1866 or of 1868 to weaken Congress, and they preferred Johnson's settlement, which asked almost nothing of them, to Congress's, which priced Black citizenship into their return.
  • The miscalculation is Section D's subject, because two cities' violence that summer destroyed the political ground the refusal stood on.

Slide 13 · Section D: Violence and the Elections of 1866 (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Two attacks stated plainly, and a Congress returned able to govern over a veto."

  • Set this section's discipline before its facts. The attacks at Memphis and New Orleans are taught in this lesson in text only, plainly and briefly, with no image of either, and the deck follows the same rule as the documents: no picture of the violence appears anywhere in this lesson.
  • What students need is the political anatomy, not spectacle. In both cities the forces of local order, police and former Confederate officers among them, were among the attackers, and the people emancipation had freed found no protection in the governments Johnson's settlement had restored.
  • That is the fact Congress's investigators documented and northern voters weighed in the fall of 1866, and it is the fact that made military Reconstruction arguable to voters who had resisted it a year earlier.

Slide 14 · Memphis and New Orleans

On the slide: four bullets — in Memphis in May 1866, mobs and police attacked Black residents; the attacks killed 46 people, and schools and churches were burned; in New Orleans in July 1866, police attacked a Unionist convention; more than 30 people were killed, and neither city offered local protection.

  • State the two attacks in the reading's own plain terms and then stop. At Memphis in May 1866, white mobs and police attacked Black residents over three days, killing 46 people and burning Black schools and churches, and the city's courts punished no one.
  • At New Orleans that July, police and former Confederates attacked a convention of Unionists and Black suffragists and the crowd outside it, killing more than 30 people.
  • Two features deserve the class's full attention. The targets were political as much as personal: schools, churches, a convention, the institutions of the new citizenship. And the attackers wore the authority of the restored governments, which is why Congress read the reports as evidence about the 1865 settlement itself, not as local crime news.

Slide 15 · The elections of 1866

On the slide: four bullets — congressional investigations printed the testimony; northern newspapers carried the reports into the campaign; Republicans held more than two thirds of both houses; two thirds was the number that could override a veto.

  • Follow the reports into the election, because that is where they did their work. The congressional investigations printed the testimony, northern newspapers carried the casualty lists and the burned schoolhouses into the campaign, and Johnson campaigned in person against Congress's program and damaged his own standing doing it.
  • The elections of 1866 returned Republicans holding more than two thirds of both houses. Translate that fraction into power for the class: two thirds is the constitutional number for overriding a veto, which means that from 1867 forward the president could delay Congress's settlement but could not stop it.
  • Every measure in the rest of this lesson, the Acts and the Tenure of Office Act alike, was passed over Johnson's veto on the strength this election supplied.

Slide 16 · Section E: The Reconstruction Acts (divider)

On the slide: the First Vote engraving at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "March 1867: the amendment plus new governments, under generals until the work is done."

  • Picture: "The First Vote," a wood engraving by Alfred R. Waud published in Harper's Weekly in November 1867, held by the Library of Congress — a registered Black electorate voting in the South under federal supervision, drawn for northern readers as an orderly line of citizens.
  • Frame the Acts as the refusal's price, stated in advance by nobody and imposed in full after the fact. Congress had offered the South readmission for an amendment. The South had refused, and Congress now answered with the amendment plus new governments, new constitutions, and a new electorate, all of it under generals until the work was done.
  • Teach the section as escalation with a stated exit: every requirement in the Acts ends at readmission, and seven states completed the route within sixteen months.

Slide 17 · The Acts at working level

On the slide: four bullets — the ten governments were declared provisional; five military districts stood under Union generals; registration came first and built an electorate that included Black men; return was priced at new constitutions, ratification, and Congress's approval.

  • Give the Acts their anatomy in the order a district commander would have met it.
  • Say: "Reconstruction Acts: the laws of March 1867, passed over Johnson's veto, which set aside the existing governments of the ten unreconstructed states, divided them into five military districts, and required new constitutions guaranteeing Black male suffrage, ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment, and Congress's approval before readmission to representation."
  • The ten governments were declared provisional, the states grouped into districts, and registration opened first, because every later step, conventions, constitutions, legislatures, depended on knowing who the electorate was.
  • Note the definition's precision about the destination: new constitutions guaranteeing Black male suffrage, ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment, and Congress's approval before readmission. Those are the quick check's terms and the writing task's evidence, so drill them as a sequence of three, in order, and have the class recite the sequence back before the next slide divides the work between the districts and the voters.

Slide 18 · The electorate the Acts created

On the slide: the First Vote engraving at right; four bullets — the rolls opened to adult male citizens of a year's residence, Black and white; leading former Confederates were barred from the rolls; conventions were elected to write new constitutions; Waud's First Vote showed the new electorate at the polls in 1867.

  • Picture: "The First Vote," by Alfred R. Waud, Harper's Weekly, November 1867 — the new electorate at the polls, the machinery of the Acts in operation.
  • Now divide the Acts' work between the soldier and the citizen, and put the second vocabulary term of the section into the notes in full.
  • Say: "military districts: the five command zones into which the Reconstruction Acts divided the ten unreconstructed southern states, each under a Union general empowered to register voters, supervise elections and conventions, remove officials, and protect persons and property where civil authority would not."
  • The commanders' powers were administrative before they were military: registering voters, supervising elections and conventions, removing officials who obstructed the work, and protecting persons and property where civil authority would not. Waud's voters are the other half of the system, the electorate those powers existed to create and guard.
  • Students should be able to say whose names went onto the rolls and whose were struck off: adult male citizens of a year's residence, Black and white, with leading former Confederates barred, a franchise broader than any southern state had ever run an election on.

Slide 19 · 700,000 Black voters entered on the rolls

On the slide: the big figure "700,000"; the label — "Black voters entered on the rolls"; the italic note — registered under the Reconstruction Acts in 1867, the electorate that chose the conventions and ratified the amendment.

  • 700,000 Black voters entered on the rolls.
  • Use the number to measure what registration meant in practice. Seven hundred thousand is an estimate built from the district returns, and students should hear it called one, but its scale is not in doubt: within months of the Acts, a Black electorate larger than the entire prewar southern electorate stood on the rolls, and in several states Black voters outnumbered white ones.
  • These were the voters who chose the conventions of 1867 and 1868, wrote public school systems and civil rights into southern constitutions for the first time, and supplied the legislatures that ratified the Fourteenth Amendment.
  • The settlement of 1868 rested on this electorate in the most literal way: remove the registration, and neither the constitutions nor the ratification exists. Lesson 4 opens with these voters governing.

Slide 20 · Section F: The Collision Over Stanton (divider)

On the slide: the Stanton portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "A tenure law, a guarded chair, and a removal in defiance of both."

  • Picture: Edwin M. Stanton, photographed by the Brady-Handy studio in the 1860s, held by the Library of Congress — Lincoln's secretary of war, who held his post into Johnson's administration. The glass plate's cracks and edge loss are visible in the photograph as the record carries them.
  • Section F turns from building governments to guarding them, and its guard is a personnel statute pointed at one chair. Stanton had run the War Department since 1862, and by 1867 the War Department ran the districts: the generals reported through it, the registration proceeded under its orders, and its secretary sided with Congress against the president he served.
  • Johnson understood that removing Stanton would put the Acts' enforcement in hands of his own choosing. Congress understood it too, which is why a law about job tenure became the tripwire of a constitutional crisis.
  • Teach the section as a contest over a single office whose occupant controlled an army of occupation, and the abstraction of the statute will take care of itself.

Slide 21 · The Tenure of Office Act

On the slide: the Stanton portrait at right; four bullets — the act passed on March 2, 1867, over Johnson's veto; the act barred removal of a Senate-confirmed official without the Senate's consent; Stanton held the War Department, and the districts with it; a removal without consent was void under the act.

  • Picture: Edwin M. Stanton, in the Brady-Handy photograph held by the Library of Congress.
  • Read both definitions whole here, because the trial ahead is unintelligible without them.
  • Say: "Tenure of Office Act: the law of March 1867, passed over Johnson's veto, which forbade the president to remove officials confirmed by the Senate without the Senate's consent, the statute Johnson's removal of Secretary of War Stanton defied in the act that triggered his impeachment."
  • Say: "Edwin M. Stanton: Lincoln's secretary of war, who held his post into Johnson's administration, sided with Congress against the president's policy, and whose removal in February 1868 in defiance of the Tenure of Office Act brought the impeachment crisis to its head."
  • Slow down on the mechanism. The act made a removal without the Senate's consent legally void, so a sustained secretary stayed secretary whatever the president ordered, and a successor held a chair the law said was not empty.
  • Stanton was no clerk caught in a crossfire but a principal in the fight, holding the enforcement machinery for Congress's side from inside the cabinet. The collision was therefore about the settlement's command, not etiquette between branches.

Slide 22 · February 21, 1868

On the slide: four bullets — Stanton was suspended in August 1867, during the recess; the Senate refused to concur in January 1868, and Stanton resumed his office; Johnson removed him outright and named Lorenzo Thomas; Stanton held his office, and the House had its dated act.

  • Run the sequence as a dated chain, because the articles of impeachment are built from its links. Johnson suspended Stanton in August 1867, while the Senate was away, as the statute's recess provision arguably allowed.
  • He reported the suspension in December, and in January 1868 the Senate refused to concur, which under the act restored Stanton to his office by operation of law.
  • On February 21 Johnson removed him outright anyway and named Lorenzo Thomas to act in his place, with the Senate in session and its refusal six weeks old. Stanton held his office rooms, Thomas was arrested for violating the act, and the House had before it a clean, dated, written act of defiance against a statute passed for exactly this case.
  • That cleanliness is why the removal, and not the vetoes or the speeches, became Article I.

Slide 23 · Section G: Impeachment, Acquittal, and the Summer of 1868 (divider)

On the slide: the Johnson portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Eleven articles, a spring trial, and a settlement that survives its builder's enemy."

  • Picture: Andrew Johnson, in the Brady studio photograph held by the Library of Congress.
  • Introduce the trial through its arithmetic before its drama. The Constitution the House invoked is the same one the amendment had just enlarged, and its impeachment clauses set a high bar by design: accusation by a House majority, conviction by two thirds of the Senate, with the Chief Justice presiding when a president is tried.
  • Johnson's portrait belongs on this divider because the trial's question was his office as much as his conduct: whether a president could nullify by removal a settlement Congress had enacted over his veto, and what would remain of congressional government if he could.
  • The House answered with eleven articles in March 1868, the first three built on the Stanton removal. The Senate's answer took until May, and it is the next slide's subject.

Slide 24 · The trial

On the slide: four bullets — the House impeached Johnson on February 24, 1868, and eleven articles followed; Article I charged the Stanton removal itself; the Senate tried the charges, with the Chief Justice presiding; seven Republicans broke with their party on the doubts.

  • Name the process whole for the notes at the point where students have watched it happen.
  • Say: "impeachment: the constitutional process by which the House of Representatives charges a federal officer and the Senate tries the charges, used against Andrew Johnson in 1868, when the Senate's vote to convict fell one short of the two thirds the Constitution requires."
  • Teach the trial's evidence fairly. The House managers argued the removal was a plain violation of a valid statute, done with intent, and that a president who could defy tenure law could dismantle the Acts' enforcement at will.
  • Johnson's defenders argued the act was unconstitutional, that it may not even have covered Stanton, a Lincoln appointee, and that policy war with Congress was not a high crime. Seven Republican senators broke with their party on those doubts. On May 16 the Senate voted 35 to 19 on the broadest article, and the trial adjourned after two more votes produced the same count.

Slide 25 · One vote short of conviction

On the slide: the big figure "1"; the label — "vote short of conviction"; the italic note — the Senate voted 35 to convict and 19 to acquit on May 16, 1868, one short of the two thirds the Constitution requires.

  • 1 vote short of conviction.
  • End the narrative on the smallest number in the lesson. One vote is the distance between the settlement's survival under a weakened president and a conviction that would have made Benjamin Wade, the Senate's president pro tempore, president of the United States.
  • Students should hold both readings of the margin at once. For Congress's side, acquittal was defeat by the narrowest count the Constitution allows. For the settlement itself, the practical result ran the other way: Johnson served out his remaining months without again striking at the Acts, Stanton's successor at the War Department left enforcement intact, and the readmission process completed on Congress's terms that summer.
  • The cost ledger of the lesson question belongs here: a presidency diminished, a statute later judged doubtful, and a settlement preserved by the margin of a single senator's doubt.

Slide 26 · Vocabulary: words for the machinery and the crisis

On the slide: four terms with their short forms — military districts (the five command zones over the ten unreconstructed states, each under a Union general), Tenure of Office Act (the March 1867 law barring removal of Senate confirmed officials without the Senate's consent), Edwin M. Stanton (Lincoln's secretary of war, whose removal in February 1868 triggered the impeachment), impeachment (the House charges and the Senate tries; Johnson's conviction vote fell one short in 1868). The full definition below matches the guided notes word for word, and students should copy it exactly.

  • Run the second vocabulary slide backward through the lesson, attaching each term to its section and date. The districts belong to March 1867 and the registration summer; the Tenure Act to the same month's guard duty; Stanton to the February removal; and the process itself should be restated once more in its full form before the exit work.
  • Say: "impeachment: the constitutional process by which the House of Representatives charges a federal officer and the Senate tries the charges, used against Andrew Johnson in 1868, when the Senate's vote to convict fell one short of the two thirds the Constitution requires."
  • Ask: "Can you place the four terms in the order events produced them?" A statute written to protect an officer, the officer whose chair it protected, the districts his department commanded, and the process his removal set off.
  • A student who can run that chain forward and backward owns the lesson's causation, which is what the writing task's verdict will be graded on.

Slide 27 · The lesson at a glance

On the slide: five bullets — testimony became a constitutional text; the text was refused, and violence returned a veto proof Congress; ten governments were rebuilt on 700,000 new Black voters; a removal and a trial ended in acquittal by one vote; by the summer of 1868, the amendment was ratified and seven states were back.

  • Synthesize in the lesson's own order before the exit work. A committee's testimony became a constitutional text; the text was refused; refusal and violence together produced a veto proof Congress; that Congress rebuilt ten governments on a registered electorate of seven hundred thousand Black voters alongside white ones; and when the president struck at the settlement's guard, the guard's removal trial consumed the government for a spring and failed by one vote.
  • Hold the final balance longest. By the summer of 1868 the Fourteenth Amendment was proclaimed ratified and seven states readmitted, so the settlement stood complete on paper and at the ballot box.
  • What it rested on, an electorate federal power had created and would need to keep protecting, is Lesson 4's opening problem.

Slide 28 · Answering the question

On the slide: four bullets — take a position: conqueror, constitution maker, or both; test your position against the refusal, the districts, and the trial; quote Station 1 and Station 4, the amendment and the article; end on what the settlement rested on and what it cost.

  • Model the two verdicts without confiscating either. Conqueror: governments set aside, generals commanding districts, classes of men struck from the rolls, readmission priced at ratification. Constitution maker: the settlement written into the Constitution itself, submitted to states, and executed through registration, elected conventions, and constitutions broader than any the South had known.
  • The both at once verdict, which the evidence best supports, must still be argued, not assumed: students must show where each description fits and name the act they judge decisive.
  • The rubric requires the rejected side stated at its strongest first, and it requires quotation work from Station 1 and Station 4, the amendment and the article, rather than impressions of Johnson gathered from the portraits or of Congress gathered from its majorities.

Slide 29 · Now your turn: answer the question and exit ticket

On the slide: the closing title "Answer the question, and exit"; the two task lines — the exit ticket (the three questions at the end of the guided notes) and the writing task (judge Congress's power from the amendment and two of its tests).

  • Close by sending students to the two pieces of written work and naming what each one checks. The exit ticket checks the amendment's definition whole, the Acts' three requirements for return in their order, and the conqueror or constitution maker judgment argued from both sides.
  • The writing task checks the larger verdict, and it should be introduced now, while the stations are fresh: claim first, Section 1 in its own words, two tests from the refusal, the districts, or the trial, the strongest fact against the position faced directly, and an ending on what the settlement of 1868 rested on and what building it had cost.
  • Tell the class the standard plainly. A verdict that never quotes the amendment or the Acts' terms is an opinion about documents it has not used.
  • The activity is The Amendment and the Machinery (five stations); the notebook page charts the lesson in one sentence; the quick check is Citizenship Written, Governments Rebuilt; the writing task is Conqueror, Constitution Maker, or Both?
