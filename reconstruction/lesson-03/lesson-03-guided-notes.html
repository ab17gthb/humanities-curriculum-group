Guided Notes: Congress Takes Charge: The Fourteenth Amendment and the Reconstruction Acts

Lesson question: How did Congress rewrite the Constitution and the South's governments between 1866 and 1868, and at what cost?

Unit question: What did Reconstruction settle about freedom, citizenship, and the federal Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Fourteenth Amendment: the amendment Congress proposed in June 1866 and the states ratified in 1868, which wrote birthright citizenship into the Constitution, forbade any state to abridge citizens' privileges or immunities, due process, or equal protection of the laws, cut the representation of states that disfranchised male voters, and barred leading former Confederates from office unless Congress removed the disability

Reconstruction Acts: the laws of March 1867, passed over Johnson's veto, which set aside the existing governments of the ten unreconstructed states, divided them into five military districts, and required new constitutions guaranteeing Black male suffrage, ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment, and Congress's approval before readmission to representation

military districts: the five command zones into which the Reconstruction Acts divided the ten unreconstructed southern states, each under a Union general empowered to register voters, supervise elections and conventions, remove officials, and protect persons and property where civil authority would not

Joint Committee on Reconstruction: the fifteen member committee of senators and representatives formed in December 1865, which took testimony on conditions across the South, drafted the Fourteenth Amendment, and reported that the seceded states had no claim to representation until Congress set the terms of their return

Tenure of Office Act: the law of March 1867, passed over Johnson's veto, which forbade the president to remove officials confirmed by the Senate without the Senate's consent, the statute Johnson's removal of Secretary of War Stanton defied in the act that triggered his impeachment

Edwin M. Stanton: Lincoln's secretary of war, who held his post into Johnson's administration, sided with Congress against the president's policy, and whose removal in February 1868 in defiance of the Tenure of Office Act brought the impeachment crisis to its head

impeachment: the constitutional process by which the House of Representatives charges a federal officer and the Senate tries the charges, used against Andrew Johnson in 1868, when the Senate's vote to convict fell one short of the two thirds the Constitution requires

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher

Teach this lesson third in the unit, after the Thirteenth Amendment and the Civil Rights Act fight and before the new political order of Black voting and officeholding, in one class period of about 50 minutes. It pairs with the lesson activity, whose five stations carry Section 1 of the Fourteenth Amendment, summaries of the Reconstruction Acts and of the 1866 attacks in Memphis and New Orleans, Article I of the impeachment charges quoted from the Avalon Project transcription, and Waud's The First Vote, and with the unit writing task. Students need presidential Reconstruction, the Black Codes, and the Civil Rights Act of 1866 from Lessons 1 and 2, named here as prior knowledge. The lesson matches the Common Core literacy standards for history and social studies on citing textual evidence and determining central ideas, and the C3 Framework on civics and history. Students will learn how the Joint Committee built the Fourteenth Amendment, how the Reconstruction Acts rebuilt the southern governments on a new electorate, and how the fight over Stanton's removal carried the settlement through impeachment to acquittal by one vote. Materials: the reading, these notes, and the activity stations.

Exit Ticket Key

1. Fourteenth Amendment: the amendment Congress proposed in June 1866 and the states ratified in 1868, which wrote birthright citizenship into the Constitution, forbade any state to abridge citizens' privileges or immunities, due process, or equal protection of the laws, cut the representation of states that disfranchised male voters, and barred leading former Confederates from office unless Congress removed the disability.

2. A new constitution guaranteeing Black male suffrage, ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment by the new legislature, and Congress's approval before readmission to representation.

3. For constitution maker: Congress wrote its settlement into the Constitution itself and made ratification the price of return, and it rebuilt state governments through elected conventions and a registered electorate. For conqueror: it set aside elected governments, divided ten states into military districts under generals, and barred classes of citizens from the rolls. Credit either judgment, or the judgment of both at once, argued from the record.
