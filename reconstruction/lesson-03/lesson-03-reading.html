Congress Takes Charge: The Fourteenth Amendment and the Reconstruction Acts

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Committee Hears the South

Congress returned to Washington in December 1865 and refused to seat the senators and representatives the restored southern states had sent. Instead it created the Joint Committee on Reconstruction, fifteen members drawn from the Senate and the House, and gave the committee the South to study. Through the winter and spring of 1866 witnesses came before it, governors, generals, planters, and freedpeople, and their testimony described a region where the old order had survived the war in law and in daily practice. The committee drew two conclusions from what it heard. The seceded states had no claim to representation until Congress set the terms of their return, and the settlement Johnson had built in 1865 could not protect the people emancipation had freed.

B. Section 1: Citizenship Defined

In June 1866 the committee reported a constitutional amendment, and Congress sent it to the states. Its first section rewrote the Constitution's answer to the question of who belonged to the country. All persons born or naturalized in the United States, the section declared, are citizens of the United States and of the state where they reside. That sentence overturned the Supreme Court's ruling in Dred Scott that Black Americans could not be citizens, and it placed citizenship beyond the reach of any state legislature or of a later Congress. The section then bound the states directly. No state could abridge the privileges or immunities of citizens of the United States. No state could deprive any person of life, liberty, or property without due process of law, or deny any person the equal protection of the laws.

C. The Amendment's Machinery and the Refusal

Sections 2 through 4 supplied the amendment's machinery. Section 2 cut the congressional representation of any state that denied the vote to its adult male citizens, a penalty written to press the South toward Black suffrage without yet commanding it. Section 3 barred leading former Confederates from federal and state office unless Congress removed the disability by a two thirds vote. Section 4 guaranteed the Union's war debt and voided every Confederate debt and every claim for the loss of enslaved people. Tennessee ratified the amendment in July 1866, and Congress readmitted its delegation at once. The other ten former Confederate states refused, most of them by overwhelming votes, and by early 1867 the amendment stood unratified and the price of a state's return stood unsettled.

D. Violence and the Elections of 1866

While the amendment hung, events in two southern cities showed the North what the committee's witnesses had described. In Memphis in May 1866, white mobs and police attacked Black residents over three days, killing 46 people and burning Black schools and churches. In New Orleans that July, police and former Confederates attacked a convention of Unionists and Black suffragists and the crowd gathered outside it, killing more than 30 people. This lesson states both attacks plainly and briefly and pictures neither one. Northern voters read the reports beside the southern legislatures' refusals, and the elections of 1866 returned a Congress in which Republicans held more than two thirds of both houses, strong enough at last to govern over the president's veto.

E. The Reconstruction Acts

Congress answered in March 1867 with the Reconstruction Acts, passed over Johnson's veto. The acts set aside the existing governments of the ten unreconstructed states as provisional only and divided those states into five military districts, each commanded by a Union general. The generals and the officers under them registered a new electorate, every adult male citizen of a year's residence, Black and white, with leading former Confederates barred from the rolls. That electorate would choose conventions, and the conventions would write new state constitutions guaranteeing Black male suffrage. A state could return to representation only when its constitution had been framed and approved, its legislature had ratified the Fourteenth Amendment, and Congress had agreed to readmit it. Registration began that spring, and some 700,000 Black voters entered the rolls across the South.

F. The Collision Over Stanton

Congress guarded its settlement against the president in the same season. The Tenure of Office Act, passed over Johnson's veto on March 2, 1867, forbade the president to remove an official confirmed by the Senate without the Senate's consent. Its test case was already sitting in the cabinet. Edwin M. Stanton, Lincoln's secretary of war, had sided with Congress against Johnson's policy, and his hold on the War Department kept the army that enforced the Reconstruction Acts out of the president's sole control. Johnson suspended Stanton in August 1867 while the Senate was out of session. When the Senate refused in January 1868 to approve the suspension, Stanton resumed his office under the act. On February 21, 1868, Johnson removed him outright and named a successor.

G. Impeachment, Acquittal, and the Summer of 1868

The House answered within days. On February 24, 1868, it voted to impeach Johnson for high crimes and misdemeanors, and in March it adopted eleven articles, the first of them charging the removal of Stanton itself as a violation of the Tenure of Office Act and of the Constitution. The Senate tried the charges through the spring, with the Chief Justice presiding. On May 16 the Senate voted on the broadest article, 35 to convict and 19 to acquit, one vote short of the two thirds that conviction required. Johnson served out his term, weakened, and the settlement Congress had built stood untouched by him. That summer the Fourteenth Amendment was proclaimed ratified, and seven reconstructed states had been readmitted on the terms the acts had set.
