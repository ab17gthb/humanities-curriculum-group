Teacher Notes

Reconstruction · Lesson 1 · After Appomattox: Presidential Reconstruction

These are the speaker notes from the slide deck, in slide order, set for printing. The deck itself carries the main ideas in a few fragments per slide; the teaching is here. Print this and keep it beside you while you teach. Each entry is headed by its slide number and shows what is on that slide, so you can find your place without flipping back to the screen. Vocabulary definitions match the student guided notes word for word.

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: After Appomattox: Presidential Reconstruction · Pardons, a bureau, the Black Codes, and freedpeople stating their terms, 1865

This lesson opens the Reconstruction unit in the weeks after Appomattox. The war was won, and nothing about the peace had been decided. Abraham Lincoln was assassinated in April 1865, and Andrew Johnson of Tennessee, a tailor turned senator who had stayed loyal when his state seceded, became president. Johnson moved at once, while Congress was out of session, offering pardons and restored property to most former Confederates and seating southern governments on three required acts. Against that speed worked one federal agency, the Freedmen's Bureau, and four million freedpeople stating their own terms for freedom in petitions, letters, and labor. The restored governments answered with the Black Codes. That contest, set in a single fall, is the whole unit in miniature.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: What did Johnson's restoration give back to the South, and what did it leave freedpeople to fight for? · Unit question: What did Reconstruction settle about freedom, citizenship, and the federal Union, and what did it le

Read the question slowly, because its two halves organize the whole lesson. The first half asks what Johnson's restoration gave back to the South: pardons, property, land, and governments seated on a president's terms, with former Confederate leaders restored to office. The second half asks what it left freedpeople to fight for: the ballot, equal rights in court, land of their own, fair wages, safety for their families, and schools for their children. Keep both halves in view all hour. A student who can answer only the first half knows the terms and misses the lesson, because the unit runs on the second half, on the petitions and letters in which freedpeople stated freedom's terms themselves, and on the Black Codes that answered them. The unit question stands beneath today's question on the slide, and it stays open all unit.

Slide 4 · Section A: The President the War Left Behind (divider)

On the slide: The President the War Left Behind · Appomattox on April 9, Lincoln dead on April 15, and every question about the peace open.

Begin with the speed of events, because students often merge the whole spring of 1865 into one moment. Lee surrendered at Appomattox on April 9. Five days later John Wilkes Booth shot Lincoln, who died the next morning. The presidency passed to Andrew Johnson, the only southern senator who had refused to leave the Union with his state, a former tailor who had taught himself to read as an apprentice and risen office by office. Lincoln had chosen him for the 1864 ticket as a War Democrat, to carry Unionist southerners and Democrats into the Republican coalition. Teach the three unanswered questions on the reading's first page as the lesson's agenda. Terms, meaning, enforcement: every section that follows answers one of the three, and the answers do not agree with each other.

Slide 5 · A successor nobody had voted on for the peace

On the slide: A successor nobody had voted on for the peace · ▪ A tailor turned senator from Tennessee / ▪ Stayed loyal when his state seceded / ▪ Lincoln's War Democrat on the 1864 ticket / ▪ Terms, meaning, enforcement: all unde

Work the succession as a constitutional fact with political consequences. Johnson owed his place to a ticket built for wartime breadth, not to any mandate on the peace, and he had never been elected president in his own right. His instincts ran toward leniency for white southerners and suspicion of federal power used on freedpeople's behalf, and both instincts are visible in the terms he set within weeks. Ask students to hold two facts together: the Constitution made him president with full authority, and the settlement he built was his personal construction, never voted by Congress and never put to any electorate that included the people it governed most closely. That combination, lawful authority used to settle a question alone, is what Congress returned in December to contest.

Slide 6 · Section B: The Amnesty Proclamation (divider)

On the slide: The Amnesty Proclamation · Andrew Johnson, photographed / about 1870 to 1880.

Section B is the settlement's engine, and it deserves slow teaching. On May 29, 1865, Johnson issued his Amnesty Proclamation. Most former Confederates could swear loyalty, receive pardon, and recover their property, with one great exception written into the offer: no one could recover the people he had enslaved, because slavery itself was dead. Fourteen classes stood outside the general pardon, high Confederate officers and men whose taxable property exceeded twenty thousand dollars among them. Those men applied to Johnson personally. He pardoned freely, thousands of applications granted, so the exception that looked like severity worked in practice as a ceremony of personal submission that restored the South's leading men one signature at a time. Name that pattern plainly for students: the proclamation punished rebellion in form and forgave it in practice.

Slide 7 · Pardon first, terms after

On the slide: Pardon first, terms after · ▪ May 29, 1865: swear loyalty, recover property / ▪ No one recovers the people he enslaved / ▪ Provisional governors, conventions called / ▪ The electorate: the white South of before the w

The portrait is Andrew Johnson, photographed about 1870 to 1880, in the copy held on Wikimedia Commons. Use the divider to fix the machinery of section B in students' minds before the detail slides. Johnson also appointed provisional governors for the seceded states, and the governors called conventions. The electorate for those conventions was the white electorate as it had stood before the war, its members restored by the pardons. Pause on the composition of that electorate, because it decides everything downstream. The conventions that would void secession, abolish slavery, and repudiate debts were chosen by the men who had made secession, and no Black southerner voted on the terms of Black freedom. Students should be able to state that fact without prompting by the end of the hour.

Slide 8 · 14 classes excepted

On the slide: 14 · classes excepted from the general pardon · High officers and the wealthy applied to Johnson personally · He pardoned freely, by the thousands

Teach the fourteen classes as a designed list, not a footnote. Johnson excepted the rebellion's leadership and its wealth, civil and military officers at the top and men of property above twenty thousand dollars, precisely the men whose submission would be noticed. The personal application was the point: each great planter or general had to ask this Tennessee tailor president for grace individually, and the asking acknowledged the federal authority the war had established. Then teach the turn. Johnson granted the applications freely, thousands of them, and the ceremony that was meant to humble the leadership restored it instead, to property, to the ballot, and within the year to office. A pardon is a legal instrument, and students should track what each pardon returned: land, standing, and eligibility, everything except the enslaved people whose freedom was the war's irreversible fact.

Slide 9 · The exception that restored the leaders

On the slide: The exception that restored the leaders · ▪ The leadership and the wealth, excepted by name / ▪ Each man applies to the president personally / ▪ The asking acknowledges federal authority / ▪ The granting restores lan

Let the number stand alone. Fourteen excepted classes sounds like a hard peace, and northern readers in May 1865 could read the proclamation as severity toward the rebellion's leaders. The slide asks students to hold the number against the practice that followed it: personal applications, freely granted, by the thousands. Numbers in this unit repay that treatment repeatedly, fifty dollars in the Vagrant Law, eleven thousand six hundred eighty dollars in Anderson's arithmetic, sixteen thousand acres on Edisto Island. Teach students to ask of every figure in a document who set it, who paid it, and what it bought. The fourteen classes bought submission in form. What they returned was the old leadership, pardoned and eligible, in time to write the Black Codes and take seats in the restored governments.

Slide 10 · Section C: Three Acts and a Claim (divider)

On the slide: Three Acts and a Claim · Void secession, abolish slavery, repudiate the debts. Nothing else was required.

Section C states the only terms Johnson required of the states themselves, and there are exactly three. Each convention had to declare its ordinance of secession void, abolish slavery within the state, and repudiate the debts the state had contracted to fight the United States. Nothing else was required: no Black suffrage, no equal rights of testimony or property, no protection for freedpeople written anywhere in the conditions. Underneath the three acts stood Johnson's constitutional claim, that the states had never legally left the Union, so restoration was a president's act of recognition rather than Congress's act of reconstruction. Have students state the claim and its consequence in one breath: if the states never left, Congress had no terms to set, and the president's recognition was the whole settlement.

Slide 11 · Three acts, and a long list of absences

On the slide: Three acts, and a long list of absences · ▪ Void the secession ordinance / ▪ Abolish slavery in the state / ▪ Repudiate the war debts / ▪ Not required: the ballot, testimony, land

Walk the three acts in order and classify each by what it settled. Voiding secession settled the past, a formal admission that the ordinance had been unlawful. Abolishing slavery settled the institution, in state law, months before the Thirteenth Amendment's ratification completed the work nationally. Repudiating the war debts settled the money, refusing Confederate creditors and bondholders any claim on the restored states. Then walk what is absent, because the absence is the lesson. The terms say nothing about the freedpeople who made up much of these states' populations: not the ballot, not testimony, not land, not wages. By the fall of 1865 elected governments replaced the provisional ones across the South, and the speed was the strategy. Every month of self government under Johnson's terms made them harder for Congress to unsettle in December.

Slide 12 · Section D: The Freedmen's Bureau at Work (divider)

On the slide: The Freedmen's Bureau at Work · Oliver Otis Howard, the Bureau's / commissioner, by Mathew Brady.

The portrait is Oliver Otis Howard, the Freedmen's Bureau's commissioner, a Union general who had lost an arm in the war. Section D introduces the one federal institution working on freedpeople's side of the line. Congress created the Bureau on March 3, 1865, before Appomattox, formally the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, placed it in the War Department, and staffed it largely with army officers. Its first work was relief on a large scale: rations issued to destitute freedpeople and to white refugees alike, hospitals run in districts where war had broken the food supply and the medical system together. Teach the breadth deliberately. The Bureau was the first federal relief agency in American history, and it fed the hungry of both races, a fact its enemies rarely mentioned and its record supports.

Slide 13 · Relief, and then the hard work

On the slide: Relief, and then the hard work · ▪ Created March 3, 1865, in the War Department / ▪ Rations and hospitals, for both races / ▪ Contracts supervised, Bureau courts sitting / ▪ Marriages legalized, schools built

The Bureau's harder work was legal and economic, and it put the agent in an impossible position that students should be able to describe. He supervised the labor contracts under which freedpeople now worked for wages, trying to see them written fairly and honored at settlement. He sat in Bureau courts hearing complaints that state courts, which would not take a freedperson's testimony against a white man, could not hear. He legalized the marriages of couples slavery had never permitted to marry in law, and he helped freed communities raise schools. On one side stood planters who wanted the old discipline restored; on the other, freedpeople who wanted wages paid, land, and schools. Behind the agent stood a handful of soldiers, or none. That exposure, federal purpose without federal force, is the Bureau's story in every county it entered.

Slide 14 · Section E: Freedom Reaches Texas (divider)

On the slide: Freedom Reaches Texas · Gordon Granger, from the Brady Handy / collection, Library of Congress.

Section E is the lesson's proof that freedom was an event, not only a text. The Emancipation Proclamation had declared freedom in Texas in 1863, but no Union force stood in Texas to enforce it, and enslaved Texans worked on. Freedom arrived when federal power arrived in person: Union troops reached Galveston in June 1865, the last Confederate army in the field had surrendered there on June 2, and on June 19 General Gordon Granger issued General Orders No. 3. Teach the sequence as the general rule of emancipation, not a Texas exception. Across the South, freedom's effective date was the date an agent, a regiment, or an order reached a place and could be enforced there. Black Texans understood the date's weight at once and marked June 19 as Emancipation Day, later Juneteenth.

Slide 15 · An order with two halves

On the slide: An order with two halves · ▪ Galveston, June 19, 1865: all slaves are free / ▪ Absolute equality of personal rights / ▪ Employer and hired labor, the new connection / ▪ Remain home, work for wages, no idleness

The portrait is Gordon Granger, from the Brady Handy collection at the Library of Congress. Read the order itself with students, because it is short enough to hold whole and it carries the lesson in miniature. It announces that all enslaved people in Texas are free, in accordance with the Emancipation Proclamation. It states an absolute equality of personal rights and rights of property between former masters and slaves. And it converts the old connection into a new one, employer and hired labor. Then, in the same document, it advises the freedmen to remain quietly at their present homes and work for wages, and warns that they will not be collected at military posts or supported in idleness. Freedom and instruction arrive in one order, from one headquarters, and students should name both halves without flinching from either.

Slide 16 · June 19, 1865

On the slide: June 19, 1865 · freedom enforced in Texas, two years after it was proclaimed · Black Texans marked the day as Emancipation Day, later Juneteenth

Let the date stand alone, because Black Texans made it stand alone in memory. June 19, 1865 was two and a half years after the Emancipation Proclamation took effect on paper and more than two months after Appomattox. Ask students what the gap measures. It measures the distance between a right declared and a right enforced, and that distance is one of the unit's recurring measures: it reappears in the Bureau agent's county, in the Edisto petitioners' lost land, and in every later lesson where a constitutional text waits on federal will. Juneteenth began as the celebration of the day enforcement arrived. Teach it that way, as a freed community's own dating of its freedom, chosen over the paper date the government preferred, because the people freed knew which date had changed their lives.

Slide 17 · Section F: The Black Codes (divider)

On the slide: The Black Codes · The restored legislatures rebuilt labor control in statute form, in public, in print.

Section F is the restored South's answer, and it came in two parts: personnel and law. The personnel came first. Southern voters returned former Confederate officers and officials to governorships, legislatures, and seats in Congress, including men who had stood in the proclamation's excepted classes and taken personal pardons. The law followed in the fall and winter of 1865 and 1866, when the restored legislatures passed the Black Codes, statutes rebuilding control of Black labor and movement. Teach the connection between the two parts without euphemism and without exaggeration. The codes were not secret plots; they were public legislation, debated and printed, by governments the president had recognized, and northern newspapers reprinted them as evidence of what Johnson's settlement had seated.

Slide 18 · Mississippi's Vagrant Law, at working level

On the slide: Mississippi's Vagrant Law, at working level · ▪ Over eighteen, no lawful employment: a vagrant / ▪ Assembly and association reached as well / ▪ Fine, jail, and hiring out by the sheriff / ▪ Black children apprenticed

Work Mississippi's Vagrant Law at the level of the person it governed. Its second section deemed vagrants all freedmen over eighteen found, from the second Monday of January 1866, with no lawful employment or business, and it reached assemblies and associations as well as idleness. The penalty was a fine of up to fifty dollars, a sum a freedman signing a yearly contract rarely held in cash, with jail at the court's discretion, and a freedman who could not pay could be hired out by the sheriff to whoever paid the fine for the least labor. Other code provisions apprenticed Black children to employers, often their parents' former enslavers, without parental consent. State the mechanism plainly: slavery was abolished, and its labor discipline was being rebuilt one statute at a time, in fines, courts, and sheriffs' sales of labor.

Slide 19 · $50

On the slide: $50 · the fine for a freedman convicted of vagrancy in Mississippi · A sum a contract laborer rarely held in cash · Unpaid, he was hired out by the sheriff

Let the fine stand alone for a full slide, the way the fee schedule stood alone in earlier units' statutes. Fifty dollars was the ceiling for a freedman convicted of vagrancy in Mississippi, and the figure only bites when students price it. A freedman working under an annual contract might be promised wages in the range Anderson earned monthly in Ohio, twenty five dollars, settled at year's end after deductions. A fifty dollar fine, with costs, could exceed everything he was owed, and the law supplied the consequence: hiring out by the sheriff. Ask the question the northern press asked of the whole code. If a state may fine a man for having no employer and then assign his labor to whoever pays the fine, what part of slavery has actually been abolished beyond its name and its sale block? Let students answer from the text.

Slide 20 · Section G: Freedpeople Answer (divider)

On the slide: Freedpeople Answer · A. R. Waud's drawing of the Freedmen's Bureau, / Harper's Weekly, 1868. Library of Congress.

The print is Alfred Waud's drawing of the Freedmen's Bureau, published in Harper's Weekly in 1868 and held by the Library of Congress; it pictures a Bureau officer standing between armed white men and freedpeople, which is the Bureau's position drawn as a scene. Section G turns from the governments to the governed, because freedpeople were not waiting to be described by other people's documents. From the first fall they wrote their own record: petitions to the president, letters to former enslavers, conventions in the next lesson, schools and churches after that. Teach section G as freedpeople setting terms, in the two documents the activity prints in full. The Edisto committee writes collectively, as a community defending land. Anderson writes alone, as a workingman pricing his own past. Both documents argue, and both deserve to be read as arguments.

Slide 21 · Edisto Island: labor and loyalty

On the slide: Edisto Island: labor and loyalty · ▪ Settled land, certificates in hand / ▪ Restoration returns it to pardoned owners / ▪ "We have made these lands what they are" / ▪ The ask: purchase land, hold it as his own

Teach the Edisto petition as a legal argument built from two grounds, labor and loyalty. The petitioners, a committee of three freedmen, Henry Bram, Ishmael Moultrie, and Yates Sampson, wrote in October 1865 because Johnson's restoration was returning the Sea Island land they had settled under wartime orders to its pardoned former owners. On labor, they claim the land by the work that made it valuable: we have made these lands what they are. On loyalty, they invert the settlement's politics: they were the only true and loyal people found in possession of these lands, against owners who had rebelled and now begged forgiveness. Their ask is precise, a provision by which every man can purchase land and hold it as his own. The answer they received is the section's last fact: the land was restored anyway.

Slide 22 · Anderson's arithmetic

On the slide: Anderson's arithmetic · ▪ Dayton, Ohio: $25 a month, children in school / ▪ The test of sincerity: pay the back wages / ▪ $11,680 for 32 years and 20 / ▪ Safety for his daughters, schools for his children

Anderson's letter is the freedman's half of the labor negotiation, conducted with complete politeness and complete firmness. Living free in Dayton, Ohio, in August 1865, earning twenty five dollars a month with his children in school, he answered his former enslaver's invitation to return by asking the questions an employer must answer: what wages, what proof of sincerity, what safety, what schools. His test is arithmetic. Thirty two years of his labor and twenty of Mandy's, priced at his Ohio wage, come to eleven thousand six hundred eighty dollars, less clothing and three doctor's visits, and until that account is settled he can have little faith in promises for the future. Then the personal conditions: whether his daughters would be safe, and whether schools stood open for his children. No return is offered on any other terms.

Slide 23 · Given back, and left to fight for

On the slide: Given back, and left to fight for · ▪ Given back: pardons, property, land, governments / ▪ Left to fight for: land, wages, the ballot / ▪ Left to fight for: safety, schools, equal rights / ▪ December 1865: Congress r

Build the synthesis as the answer to the lesson question, in the question's own two halves. Given back to the South: pardons and property for most former Confederates, personal pardons for the excepted leaders, governments seated on three acts by the old white electorate, ex-Confederate officers in office, and the land itself, restored even where freed families had settled it under federal orders. Left to freedpeople to fight for: land of their own, wages honestly paid and back wages acknowledged, the ballot, equal rights in court, safety for their daughters, and schools for their children, against Black Codes rebuilding labor control by statute. The one federal instrument on their side was the Bureau, working county by county with few soldiers. Then state the date that closes the lesson: December 1865, when Congress returned to a settlement already built, and had to decide whether it would stand.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: the period, the president, the plan, the pardon

On the slide: The period, the president, the plan, the pardon · Reconstruction · the period from 1865 to 1877 · Andrew Johnson · the Tennessee tailor and Unionist Democrat · presidential Reconstruction · the restoration program Johnso

Copy these definitions exactly. Reconstruction: the period from 1865 to 1877 in which the United States decided how the seceded states would return to the Union, what freedom would mean for the four million people emancipation had freed, and how much of the settlement the federal government would enforce Andrew Johnson: the Tennessee tailor and Unionist Democrat who became vice president under Lincoln in 1864 and president on Lincoln's assassination in April 1865, whose lenient restoration plan and vetoes of Congress's measures set the first terms of Reconstruction and brought him to impeachment in 1868 presidential Reconstruction: the restoration program Johnson ran from April 1865, offering amnesty and pardons to most former Confederates, letting the southern states organize governments once they abolished slavery, repudiated their secession ordinances and Confederate debts, and left the terms of Black freedom to those governments Amnesty Proclamation: Johnson's proclamation of May 29, 1865, which pardoned most former Confederates who swore a loyalty oath and restored their property except in enslaved people, while excepting fourteen classes, including high officers and the wealthy, who had to apply to him personally for pardon

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: the bureau, the codes, the freedman

On the slide: The bureau, the codes, the freedman · Freedmen's Bureau · the federal agency Congress created in March 1865 · Black Codes · the laws the restored southern governments passed in 1865 and 1866 · Jourdon Anderson · the Tenn

Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly, and students should copy them as written. Freedmen's Bureau: the federal agency Congress created in March 1865, formally the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, which fed the hungry of both races, ran hospitals and courts, supervised labor contracts, legalized marriages, and built schools across the South, the first federal relief agency in American history Black Codes: the laws the restored southern governments passed in 1865 and 1866 to control Black labor and movement, imposing vagrancy penalties, forced apprenticeships of Black children, and contract rules that tied freedpeople to plantations and denied them equal rights of testimony, arms, and property Jourdon Anderson: the Tennessee freedman who in August 1865 answered his former enslaver's invitation to return with a dictated letter demanding back wages for thirty two years of his own labor and twenty for his wife's, and asking whether his daughters would be safe and his children schooled, freedom's terms stated by a freedman himself

Slide 26 · Now your turn: the settlement on paper

On the slide: The settlement on paper · For each record, state whose freedom it enlarged or narrowed and by what instrument. Then judge which record a freed family in December 1865 would have felt most in its daily life, and defend yo

Close by returning to the lesson question and handing the lesson to the work. The activity, The Settlement on Paper, runs five stations through the evidence: the restoration terms as a labeled teaching summary, Mississippi's Vagrant Law, the Edisto petition, Anderson's letter, and General Orders No. 3, ending in a table of whose freedom each record enlarged or narrowed and a verdict on which record a freed family would have felt most. The notebook page asks students to rebuild six pieces of the story with evidence. The quick check tests the Vagrant Law's categories and penalties against their background. The writing task weighs what was given back against what was left to fight for, closing on freedpeople's own stated terms. The exit ticket has three parts: the three acts stated, the Bureau's position explained, and the lesson question answered with two restorations and two fights.
