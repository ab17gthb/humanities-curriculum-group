Writing Task: Given Back, and Left to Fight For

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: what did Johnson's restoration give back to the South, and what did it leave freedpeople to fight for? A strong answer does more than list the pardons. It states the terms exactly, works through the Black Codes at the level of the freedman facing a fine he could not pay, and weighs what the Bureau could do against what the Edisto petitioners and Jourdon Anderson asked for and did not get. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading, stations, and documents. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank wording, except where a bank item quotes a document.

Evidence Bank

1. Johnson's Amnesty Proclamation of May 29, 1865 pardoned most former Confederates who swore loyalty and restored their property except in enslaved people. Fourteen classes, including high officers and men worth over twenty thousand dollars in taxable property, had to apply personally, and Johnson pardoned freely, by the thousands. (Lesson reading, section B.)

2. Each state convention, elected by the prewar white electorate alone, had to void its secession ordinance, abolish slavery, and repudiate its war debts. No condition required Black suffrage or equal rights, Congress set none of the terms, and by the fall of 1865 elected governments stood across the South. (Lesson reading, sections B and C.)

3. The Freedmen's Bureau, created March 3, 1865 and formally the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, fed the hungry of both races, ran hospitals and courts, supervised labor contracts, legalized marriages, and built schools, its agents standing between planters and freedpeople with few soldiers behind them. (Lesson reading, section D.)

4. Mississippi's Vagrant Law deemed every freedman over eighteen found without lawful employment a vagrant, fined him up to fifty dollars with jail, and hired out those who could not pay, while other Code provisions apprenticed Black children, often to their parents' former enslavers, without parental consent. (Audited bank record, quoted in the activity, Station 2.)

5. General Orders No. 3, issued at Galveston on June 19, 1865, announced an absolute equality of personal rights between former masters and slaves and in the same order advised freedpeople to remain home and work for wages and warned they would not be supported in idleness. (Audited bank record, quoted in the activity, Station 5.)

6. The Edisto committee argued from labor and loyalty, "we have made these lands what they are," and asked that every man be allowed to purchase land and hold it as his own; the land was restored to its former owners anyway. Jourdon Anderson demanded $11,680 in back wages for thirty two years of his labor and twenty of Mandy's, and asked whether his daughters would be safe and schools stood open for his children. (Audited bank records, quoted in the activity, Stations 3 and 4.)

Plan Before You Write

My claim (one sentence that answers the question's two halves at once):

My three evidence picks (bank numbers) and what each one proves:

The strongest fact on the other side of my claim, and how I will answer it:

Scaffold. Build the essay in this shape. Open with the claim. A first body paragraph on what was given back: the pardons, the restored property, and governments seated on three acts with no congressional terms. A second body paragraph on what was left to fight for: the Codes at the level of the fine and the hiring out, the land restored at Edisto, and the wages, safety, and schools Anderson named. A closing move on the Bureau: state what the one federal instrument could and could not hold, and defend your judgment from your evidence.

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your claim, develop it with your three pieces of evidence in the order of the lesson, answer the strongest fact on the other side in one or two sentences, and end on freedpeople's own stated terms.

Essay, continued. Keep your evidence in the order of the lesson, and end on freedpeople's own stated terms.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the question in one judgment, for example that Johnson's restoration returned the South's governments, land, and leaders on three acts that never named freedpeople's rights, leaving freedpeople to fight for land, wages, safety, and schools against codes written by the governments the president had seated. A claim that lists only the pardons, or only condemns the Codes, earns partial credit before the evidence is read. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 carries the pardons and the excepted classes. (Blueprint 1) Bank item 2 carries the three acts, the white electorate, and the absence of congressional terms. (Blueprint 1) Bank item 3 carries the Bureau's work and its agents' exposed position. (Blueprint 2) Bank item 4 carries the Vagrant Law's fine, jail, and hiring out and the apprenticeship provisions. (Blueprint 2) Bank item 5 carries freedom's announcement and its instructions in a single order. (Blueprint 2) Bank item 6 carries freedpeople's stated terms, land at Edisto and wages, safety, and schools in Anderson's letter, and the outcomes both records met. (Blueprint 2)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive link is terms against outcomes: strong essays argue whether a settlement whose required acts never mentioned the ballot, equal testimony, or land could have produced any outcome other than the Codes, and the Edisto restoration and the $50 fine are the evidence that decides between those readings.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest fact on the other side, such as the Bureau's real relief and schools answered from bank item 3: an agency whose agents stood with few soldiers behind them could feed and teach, and could not by itself hold land, wages, or the ballot against the governments Johnson had seated.

Model opening for teacher reference. Johnson's restoration gave the South back nearly everything the war had taken except slavery itself. The pardons came first, property restored and the excepted leaders forgiven by the thousands on personal application. Then the governments: conventions of the old white electorate voided secession, abolished slavery, and repudiated the war debts, and on those three acts alone the states stood restored, with former Confederate officers in office and Congress unconsulted. What the terms never named, freedpeople had to fight for. Mississippi answered with a vagrancy law that fined an unemployed freedman fifty dollars he did not have and hired him out when he could not pay. A full model continues in this pattern, through the Bureau's exposed agents and the Galveston order's two halves, and closes on Edisto and Anderson, where freedpeople stated freedom's terms themselves, in land, wages, safety, and schools, and were refused or left unanswered.

Common weak answers. Johnson was lenient and the South was ungrateful (two labels, not an answer) skips both halves of the question and every term the prompt requires. An essay that treats the Black Codes as a southern defiance of Johnson's plan, rather than as the work of governments his plan seated, has misread bank item 2 and should be sent back to the reading, sections B and C. An essay that presents the Bureau as proof the settlement protected freedpeople ignores bank item 3's closing fact, the agents' few soldiers, and bank item 6's outcomes.
