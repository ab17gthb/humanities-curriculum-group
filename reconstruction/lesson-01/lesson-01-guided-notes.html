Guided Notes: After Appomattox: Presidential Reconstruction

Lesson question: What did Johnson's restoration give back to the South, and what did it leave freedpeople to fight for?

Unit question: What did Reconstruction settle about freedom, citizenship, and the federal Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Reconstruction: the period from 1865 to 1877 in which the United States decided how the seceded states would return to the Union, what freedom would mean for the four million people emancipation had freed, and how much of the settlement the federal government would enforce

Andrew Johnson: the Tennessee tailor and Unionist Democrat who became vice president under Lincoln in 1864 and president on Lincoln's assassination in April 1865, whose lenient restoration plan and vetoes of Congress's measures set the first terms of Reconstruction and brought him to impeachment in 1868

presidential Reconstruction: the restoration program Johnson ran from April 1865, offering amnesty and pardons to most former Confederates, letting the southern states organize governments once they abolished slavery, repudiated their secession ordinances and Confederate debts, and left the terms of Black freedom to those governments

Freedmen's Bureau: the federal agency Congress created in March 1865, formally the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, which fed the hungry of both races, ran hospitals and courts, supervised labor contracts, legalized marriages, and built schools across the South, the first federal relief agency in American history

Black Codes: the laws the restored southern governments passed in 1865 and 1866 to control Black labor and movement, imposing vagrancy penalties, forced apprenticeships of Black children, and contract rules that tied freedpeople to plantations and denied them equal rights of testimony, arms, and property

Amnesty Proclamation: Johnson's proclamation of May 29, 1865, which pardoned most former Confederates who swore a loyalty oath and restored their property except in enslaved people, while excepting fourteen classes, including high officers and the wealthy, who had to apply to him personally for pardon

Jourdon Anderson: the Tennessee freedman who in August 1865 answered his former enslaver's invitation to return with a dictated letter demanding back wages for thirty two years of his own labor and twenty for his wife's, and asking whether his daughters would be safe and his children schooled, freedom's terms stated by a freedman himself

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher

Teach this lesson first in the Reconstruction unit. It sets the settlement of 1865 that every later lesson tests: Johnson's restoration terms, the Freedmen's Bureau, and the Black Codes, with freedpeople's own first answers in the Edisto Island petition and Jourdon Anderson's letter. It pairs with the lesson reading and with the activity, The Settlement on Paper, whose five stations carry the restoration terms as a labeled teaching summary and four audited bank records. Prior knowledge: students need the Sectional Crisis and Civil War unit's ground, emancipation as a war measure, Appomattox, and Lincoln's assassination named there and in this lesson's first section. Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies (cite textual evidence, determine central ideas, and analyze a sequence of events) and the C3 Framework, Dimension 2, History (change and continuity, and the uses of evidence), stated state neutrally for teachers to map to their own frameworks. Students will learn what Johnson's plan restored and on what terms, what the Bureau did and where its agents stood, how the Black Codes rebuilt labor control in law, and how freedpeople stated their own terms for freedom in petitions and letters. Timing: one period for the reading and these notes, a second period for the activity and writing task. Materials: the reading, these notes, the activity file, and the notebook page.

Summary table. Pardons and property: the Amnesty Proclamation of May 29, 1865 pardoned most former Confederates who swore loyalty and restored their property except in enslaved people, and Johnson pardoned the excepted fourteen classes freely by personal application. Governments restored: provisional governors called conventions of the prewar white electorate, each state voided secession, abolished slavery, and repudiated its war debts, and by the fall of 1865 elected governments stood across the South with former Confederate officers in office and no congressional terms imposed. The Bureau and the Codes: the Bureau fed the hungry of both races, ran hospitals and courts, supervised labor contracts, legalized marriages, and built schools, while the restored legislatures answered with Black Codes, Mississippi's Vagrant Law fining an unemployed freedman up to fifty dollars and hiring out those who could not pay. Freedpeople's answers: General Orders No. 3 carried freedom to Texas on June 19, 1865 with instructions to stay and work for wages, the Edisto committee petitioned from labor and loyalty for the right to buy the land they had made, and Jourdon Anderson demanded $11,680 in back wages and asked after his daughters' safety and his children's schools.
