The Settlement on Paper

Directions: Work through the five stations in order. Each station holds one record of the settlement of 1865: the president's terms, a restored state's law, and three records in which freedpeople or the federal government speak. For each record, state whose freedom it enlarged or narrowed and by what instrument: a pardon, a statute, a petition, a letter, or an army order. At the end, judge which record a freed family in December 1865 would have felt most in its daily life, and defend your judgment from the documents.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: Johnson's Restoration Terms, 1865 (Teaching Summary)

Source: Teaching summary, written for this lesson from the public record of President Andrew Johnson's Amnesty Proclamation of May 29, 1865, and his proclamations appointing provisional governors in 1865. This station is a labeled summary, not a quotation.

Johnson offered pardon and restoration of property, except in enslaved people, to most former Confederates who swore loyalty to the Union. Fourteen classes were excepted, including high Confederate officers and men whose taxable property exceeded twenty thousand dollars; they had to apply to the president personally, and he pardoned freely. Johnson appointed provisional governors, who called conventions of the prewar white electorate. Each convention had to declare secession void, abolish slavery, and repudiate the state's war debts. A state that did so was treated as restored, its government recognized and its members entitled to their seats, without terms set by Congress and without any requirement of Black suffrage or equal rights.

State whose freedom this record enlarged, and by what instruments. Name two things restored to former Confederates and the three acts required of each state.

State what this record does not require. Name two rights freedpeople are not given anywhere in these terms, and the body whose terms Congress did not set.

Station 2: Mississippi's Vagrant Law, 1865

Source: Mississippi Legislature, 'Mississippi Vagrant Law,' 1865 (Laws of the State of Mississippi, 1866; University of Nebraska-Lincoln transcription). Quoted verbatim from the audited bank record. Cuts are marked with ellipses and disclosed here.

Section 1. Be it enacted by the Legislature of the State of Mississippi, That all rogues and vagabonds, idle and dissipated persons, beggars, jugglers, or persons practicing unlawful games or plays, runaways, common drunkards, common night-walkers, pilferers, lewd, wanton, or lascivious persons, in speech or behavior, common railers and brawlers, persons who neglect their calling or employment, misspend what they earn, or do not provide for the support of themselves or their families, or dependants, and all other idle and disorderly persons, including all who neglect all lawful business, or habitually misspend their time by frequenting houses of ill-fame, gaming-houses or tippling shops, shall be deemed and considered vagrants under the provisions of this act ... Sec. 2. Be it further enacted, That all freedmen, free negroes and mulattoes in this State, over the age of eighteen years, found on the second Monday in January, 1866, or thereafter, with no lawful employment or business, or found unlawfully assembling themselves together either in the day or night time, and all white persons so assembling with freedmen, free negroes or mulattoes, or usually associating with freedmen, free negroes or mulattoes on terms of equality, or living in adultery or fornication with a freedwoman, free negro, or mulatto, shall be deemed vagrants, and on conviction thereof, shall be fined in the sum of not exceeding, in the case of a freedman, free negro or mulatto, fifty dollars, and a white man two hundred dollars, and imprisoned at the discretion of the court, the free negro not exceeding ten days, and the white man not exceeding six months.

State whose freedom this law narrowed, and by what instrument. Quote the words that define the offense for a freedman, and give the fine and jail figures for a freedman exactly as printed.

State what else the law punishes besides being without work. Name the assemblies and associations Section 2 reaches, and the different fines it sets for a freedman and a white man.

Station 3: The Edisto Island Petition, October 28, 1865

Source: Henry Bram, Ishmael Moultrie, Yates Sampson (committee of freedmen), 'Petition of the Freedmen of Edisto Island,' 1865 (Critical Explorers; original spellings preserved; original spelling preserved). Quoted verbatim from the audited bank record, which carries cuts marked [...] in the record itself, disclosed here.

Edisto Island S.C. Oct 28th 1865.

We the freedmen of Edisto Island South Carolina have learned from you through Major General O. O. Howard, commissioner of the Freedmen's Bureau, with deep sorrow and painful hearts, of the possibility of goverment restoring these lands to the former owners.

[...]

We have been encouraged by government to take up these lands in small tracts, receiving certificates of the same. We have thus far taken sixteen thousand (16000) acres of land here on this Island. We are ready to pay for this land when government calls for it. And now after what has been done will the good and just government take from us all this right and make us subject to the will of those who have cheated and oppressed us for many years? God forbid! We the freedmen of this island and of the State of South Carolina do therefore petition to you as the President of these United States, that some provisions be made by which every colored man can purchase land and hold it as his own. We wish to have a home if it be but a few acres. Without some provision is made our future is sad to look upon, yes our situation is dangerous.

[...]

State whose freedom this record enlarges, if it is granted, and by what instrument. Name what the petitioners ask the president to provide, in their closing words.

State the petitioners' two grounds. Quote the sentence in which they say what they have made, and state the comparison they draw between themselves and the former owners.

Station 4: Jourdon Anderson's Letter, August 7, 1865

Source: Jourdon Anderson, 'Letter to Colonel P. H. Anderson,' 1865 (Bill of Rights Institute; originally in the Cincinnati Commercial, 1865). Quoted verbatim from the audited bank record. Cuts are marked with ellipses in the record and disclosed here.

I want to know particularly what the good chance is you propose to give me. I am doing tolerably well here. I get twenty-five dollars a month, with victuals and clothing; have a comfortable home for Mandy (the folks call her Mrs. Anderson) and the children, Milly, Jane, and Grundy, go to school and are learning well. The teacher says Grundy has a head for a preacher. They go to Sunday school, and Mandy and me attend church regularly. We are kindly treated. Sometimes we overhear others saying, "Them colored people were slaves" down in Tennessee. The children feel hurt when they hear such remarks; but I tell them it was no disgrace in Tennessee to belong to Colonel Anderson. [...] Now if you will write and say what wages you will give me, I will be better able to decide whether it would be to my advantage to move back again.

As to my freedom, which you say I can have, there is nothing to be gained on that score, as I got my free papers in 1864 from the Provost-Marshal-General of the Department of Nashville. Mandy says she would be afraid to go back without some proof that you were disposed to treat us justly and kindly; and we have concluded to test your sincerity by asking you to send us our wages for the time we served you. This will make us forget and forgive old scores, and rely on your justice and friendship in the future. I served you faithfully for thirty-two years, and Mandy twenty years. At twenty-five dollars a month for me, and two dollars a week for Mandy, our earnings would amount to $11,680. Add to this the interest for the time our wages have been kept back, and deduct what you paid for our clothing, and three doctor's visits to me, and pulling a tooth for Mandy, and the balance will show what we are in justice entitled to. Please send the money by Adams's Express, in care of V. Winters, Esq., Dayton, Ohio. If you fail to pay us for faithful labors in the past, we can have little faith in your promises in the future. We trust the good Maker has opened your eyes to the wrongs which you and your fathers have done to me and my fathers, in making us toil for you for generations without recompense. Here I draw my wages every Saturday night; but in Tennessee there was never any pay-day for the negroes any more than for the horses and cows. Surely there will be a day of reckoning for those who defraud the laborer of his hire.

State whose freedom this letter enlarges, and by what instrument. Name the test of sincerity Anderson sets, and give his figures: his monthly wage in Ohio, the years he and Mandy served, and the total he says they are owed.

State Anderson's two closing conditions. Name what he asks about his daughters and what he asks about schools, and state what each question assumes about Tennessee as it was.

Station 5: General Orders No. 3, Galveston, June 19, 1865

Source: Gordon Granger, 'General Orders No. 3,' June 19, 1865 (Gilder Lehrman Institute; original held by the National Archives). Quoted verbatim and in full from the audited bank record.

The people of Texas are informed that, in accordance with a proclamation from the Executive of the United States, all slaves are free. This involves an absolute equality of personal rights and rights of property between former masters and slaves, and the connection heretofore existing between them becomes that between employer and hired labor. The freedmen are advised to remain quietly at their present homes and work for wages. They are informed that they will not be allowed to collect at military posts and that they will not be supported in idleness either there or elsewhere.

State whose freedom this order enlarges, and by what instrument. Quote the phrase in which it states the new standing of former masters and slaves.

State what the order asks of the people it frees, in the same document. Quote its advice and its warning, and name the new connection it sets between employer and labor.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: Johnson's Restoration Terms (Teaching Summary)

Whose freedom enlarged, by what instruments? Former Confederates'. The instruments are the presidential pardon and the restoration of property, plus recognition of governments seated after the three acts. Restored: pardons for most who swore loyalty, property except in enslaved people, and political standing for the excepted classes pardoned on personal application. The three acts: secession declared void, slavery abolished, war debts repudiated. This station is a labeled teaching summary built from the Amnesty Proclamation of May 29, 1865, and the provisional governor proclamations; it is not a quotation, and students should be able to say so.

What is not required? No Black suffrage and no equal rights in court, testimony, or property are required anywhere in the terms. Congress set none of the terms; Johnson ran restoration from the presidency while Congress was out of session, and the conventions were elected by the prewar white electorate alone.

Station 2: Mississippi's Vagrant Law

Whose freedom narrowed, by what instrument? Freedpeople's, by state statute. The offense for a freedman: being over eighteen and found, from the second Monday in January 1866, with no lawful employment or business. Figures exactly as printed: a freedman fined not exceeding fifty dollars and imprisoned at the court's discretion, the free negro not exceeding ten days.

What else does it punish? Assembly and association. Section 2 reaches freedmen found unlawfully assembling together day or night, white persons assembling with them or usually associating with them on terms of equality, and interracial adultery or fornication. The fines differ by race: not exceeding fifty dollars for a freedman, free negro or mulatto, and two hundred dollars for a white man, with imprisonment up to ten days against up to six months.

Station 3: The Edisto Island Petition

Whose freedom enlarged, by what instrument? The Edisto freedpeople's, if granted, by presidential provision, the petition asks the president to make some provision by which every colored man can purchase land and hold it as his own, closing: We wish to have a home if it be but a few acres. The instrument sought is a purchase right secured by the same federal authority that was restoring the land to its former owners.

The two grounds. Labor and loyalty. The labor sentence: This is our home, we have made these lands what they are. The comparison: the petitioners were the only true and loyal people found in possession of the lands, against the former owners who were found in rebellion against this good and just Goverment and now come asking if their lands cannot be restored to them. The petition also records that they had taken sixteen thousand acres under government encouragement and stood ready to pay for it.

Station 4: Jourdon Anderson's Letter

Whose freedom enlarged, by what instrument? Anderson's own family's, by a private letter used as a negotiation. His test of sincerity: send the wages for the time served. Figures: twenty five dollars a month in Ohio; he served thirty two years and Mandy twenty; at twenty five dollars a month for him and two dollars a week for Mandy the earnings amount to $11,680, plus interest, less clothing, three doctor's visits, and pulling a tooth for Mandy.

His two closing conditions. Safety and schools. He asks whether there would be any safety for Milly and Jane, his grown daughters, saying he would rather stay and starve than have his girls brought to shame by the violence and wickedness of their young masters, which assumes the old Tennessee offered enslaved girls no protection. He asks whether any schools have been opened for the colored children in the neighborhood, calling education the great desire of his life now, which assumes none had been open to them under slavery.

Station 5: General Orders No. 3

Whose freedom enlarged, by what instrument? Enslaved Texans', by a Union army order enforcing the Emancipation Proclamation. The standing phrase: an absolute equality of personal rights and rights of property between former masters and slaves.

What does it ask of the people it frees? Its advice: the freedmen are advised to remain quietly at their present homes and work for wages. Its warning: they will not be allowed to collect at military posts and will not be supported in idleness either there or elsewhere. The new connection is that between employer and hired labor, freedom and wage work stated in a single sentence.

Part 2: Grading Guide

Strong tables keep the instrument column concrete: pardon and recognition for Station 1, a state vagrancy statute enforced by fine, jail, and hiring out for Station 2, a petition asking presidential provision of a purchase right for Station 3, a dictated letter setting a wage test for Station 4, and a military order for Station 5. Verdicts earn full credit on either of the two strongest choices when defended against the other. The Vagrant Law case runs on daily compulsion: a freed family felt the code every morning it chose work, an employer, or a road. The Edisto case runs on the material base: land decided whether every other freedom could be kept, and the petitioners lost it. A General Orders verdict earns credit only if it answers the order's own second half, the stay and work instructions. A verdict for the restoration terms earns credit when it argues, from Station 1, that every other record in the file is downstream of the pardons.
