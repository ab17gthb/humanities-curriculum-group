After Appomattox: Presidential Reconstruction

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The President the War Left Behind

In April 1865 the Civil War ended in the field and opened in the law. Lee surrendered at Appomattox on April 9, and five days later John Wilkes Booth shot President Abraham Lincoln, who died the next morning. Andrew Johnson of Tennessee, a tailor turned senator who had stayed loyal when his state seceded, succeeded him. Lincoln had placed Johnson on the 1864 ticket as a War Democrat. Every question about the peace stood unanswered. Who would set the terms on which the seceded states returned? What would freedom mean for the four million people emancipation had freed? Who would enforce whatever was decided?

B. The Amnesty Proclamation

Johnson answered by acting himself, at once, while Congress was out of session. On May 29, 1865, he issued his Amnesty Proclamation. It pardoned most former Confederates who swore loyalty and restored their property, though no one could recover the people he had enslaved. Fourteen classes were excepted, among them high officers and men whose taxable property exceeded twenty thousand dollars. Those men had to apply to Johnson personally, and he granted pardons freely, thousands of them.

Johnson appointed provisional governors to call state conventions. The voters were, in practice, the white electorate as it had stood before the war, restored by the pardons. No Black southerner voted for the conventions deciding what Black freedom meant, and no condition required the new governments to give freedpeople the ballot or equal rights.

C. Three Acts and a Claim

Each convention had three acts to perform: declare its ordinance of secession void, abolish slavery, and repudiate the debts it had contracted to fight the United States. A state that performed them was, in Johnson's view, restored, entitled to send members to Washington. He did not ask Congress to approve, because in his account the states had never legally left the Union and needed only recognition to resume their places.

By the fall of 1865 elected governments had replaced provisional ones across the South. The speed was the point. Every month the South governed itself under Johnson's terms made them harder for Congress to unsettle in December.

D. The Freedmen's Bureau at Work

One federal presence worked on the ground. Congress had created the Freedmen's Bureau on March 3, 1865, formally the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, under General Oliver O. Howard and staffed largely by army officers. The Bureau fed the hungry of both races, issued rations to destitute freedpeople and white refugees, and ran hospitals where war had broken the food supply.

Its harder work was legal and economic. Bureau agents supervised the labor contracts under which freedpeople now worked for wages. Bureau courts heard complaints where state courts would not take freedpeople's testimony. Bureau officers legalized marriages slavery had never allowed in law, and built schools. Its agents stood between planters wanting the old discipline back and freedpeople wanting wages, land, and schools, with few soldiers to back them.

E. Freedom Reaches Texas

Freedom arrived at the pace of federal power. In Texas, emancipation remained paper until Union troops reached Galveston. On June 19, 1865, General Gordon Granger issued General Orders No. 3, announcing that all enslaved people in Texas were free, that former masters and slaves stood in absolute equality of personal rights, and that their connection became that of employer and hired labor. The same order advised freedpeople to remain home, work for wages, and warned they would not be supported in idleness. Black Texans marked the day as Emancipation Day, later Juneteenth.

F. The Black Codes

The restored governments answered in their own interest. Southern voters elected former Confederate officers to governorships and legislatures, including men excepted by the proclamation and then pardoned. In late 1865 and 1866 the new legislatures passed the Black Codes, laws rebuilding control of Black labor and movement in statute form.

Mississippi's Vagrant Law shows the machinery. It declared every freedman over eighteen found without lawful employment on the second Monday of January 1866 a vagrant, liable to a fine of up to fifty dollars and jail, and one who could not pay could be hired out by the sheriff. Other provisions apprenticed Black children, often to their parents' former enslavers, without parental consent. Slavery was abolished. Its labor discipline was being rebuilt one statute at a time.

G. Freedpeople Answer

Freedpeople answered in writing from the first fall. On Edisto Island, South Carolina, freed families had settled land abandoned by fleeing planters. When Johnson's policy restored that land to its pardoned former owners, a committee of three freedmen, Henry Bram, Ishmael Moultrie, and Yates Sampson, petitioned the president in October 1865. They argued from labor and loyalty: they had made the lands what they were and asked that every man be allowed to purchase land and hold it as his own. The land was restored anyway.

Jourdon Anderson answered with the same logic. Living free in Dayton, Ohio, he got a letter from his former enslaver asking him to return and work, and dictated his reply in August 1865. He asked what wages would be paid, noted his twenty five dollars a month in Ohio, and proposed a test: pay the back wages owed, eleven thousand six hundred eighty dollars for thirty two years of his labor and twenty of his wife Mandy's, less clothing and doctor's visits. He asked whether his daughters would be safe and schools stood open for his children. By December, the South had its governments, pardons, and land back. Freedpeople had a bureau and a fight for everything else.
