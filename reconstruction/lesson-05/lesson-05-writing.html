Writing Task: Built Without Land

The prompt. Write a developed essay of about 300 words answering the lesson question from this lesson's documents: what did freedpeople build, and what did the land settlement they never got cost what they built? Judge whether the sharecropping settlement was a compromise freed families could build on, a defeat that taxed everything they built, or both at once for different families. Defend the judgment against the strongest rival reading in the record. Your essay must account for the standard terms of a share contract and for the Edisto petitioners' claim in their own words. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the Freedom's Ledger activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording. Keep the paired records whole wherever you use them: the family's plot and pace beside the pledge and the lien, and the schools and churches beside the land their builders were refused.

Evidence Bank

1. Special Field Orders No. 15, issued in January 1865, reserved some four hundred thousand coastal acres for freed families in plots of not more than forty acres, and some forty thousand freedpeople settled under possessory titles. Johnson's restoration returned the land to its pardoned owners within the year, Congress defeated Stevens's confiscation plan, and the Southern Homestead Act delivered mostly poor acres.

2. The Edisto petitioners claimed the land on two grounds, that they had made these lands what they are and that they were the only loyal people in possession of them. They stated they had taken up sixteen thousand acres, offered to pay for the land when the government called for it, and asked for a provision by which every colored man could purchase land and hold it as his own.

3. Freedpeople refused gang labor under overseers and won family plots worked at their own pace, with many women withdrawing field labor to their own households. Sharecropping paid the landowner a share of the crop, commonly half, at a settlement held once a year.

4. Under a share contract's standard terms, the family pledged its crop and all its livestock, tools, and personal property, waived homestead protection, ginned all its cotton at the owner's gin, and repaid supply advances from its first picking. The furnishing merchant advanced seed, tools, food, and clothing against a crop lien at prices and interest he set, and a shortfall at settlement renewed the debt.

5. Freedpeople taxed themselves, bought lots, and built schoolhouses across the South, taught by Black and northern teachers and enrolling some quarter of a million students by 1870. Beside the schools, tens of thousands of families saved in the Freedmen's Savings Bank, chartered in 1865, until speculation and the Panic of 1873 brought it down in 1874 and cost depositors much of what they had saved.

6. The African Methodist Episcopal Church, organized in Philadelphia in 1816, carried its congregations across the South by the thousands, and its meetinghouses, often the first property a Black community owned outright, served as schoolhouses, league halls, and the organizing ground of the new politics.

Scaffold

Build the essay in four moves. Open with your judgment in one sentence, naming what was built and the base it stood on. Then give the building at full height, the schools, the churches, and the savings, before you qualify it. Then turn to the base: the land revoked, the contract's pledges, or the lien's arithmetic, and show one document working inside that base. Close by answering the strongest rival reading and stating what a different land settlement would have changed.

Sentence starters you may use: "Freedpeople built ___ on a base of ___, and the building shows ___." "The strongest evidence for a workable compromise is ___, because ___." "The term that taxed the building was ___, and ___ shows it at work." "Against the reading that ___, the record shows ___."

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment choosing one reading, compromise, defeat, or both at once for different families, stated directly and held through the essay. Any of the three can earn full credit if the contract's terms and the petition's claim are carried and the rival reading is answered. An essay that lists schools and churches in order without judging the base under them has summarized the lesson rather than answered it. (Blueprint 9, Blueprint 10)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, drawn from at least two different parts of the record, the land settlement, the labor and credit system, the schools and the bank, or the churches. An essay built on the building alone cannot judge what the building cost. (Blueprint 10)
  • The share contract's terms stated exactly wherever the verdict leans toward compromise: the crop and the family's property pledged, homestead protection waived, the owner's gin required, and advances repaid from the first picking. A compromise essay that omits the pledge has described a different contract than the one in the record. (Blueprint 9)
  • The Edisto petition accounted for, not decorated with: the claim rested on labor and on loyalty, the petitioners offered to pay, and what they asked was a provision to purchase and hold land as their own. The petition is the standard against which the settlement is judged, because it states the settlement the families themselves proposed. (Blueprint 9)
  • The strongest rival reading stated at full strength and answered. Against a defeat verdict: families chose the family plot over the gang, built a quarter of a million students' worth of schools, and saved toward land inside the system, conduct that built real and lasting institutions. Against a compromise verdict: a contract that pledges the crop before it grows and renews the debt at a settlement the creditor conducts has fixed the family's ceiling as well as its floor, and the bank's failure removed the savings the climbing required. (Blueprint 9, Blueprint 10)
  • The cost assigned at the close: the institutions stood on shares of crops grown on other men's land, financed at other men's prices, so their growth was taxed at every settlement by the land settlement the petitioners had asked the government to make and it refused. (Blueprint 9)

Model opening for teacher reference. Freedpeople built the institutions of a free people faster than the country had any right to expect, and they built every one of them on rented ground. By 1870 their schoolhouses enrolled a quarter of a million students, their churches owned the first property their communities held outright, and their savings filled a national bank. Underneath all of it stood a share contract that pledged the crop before it grew, an account settled by the creditor once a year, and a land claim, stated by the Edisto petitioners in the language of labor and loyalty and an offer to pay, that the government had heard in full and refused. The building was real. So was the rent it paid, every year, to the settlement that denied its builders the ground.

Common weak answers. A compromise essay built on bank 3 alone cannot explain the pledge in bank 4 or the petitioners' refusal of the terms on offer in bank 2. A defeat essay built on bank 4 alone cannot explain why the schools and churches of banks 5 and 6 outlasted the system that taxed them.
