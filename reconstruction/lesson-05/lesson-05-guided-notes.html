Guided Notes: Freedom on the Ground

Lesson question: What did freedpeople build, and what did the land settlement they never got cost what they built?

Unit question: What did Reconstruction settle about freedom, citizenship, and the federal Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Special Field Orders No. 15: General William T. Sherman's military order of January 16, 1865, which reserved the Sea Islands and a coastal strip of South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida for settlement by freed families in plots of not more than forty acres, under which some forty thousand freedpeople settled before Johnson restored the land to its former owners

forty acres and a mule: the freedpeople's shorthand for land redistribution, drawn from Special Field Orders No. 15 and the army mules lent to the settlers, the expectation that freedom's material base was land of one's own, whose defeat fixed the land question at the center of Reconstruction's unfinished business

sharecropping: the labor system that spread across the cotton South after emancipation, in which a family farmed a plot of a landowner's land in return for a share of the crop, commonly half, settling accounts once a year, the compromise between freedpeople's refusal of supervised gang labor and landowners' refusal to sell land or pay steady wages

crop lien: the credit system that financed sharecropping, in which a furnishing merchant advanced seed, tools, food, and clothing against a legal claim on the crop before it was planted, at prices and interest the merchant set, so that a poor harvest or a disputed account renewed the family's debt at every settlement

freedmen's schools: the schools freedpeople founded, taxed themselves to sustain, and filled across the South, taught by Black teachers and by northern missionaries under Bureau and church sponsorship, enrolling some quarter of a million students by 1870 and laying the foundation of the South's first public school systems

African Methodist Episcopal Church: the independent Black denomination organized in Philadelphia in 1816, which carried its churches across the South by the thousands after emancipation, its ministers doubling as organizers of the new politics and its meetinghouses serving as schoolhouses, league halls, and the first property Black communities owned outright

Freedmen's Savings Bank: the bank Congress chartered in March 1865 to hold the savings of Black soldiers and freedpeople, which took in the deposits of tens of thousands of families before speculation and the Panic of 1873 brought it down in 1874, costing its depositors much of what they had saved

Teacher Answer Key

E. The advance and the claim: a furnishing merchant, or the landowner acting as one, advanced seed, tools, food, and clothing against a crop lien, a legal claim on the crop before it was planted; the merchant set the prices, charged credit prices above cash prices, and took interest. Why the debt renewed: a share that fell short at settlement carried into the next year, a poor harvest or a disputed account added to it, and the lien required planting cotton, the only crop a merchant would advance against.

F. The schools: freedpeople taxed themselves, raised money, bought lots, and built schoolhouses, often before churches; Black teachers and northern teachers taught in them under Bureau and church sponsorship, enrolling some quarter of a million students by 1870. The bank: Congress chartered the Freedmen's Savings Bank in March 1865 to hold the savings of Black soldiers and freedpeople; tens of thousands of families deposited, and speculation and the Panic of 1873 brought it down in 1874, costing depositors much of what they had saved.

G. The churches and their uses: the African Methodist Episcopal Church, organized in Philadelphia in 1816, carried its congregations across the South by the thousands after emancipation, and its meetinghouses, often the first property a community owned outright, served as schoolhouses, league halls, and the organizing ground of the new politics. The doubled verdict: freedpeople built schools, churches, and savings at speed out of almost nothing, and built them on an economic base the land settlement had denied them.

Freedom on the Ground at a Glance. The land, 1865: Field Orders No. 15 settled some forty thousand freedpeople on forty acre plots, and Johnson's restoration returned the land to its pardoned owners. Labor and credit: freedpeople refused gang labor, sharecropping compromised on a share commonly half, and the crop lien financed the year and renewed the debt. Schools and savings: communities taxed themselves to build schools enrolling a quarter of a million students by 1870, and the Freedmen's Savings Bank held family savings until its 1874 failure. Churches: AME and independent Baptist congregations owned the first community property and housed schools, leagues, and politics.

Exit ticket 1. Sharecropping is the labor system that spread across the cotton South after emancipation, in which a family farmed a plot of a landowner's land in return for a share of the crop, commonly half, settling accounts once a year. The crop lien is the credit system that financed sharecropping, in which a furnishing merchant advanced seed, tools, food, and clothing against a legal claim on the crop before it was planted, at prices and interest the merchant set. The share commonly paid was half.

Exit ticket 2. Freedpeople taxed themselves to build schoolhouses across the South, enrolling some quarter of a million students by 1870, and raised independent churches whose meetinghouses served as schoolhouses and league halls as well as places of worship.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer carrying all three. Example: Freedpeople built schools enrolling a quarter of a million students, independent churches that housed the new politics, and savings in a national bank. The land settlement denied them land of their own. What they built therefore stood on shares of crops grown on other men's land, under liens other men held and priced.
