Freedom on the Ground

Reconstruction · Lesson 5 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Reconstruction, Lesson 5 of 8 — teach it fifth in the unit, after The New Political Order (Lesson 4) and before The Counterrevolution and the Federal Answer (Lesson 6). Lessons 1 to 4 settled freedom's legal and political terms on paper; this lesson follows those terms onto the ground, through the land freedpeople settled and lost, the labor system they fought over, the schools and churches they built, and the bank that held and then lost their savings. The lesson question asks what freedpeople built and what the land settlement they never got cost what they built, and the lesson holds the building and the cost together from the first slide to the last.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 4 (the electorate and governments of the new political order worked and saved on the ground this lesson describes) and Lesson 6 (the terror aimed at the schools, churches, and voters built here, and the federal answer to it). Inside the lesson, the activity, Freedom's Ledger, carries a labeled teaching summary of Field Orders No. 15, a labeled teaching summary of a share contract's standard terms drawn from the bank's 1879 Louisiana contract record and disclosed as such, labeled teaching summaries of the school movement and of the Freedmen's Savings Bank, and the Edisto Island petition quoted verbatim from the audited bank record, revisited from Lesson 1 and disclosed as revisited. The writing task, Built Without Land, turns the lesson question into a verdict defended from the bank.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: emancipation and the Freedmen's Bureau from Lessons 1 and 2, Johnson's restoration policy from Lesson 1, and the Edisto petition's first reading in Lesson 1. Every figure in the lesson is taught as the documents state it — some 400,000 acres reserved, some 40,000 freedpeople settled, a share commonly half, some 250,000 students enrolled by 1870, a bank that failed in 1874. No image of violence is used anywhere in this lesson, and no verified public domain period image of an AME congregation, a Freedmen's Savings Bank branch, or a sharecropping family at work was located, so the churches, the bank, and the crop work are carried in prose and in text and stat slides rather than pictured with images from another era.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (a military order, a petition, a contract's terms, and institutional records read as one settlement), D2.His.16 (freedpeople as historical actors building institutions on a denied base), D2.Eco.1 (land, labor, and credit arrangements compared by what each side received and risked), D2.Eco.9 (the crop lien read as arithmetic — prices, interest, and a settlement conducted by the creditor); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.8, WHST.9-10.1 (a verdict on the sharecropping settlement defended from the records).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to state the terms of Special Field Orders No. 15 and the scale of the settlement it created; explain how Johnson's restoration took the land back, how the Edisto petitioners answered, and why confiscation and the Southern Homestead Act failed as alternatives; describe the labor fight between the gang system and the family plot and the sharecropping compromise it produced, including the share commonly half; work the crop lien step by step and explain why its debt renewed itself; and account for what freedpeople built — schools enrolling some 250,000 students by 1870, a savings bank that failed in 1874 through other men's speculation, and churches that owned their own ground — holding the achievement and the cost in one answer.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1-3) 5 minutes; the land reserved (slides 4-6) 6 minutes; the land taken back (slides 7-8) 5 minutes; work without land (slides 9-10) 5 minutes; sharecropping, the compromise (slides 11-13) 6 minutes; the crop lien (slides 14-16) 6 minutes; schools built, savings lost (slides 17-20) 8 minutes; the churches (slides 21-22) 4 minutes; glance, vocabulary, and exit (slides 23-28) 5 minutes. The activity takes a second period if the schedule allows. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes, and Freedom's Ledger (five stations). The notebook page charts the land, the labor fight, the lien, the schools, and the churches in stages; the quick check is "The Petition and the Settlement: the claim of 1865 beside the terms that followed"; the writing task is "Built Without Land"; the exit ticket has three parts — sharecropping and the lien defined, the building stated in two sentences, and the lesson question answered.

Key vocabulary

Special Field Orders No. 15 · forty acres and a mule · sharecropping · crop lien · freedmen's schools · African Methodist Episcopal Church · Freedmen's Savings Bank

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Title: Freedom on the Ground

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title "Freedom on the Ground"; the standfirst — "Land, labor, schools, and churches: what freedpeople built, and on what base."

  • Picture: A wide crop of Samuel A. Cooley's photograph of a freedmen's school on Edisto Island, South Carolina, held by the Library of Congress.
  • [Open with the pairing the picture already teaches.] On this island, freed families settled land under a general's order in 1865, lost it to a president's policy within the year, petitioned for the right to buy it, and then built the school in the photograph on the settlement that followed.
  • This lesson follows that sequence across the whole South. It asks what freedpeople built, in land, labor, schools, savings, and churches, and what the land settlement they never got cost everything they built.
  • Hold both halves of the question from the first slide to the last, because the lesson's verdict is doubled by design.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "What did freedpeople build, and what did the land settlement they never got cost what they built?"
  • The lesson question has a building half and a costing half, and students should keep them separate all period.
  • What did freedpeople build is a question about work and institutions: a land settlement held for a season, a labor system fought over and compromised, schools enrolling a quarter of a million students, a savings bank filled family by family, and churches that owned their own ground.
  • What the land settlement cost what they built is a question about a base: every institution in the first half stood on shares of crops grown on other men's land, financed at other men's prices.
  • The unit question stands behind both halves, because this is the lesson where freedom's meaning stops being a legal text and becomes a ledger of land, labor, and institutions.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "Land denied, institutions built"; the seven section rows, A-G.

  • The map follows the guided notes section by section. Section A reserves the coastal land under Special Field Orders No. 15 and settles some forty thousand freedpeople on it.
  • Section B takes the land back, through Johnson's restoration, the defeat of confiscation, and the Homestead Act's poor acres. Section C fights over labor, gang against family plot.
  • Section D states the compromise that fight produced, sharecropping. Section E works the crop lien, the credit system that financed sharecropping and renewed its debts.
  • Section F builds, the schools freedpeople taxed themselves to sustain and the savings bank that held their deposits until 1874. Section G gathers the churches, the first property communities owned outright, and closes on the lesson's doubled verdict, a great building on a denied base.

Slide 4 · Section A divider: The Land Reserved

On the slide: the Edisto school portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "A school on Edisto Island, where freed families first settled the land this section describes."

  • Picture: A portrait crop of Cooley's freedmen's school on Edisto Island, the island this section's land story is named for.
  • [Teach the order as a military act with a human meeting inside it.] On January 12, 1865, Sherman and Secretary Stanton sat with twenty Black ministers in Savannah, and their spokesman, Garrison Frazier, told them freedpeople could best take care of themselves on land worked by their own labor.
  • Four days later the order came. Students should notice whose statement of freedom's terms stands at the head of the document: not the general's staff, but the men who would farm the reserve.
  • The order that followed is read in the next slide as a set of terms, acreage, titles, management, and limits, because its limits decided its fate.

Slide 5 · Special Field Orders No. 15, January 1865

On the slide: the Edisto school portrait at right; five bullets — the Sea Islands and a coastal strip were reserved for freed families; the reserve covered some 400,000 acres; each family could claim a plot of not more than forty acres; army inspectors issued possessory titles to the settlers; the settlers managed their own affairs.

  • Picture: Cooley's freedmen's school on Edisto Island, held by the Library of Congress — the island at the reserve's heart, pictured in the settlement that followed the order.
  • [Read the order's terms as a settlement on paper.] The reserve ran down the Sea Islands and the river country behind them, some four hundred thousand acres of some of the richest cotton and rice land in the South.
  • A family's plot was capped at forty acres of tillable ground, enough, worked by a family, for independence rather than a fortune, and that cap is the order's social design stated in a number.
  • The possessory title was real paper and a weak instrument at once: it evidenced the claim, and it rested on the army's war power rather than on a deed or a statute.
  • Management of the settlements was left to the freed people themselves, subject to military authority and Congress, a sentence students should hear twice, because self government inside the reserve was part of what was later taken away.

Slide 6 · Stat: forty thousand settlers

On the slide: the big figure "40,000"; the label — "freedpeople settled in the Sherman reserve by the summer of 1865"; the italic note — whole communities, farming land they expected to keep.

  • 40,000 freedpeople settled in the Sherman reserve by the summer of 1865.
  • [State the number slowly.] By the summer of 1865 some forty thousand freedpeople, whole communities of men, women, and children, were settled in the Sherman reserve, planting crops, building cabins, and organizing their settlements on land they expected to keep.
  • Set the figure beside the scale of emancipation: four million people were freed, so the reserve held about one in a hundred of them, a small share, and yet it is the settlement the whole country's argument about land ran through, because it was the one place the federal government actually put land in freed families' hands.
  • When the land was restored to its former owners, the dispossession was also counted in these same families, which is why the Edisto petition, written from inside this number, carries the weight it does in this unit.

Slide 7 · Section B divider: The Land Taken Back

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Johnson's restoration returns the land to its pardoned owners, the Edisto petitioners answer, and Congress defeats confiscation."

  • [Open the section by naming its mechanism plainly, because students tend to picture the land settlement expiring on its own.] It did not expire. It was reversed, by a president's restoration policy that pardoned the former owners and ordered abandoned and confiscated property returned to them, carried out island by island through the fall of 1865.
  • Commissioner Howard of the Freedmen's Bureau, the agency charged with the freedpeople's welfare, was sent to tell the Edisto settlers himself, and he did, in a meeting the island remembered.
  • The petition students read at Station 5 of the activity was the settlers' written answer to that errand.
  • The section then widens: confiscation argued in Congress and defeated, and a homestead law that opened poor land to people with no money for tools, the two roads not taken.

Slide 8 · Restoration, refusal, and the roads not taken

On the slide: five bullets — pardons returned the owners, and orders returned the land; Commissioner Howard carried the news to Edisto Island himself; the Edisto petition answered with labor, loyalty, and an offer to pay; Congress defeated Stevens's confiscation plan; the Southern Homestead Act offered poor acres to people with empty pockets.

  • [Take the three reversals in order.] First, restoration: pardons returned the owners, and Bureau officers, under orders, returned the land, in some places requiring the settlers to sign labor contracts with the returning owners or leave.
  • Second, the petition and its refusal: the Edisto freedpeople answered on grounds of labor and loyalty and offered to pay for the land, and the answer, delivered by policy rather than by letter, was no.
  • Third, the alternatives: Thaddeus Stevens argued that the great estates should be confiscated and divided, forty acres to a family from the property of the men who had made the rebellion, and Congress, including most Republicans, would not take other men's land as a settled policy, then or later.
  • The Southern Homestead Act opened public land in five states, and poor soil, fees, and empty pockets kept it from becoming the settlement Stevens had wanted.

Slide 9 · Section C divider: Work Without Land

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Planters press for the gang; freed families demand the family plot, and the working day itself becomes the argument."

  • [Introduce the labor fight as a fight, because both sides understood the stakes exactly.] The planter's problem was a crop that required labor on a schedule, and his answer was the labor system he already knew: squads in the field under direction, paid when the crop sold.
  • The freed family's problem was a freedom that had to be felt in the working day, in who decided the hours, the pace, and where a wife and children spent their labor.
  • Bureau contracts papered the fight for the first seasons, with an agent's signature standing in for trust neither side felt.
  • When students reach sharecropping in the next section, they should arrive knowing it was nobody's first choice. It was the ground the two sides met on when neither could impose its own system whole, which is the definition of the compromise the lesson asks them to judge.

Slide 10 · Two programs for the working day

On the slide: five bullets — planters wanted squads working under an overseer, with wages paid at year's end; freedpeople refused the gang and the overseer; families demanded plots they could work at their own pace; many women withdrew their labor from the fields to their own households; Bureau contracts papered over a standoff that neither side kept.

  • [Read the bullets as two programs in collision.] The gang system offered the planter supervision and the worker a wage that existed mostly as a promise at year's end, and freedpeople associated it, accurately, with slavery's field discipline.
  • Their counter program had three parts students should list: family plots, worked by the household; a pace of their own, with rest, schooling, and garden time inside the week; and women's labor withdrawn from the fields to the household, which planters denounced as idleness and families experienced as freedom's first domestic fact, mothers raising their own children in their own cabins.
  • The Bureau's yearly contracts tried to bind both programs into one signature. Each side broke the contracts when the terms pinched, planters by withholding wages, workers by leaving at planting or harvest.
  • The system's instability is what sharecropping was invented, by practice rather than by plan, to settle.

Slide 11 · Section D divider: Sharecropping, the Compromise

On the slide: the South Carolina school portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "A freedmen's school in South Carolina: the farm family's children, in the compromise's own country."

  • Picture: A portrait crop of Sam Cooley's freedmen's school in South Carolina, held by the Library of Congress. The photograph is cataloged with a question mark in its title; describe it as a freedmen's school in South Carolina, following the cataloged title, and claim no more specific place or date for it.
  • [Make the transition explicit for the class.] Sharecropping is not a statute anybody passed; no legislature sat down and designed it. It grew, county by county in the late 1860s, out of the standoff of the last section, planters without cash to pay wages and families refusing the gang.
  • Its unit is the household, its ground is a plot of another man's land, and its calendar is the crop year, planting to settlement.
  • Everything in the next two slides, the share, the furnishing, and the lien, follows from those three facts.
  • Note: No verified public domain period image of a sharecropping family at work was located for this lesson; the photographs search returned are Farm Security Administration images from the 1930s, a different era, and they were not used.

Slide 12 · What each side received

On the slide: five bullets — the family received a plot of its own to farm for the year; the family worked with no overseer and no gang, at its own pace; the owner received labor settled on his land without paying cash wages; a share of the crop went to the owner as rent, commonly half; accounts were settled once a year, when the crop was sold.

  • [State the compromise's terms as an exchange, and make the class name what each side received.] The family received a plot to farm as a household, freedom from the overseer and the gang, and a share of a real crop it could see growing, commonly half, in place of a wage that might never be paid.
  • The landowner received a labor force settled on his land without cash wages, a share of the crop as rent, and a family whose own interest in the harvest policed its work better than a driver could.
  • The settlement, the accounting once a year when the crop sold, is the system's hinge and its danger, the subject of the next sections: between settlements the family lived on credit, and the man who kept the book was usually the man the family owed.
  • A good year and an honest book could carry a family forward. The terms themselves decided how often that happened.

Slide 13 · Stat: one half of the crop

On the slide: the big figure "1/2"; the label — "of the crop, commonly, went to the landowner at settlement"; the italic note — half of the harvest, against the whole of the family's labor for the year.

  • 1/2 of the crop, commonly, went to the landowner at settlement.
  • Half went as rent for the land and often for the mule, the tools, and the furnishing advanced against the year.
  • Set the fraction beside the lesson's other counted things, the forty thousand settlers of Section A and the quarter of a million students ahead in Section F, because this lesson's argument is carried in numbers a student can hold.
  • Half sounds like an equal division, and students should be asked to test it: half of the crop, against the whole of the family's labor for the year, on land the family did not own and would hand back at the year's end.
  • The fraction is the compromise's generosity and its limit in a single figure, and strong verdicts in the writing task will use it exactly that way.

Slide 14 · Section E divider: The Crop Lien

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "The family eats on credit from planting to harvest, and the merchant's claim on the crop is filed before the crop is planted."

  • [Open the section with the year's calendar, because the lien only makes sense inside it.] A crop is planted in spring and sold in fall, and a family must eat in every month between.
  • Under slavery the owner's interest had fed the labor force; under freedom, the family fed itself, and it did so on credit extended against a crop that did not yet exist.
  • The furnishing merchant, a storekeeper in town, or the landowner running a plantation store, supplied that credit, and the crop lien was the legal instrument that secured him: a claim, filed under state lien laws, on the growing crop before it was planted.
  • The section teaches the lien as arithmetic rather than as atmosphere, prices, interest, and a settlement conducted by the creditor, because that is how the families who lived under it experienced it, one account at a time.

Slide 15 · The lien, step by step

On the slide: five bullets — the merchant advanced seed, tools, food, and clothing through the year; each advance was recorded at credit prices, with interest; the lien took the first claim on the growing crop; the crop was ginned and sold through the creditor; at settlement the book was read, and the family learned how its year had ended.

  • [Work the sequence slowly, step by step, as a chain in which each link hands power the same direction.] The merchant advances seed, tools, bacon, meal, and clothing through the year.
  • He records each advance at credit prices, higher than the cash prices in the same store, and interest runs on the account. The lien gives his claim first place against the crop, ahead of the family's share and in practice alongside the landlord's rent.
  • In fall the crop is ginned, weighed, and sold, very often to or through the same merchant or landlord, at a price the family does not set.
  • Then the book is read: the share is credited, the account is charged, and the family learns whether the year ended ahead, even, or behind.
  • Notice who holds the pencil at every step after the planting. The family's labor is the only entry in the whole chain that the family itself records.

Slide 16 · Why the debt renewed itself

On the slide: five bullets — a shortfall at settlement opened the next year's book; new advances were added at new prices and new interest; merchants financed cotton only, the one crop they could always sell; no acres could be spared for food crops that would have cut the store bill; a poor year or a disputed account started the cycle again.

  • [Teach the renewal as arithmetic, not as fate.] Start with a family whose share sells for a sum a little smaller than its account: the shortfall does not vanish at settlement; it opens the next year's book, and the new advances are added to it, at new credit prices and new interest.
  • Add the cotton requirement: a merchant would advance only against cotton, the one crop he could always sell, so a family could not shift acres into corn and food that would have cut its store bill, because food crops would not carry the lien.
  • Add a poor year, a fall in cotton's price, or an account the family believed was wrong and could not audit, and the renewal explains itself.
  • The debt was not every family's story every year. It was the story the terms made available whenever a year went wrong.

Slide 17 · Section F divider: Schools Built, Savings Lost

On the slide: the Cooke schoolroom portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "A Bureau schoolroom in Richmond, 1866: the schools freed communities taxed themselves to build."

  • Picture: A portrait crop of James E. Taylor's sketch of the Misses Cooke's school room in Richmond, a Freedmen's Bureau school of 1866, held by the Library of Congress. Students will meet it again at Station 3 of the activity.
  • [Reverse the class's expectation.] The schools were not a gift delivered to freedpeople by the Bureau or by northern societies, welcome as that help was.
  • They were built, taxed for, and filled by the freed communities themselves, often before a church, often in the face of neighbors who burned schoolhouses to stop them.
  • The federal and missionary contribution was real, teachers, books, buildings repaired, and it stood on a foundation of community money and labor that the Bureau's own reports took care to record. The next slide states who paid, who taught, and who sat in the seats.

Slide 18 · Who paid, who taught, who sat in the seats

On the slide: the Cooke schoolroom portrait at right; five bullets — freed communities taxed themselves and raised the schoolhouse frames; missionary societies sent teachers and money; the Freedmen's Bureau supplied buildings and kept the count; Black men and women and northern teachers taught in the schools; children attended by day, and adults attended at night and on Sundays.

  • Picture: Taylor's sketch of the Misses Cooke's school room, Richmond, 1866, held by the Library of Congress — a Bureau schoolroom of the kind this slide counts.
  • [Take the schools in the order of who supplied what.] The communities supplied the demand and much of the means: taxes families voted on themselves, tuition paid in cash or labor, lots bought, and frames raised by donated work, a record the Bureau's education reports state with something like wonder.
  • The missionary societies, the American Missionary Association first among them, supplied teachers and northern money, and the Bureau supplied buildings, rents, repairs, and the reporting system that lets us count the result.
  • The teachers were Black men and women in growing numbers, many educated in the freedmen's schools themselves, and northern teachers, most of them women, who came south into real hostility.
  • The students filled day schools as children and night and Sabbath schools as adults, parents learning letters beside their children, in communities where teaching an enslaved person to read had lately been a crime.

Slide 19 · Stat: a quarter of a million students

On the slide: the big figure "250,000"; the label — "students enrolled in the freedmen's schools by 1870"; the italic note — enrolled in a region that five years earlier had forbidden most of them literacy by law.

  • 250,000 students enrolled in the freedmen's schools by 1870.
  • [Put the count to work as evidence rather than as decoration.] By 1870 the freedmen's schools enrolled some two hundred fifty thousand students, children and adults together, in a region that five years earlier had forbidden most of them literacy by law.
  • It measures attendance and ambition, not completion: terms were short, attendance broke for planting and harvest, and many schools closed when Bureau support ended.
  • What the figure proves is scale of demand, communities across an entire region taxing themselves for a good they had been denied, at a rate no one had predicted.
  • The Reconstruction constitutions then wrote public school systems into state law, and the lesson's point is the direction of the building: the state systems grew from roots the freed communities had planted, paid for, and defended themselves.

Slide 20 · The Freedmen's Savings Bank, 1865 to 1874

On the slide: five bullets — Congress chartered the bank in March 1865; branches spread across the South and took deposits of a few cents; passbooks held savings toward a lot, tools, and schooling; in 1870 Congress loosened the charter, and the managers speculated; the bank failed in 1874, and depositors lost much of what they had saved.

  • [Place the bank in this section because it was the same discipline in another form.] Small sums, set aside on purpose, against a future a family meant to buy.
  • Congress chartered the bank in March 1865 to hold Black soldiers' and freedpeople's savings, branches spread across the South, and tens of thousands of families carried passbooks and deposited wages and garden money a few cents at a time.
  • [Teach the failure without flinching and without blaming the savers.] Congress loosened the charter in 1870, the managers speculated with the deposits in railroads and real estate, the Panic of 1873 broke the borrowers, and the bank failed in 1874, costing depositors much of what they had saved.
  • A depositor could see the branch and the passbook. She could not see the loan book, and that invisibility is the section's judgment on where the failure belongs.
  • Note: No verified public domain period image of a Freedmen's Savings Bank branch was located for this lesson, so the bank is carried in text by judgment.

Slide 21 · Section G divider: The Churches, a Permanent Address

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "The one institution on the lesson's chart that owned its own ground, and the doubled verdict the whole lesson builds toward."

  • [Open the final section with the institution that could not be repossessed at settlement.] Land could be restored to its former owners, a contract could be canceled, a bank could fail, but a congregation that owned its meetinghouse held an asset no landlord's book could reach.
  • The African Methodist Episcopal Church had been an independent Black denomination since 1816, and emancipation opened the South to it: missionaries followed the armies, congregations formed by the thousands, and independent Black Baptist churches multiplied beside them.
  • A meetinghouse was often the first property a Black community owned outright, bought with the same small savings the last sections described.
  • The next slide fills in what happened inside those walls on the other six days of the week, because the church's weekday life is where this lesson's institutions join into one building.
  • Note: No verified public domain period image of an AME congregation was located for this lesson, so the churches are carried in text by judgment.

Slide 22 · One building, the whole week

On the slide: five bullets — the AME Church, independent since 1816, was carried South by the thousands; the meetinghouse was often the first property a community owned outright; on weekdays the building served as a schoolhouse; on meeting nights it served as a Union League hall; its ministers worked as teachers, organizers, and officeholders.

  • [Read the meetinghouse's week as the lesson's whole building program under one roof.] On weekdays the room was a schoolhouse, where the community's children, and at night its adults, learned to read.
  • On meeting nights it was a Union League hall, where the new electorate of Lesson 4 registered, organized, and chose its candidates. On Sundays it was a church.
  • Its minister was commonly among the community's most educated men, doubling as teacher, as organizer, and in many places as officeholder, the ministry serving as the new politics' recruiting ground.
  • [State the ownership point once more, because it is the section's claim.] This was property, held by the community itself, in a lesson where nearly everything else freedpeople built stood on rented land, borrowed money, or federal protection that later lessons watch being withdrawn. The churches are the exception that proves the rule about the base.

Slide 23 · Freedom on the ground at a glance

On the slide: the title "Land denied, institutions built"; four rows — the land, 1865; labor and credit; schools and savings; churches.

  • This slide mirrors the closing table of the guided notes, Freedom on the Ground at a Glance, and students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • The land, 1865: forty thousand settled under Field Orders No. 15, and the land restored to its pardoned owners within the year. Labor and credit: gang labor refused, sharecropping compromised at a share commonly half, and the crop lien financing the year and renewing the debt.
  • Schools and savings: communities taxing themselves to enroll a quarter of a million students by 1870, and a savings bank holding family deposits until its failure in 1874.
  • Churches: AME and independent Baptist congregations owning the first community property outright and housing schools, leagues, and politics. Four rows, one verdict: a great building, on a base its builders were denied.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: the land

On the slide: two terms with their short forms — Special Field Orders No. 15 (Sherman's order, January 16, 1865), forty acres and a mule (the shorthand for land of one's own). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Two terms, in full, in the guided notes wording. Notice the division between them: the order's definition carries the instrument, its date, its geography, and its cap, while the phrase's definition carries the expectation the order created and the land question its defeat fixed at the center of the unit's unfinished business.
  • Say: "Special Field Orders No. 15: General William T. Sherman's military order of January 16, 1865, which reserved the Sea Islands and a coastal strip of South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida for settlement by freed families in plots of not more than forty acres, under which some forty thousand freedpeople settled before Johnson restored the land to its former owners."
  • Say: "forty acres and a mule: the freedpeople's shorthand for land redistribution, drawn from Special Field Orders No. 15 and the army mules lent to the settlers, the expectation that freedom's material base was land of one's own, whose defeat fixed the land question at the center of Reconstruction's unfinished business."

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: labor and credit

On the slide: two terms with their short forms — sharecropping (a family plot for a share of the crop), crop lien (the merchant's claim on the crop before it grew). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Two terms, in full, in the guided notes wording. Read as a pair, the definitions state the compromise and its financing in two sentences: the first names what each side refused, gang labor on one side and land sales and steady wages on the other, and the second names the merchant, the advance, and the settlement where the debt renewed, which is the whole of Sections D and E carried in vocabulary form for the test.
  • Say: "sharecropping: the labor system that spread across the cotton South after emancipation, in which a family farmed a plot of a landowner's land in return for a share of the crop, commonly half, settling accounts once a year, the compromise between freedpeople's refusal of supervised gang labor and landowners' refusal to sell land or pay steady wages."
  • Say: "crop lien: the credit system that financed sharecropping, in which a furnishing merchant advanced seed, tools, food, and clothing against a legal claim on the crop before it was planted, at prices and interest the merchant set, so that a poor harvest or a disputed account renewed the family's debt at every settlement."

Slide 26 · Vocabulary: the institutions

On the slide: three terms with their short forms — freedmen's schools (a quarter of a million students by 1870), African Methodist Episcopal Church (independent since 1816), Freedmen's Savings Bank (chartered 1865; failed 1874). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Three terms, in full, in the guided notes wording. Set side by side, the three state the lesson's argument about ownership: the schools and churches endured as the community's own, and the bank, managed by others, ends in loss.
  • Say: "freedmen's schools: the schools freedpeople founded, taxed themselves to sustain, and filled across the South, taught by Black teachers and by northern missionaries under Bureau and church sponsorship, enrolling some quarter of a million students by 1870 and laying the foundation of the South's first public school systems."
  • Say: "African Methodist Episcopal Church: the independent Black denomination organized in Philadelphia in 1816, which carried its churches across the South by the thousands after emancipation, its ministers doubling as organizers of the new politics and its meetinghouses serving as schoolhouses, league halls, and the first property Black communities owned outright."
  • Say: "Freedmen's Savings Bank: the bank Congress chartered in March 1865 to hold the savings of Black soldiers and freedpeople, which took in the deposits of tens of thousands of families before speculation and the Panic of 1873 brought it down in 1874, costing its depositors much of what they had saved."

Slide 27 · Closing synthesis: two findings, held together

On the slide: the title "Two findings, held together"; two paragraphs — refused the land they asked to buy, freedpeople built schools, churches, and savings at speed, and they built them on shares of crops grown on other men's land, at prices other men set, so the land settlement they never got taxed everything they built.

  • [Close the lesson by holding the two findings together, because the verdict work is graded on exactly that discipline.] The first finding is about capacity: a people freed with almost nothing, and refused the land they asked to buy, built schools for a quarter of a million students, churches that owned their ground, and savings gathered coin by coin, institutions that outlasted Reconstruction itself.
  • The second finding is about the base: the building stood on crop shares from other men's land, at prices other men set, in a bank other men looted, so every institution paid a yearly tax to the land settlement its builders had petitioned against in 1865.
  • Students who hold only the first finding have a celebration. Students who hold only the second have a dismissal. The lesson's question needs both, stated in one answer: what they built, and what the missing land cost it.

Slide 28 · Now your turn: answer the question and exit ticket

On the slide: the closing title "Answer the question"; the task line — judge the sharecropping settlement, account for the contract's terms, and state the petitioners' claim in their own grounds; the rows — Activity (Freedom's Ledger: the order, the contract, the schools, the bank, the petition), Notebook (the land, the labor fight, the lien, the schools, the churches), Quick check (The Petition and the Settlement: the claim of 1865 beside the terms that followed), Writing (Built Without Land: your verdict defended from the bank); the exit ticket line.

  • Close the lesson by handing the judgment to the students, which is what this lesson exists to do.
  • The activity, Freedom's Ledger, gives them a labeled teaching summary of Field Orders No. 15, a labeled teaching summary of a share contract's standard terms drawn from the bank's 1879 Louisiana contract record and disclosed as such, labeled teaching summaries of the school movement and of the Freedmen's Savings Bank, and the Edisto Island petition quoted verbatim from the audited bank record, revisited from Lesson 1 and disclosed as revisited, and asks whether the sharecropping settlement was a compromise, a defeat, or both at once for different families.
  • The notebook page charts the land, the labor fight, the lien, the schools and savings, and the churches in stages. The quick check sets the petition beside the settlement's terms. The writing task asks for the same verdict in an essay, defended against the strongest rival reading.
  • The exit ticket has three parts: sharecropping and the lien defined, the building stated in two sentences, and the lesson question answered with the achievement and the cost held together.
