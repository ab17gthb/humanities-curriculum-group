Teacher Answer Key

StageWhat happenedWhat it put in freedpeople's hands, and what it cost
The land reserved and returned, 1865Special Field Orders No. 15 reserved some four hundred thousand coastal acres for freed families in plots of not more than forty acres, and some forty thousand freedpeople settled. Johnson's restoration pardoned the former owners and returned the land, Stevens's confiscation plan was defeated, and the Southern Homestead Act delivered mostly poor acres.For one season the settlers held land, possessory titles, and the management of their own affairs. The cost was the settlement itself: a claim resting on a military order was revoked by a president's policy, and the Edisto petitioners' offer to purchase and hold land as their own was refused.
The labor fightPlanters pressed for gang labor under overseers, paid at year's end. Freedpeople refused the gang, demanded family plots at their own pace, and many women withdrew from field labor to their own households. Bureau contracts held the standoff on paper.Freedpeople won control of the working day and of family labor, the first economic freedom emancipation could be felt in. The cost was the ground the fight was fought on: without land, the family's labor was still sold on the owner's terms at settlement.
Sharecropping and the lienA family farmed a plot for a share of the crop, commonly half, settling once a year. The furnishing merchant advanced seed, tools, food, and clothing against a crop lien, at prices and interest he set, and a shortfall at settlement renewed the debt the next year.The system put a family plot, its own pace, and a share of a real crop in a family's hands, and put the crop, the gin, the account, and often the family's mules and tools in the creditor's. Cotton only, debt renewed, and land still another man's at every settlement.
Schools and savingsCommunities taxed themselves, bought lots, and built schoolhouses, taught by Black and northern teachers, enrolling some quarter of a million students by 1870. The Freedmen's Savings Bank, chartered in 1865, took tens of thousands of families' deposits before speculation and the Panic of 1873 brought it down in 1874.The schools put literacy, the skill every other claim required, in a generation's hands, and the bank put a passbook and a discipline of saving in a family's. The cost stood in the bank's failure: much of what families had saved toward land and tools was lost through managers they had not chosen and loans they could not see.
The churchesThe African Methodist Episcopal Church, organized in 1816, carried its congregations across the South by the thousands, and independent Black Baptist congregations multiplied beside it. Meetinghouses served as schoolhouses, league halls, and the organizing ground of the new politics.A church was often the first property a Black community owned outright, an institution no landlord could cancel at settlement. Its ministers doubled as teachers and organizers, and the institution outlasted Reconstruction itself, the one asset on this chart the land settlement could not reach.

The lesson in one paragraph. Strong answers carry the building and the base together. Example: Freedpeople built schools that enrolled some quarter of a million students by 1870, independent churches whose meetinghouses housed schools and the new politics, and savings in a national bank, all of it raised by communities freed with almost nothing. The Edisto petitioners had claimed land on the ground of their labor, that they had made these lands what they are, and offered to pay for it, and the claim was refused. What they built therefore stood on shares of crops grown on other men's land under other men's liens, and the bank's failure and the renewed crop debt are the price the missing land charged the institutions built without it.
