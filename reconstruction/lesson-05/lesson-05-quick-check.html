Quick Check: The Petition and the Settlement

Directions: Read the record and the background note, then answer the questions.

Background. In January 1865 Special Field Orders No. 15 settled freed families on the Sea Islands and the coastal strip, in plots of not more than forty acres. That fall, President Johnson's restoration returned the land to its pardoned former owners. The record below is the answer the freedpeople of Edisto Island sent on October 28, 1865. You first read this petition in Lesson 1; it returns here because this lesson is where its claim was decided.

"[...] Here is where secession was born and nurtured. Here is where we have toiled nearly all our lives as slaves and were treated like dumb driven cattle. This is our home, we have made these lands what they are. We were the only true and loyal people that were found in possession of these lands. We have been always ready to strike for liberty and humanity, yea to fight if needs be to preserve this glorious union. Shall not we who are freedmen and have been always true to this Union have the same rights as are enjoyed by others? Have we broken any law of these United States? Have we forfieted our rights of property in land? If not then! are not our rights as a free people and good citizens of these United States to be considered before the rights of those who were found in rebellion against this good and just Goverment (and now being conquered) come (as they seem) with penitent hearts and beg forgiveness for past offences and also ask if thier lands cannot be restored to them? Are these rebellious spirits to be reinstated in thier possessions and we who have been abused and oppressed for many long years not to be allowed the Privilige of purchasing land but be subject to the will of these large land owners?"

"[...] We have been encouraged by government to take up these lands in small tracts, receiving certificates of the same. We have thus far taken sixteen thousand (16000) acres of land here on this Island. We are ready to pay for this land when government calls for it. And now after what has been done will the good and just government take from us all this right and make us subject to the will of those who have cheated and oppressed us for many years? God forbid! We the freedmen of this island and of the State of South Carolina do therefore petition to you as the President of these United States, that some provisions be made by which every colored man can purchase land and hold it as his own. We wish to have a home if it be but a few acres. Without some provision is made our future is sad to look upon, yes our situation is dangerous."

Source: Henry Bram, Ishmael Moultrie, Yates Sampson (committee of freedmen), 'Petition of the Freedmen of Edisto Island,' 1865, as carried in the audited TPSP bank record (Critical Explorers; original spellings preserved, as the record's sourceNote names). Cuts are marked with ellipses in brackets, and the petition's original spellings stand as the edition carries them.

1. State the petitioners' claim: the two grounds on which they rest their right to the land, the acreage they say they have taken up, and the provision they ask the president to make.

2. Using the lesson's record, state how a sharecropping family was paid and financed, naming the share commonly paid to the landowner, when accounts were settled, and the claim the furnishing merchant held on the crop.

3. State what freedpeople built in schools and churches, naming how the schools were paid for, the enrollment they reached by 1870, and two uses of a church meetinghouse besides worship.

4. A student writes that the petitioners lost everything, because the land went back to its former owners. Test the claim against this lesson's record. Name two things the claim leaves out, one about what the petition itself shows freedpeople doing in 1865 and one about what their communities held by 1870, and state what each one shows.

Teacher Answer Key

1. The petitioners' claim. They rest their right on labor, "we have made these lands what they are," work done over lives spent enslaved on the island, and on loyalty, "We were the only true and loyal people that were found in possession of these lands," set against owners found in rebellion. They state that they have taken up sixteen thousand (16000) acres under government encouragement and certificates, offer to pay for the land when the government calls for it, and ask for a provision by which every colored man can purchase land and hold it as his own. (Blueprint 9)

2. Paid and financed. A sharecropping family farmed a plot of a landowner's land and at harvest paid the owner a share of the crop, commonly half, in place of rent or wages, with accounts settled once a year when the crop was sold. Through the year the furnishing merchant, or the landowner acting as one, advanced seed, tools, food, and clothing against a crop lien, a legal claim on the crop before it was planted, at prices and interest the merchant set, so a shortfall at settlement renewed the family's debt. (Blueprint 9)

3. Schools and churches built. Freedpeople taxed themselves, paid tuition, bought lots, and built schoolhouses across the South, with help from northern missionary societies and the Freedmen's Bureau, taught by Black teachers and northern teachers, enrolling some quarter of a million students by 1870. Their churches, AME congregations carried across the South by the thousands and independent Baptist congregations beside them, were often the first property their communities owned outright, and the meetinghouses served as schoolhouses on weekdays and as Union League halls and the organizing ground of the new politics. (Blueprint 10)

4. Test the claim of total loss. Any two facts, accurately used, one of each kind. About the petition itself: in 1865 the Edisto freedpeople were already acting as a political community, organizing a committee, taking up and working sixteen thousand acres under certificates, and framing a legal claim in the language of labor, loyalty, and purchase, which is not the record of a people who held nothing. About 1870: their communities held schools enrolling a quarter of a million students, church property owned outright, and savings gathered family by family, institutions the land's return did not cancel, even though the Freedmen's Savings Bank's failure in 1874 later cost the savers much of what they had built. What the claim gets right is the land: it went back, and with it the economic base. What it misses is everything the loss could not repossess. (Blueprint 9, Blueprint 10)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Question 1 is the one to read closely: a student who can state both grounds, labor and loyalty, and the offer to pay holds the petition as an argument rather than as a plea. Question 4 separates students who can weigh a settlement on both sides of a ledger from students who treat the land's return as the whole of the record.
