The New Electorate Speaks and Is Pictured

Directions: The five stations below are the record of the new political order as it spoke and was pictured between 1867 and 1871. Study each station and answer its questions. At every station, state what claim about Black citizenship the record makes, and who is making it. In Part 2, judge which record a skeptical northern voter of 1870 would have found hardest to answer, and defend your judgment.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: The Joint Resolution Proposing the Fifteenth Amendment, 1869

Source: U.S. Congress, 'Joint Resolution Proposing the Fifteenth Amendment,' 1869-1870 (Hamilton Fish's certification of ratification, via the National Park Service)., as carried in the audited TPSP bank record. The resolution is quoted in full.

"A Resolution proposing an amendment to the Constitution of the United States. Resolved by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled (two-thirds of both Houses concurring), That the following article be proposed to the legislatures of the several States as an amendment to the Constitution of the United States, which, when ratified by three-fourths of said legislatures, shall be valid as a part of the Constitution, viz. ARTICLE XV. SECTION 1. The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States, or by any State, on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. SEC. 2. The Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation."

State what this record does, in its own terms: whose right it protects, the three grounds on which that right may not be denied or abridged, and the power its second section hands to Congress. Then read the wording the way a lawyer for a state bent on closing the polls would read it. The resolution forbids denial on three named grounds and on no others. What does that silence leave a state free to try, and why would the men who needed three fourths of the state legislatures to ratify have written the protection no wider than this?

Station 2: Senator Revels on the District of Columbia Schools, February 1871

Source: Hiram Rhodes Revels, 'Speech on School Desegregation in the District of Columbia,' 1871 (Congressional Globe; via BlackFacts and BlackPast)., as carried in the audited TPSP bank record. Cuts are marked with ellipses. Revels, the first Black member of Congress, is speaking on the Senate floor for a bill to abolish the separate colored schools of the District of Columbia, which Congress governed directly.

"In regard to the wishes of the colored people of this city, I will simply say that the trustees of colored schools and some of the most intelligent colored men of this place have said to me that they would have before asked for a bill abolishing the separate colored schools and putting all children on an equality in the common schools if they had thought they could obtain it. They feared they could not; and this is the only reason why they did not ask for it before."

"[...] This Mr. President, I do seriously regret. And is this prejudice right? Have the colored people done anything to justify the prejudice against them that does exist in the hearts of so many white persons, and generally of one great political party in this country? Have they done anything to justify it? No, sir. Can any reason be given why this prejudice should be fostered in so many hearts against them simply because they are not white? I make these remarks in all kindness, and from no bitterness of feeling at all."

"Mr. President, if this prejudice has no cause to justify it, then we must admit that it is wicked, we must admit that it is wrong; we must admit that it has not the approval of Heaven."

State Revels's report first: what, he says, had the trustees of the colored schools and the Black men of the city told him they wanted, and what had kept them from asking for it before. Then state his argument about prejudice: the question he puts to the Senate twice, in the same words, and the three admissions he says follow if prejudice has no cause to justify it, naming the words wicked and wrong among them. Revels insists he speaks in all kindness and from no bitterness. Name the audience that sentence is built for, and state what work the kindness does in a chamber where his side is about to lose the vote.

Station 3: "The First Vote," by Alfred R. Waud, 1867

Source: Alfred R. Waud, 'The First Vote,' 1867 (Harper's Weekly, November 1867; image described)., as carried in the audited TPSP bank record, which presents the image by description. The print itself is reproduced below from the Library of Congress copy held on Wikimedia Commons (catalog record LCCN00651117; public domain), verified during this lesson's preparation.

"A Harper's Weekly wood engraving showing a long line of Black men waiting to cast ballots at the polling table in the Reconstruction South. A white election official oversees the vote as each voter steps forward to drop a ballot into a glass ballot bowl."

State what the picture shows, part by part: the line, the table, the official, and the act each man performs as he steps forward. The claim here is made by arrangement, not by argument. Waud drew Black voters dignified, patient, and orderly for the readers of the North's most popular illustrated newspaper, in the year the Reconstruction Acts first put Black men on the rolls. What fear in that readership is the arrangement answering, and what would the picture have claimed if the line had been drawn crowding, shouting, or turned away at the table?

Station 4: "Emancipation," by Thomas Nast, 1863 and 1865

Source: Thomas Nast, 'Emancipation,' 1865 (Library of Congress; image described)., as carried in the audited TPSP bank record, which presents the image by description. Nast first drew the design for Harper's Weekly in January 1863, as the Emancipation Proclamation took effect, and Philadelphia printers King & Baird reissued it as a large print in 1865, with Lincoln's portrait at the center. The print is taught from the bank's description and is not reproduced here. Its scenes of enslavement include a whipping, and this unit teaches that violence in text and testimony, not as a picture on a classroom page.

"A large commemorative wood engraving by Thomas Nast contrasting slavery's brutal past with an imagined free future. On the left, scenes of enslavement, including a slave auction, a whipping, and fugitives being hunted. On the right, scenes of freed life, including Black children in school and farmers being paid for their labor. In the center, a Black family sits before a stove labeled 'Union' beneath Lincoln's portrait, with Columbia, personification of the nation, presiding."

Rebuild the print's design from the description: what stands on the left, what stands on the right, and what occupies the center, naming the figure who presides and the portrait above the family. The pairing is the claim. State what the left side says freedom ended and what the right side says freedom begins, and then state whose imagination is doing the claiming: a northern artist, drawing for northern buyers, picturing a future for people who are shown but do not speak in it. Set this station beside Station 2. What can Revels claim in his own voice that Nast's hopeful center cannot claim for him?

Station 5: The South Carolina Convention and Legislature, 1868 (Teaching Summary)

Source: a teaching summary built from the public record of the 1868 South Carolina constitutional convention and the legislature elected under its constitution. It is a summary, not a quotation, and it is labeled as one here so that nothing in it is mistaken for a period text.

In January 1868 the voters registered under the Reconstruction Acts chose a constitutional convention for South Carolina, and 124 delegates met at Charleston. Seventy six of them were Black men, the only Black majority in any of the South's Reconstruction conventions. The constitution they wrote and the voters ratified that spring established the state's first public school system, opened the vote to all adult male citizens without a property test, abolished imprisonment for debt, and based representation on population rather than on a mix of population and wealth. The legislature elected under it held a Black majority in its lower house, the only one in the South, and its members included men who had been enslaved within the decade. That body funded the new schools, wrote civil rights protections into state law, and ran a state government whose electorate, officers, and lawmakers had all been made within a single year of registration.

State the summary's three hard facts: the convention's size and its Black majority, two things its constitution established, and the lower house's distinction among southern legislatures. The claim at this station is made by a roster rather than by a speech or a picture. A skeptical reader in 1870 could dispute a cartoon or discount a senator as an exception. What is harder to dispute about a majority, counted delegate by delegate, writing a constitution and then sitting in the legislature it created?

Part 2: The Closing Verdict

The five stations, with the reading, are the evidence for your judgment of what the new electorate claimed, and who claimed it. Sort first, then judge.

The sorting. Enter each station number under what it claims, in one line each, with one quoted phrase, one named detail from the description or summary, or one counted fact as the evidence: the Constitution itself now forbids racial denial of the vote, prejudice has no justifying cause, Black voters act with the order citizenship requires, freedom's future as a northern artist imagines it, and Black lawmakers govern in their own majority. For each, name who is making the claim.

Your verdict. Judge which record a skeptical northern voter of 1870 would have found hardest to answer. Defend the judgment in a paragraph. Your verdict must use at least three stations' evidence, cited by number, and it must account for Station 1, where the claim is made by constitutional text rather than by any person's conduct, and for Station 4, where the claim is made by a white artist's imagination rather than by Black citizens themselves.

The strongest counterclaim. A classmate argues for the record you did not choose, and adds that a voter is convinced by what officials do, not by texts, speeches, or pictures. State that claim at full strength from the documents, and then answer it: what in your chosen record survives the objection, and what in the record of 1870, the ratified amendment and the seated senator among it, limits how far the objection can run?

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: The Joint Resolution Proposing the Fifteenth Amendment

The protection, the grounds, and the silence. The resolution proposes an article under which the right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any state on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude, and Section 2 gives Congress power to enforce the article by appropriate legislation. The text grants the vote to no one; it forbids three grounds of denial. A state bent on closing the polls is therefore left free to try any test that names no racial ground, a literacy test, a poll tax, a property or residency rule, and to administer it against Black voters while defending its text as neutral. The narrowness was the price of ratification: a wider article, granting the vote outright or barring tests of literacy and property, could not have carried two thirds of Congress and three fourths of the legislatures in 1869, and students should see the loophole and the coalition arithmetic as one fact, not as an oversight.

Station 2: Senator Revels on the District of Columbia Schools

The report, the argument, and the kindness. Revels reports that the trustees of the colored schools and some of the most intelligent colored men of the city had told him they would have asked long before for a bill abolishing the separate colored schools and putting all children on an equality in the common schools, if they had thought they could obtain it; fear that they could not is the only reason they had not asked. His argument runs through a question he asks twice, word for word: have the colored people done anything to justify the prejudice against them? He answers it himself, no, sir, and if prejudice has no cause to justify it, the Senate must admit it is wicked, wrong, and without the approval of Heaven. The kindness is built for the white senators who hold his bill's fate and for the northern newspaper readers behind them: it denies opponents the excuse of his anger, so the vote against him must be cast against a request made without bitterness, and the record preserves that fact even in defeat.

Station 3: "The First Vote," by Alfred R. Waud

The line, the bowl, and the fear answered. The engraving shows a long line of Black men waiting at a polling table in the Reconstruction South, a white election official overseeing the vote, and each voter stepping forward in turn to drop his ballot into a glass ballot bowl. Nobody pushes, nobody is turned away, and the voter at the table performs the same act the reader performs. The arrangement answers the northern fear, fed by Democratic print all through 1867, that Black suffrage meant disorder, ignorance at the polls, and elections as mob scenes. Waud's voters are workers and a soldier among them, drawn dignified and composed, doing a constitutional act in constitutional form. Drawn crowding or shouting, the same line would have claimed that the new electorate was a danger to be contained; drawn turned away, it would have claimed the experiment had already failed. The picture's order is its argument, and students should be pressed to say that the argument is made by a white artist for a white readership, which is both its persuasive design and its limit.

Station 4: "Emancipation," by Thomas Nast

The pairing, the center, and the imagining voice. On the left, scenes of enslavement: a slave auction, a whipping, and fugitives hunted. On the right, scenes of freed life: Black children in school and farmers paid for their labor. At the center a Black family sits before a stove labeled "Union" beneath Lincoln's portrait, with Columbia, the personification of the nation, presiding over the whole design. The left says freedom ended sale, violence, and pursuit; the right says freedom begins in the schoolroom and the paid field, with the family at home under the nation's eye as the design's heart. The claiming voice is Thomas Nast's, a northern Unionist artist drawing first for Harper's Weekly in 1863 and then for the buyers of a celebratory print in 1865: the freed family is imagined, honored, and silent. Beside Station 2 the limit is plain. Revels can state what Black Washingtonians actually asked for, report their fear of asking, and call prejudice wicked in the Senate's own record. Nast's center can picture a welcome. It cannot report a demand, and the difference between being pictured kindly and speaking is one of the lesson's central distinctions.

Station 5: The South Carolina Convention and Legislature, 1868 (Teaching Summary)

The counted facts and the roster's claim. The convention numbered 124 delegates, 76 of them Black men, the only Black majority in any southern Reconstruction convention. Its constitution established the state's first public school system and opened the vote to all adult male citizens without a property test, alongside the abolition of imprisonment for debt and representation based on population. The lower house of the legislature elected under it held a Black majority, the only one in the South. A roster claims by enumeration rather than by eloquence or by art. A cartoon can be called a fantasy and a senator can be called an exception placed in Washington by a legislature, but a majority of 76 named delegates who wrote a constitution, and a house of lawmakers who then governed under it, cannot be answered as decoration. The claim is also the most exposed one in the file, because a majority that governs can be voted out, driven out, or dismantled, as the later lessons of this unit record.

Part 2: The Closing Verdict (Grading Guide)

Full credit should be awarded if the verdict:

  • Sorts every station with one quoted phrase, described detail, or counted fact for each, and names the maker of each claim, Congress, Revels, Waud, Nast, or the South Carolina electorate and its delegates.
  • Chooses one record as hardest for a skeptical northern voter of 1870 to answer, and defends the choice from at least three stations, cited by number, rather than from the era's reputation in general.
  • Accounts for Station 1: the amendment's claim does not depend on any voter's conduct or any artist's skill, because it is constitutional text, ratified in February 1870, with an enforcement clause; a verdict that ignores it has skipped the one record a skeptic must answer as a citizen rather than as a critic.
  • Accounts for Station 4: Nast's print is the file's most hopeful record and its most answerable, because its future is imagined by a northern artist for northern buyers; a verdict choosing it must explain why an imagining could outweigh records of acts, and a verdict against it must still explain its power in 1865 and 1870.
  • States the strongest rival claim at full strength before answering it. For a Station 2 verdict: a single senator's speech, however unanswerable in logic, lost its vote, and skeptics could call Revels the exception his enemies said he was. For a Station 5 verdict: a majority in one state's lower house is one state, and its constitution could be and later was replaced. For a Station 1 verdict: a text that leaves literacy and property tests standing hands the skeptic his answer in its own silence.
  • Answers the rival by naming what it cannot hold, most often the accumulation of 1870: by that year the skeptic faced not one record but all five kinds at once, a ratified amendment, a seated senator arguing equality on the floor, an orderly electorate in the North's own newspaper, and a Black majority governing a state, each record covering the weakness of the others.

Teacher note on the verdicts. The most common failure is a verdict argued from Station 3 alone, in which the picture's orderliness is treated as a report of how elections went everywhere. Station 3 is an argument drawn for a readership, and strong answers say so while still crediting its force. The second failure is treating the teaching summary at Station 5 as a period quotation. It is labeled a summary on its face, and its facts may be used, but its sentences may not be quoted as the words of 1868.
