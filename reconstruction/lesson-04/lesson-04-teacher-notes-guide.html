The New Political Order

Reconstruction · Lesson 4 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Reconstruction, Lesson 4 of 8 — teach it fourth in the unit, after Congress Takes Charge (Lesson 3) and before Freedom on the Ground (Lesson 5). The Reconstruction Acts ordered the registration in Lesson 3; this lesson follows the electorate that registration made as it organized, wrote constitutions, staffed governments, and carried men to the Senate and a governor's chair, while Congress proposed and the states ratified the Fifteenth Amendment. The lesson question asks who governed the reconstructed South and what that amendment settled and left open, and the lesson holds the achievement and the opening together from the first slide to the last.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 3 (the voters the Reconstruction Acts registered open this lesson, governing) and Lesson 5 (land and labor, the questions registration did not settle, follow next). Inside the lesson, the activity, The New Electorate Speaks and Is Pictured, carries the Fifteenth Amendment's joint resolution and Revels's schools speech from the audited bank records, Waud's First Vote and Nast's Emancipation as the bank presents them, and a labeled teaching summary of the South Carolina convention and legislature. The writing task, Settled in Text, Resting on Protection, turns the lesson question into a judgment defended from the bank.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: the Reconstruction Acts and military districts and the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship from Lesson 3, and emancipation from Lessons 1 and 2. Every figure in the lesson is taught as the documents state it — some 700,000 voters enrolled in 1867, 76 of 124 delegates in South Carolina, a seating vote of 48 to 8, five weeks in a governor's chair, three grounds forbidden. No image of violence is used anywhere in the lesson, in a document, the deck, or these notes, following the unit's image discipline. No verified public domain image of a Union League chapter meeting was located for this lesson, so the League is carried in prose and in text slides rather than pictured with an unverified image.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (a constitutional amendment's text, a Senate speech, an engraving, and a legislature's composite portrait read as one political order), D2.His.16 (Black voters, delegates, and officeholders as historical actors building governments), D2.Civ.12 (a registered electorate and the coalition it built, against the hostile names aimed at both), D2.Civ.14 (an amendment read for what its words forbid and what they leave standing); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.8, WHST.9-10.1 (a judgment on what the settlement of 1870 settled and left open, defended from the records).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain how registration under the Reconstruction Acts enrolled some 700,000 Black voters in a single year, 1867, and how the Union League organized that electorate in churches and schoolhouses; state what the conventions of 1867 and 1868 wrote, including public schools and civil rights, and name the governing coalition and the hostile names it carried, carpetbaggers and scalawags; follow the roster at the top, Revels seated in the Senate in 1870 and arguing in February 1871 that prejudice has no justifying cause, Pinchback governing Louisiana for five weeks in the winter of 1872 and 1873, and Bruce serving a full Senate term from 1875 to 1881; and read the Fifteenth Amendment closely, for the three grounds it forbids, the enforcement power it gives Congress, and the grounds and the voters it leaves standing outside its text.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1-3) 5 minutes; an electorate made in a year (slides 4-6) 5 minutes; the leagues, the conventions, and the coalition (slides 7-11) 9 minutes; Revels takes a Senate seat (slides 12-14) 7 minutes; a governor and a full term (slides 15-17) 6 minutes; the Fifteenth Amendment (slides 18-21) 9 minutes; what the new order rested on (slides 22-23) 4 minutes; vocabulary and exit (slides 24-29) 5 minutes. The activity takes a second period if the schedule allows. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes, and The New Electorate Speaks and Is Pictured (five stations). The notebook page charts the electorate, the conventions, the coalition, the roster, and the amendment in stages; the quick check is "The Amendment and the Senator: the text of 1870 beside the argument of 1871"; the writing task is "Settled in Text, Resting on Protection"; the exit ticket has three parts — the League and the amendment defined, the coalition stated in two sentences, and the lesson question answered with the achievement and the opening held together.

Key vocabulary

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Title: The New Political Order

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title "The New Political Order"; the standfirst — "The vote, the coalition, and the men it carried to the Senate and a governor's chair."

  • Picture: Alfred R. Waud's wood engraving The First Vote, published in Harper's Weekly on November 16, 1867, from the Library of Congress copy. It pictures Black men, in a line, casting ballots under law, two years after emancipation.
  • [Open with what the picture is already teaching before a word is said.] Black men, in a line, casting ballots under law, two years after emancipation. This lesson follows that line to where it led.
  • Within three years the men in it and their neighbors across the South had written constitutions, staffed governments, sent the first Black member to Congress, and placed a Black governor in a statehouse, while Congress proposed and the states ratified the Fifteenth Amendment.
  • The lesson question asks who governed the reconstructed South and what that amendment settled and left open, and the lesson holds the achievement and the opening together from the first slide to the last.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "Who governed the reconstructed South, and what did the Fifteenth Amendment settle and leave open?"
  • The lesson question has two halves, and students should keep them separate all period.
  • Who governed the reconstructed South is a question about people: an electorate of some seven hundred thousand new Black voters, the Union League that organized them, the coalition they built with northern newcomers and white southern Republicans, and the officeholders that coalition raised, Revels, Pinchback, and Bruce among them.
  • What the Fifteenth Amendment settled and left open is a question about a text: it settled that the vote could not be denied on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude, and it left open every other ground of denial, together with the question of who would enforce it.
  • The unit question stands behind both halves, because this is the lesson where Reconstruction's settlement reaches its full height and its limit comes into view at the same time.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "An electorate, a coalition, an amendment"; the seven section rows, A-G.

  • The map follows the guided notes section by section. Section A builds the electorate in a single year of registration under the Reconstruction Acts.
  • Section B gives the organization, the Union League, and the conventions and constitutions of 1867 and 1868. Section C names the governing coalition and the hostile names it carried.
  • Section D takes Revels to the Senate and to his 1871 argument for equal schools in the District of Columbia. Section E completes the roster with Pinchback's five weeks as acting governor and Bruce's full Senate term.
  • Section F reads the Fifteenth Amendment closely, for what it forbids and for the grounds it leaves standing. Section G closes on the order's foundation, federal law and federal protection, and on the question its survival hung on, whether the North would keep enforcing what it had written.

Slide 4 · Section A divider: An Electorate Made in a Year

On the slide: the First Vote portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Registration under the Reconstruction Acts, and a line of new voters forms across the South."

  • Picture: A portrait crop of Waud's First Vote, the engraving students will study at Station 3 of the activity.
  • [Teach the registration as an act performed by hand.] Students tend to picture an electorate appearing by proclamation. It did not.
  • The Reconstruction Acts ordered registration across the ten unreconstructed states, army officers ran the boards, and men who had been enslaved walked to courthouses and schoolhouses to give their names. Many had never held a legal surname of their own choosing in a public record.
  • The line in Waud's picture is orderly because the men in it understood the act's weight. By the fall of 1867 the rolls held an electorate that had not existed a year earlier, and everything else in this lesson — conventions, constitutions, governments, and senators — was built on those rolls.

Slide 5 · Registration: names on the rolls, 1867

On the slide: the First Vote portrait at right; five bullets — registration boards opened across the ten unreconstructed states; army officers supervised the enrollment; Black registrants formed a majority in several states; the registered rolls chose the conventions of 1867; the vote arrived first, before land or wages.

  • Picture: Waud's First Vote, Harper's Weekly, November 1867 — the new electorate at the polls, the registration of this section in operation.
  • [Read the bullets as a sequence of cause and result.] The acts supplied the legal order, military supervision made registration possible in counties whose officials would not have run it, and the men who registered supplied the political fact.
  • In South Carolina, Georgia, Louisiana, Florida, and Alabama, Black registrants formed a majority or near majority of the new electorate, a fact to state plainly because it explains both the governments that followed and the fury aimed at them.
  • Registration also created the voter lists from which convention delegates were chosen in the fall elections of 1867.
  • [Note what registration did not do.] It conferred no land, no wages, and no protection once the boards closed. The vote arrived first and stood, for the moment, in front of every other question freedom had raised, which is why this lesson treats it as the new order's foundation and its most exposed wall.

Slide 6 · Stat: seven hundred thousand voters

On the slide: the big figure "700,000"; the label — "Black voters enrolled in a single year, 1867"; the italic note — enrolled where none had voted before, in states that had counted them as property.

  • 700,000 Black voters enrolled in a single year, 1867.
  • [State the number slowly.] In a single registration season, under the Reconstruction Acts, some seven hundred thousand Black men enrolled as voters in the ten states the acts covered, where two years earlier nearly all of them had been enslaved and none had voted.
  • Set the number beside the country's scale: the entire national electorate in 1868 numbered under six million, so the registration added more than a tenth of a nation's voters in one year, in one region, from a people the law had previously counted as property for representation and as noncitizens for rights.
  • Politicians in both parties did this arithmetic at once. Republicans gained an electorate bound to the party of emancipation. Their opponents gained a target, and the counterrevolution of the later lessons begins with attempts to strike names back off these rolls.

Slide 7 · Section B divider: The Leagues and the Conventions

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "The Union League organizes the new electorate in churches and schoolhouses, and the conventions write the South's most democratic charters."

  • [Introduce the League as the machine that turned a roll into a party.] Registration alone elects nobody.
  • The Union League began as a northern patriotic organization and spread South from 1867 under the Loyal League name, carried by teachers, ministers, Bureau agents, and Black organizers themselves.
  • Its chapters met where Black communities already gathered, in churches and schoolhouses, often at night and sometimes in secret where employers or landlords watched.
  • A meeting taught the mechanics of the new politics: how a ballot worked, what a convention was, which candidates the chapter would support, and why the ticket had to be voted whole. The League also disciplined its electorate, turning out voters on election day and protecting them in groups on the road to the polls.
  • When the conventions of 1867 and 1868 produced governments rather than petitions, the League's chapters are a large part of the reason why.
  • Say: "Union League: the organization of patriotic clubs, spread through the South from 1867 under the Loyal League name, which organized Black voters and white Unionists into the Republican Party, meeting in churches and schoolhouses to register voters, teach the new politics, and turn out the electorate the Reconstruction Acts created."

Slide 8 · Organization, then constitutions

On the slide: five bullets — Union League chapters spread under the Loyal League name; members taught ballots, conventions, and tickets at night; Black delegates sat in every convention; Black delegates held a majority in South Carolina's convention; public schools and civil rights were written into law.

  • [Take the two halves in order.] The conventions came first: delegates elected in the fall of 1867 met through the winter, with Black delegates in every convention and a majority in South Carolina's, and wrote constitutions that were, as the reading says, the most democratic charters the South had known.
  • Public school systems appear here as a constitutional creation, not a charity: the same electorate that had been forbidden literacy under slavery taxed itself, through its conventions, for schools open to all children.
  • Civil rights in state law and taxation shifted onto land and wealth belong on the same list, because they show the conventions governing the whole of a state's life, not holding a ceremony.
  • Then the governments: legislatures, governors, judges, sheriffs, and school officers elected under those charters, the subject of the next section, which names the coalition that staffed them and the names its enemies gave it.
  • Note: No verified public domain image of a Union League chapter meeting was located for this lesson, so this slide stays text by judgment and the League is carried in prose.

Slide 9 · Divider: A Legislature, Counted

On the slide: the South Carolina legislature portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "The composite portrait of South Carolina's first legislature after the war: a roster, not a symbol."

  • Picture: A portrait crop of the composite photograph of the Radical members of the first South Carolina legislature after the war, held by the Library of Congress. The print is cataloged with the date 1876 and portrays members of the legislature first elected in 1868; describe it by its cataloged title rather than by a date of sitting.
  • [Make the methodological point students will need at Station 5 of the activity.] This is a roster rendered as a picture: row on row of named men, most of them Black, each holding a seat in a legislature created by the constitution of 1868.
  • A cartoon can be dismissed as an artist's hope, and a single senator can be dismissed as an exception, but a legislature is a counted body that passed laws, raised taxes, and funded schools.
  • South Carolina's lower house held the South's only Black majority, and its members included men who had been enslaved within the decade. When opponents wanted to argue that Black government was a fiction staged by outsiders, this photograph was the standing answer.

Slide 10 · Three groups, two hostile names

On the slide: the South Carolina legislature portrait at right; five bullets — Black voters formed the largest bloc in most states; northern newcomers were called carpetbaggers; white southern Republicans were called scalawags; the coalition placed officeholders by the hundreds in the legislatures; the names were weapons, and they should be read as evidence.

  • Picture: The composite portrait of South Carolina's first legislature after the war, held by the Library of Congress — the coalition's officeholders counted in rows.
  • [Teach the two hostile names as evidence, because that is what they are.] Carpetbaggers and scalawags were not neutral descriptions coined by historians. They were weapons words of the era, built to make the coalition's members illegitimate by origin: the northerner as a thief with a suitcase, the white southern Republican as a traitor and a worthless animal, the word scalawag's older meaning.
  • Behind the names stood real and varied people, teachers and investors among the northerners, Unionists who had opposed secession at real cost among the southerners.
  • And behind both stood the coalition's largest bloc, Black voters, who needed no nickname because their presence was the fact the names were designed to distract from.
  • Students who can say who coined a label, and what work it was meant to do, hold the section. The governments these three groups built together are what the labels were trying to unmake in advance.
  • Say: "carpetbaggers: the hostile name white southern Democrats gave northern men who came South during Reconstruction, teachers, ministers, merchants, investors, Union veterans, and office seekers among them, pictured in Democratic print as adventurers carrying everything they owned in a carpetbag."
  • Say: "scalawags: the hostile name white southern Democrats gave white southerners who joined the Republican Party, upcountry Unionists, former Whigs, and poor farmers among them, whose votes, alongside Black voters and northern newcomers, built the Reconstruction governments."

Slide 11 · Stat: seventy six of one hundred twenty four

On the slide: the big figure "76 of 124"; the label — "delegates to South Carolina's convention of 1868 were Black men"; the italic note — the only Black majority in any southern Reconstruction convention.

  • 76 of 124 delegates to South Carolina's convention of 1868 were Black men.
  • South Carolina's constitutional convention of 1868 seated 124 delegates, and 76 of them were Black men, the only Black majority in any southern Reconstruction convention.
  • Many of the 76 had been enslaved within the decade; some had bought their freedom, some had been freed by the war, and all of them now sat to write a state's fundamental law.
  • The constitution they produced founded public schools, opened the vote without a property test, abolished imprisonment for debt, and counted population for representation.
  • Hold the fraction beside the unit's other great fraction, the one vote by which Andrew Johnson escaped conviction in Lesson 3. There, a single vote preserved a president. Here, a majority of votes wrote a constitution. Both fractions are the era in miniature: its outcomes turned on counted people, which is why control of the rolls became the counterrevolution's first object.

Slide 12 · Section D divider: Revels Takes a Senate Seat

On the slide: the Revels portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "A Mississippi minister who recruited Black regiments takes the seat Jefferson Davis had left."

  • Picture: Hiram Rhodes Revels, photographed by the Brady-Handy studio, held by the Library of Congress in its Brady-Handy collection.
  • [Build the man before the milestone.] Revels is too often taught as a symbol with no biography. He was freeborn in North Carolina, educated in seminaries in Indiana and Illinois, ordained a minister, and imprisoned in Missouri in 1854 for preaching to enslaved people, a fact from his own recollection that students should hear.
  • In the war he recruited Black regiments in Maryland and Missouri and served as a chaplain, present at Vicksburg.
  • Settling at Natchez after the war, he rose quickly: alderman in 1868, state senator in 1869, and then the choice of Mississippi's legislature for the United States Senate.
  • The speed was not accident. The new electorate's representatives were selecting men whose lives already joined the ministry, the army, and the schoolroom, the institutions freedom was building.
  • Say: "Hiram Rhodes Revels: the Mississippi minister, freeborn in North Carolina, who had recruited Black regiments and served as a Union army chaplain, elected to the United States Senate in 1870 as the first Black member of Congress, seated in the chamber Jefferson Davis had left, where he argued for desegregating the District of Columbia schools."

Slide 13 · Seated, 48 to 8, February 1870

On the slide: the Revels portrait at right; five bullets — the Mississippi legislature elected him to the Senate; he became the first Black member of Congress; opponents cited Dred Scott and the nine-year citizenship requirement; the Senate seated him on the authority of the new Constitution; he took the seat Jefferson Davis had held.

  • Picture: Hiram Rhodes Revels, in the Brady-Handy photograph held by the Library of Congress.
  • [State the seating fight, because it is the Fourteenth Amendment's meaning tested in a single vote.] When Revels arrived in Washington in February 1870, Senate Democrats argued he could not be seated: under the Dred Scott decision, they said, Black men had become citizens only with the Fourteenth Amendment in 1868, and a senator must have been a citizen for nine years.
  • The argument asked the Senate to treat Dred Scott as living law for the purpose of excluding the first man whose election refuted it.
  • Republicans answered that citizenship had come with the war's settlement and the amendment's text, and the Senate voted 48 to 8 to seat him.
  • He took the Mississippi seat left vacant at secession, the seat Jefferson Davis had held. Students should hold the two facts together: the chamber admitted him on the new Constitution's authority, and it did so over an objection built from the old one's most notorious decision.

Slide 14 · February 1871: prejudice has no justifying cause

On the slide: five bullets — the trustees' report called for equality in the common schools; they had not asked earlier, fearing they could not obtain it; prejudice fell equally on Black people, whatever their conduct; Revels argued that prejudice had to be admitted wicked, and wrong; his side lost the vote.

  • [Give the speech a full teaching pass.] The speech students read at Station 2 of the activity is the citizenship claim argued at its highest level.
  • Revels reports first, as a witness: the trustees of the District's colored schools and leading Black men of the city had told him they wanted the separate schools abolished and all children placed on an equality in the common schools, and had not asked earlier only because they feared they could not obtain it.
  • Then he argues: prejudice falls equally on the refined and on the most degraded, whatever a Black person's conduct, so it cannot be an answer to anything its target has done; if it has no justifying cause, it must be admitted wicked and wrong.
  • His side lost the vote, and the District's schools stayed separate. The loss belongs in the teaching beside the argument, as the first measure of the distance between the settlement's logic and its enforcement.

Slide 15 · Section E divider: A Governor and a Full Term

On the slide: the Pinchback portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Louisiana carries the new order to the governor's chair, and Mississippi sends a senator for a full term."

  • Picture: P. B. S. Pinchback, photographed by the Brady-Handy studio, held by the Library of Congress, whose catalog description names him Governor Pinchback.
  • [Introduce Louisiana's fact as the product of a career, not a curiosity.] Pinchback was freeborn in Georgia, schooled in Cincinnati, and a steamboat man on the Mississippi before the war made him one of the Union army's few Black commissioned officers, a captain in the Corps d'Afrique.
  • He arrived in New Orleans in 1862 and stayed to build a political life: the constitutional convention of 1867 and 1868, the state senate, its presidency, and then the lieutenant governorship when Oscar Dunn's death left it vacant.
  • Every step ran through the new electorate Louisiana's registration had made. When the governorship opened in December 1872, the man in line for it was there because the machinery of the new order had carried him, office by office, to the top of it.
  • Say: "P. B. S. Pinchback: the Louisiana leader, freeborn in Georgia and a Union officer in the war, who rose through the state senate to serve as acting governor of Louisiana for five weeks in the winter of 1872 and 1873, the first Black man to govern an American state."

Slide 16 · Pinchback: five weeks in the governor's chair

On the slide: the Pinchback portrait at right; five bullets — he was freeborn in Georgia and served as a Union captain in the war; he became state senate president, then lieutenant governor; he served as acting governor from December 9, 1872, to January 13, 1873; he was the first Black man to govern an American state; he governed as a caretaker in a contested state.

  • Picture: P. B. S. Pinchback, in the Brady-Handy photograph held by the Library of Congress.
  • [Narrate the five weeks precisely, because their brevity is part of their meaning.] Governor Henry Clay Warmoth, a Republican whose feud with his own party's Custom House faction had split Louisiana's government, was impeached by the state house in December 1872 and suspended from office with weeks left in his term.
  • Pinchback, as acting lieutenant governor, took the governor's chair on December 9, 1872, the first Black man to govern an American state, and held it until January 13, 1873, while rival claimants organized a competing government and the state's politics moved toward the violence of the following spring.
  • He governed as a caretaker in a crisis, keeping the administration running, and then stepped down when the term ended.
  • Students should resist two easy readings: the five weeks were not a token, because the office and its powers were real, and they were not secure, because the settlement that produced them was already contested by armed politics in his own state.

Slide 17 · Bruce: the full term, 1875 to 1881

On the slide: the Bruce portrait at right; five bullets — he was born enslaved in Virginia in 1841; he served as registrar, sheriff, and superintendent of education; Mississippi elected him to the Senate in 1874; he was the first Black senator to serve a full term; he took his seat as the settlement was being dismantled.

  • Picture: Blanche K. Bruce, photographed by Mathew Brady, held by the Library of Congress in the Brady-Handy collection.
  • Bruce closes the roster by completing it: where Revels served the remainder of a term and Pinchback served five weeks, Bruce served six full years in the Senate, from 1875 to 1881, working committees, presiding over the Senate itself in 1879, and becoming a figure of national standing.
  • His life states the era's distance in one line: born enslaved in Virginia in 1841, he had escaped to freedom during the war, taught, farmed, and risen through Mississippi offices — registrar, tax assessor, sheriff, and superintendent of education — before the legislature elected him senator in 1874.
  • [Teach the date hard.] He was elected as the settlement was already being dismantled, after the Panic of 1873 and in the year of the White League's rise in Louisiana, and he served his full term anyway, a working senator inside a retreating settlement.
  • Say: "Blanche K. Bruce: the Mississippi officeholder born enslaved in Virginia, who rose through tax and education offices to election to the United States Senate in 1874, the second Black senator and the first to serve a full term, seated while the settlement that elected him was already being dismantled."

Slide 18 · Section F divider: The Fifteenth Amendment

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Proposed in February 1869 and ratified in February 1870, the third and last of the Reconstruction amendments, read here word by word."

  • [Frame the amendment as a drafted text before it is a symbol.] Its wording is the lesson's legal core.
  • By 1869 the vote stood on statutes and on the Reconstruction Acts' registration, which Congress could repeal and which applied, as commands, only to the states under the acts. Black men in the border states and the North itself still faced racial bars in several states.
  • Republicans therefore wrote the rule into the Constitution, and they wrote it in the form the next two slides examine: a prohibition, not a grant, forbidding denial on three named grounds and arming Congress to enforce the prohibition.
  • The drafting choices were made by men counting ratification votes in state legislatures, and every limit in the text has that arithmetic inside it. What looks like caution was also the maximum the coalition of 1869 could carry, which is a distinction students should learn to make about constitutional texts generally.
  • Say: "Fifteenth Amendment: the amendment Congress proposed in February 1869 and the states ratified in February 1870, which forbade the United States and the states to deny or abridge a citizen's right to vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude, the third and last of the Reconstruction amendments."

Slide 19 · Section 1, and the power behind it

On the slide: five bullets — Section 1 protected the right of citizens to vote; that right could not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any state; denial on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude was forbidden; Section 2 gave Congress the power to enforce the article; the amendment was a prohibition, not a grant of the vote.

  • [Read Section 1 word by word with the class.] The right protected is the right of citizens to vote, and the actors bound are the United States and any state.
  • The grounds forbidden are three: race, color, or previous condition of servitude, the last phrase reaching the man whose grandfather had been free and the man freed in 1865 with the same sentence.
  • Section 2 then hands Congress the enforcement power, the clause on which the Enforcement Acts of the next lesson would rest.
  • [Name the text's restraint as design rather than failure, before students reach the next slide.] An amendment granting the vote affirmatively, or barring literacy and property tests by name, had been proposed in the debates and had failed to command two thirds of Congress.
  • The resolution that passed, and that three fourths of the states ratified by February 3, 1870, was this one, and the lesson reads it as its makers left it, strong on its three grounds and silent on the rest.

Slide 20 · What the wording leaves standing

On the slide: five bullets — literacy tests and poll taxes named no racial ground; they were written neutrally and aimed at the new electorate; within a generation, the polls closed again to many Black voters; the woman suffrage alliance broke over the amendment's text; the amendment protected men's votes and said nothing of women's.

  • [Give the silences the same close reading as the text.] Because the amendment forbids only racial grounds, a state could close its polls with a literacy test, a poll tax, or a property rule written without a racial word, and defend the law as permitted by the amendment's own terms, which is precisely the road several southern states took within a generation, once federal protection had been withdrawn.
  • The second silence is about women. The amendment protects male citizens in practice and says nothing of sex, and the old alliance between abolition and woman suffrage broke over it, with leaders who had worked together since the antislavery conventions dividing over whether to support an amendment that left women outside.
  • State the split plainly, without taking a side in it for the class: it is the record of a coalition deciding whose claim came first, and of the cost that decision charged to both movements afterward.

Slide 21 · Stat: three grounds forbidden

On the slide: the big figure "3"; the label — "grounds of denial the amendment forbids"; the italic note — race, color, previous condition of servitude, and every other ground left standing.

  • 3 grounds of denial the amendment forbids.
  • Race · color · previous condition of servitude.
  • Three is a small number carrying a large consequence, and this slide should slow the class down on it. The amendment names the grounds on which the old exclusion had actually operated, the grounds written into the Black Codes' world and into state constitutions that had barred Black voters by name.
  • Against those three, its prohibition is complete, and no state has lawfully denied the vote in those words since.
  • But a prohibition with a list invites the reader to ask about everything off the list, and the states that later disfranchised their Black electorates worked from the off list items, literacy, taxes, and tests of understanding administered by hostile registrars.
  • The number therefore teaches the lesson's method in miniature. Constitutional settlements settle what their words reach. The politics of the following decades is fought, again and again, in the space between what a text forbids and what it leaves standing.

Slide 22 · Section G divider: What the New Order Rested On

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Federal law made it, federal presence guarded it, and its survival hung on a national will to keep enforcing it."

  • [Return to the line in Waud's picture, and ask what stood behind it.] Behind the voter at the table stood a registration an army had supervised, a League chapter that had taught him the ticket, a constitution his delegates had written, and, after 1870, an amendment with an enforcement clause.
  • Ask: "What happens to each of those supports if the federal presence is removed?" Remove the federal presence from that list and each remaining item weakens: registrars could refuse names, employers and night riders could punish voters, and state officials could administer tests the amendment's text allowed.
  • The new governments knew this and asked Washington for protection repeatedly, which is not a sign of their failure to stand but a statement of what they were: governments built by a national settlement, inside states where a large part of the white population rejected their legitimacy from the first day.
  • The question Section G leaves open is whether the nation that built them would stay.

Slide 23 · The achievement and its foundation

On the slide: five bullets — an electorate was enrolled, and constitutions were written; schools were founded, and officeholders served at every level; an amendment was ratified with an enforcement clause; all of it rested on federal protection; power given was not yet power used.

  • [Read the bullets as the lesson's ledger.] On one side: an electorate enrolled, constitutions written, schools founded, officeholders seated at every level up to the Senate and a governor's chair, and a constitutional amendment ratified, achievements no honest account can shrink.
  • On the other side, a single line: all of it rested on federal protection the new governments could not supply themselves, because the army was small and withdrawing, the marshals were few, and the northern electorate's attention was already turning to hard times at home.
  • The Fifteenth Amendment gave Congress power to enforce the vote, and power given is not power used.
  • The next lessons take up what happened when the using faltered: the Klan's terror, the Enforcement Acts that answered it, and the retreat that followed. Students should leave this lesson able to state the height and the foundation in one breath, because the unit's essential question will be answered from exactly that pairing.

Slide 24 · The new order at a glance

On the slide: the title "An electorate, a coalition, an amendment"; four rows — electorate and charters; the coalition; the roster; the amendment.

  • This slide mirrors the closing table of the guided notes, The New Order at a Glance, and students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • An electorate and its charters: registration enrolled some seven hundred thousand Black voters in 1867, the League organized them, and the conventions wrote constitutions founding public schools and civil rights.
  • The coalition and its names: Black voters with northern newcomers called carpetbaggers and white southern Republicans called scalawags. The roster at the top: Revels in Davis's Senate seat in 1870, Pinchback governing Louisiana in 1872 and 1873, Bruce serving a full term from 1875.
  • The amendment and its limits: proposed in 1869, ratified in 1870, forbidding three grounds of denial, arming Congress, and leaving every other ground standing for later use.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: the amendment and the senator

On the slide: two terms with their short forms — Fifteenth Amendment (proposed 1869, ratified 1870), Hiram Rhodes Revels (the first Black member of Congress, 1870). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Two terms, in full, in the guided notes wording. Notice how much of the lesson each definition carries: the amendment's definition is its text and dates, and Revels's is a whole career in one sentence.
  • Say: "Fifteenth Amendment: the amendment Congress proposed in February 1869 and the states ratified in February 1870, which forbade the United States and the states to deny or abridge a citizen's right to vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude, the third and last of the Reconstruction amendments."
  • Say: "Hiram Rhodes Revels: the Mississippi minister, freeborn in North Carolina, who had recruited Black regiments and served as a Union army chaplain, elected to the United States Senate in 1870 as the first Black member of Congress, seated in the chamber Jefferson Davis had left, where he argued for desegregating the District of Columbia schools."

Slide 26 · Vocabulary: the governor and the full term

On the slide: two terms with their short forms — Blanche K. Bruce (the first full Senate term, from 1875), P. B. S. Pinchback (acting governor of Louisiana, five weeks). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Two more terms, in full. Set the two definitions side by side and the roster's shape appears: one man who completed a full term at the settlement's height of office, and one who held a governorship for five weeks at its most contested point, each carried there by the electorate the registration of 1867 had made.
  • Say: "Blanche K. Bruce: the Mississippi officeholder born enslaved in Virginia, who rose through tax and education offices to election to the United States Senate in 1874, the second Black senator and the first to serve a full term, seated while the settlement that elected him was already being dismantled."
  • Say: "P. B. S. Pinchback: the Louisiana leader, freeborn in Georgia and a Union officer in the war, who rose through the state senate to serve as acting governor of Louisiana for five weeks in the winter of 1872 and 1873, the first Black man to govern an American state."

Slide 27 · Vocabulary: the coalition and its organization

On the slide: three terms with their short forms — carpetbaggers (the hostile name for northern newcomers), scalawags (the hostile name for white southern Republicans), Union League (the clubs that organized the new electorate). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Three terms, in full, and notice that two of the three are names given by enemies.
  • Say: "carpetbaggers: the hostile name white southern Democrats gave northern men who came South during Reconstruction, teachers, ministers, merchants, investors, Union veterans, and office seekers among them, pictured in Democratic print as adventurers carrying everything they owned in a carpetbag."
  • Say: "scalawags: the hostile name white southern Democrats gave white southerners who joined the Republican Party, upcountry Unionists, former Whigs, and poor farmers among them, whose votes, alongside Black voters and northern newcomers, built the Reconstruction governments."
  • Say: "Union League: the organization of patriotic clubs, spread through the South from 1867 under the Loyal League name, which organized Black voters and white Unionists into the Republican Party, meeting in churches and schoolhouses to register voters, teach the new politics, and turn out the electorate the Reconstruction Acts created."

Slide 28 · Closing synthesis: two findings, held together

On the slide: the title "Two findings, held together"; two paragraphs — the broadest democracy the country had yet run, built between 1867 and 1870, and an amendment that forbade three grounds of denial and left the rest standing, over governments that rested on federal protection.

  • [Close by holding the two findings together, because the verdict work is graded on exactly that.] The first finding is about scale: between 1867 and 1870 the South built, from registration rolls upward, the broadest democracy the country had yet run, with Black men voting, legislating, governing a state, and sitting in the Senate, and it wrote the vote's protection into the Constitution.
  • The second finding is about foundations: the amendment forbade three grounds of denial and left the rest standing, and the governments rested on a federal protection they could not supply themselves, so the settlement's survival depended on a national will outside the South's own control.
  • Students who hold only the first finding have a celebration. Students who hold only the second have a dismissal. The lesson's question needs both, stated together: who governed, what was settled, and what was left open.

Slide 29 · Now your turn: answer the question and exit ticket

On the slide: the closing title "Answer the question"; the task line — judge the records, account for the amendment's wording, and state what the new order rested on; the rows — Activity (The New Electorate Speaks and Is Pictured: the amendment, the speech, two pictures, a roster), Notebook (the electorate, the conventions, the coalition, the roster, the amendment), Quick check (The Amendment and the Senator: the text of 1870 beside the argument of 1871), Writing (Settled in Text, Resting on Protection: your judgment defended from the bank); the exit ticket line.

  • Close the lesson by handing the judgment to the students, which is what this lesson exists to do.
  • The activity gives them the Fifteenth Amendment's joint resolution and Revels's schools speech from the audited bank records, Waud's First Vote and Nast's Emancipation as the bank presents them, and a labeled teaching summary of the South Carolina convention and legislature, and asks which record a skeptical northern voter of 1870 would have found hardest to answer.
  • The notebook page charts the electorate, the conventions, the coalition, the roster, and the amendment in stages. The quick check sets the amendment's text beside Revels's argument. The writing task asks whether the settlement of 1870 completed the work of securing the vote or left it resting on protection, defended against the strongest rival reading.
  • The exit ticket has three parts: the League and the amendment defined, the coalition stated in two sentences, and the lesson question answered with the achievement and the opening held together.
