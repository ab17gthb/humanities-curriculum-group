The Counterrevolution

Reconstruction · Lesson 6 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Reconstruction, Lesson 6 of 8 — teach it sixth in the unit, after Freedom on the Ground (Lesson 5) and before The Retreat Begins (Lesson 7). Lessons 1 to 5 settled freedom's legal and political terms and followed freedpeople onto the ground; this lesson meets the organized force raised to destroy that settlement, reads Abram Colby's testimony against it, and follows Congress's answer in the three Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871. The lesson question asks what the Klan's terror aimed at and what it took to break the first Klan, and the lesson holds purpose and machinery together from the first slide to the last.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 5 (the voters, schools, churches, and officeholders built there are the targets named here) and Lesson 7 (the retreat that begins when the will behind enforcement fails). Inside the lesson, the activity, Testimony and Answer, carries Abram Colby's testimony quoted verbatim from the audited bank record, the Enforcement Act of 1871 quoted verbatim from the audited bank record, and three labeled teaching summaries — a second witness, the South Carolina proclamation and prosecutions, and Forrest before the committee. The writing task, Civil Rights Law, Counterinsurgency, or Both, turns the lesson question into a verdict defended from the bank.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: the Reconstruction governments and electorate from Lesson 4, the schools, churches, and officeholders from Lesson 5, and the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments from Lessons 3 and 4. Every figure in the lesson is taught as the documents state it — six founders at Pulaski in 1866, the Invisible Empire organized in 1867, Colby's offers of $5,000 and $2,500 and the attack of October 29, 1869, three Enforcement Acts in 1870 and 1871, and nine South Carolina counties under the proclamation of October 17, 1871. No image of the Klan, of night riders, of victims, or of any act of violence appears anywhere in this lesson, by design; the violence is taught in testimony and text, stated plainly. No verified public domain likeness of Abram Colby was located, so Colby is carried in testimony and text only.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (testimony, a statute, and labeled summaries read as one record and weighed by kind), D2.His.16 (Colby, Forrest, and Grant read as historical actors with stated reasons), D2.Civ.10 (federal supervisors, marshals, courts, and a presidential proclamation analyzed as enforcement machinery), D2.Civ.14 (rights read with the courts and officers that made them effective); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.8, WHST.9-10.1 (a verdict on the Enforcement Acts defended from the records).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to state how the Klan was founded at Pulaski in 1866 and organized as the Invisible Empire in 1867 under Forrest; describe night riding, its targets, and its timing at elections, school terms, and contract settlements; recount Colby's offers, refusal, attack, and 1872 testimony; weigh Forrest's dissolution order of 1869 against his denials of 1871; describe the machinery of the three Enforcement Acts, including supervisors, marshals, and federal courts; explain the Ku Klux Klan Act's two reaches and its use in nine South Carolina counties; and state both findings of the close — that the first Klan was broken by 1872 where federal power was used whole, and that the breaking rested on a will the statute could not supply.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1-3) 5 minutes; terror organized (slides 4-5) 5 minutes; night riding (slides 6-8) 6 minutes; Colby tells Congress (slides 9-11) 8 minutes; Forrest (slides 12-13) 5 minutes; Congress arms the federal government (slides 14-16) 7 minutes; the Klan Act and South Carolina (slides 17-20) 7 minutes; broken, and what it depended on (slides 21-22) 3 minutes; glance, vocabulary, and exit (slides 23-28) 4 minutes. The activity takes a second period if the schedule allows. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes, and Testimony and Answer (five stations). The notebook page charts the organization, the method, the testimony, and the answer; the quick check is "Testimony and Statute: the two quoted records tested against each other"; the writing task is "Civil Rights Law, Counterinsurgency, or Both"; the exit ticket has three parts — night riding and the Enforcement Acts defined, the terror's aim stated in two sentences, and the lesson question answered.

Key vocabulary

Ku Klux Klan · Nathan Bedford Forrest · Abram Colby · night riding · Enforcement Acts · Ku Klux Klan Act · federal marshals

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Title: The Counterrevolution

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title "The Counterrevolution"; the standfirst — "The Klan's terror, the testimony against it, and the federal answer of 1870 and 1871."

  • Picture: A photograph of the United States Capitol at Washington, taken by Mathew Brady about 1860 to 1865 and held by the National Archives.
  • [Open with what the picture teaches.] The testimony students will read was given to a committee of this building, and the laws they will read were passed inside it. What happened in Georgia and South Carolina farmhouses at night was decided, in the end, by what this building and the president were willing to do about it.
  • [Say the lesson's discipline aloud at the start.] The violence is taught in testimony and text, stated plainly, and no picture of the Klan, of its riders, or of any victim appears anywhere in this lesson.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "What did the Klan's terror aim at, and what did it take to break the first Klan?"
  • The lesson question has two halves, and students should keep them separate all period. What the terror aimed at is a question about purpose: the riders were not random criminals, and the testimony shows purchase attempted first, violence second, and the target always political — a vote, a seat, a school, a contract.
  • What it took to break the first Klan is a question about machinery: statutes, supervisors, marshals, troops, prosecutors, and a presidential proclamation, all of it resting on a northern will to sustain the effort.
  • The unit question stands behind both halves, because this lesson is where the settlement of the earlier lessons meets the organized force raised to destroy it, and where students learn that enforcement is part of a settlement, not an afterthought to it.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "Terror organized, testimony given, law answered"; the seven section rows, A-G.

  • The map follows the guided notes section by section. Section A builds the organization, founded at Pulaski in 1866 and refounded as the Invisible Empire in 1867 under Forrest.
  • Section B gives the method, night riding, and its chosen targets and timing. Section C reads Abram Colby's testimony whole — the offers, the attack, and the refusal carried to Congress in 1872.
  • Section D hears Forrest's answer, the dissolution order of 1869 and the denials of 1871. Section E builds the federal machinery of the Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871.
  • Section F watches the Ku Klux Klan Act at work in South Carolina in the fall of 1871. Section G closes on the breaking and on the premise underneath it, the will the statute could not supply.

Slide 4 · Section A divider: Terror Organized as Politics

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Founded at Pulaski in 1866 and organized as the Invisible Empire in 1867, with disguise as its first instrument."

  • [Slow down on the founding, because students often imagine the Klan springing up armed and organized in a single night.] It began in 1866 at Pulaski, Tennessee, as a club formed by six former Confederate soldiers, and it became dangerous when it was organized: the Nashville meeting of 1867 gave it a name built for awe, the Invisible Empire, a structure of dens, and officers with borrowed romantic titles.
  • [Teach the disguise as an instrument, not a costume detail.] A disguised rider could not be identified by his victim, could not be named to a sheriff, and could sit in church or court the next day as a respectable neighbor.
  • The organization and the anonymity together are the invention this section names.

Slide 5 · A club becomes an empire

On the slide: five bullets — six former Confederate soldiers founded the club at Pulaski in 1866; the Nashville meeting of 1867 organized the clubs as the Invisible Empire; local dens followed rituals and officers who used borrowed romantic titles; Nathan Bedford Forrest was elected the first Grand Wizard; the disguise hid the man and defeated the law that depended on identification.

  • [Work the bullets in order.] The founders are former soldiers, which is why the Klan could organize like an army from its first year.
  • The Nashville meeting is the turning point because it turned local clubs into a single named empire with Forrest at its head as Grand Wizard, and Forrest's standing as the Confederacy's most feared cavalry commander gave the name its threat before a single ride.
  • The dens are the local unit students should picture: a county organization, recruited from the neighborhood, which is why its victims so often knew their attackers' voices and still could not prove a name in court.
  • [Close on the last bullet.] The sheet and false face did three jobs at once: they hid the man, they frightened the household, and they defeated the legal process that depended on identification.

Slide 6 · Section B divider: Night Riding and Its Targets

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "The method taught in testimony and text, stated plainly. No image of the riders or their work appears in this lesson."

  • [Name the absence plainly.] This divider carries no picture, and that is deliberate. Section B teaches the method of the terror, and the method is taught in words and testimony, never in an image of riders or of violence.
  • Tell students what night riding was with the plainness the reading uses: mounted men, in disguise, after dark, at a named person's house.
  • The terror was scheduled, and the schedule is the section's real content. Raids came before elections, when a vote could still be changed. They came at the opening of school terms, when a teacher could still be driven off. They came at contract settlements, when a family's year of labor was priced.
  • Terror that keeps a calendar is a policy, and students should learn to read it as one.

Slide 7 · The method and the message

On the slide: five bullets — mounted men rode in disguises after dark; riders called their targets out by name at their houses; Black voters and Republican organizers were the first targets; teachers, officeholders, and their allies were also targeted; one whipping was addressed to a whole community.

  • [Teach the method as a system with three parts.] The disguise protected the rider. The hour and the visit to a house made the whole family, and the whole neighborhood within earshot, the audience.
  • The selection of the target made the lesson general: whip one organizer and every organizer in the county recalculates; threaten one teacher and every school in the district hears of it.
  • [Name the targets in the reading's order.] Black voters and Republican organizers first, because the electorate was the settlement's foundation; teachers, because the schools built the future the terror meant to close; officeholders, because they were the governments in person; and white southerners who worked with those governments, because the coalition had to be broken at every joint.
  • One whipping was addressed to a whole community, and students should be able to say why.

Slide 8 · Stat: three appointments the raids kept

On the slide: the big figure "3"; the label — "appointments the raids kept"; the italic note — before elections, at the opening of school terms, at contract settlements.

  • 3 appointments the raids kept: before elections, at the opening of school terms, at contract settlements.
  • Three is a small number for a giant slide, and it earns its size. Each appointment was a moment when the new order became real in daily life — when a man voted, when a child entered a schoolroom, when a family's labor was converted into money or debt.
  • The Klan struck at exactly those moments, which is how students can tell the terror was aimed at the settlement and not at disorder in general. A bandit strikes when the money moves; this terror struck when freedom acted.
  • [Have the class say the three moments aloud, in order, before moving on,] because the exit ticket and the writing task both return to this timing as evidence of purpose.

Slide 9 · Section C divider: Abram Colby Tells Congress

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "A Georgia legislator, whipped in 1869 for refusing to sell his seat, carries the account to Congress in 1872."

  • [Teach this section at reading pace, not summary pace.] Section C is the lesson's center. Abram Colby was a freedman elected to the Georgia legislature, an organizer who carried Black voters to the Republican ticket, and the Klan came for him in the sequence the testimony records: first the offers, money for his allegiance and more money for his seat, then, when purchase failed, the visit in the night.
  • Tell students before they read that they will hear the whole account in Colby's own words at the activity's first station, given to a congressional committee in 1872.
  • His testimony is the documentary heart of the lesson: a named man, a date, two sums of money, and a refusal, all sworn and printed by the government the terror was fighting.
  • Note: No verified public domain likeness of Abram Colby was located, so Colby is carried in testimony and text only, and no image is attached to his name.

Slide 10 · Purchase first, violence second

On the slide: five bullets — Colby was offered $5,000 to change sides and $2,500 for his seat; Colby refused at both prices, in his own words; on October 29, 1869, attackers broke open his door; attackers whipped Colby for three hours in the woods and left him for dead; under the whipping, Colby said he would vote the Radical ticket tomorrow.

  • [Walk the sequence slowly, because the order is the argument.] Two days before the attack, the offers came: five thousand dollars to go with them, twenty five hundred in cash to let another man take his seat in the legislature. That is not the behavior of a mob; it is a price quoted for a piece of the new government.
  • Colby refused at both prices, in words the class will read verbatim: he would not do it if they gave him all the county was worth.
  • Then came October 29, 1869: the door broken open, the woods, three hours of whipping, and under the whipping the same refusal in political form, that if there was an election tomorrow he would vote the Radical ticket.
  • Purchase first, violence second, refusal throughout: fix that order in memory.

Slide 11 · 1872: the refusal goes on the record

On the slide: the Capitol portrait at right; five bullets — a congressional committee heard Colby's account; Colby named his attackers by standing as a lawyer, a doctor, and farmers; the attackers were first class men of the town in disguise; Colby said no man could make a free speech in his county or in Georgia; testimony like Colby's became the foundation for the Enforcement Acts.

  • Picture: A portrait crop of Mathew Brady's photograph of the United States Capitol, about 1860 to 1865, held by the National Archives — the building whose committee heard and printed Colby's testimony.
  • [Teach Colby in 1872 as an actor, not only a victim in 1869.] He named the standing of his attackers to the committee: some are first class men in our town, a lawyer, a doctor, and farmers among them.
  • That sentence dismantles the defense that the Klan was a rabble of the poor and the desperate; in Colby's county it was the respectable center of the community in disguise.
  • He closed by widening the account from his county to his state: no man could make a free speech in his county, and he did not believe it could be done anywhere in Georgia.
  • Congress printed sentences of that kind by the volume, and the Enforcement Acts rest on the record they built.

Slide 12 · Section D divider: Forrest: Dissolution and Denial

On the slide: the Forrest portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "The Grand Wizard who ordered the Klan dissolved in 1869, and denied directing it in 1871."

  • Picture: Nathan Bedford Forrest as a Confederate general, photographed during the war by E. and H. T. Anthony — a wartime portrait, used here because the lesson pictures the man, never his organization at work.
  • Section D is the record's other voice. Forrest had been elected the Klan's first Grand Wizard in 1867, and in January 1869 he ordered the organization dissolved, declaring that its violence had escaped his control.
  • When Congress questioned him in 1871, he denied directing the violence and denied knowing outrages as policy.
  • [Teach this section as a source problem, not a verdict.] The man best placed to describe the Klan's command was also the man with the most reason to shape its description, and the committee had to weigh his admissions against his denials.

Slide 13 · Admissions and denials

On the slide: five bullets — Forrest was elected Grand Wizard in 1867, by his own account; in January 1869, Forrest ordered the organization dissolved; Forrest said the Klan's violence had escaped his control; in 1871, Forrest said he had given no direction and knew no policy of violence; the committee weighed Forrest's account against the record.

  • [Give Forrest his full account before weighing it, the way the reading does.] His admissions run against his interest and are therefore strong evidence: he accepted election as Grand Wizard, he found the name and disguises taken up by men he could not control, and he ordered the organization destroyed and its regalia burned in January 1869 because its violence had escaped his purposes.
  • His denials serve his interest and must be weighed against the testimony: he said he directed no violence, knew no outrages as policy, and had advised peace across his section.
  • Students should notice the shape of the defense. It concedes the terror and disputes only its command, which protects the founder while surrendering the organization.
  • The committee's majority reported its skepticism; the country read both accounts side by side.

Slide 14 · Section E divider: Congress Arms the Federal Government

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Three Enforcement Acts, 1870 and 1871: rights given officers, courts, and a reach past the states."

  • [Name the turn plainly.] Section E turns from the terror to the answer, and the answer begins as law, not as troops. Congress passed three Enforcement Acts in 1870 and 1871, and students should learn the machinery piece by piece, because the writing task asks them to judge what kind of instrument the acts were.
  • The acts made conspiracy against citizens' constitutional rights a federal crime. They reached the disguised rider by name, in the statute's own words.
  • They placed federal supervisors at the polls to watch registration and counting, and they armed federal marshals to guard polling places, make arrests, and summon posses.
  • Above all, they moved the cases: out of county courts that would not convict, into federal court, before federal judges and juries. A right with a court behind it is a different thing from a right on paper.

Slide 15 · Law built for the emergency

On the slide: five bullets — conspiracy against constitutional rights became a federal crime; the statute named the disguised rider directly; federal supervisors watched at the polls; federal marshals guarded the polls, made arrests, and summoned posses; cases were moved into federal court.

  • [Work the bullets as a chain of failures and remedies.] The state process failed first, at three points the testimony names: sheriffs would not arrest, witnesses would not testify for fear of the next visit, and juries would not convict their neighbors.
  • Each piece of the federal machinery answers one failure. Marshals answer the sheriff. Federal protection and federal venue answer the frightened witness. A federal jury, drawn wider than one county, answers the local jury.
  • The supervisors at the polls answer the election itself, the place where the terror had aimed to decide outcomes before a ballot was cast.
  • [Close by asking what this machinery assumed:] that the federal government was willing to station its own officers inside southern elections, county by county, for as long as the terror lasted.

Slide 16 · Supervisors watch; marshals act

On the slide: five bullets — supervisors watched registration, polling, and the count; marshals guarded the polls, made arrests, and summoned posses; each kind of officer answered a named local failure; some sheriffs would not arrest and some juries would not convict; federal authority stood in the county in person.

  • [Separate the two officers cleanly, because students mix them up.] The supervisors watched the process: registration lists, polling places, the counting of ballots, the points where a legal election could be stolen administratively.
  • The marshals carried the force: they guarded the polls, arrested offenders against the election and civil rights laws, and could summon a civilian posse when a county's own officers stood aside.
  • Both kinds of officer were the settlement made visible at ground level, federal authority standing in a county that had its own courthouse and its own sheriff.
  • When students write about the acts as law, these officers are their evidence; when they write about the acts as a campaign, the same officers, backed by troops in South Carolina, are the evidence on that side too.

Slide 17 · Section F divider: The Klan Act and South Carolina

On the slide: the Grant portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "The act of April 1871, used at full strength in nine South Carolina counties that fall."

  • Picture: President Ulysses S. Grant, from the Brady-Handy photograph collection at the Library of Congress, taken between 1870 and 1880.
  • Section F is the statute at full stretch. The third Enforcement Act, the Ku Klux Klan Act of April 1871, made conspiracy under color of state law a federal offense and authorized the president to use the army against insurrectionary combinations.
  • Grant chose the ground where the dens were strongest: nine upcountry counties of South Carolina. In October 1871 he proclaimed those combinations an insurrection, ordered them to disperse, and suspended habeas corpus in the nine counties.
  • What followed was the full machinery of Section E — marshals, troops, prosecutors, and federal court, used at once and at strength. Teach the sequence as a deliberate escalation the statute had authorized in advance.

Slide 18 · One act, two reaches

On the slide: five bullets — conspiracy under color of state law became a federal offense; under Section 1, the injured party could sue in federal court; the president could use the army against insurrectionary combinations; combinations could be proclaimed an insurrection; the act was a rights statute with a campaign inside it.

  • [Read the act's two reaches carefully, because the verdict task turns on them.] The first reach is legal: conspiracy under color of state law to deprive citizens of constitutional rights became a federal offense, and Section 1 of the same act let the injured person sue in federal court. That is the language of civil rights law — rights, liability, and courts.
  • The second reach is military: the president could use the army against combinations he proclaimed insurrectionary, and suspend habeas corpus where the insurrection ran. That is the language of counterinsurgency written into a statute.
  • Students who see only one reach will misjudge the act. Congress knew the terror was both a crime problem and a political war against a constitutional settlement, and it wrote an instrument with a hand for each.

Slide 19 · South Carolina, October 1871 to 1872

On the slide: the Grant portrait at right; five bullets — Grant's proclamation of October 17, 1871, ordered the combinations to disperse; habeas corpus was suspended in nine counties; marshals and troops arrested hundreds of men; hundreds were indicted, and the accused pleaded, were convicted, or fled; by 1872, the first Klan in South Carolina was broken.

  • Picture: President Grant, from the Brady-Handy collection at the Library of Congress — the president whose proclamation opened the South Carolina campaign.
  • [Recite the campaign's steps in order.] The proclamation came on October 17, 1871, naming nine counties and ordering the armed combinations to disperse and give up their arms and disguises.
  • When the order was defied, habeas corpus was suspended in those counties, and marshals backed by federal troops arrested hundreds of men. Federal prosecutors brought hundreds of indictments for conspiracy against citizens' rights.
  • The accused pleaded guilty, confessed and turned evidence against their dens, were convicted at trial, or fled the state. By 1872 organized Klan activity in South Carolina had collapsed, and the first Klan as an organization did not survive the campaign.
  • One state received the full dose; students should ask, in the next section, what the dose cost and what it depended on.

Slide 20 · Stat: nine counties

On the slide: the big figure "9"; the label — "counties under the proclamation"; the italic note — Spartanburg, York, Union, Chester, Laurens, Newberry, Fairfield, Lancaster, Chesterfield.

  • 9 counties under the proclamation: Spartanburg, York, Union, Chester, Laurens, Newberry, Fairfield, Lancaster, and Chesterfield.
  • [State the precision.] Grant did not proclaim the whole South an insurrection, and he did not suspend habeas corpus generally. He named nine upcountry South Carolina counties, the counties where the dens were strongest and the testimony worst.
  • The precision is part of the record's meaning: the federal answer was aimed by evidence, county by county, on the findings the congressional investigation had built.
  • It is also the measure of the answer's limits. Nine counties got the full machinery. The rest of the South got the statute, the marshals, and the courts, in doses that varied with local juries and northern attention, which is why the Klan died as an organization while its method survived to be reused.

Slide 21 · Section G divider: Broken, and What the Breaking Depended On

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Federal power, used whole, worked. The power rested on a will the statute could not supply."

  • [Teach this section as an open ledger rather than a celebration.] What the breaking proved is stated first, and it is a large proof: federal power, used at full strength and in combination, worked. Marshals, troops, prosecutors, and courts moving together dissolved the Invisible Empire in the state where it was strongest, within about a year.
  • No student should leave doubting that the first Klan was breakable, because it was broken.
  • Then the ledger's other column. The power rested on a president willing to proclaim insurrection in an American state, a Congress willing to pay for enforcement, and a northern public willing to sustain both. None of those three wills was written into the statute.
  • The next lesson begins where they start to fail.

Slide 22 · Capacity, and dependence

On the slide: five bullets — marshals, troops, prosecutors, and courts acted together; the Invisible Empire collapsed within a year; the campaign proved the first Klan was breakable and broken; the breaking rested on a president, a Congress, and a public willing to sustain it; the method survived and changed its clothes.

  • [Hold the two findings together and resist letting either swallow the other.] The first finding is about capacity: the settlement of 1868 to 1870 included an enforcement arm, and when the arm was used whole, organized terror collapsed.
  • The second finding is about dependence: the arm moved only while a national majority willed it to move, and the men who had ridden in disguise understood that arithmetic as well as anyone in Washington.
  • They did not need to defeat the Enforcement Acts. They needed to outwait them, and to make each year of enforcement cost the North more patience than the year before.
  • The violence also changed its form: the rifle clubs and leagues of the middle seventies would do openly, by daylight, much of what the first Klan had done in disguise, and Lesson 7 takes up that transformation.

Slide 23 · Terror and answer at a glance

On the slide: the title "An organization, a method, a testimony, an answer"; four rows — the organization; the method; the testimony; the answer.

  • [Consolidate row by row, in the lesson's order.] The organization row fixes the founding, the empire, and the dissolution order. The method row fixes night riding, its targets, and its three appointed moments.
  • The testimony row fixes Colby: the sums, the night, the refusal, and the naming of respectable men. The answer row fixes the machinery and its proof case: three acts, supervisors and marshals, the April 1871 act, the October proclamation, nine counties, and a Klan broken by 1872.
  • Every row of this table reappears in the notebook chart and in the evidence bank of the writing task, so time spent here is revision for three assignments at once.
  • [Ask students to supply each row's hardest fact from memory before you confirm it.]

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: the organization and its chief

On the slide: two terms with their short forms — Ku Klux Klan (the Invisible Empire, from 1866), Nathan Bedford Forrest (its first Grand Wizard, 1867). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Two terms anchor the organization and its chief, and their full definitions are carried here word for word as the guided notes give them. Teach the pairing: the definition of the Klan names its victims' categories, and the definition of Forrest names the arc the documents test — command claimed, dissolution ordered, direction denied.
  • Say: "Ku Klux Klan: the secret terrorist organization founded at Pulaski, Tennessee, in 1866 and spread across the South as the Invisible Empire, whose disguised night riders whipped, threatened, exiled, and murdered Black voters, Republican organizers, teachers, and officeholders to destroy the Reconstruction governments' electorate."
  • Say: "Nathan Bedford Forrest: the former Confederate cavalry general and Memphis slave trader elected the Klan's first Grand Wizard in 1867, who ordered the organization dissolved in 1869 as its violence escaped control and denied directing it when Congress questioned him in 1871."

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: the testimony and the method

On the slide: two terms with their short forms — Abram Colby (the legislator who refused, and testified), night riding (the Klan's characteristic method). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Two terms carry the testimony and the method, and their full definitions are carried here word for word as the guided notes give them. Teach the pairing: Colby's definition is built from his refusal, and night riding's definition ends on the point students must carry into the writing task, that the terror was aimed at a whole community's conduct and not only at its victim.
  • Say: "Abram Colby: the Georgia freedman and state legislator whom Klansmen dragged from his house in October 1869 and whipped nearly to death for refusing to abandon Republican politics or sell his vote, and who in 1872 told a congressional committee what was done to him, naming the men and refusing their bribes on the record."
  • Say: "night riding: the Klan's characteristic method, mounted men in disguises riding after dark to the houses of their targets, calling them out by name and whipping or shooting them, terror timed to elections, school terms, and contract settlements and aimed at a whole community's conduct, not only its victim."

Slide 26 · Vocabulary: the federal answer

On the slide: three terms with their short forms — Enforcement Acts (the three laws of 1870 and 1871), Ku Klux Klan Act (the third act, April 1871), federal marshals (the settlement's enforcement arm). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Three terms carry the federal answer, in the guided notes' words. The staircase runs acts, act, officers.
  • Say: "Enforcement Acts: the three laws of 1870 and 1871 by which Congress armed the federal government against the Klan and election violence, making it a federal crime to conspire against citizens' constitutional rights, placing federal supervisors and marshals at the polls, and empowering the president to use troops and suspend habeas corpus against insurrectionary combinations."
  • Say: "Ku Klux Klan Act: the third Enforcement Act, of April 1871, which made conspiracy under color of state law to deprive citizens of constitutional rights a federal offense, authorized the president to use the army against such conspiracies, and supplied the authority under which Grant broke the first Klan in South Carolina."
  • Say: "federal marshals: the officers of the federal courts, and their deputies, whom the Enforcement Acts empowered to guard polling places, arrest offenders against the election and civil rights laws, and summon civilian posses, the settlement's enforcement arm in counties whose own officers would not act."

Slide 27 · Closing synthesis: two findings, held together

On the slide: the title "Two findings, held together"; two paragraphs — the terror aimed at the electorate the Reconstruction Acts had made, and it was broken, where the federal government used its full power, within a year; the breaking rested on a president, a Congress, and a northern public willing to sustain it, and the statute could not supply the will.

  • [Close the lesson by returning to its question with the whole record in view.] The terror aimed at an electorate: at the voters the Reconstruction Acts had enrolled, the teachers who staffed the new schools, the officeholders who staffed the new governments, and the coalition that held them together.
  • It was broken, where it was broken, by the settlement's own machinery used at full strength, and the breaking is the strongest evidence in the unit that the settlement could have been held.
  • What it could not supply was the will to hold it everywhere and indefinitely. Students should leave able to argue either half and required to hold both, because the unit's remaining lessons are the story of the second half overtaking the first.
