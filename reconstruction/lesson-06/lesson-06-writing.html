Writing Task: Civil Rights Law, Counterinsurgency, or Both

The prompt. Write a developed essay of about 300 words answering the lesson question from this lesson's documents: what did the Klan's terror aim at, and what did it take to break the first Klan? Judge whether the Enforcement Acts are better described as civil rights law, counterinsurgency, or both. Defend the judgment against the strongest rival reading in the record. Your essay must account for Colby's testimony and for the South Carolina campaign of 1871. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the Testimony and Answer activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording. Keep the paired records whole wherever you use them: the statute's text beside the campaign that enforced it, and the testimony beside the denials it answered.

Evidence Bank

1. The Klan was founded at Pulaski, Tennessee, in 1866 and organized as the Invisible Empire at Nashville in 1867 under Nathan Bedford Forrest as Grand Wizard. Its method was night riding: disguised men visited targets' houses after dark, timed to elections, school terms, and contract settlements, against Black voters, Republican organizers, teachers, and officeholders.

2. Abram Colby, a Georgia legislator, refused $5,000 to change sides and $2,500 to surrender his seat, and was whipped for three hours on October 29, 1869. Under the whipping he said he would vote the Radical ticket at an election tomorrow. In 1872 he testified to Congress, naming a lawyer, a doctor, and farmers among his attackers.

3. The Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871 made conspiracy against citizens' constitutional rights a federal crime, reached conduct under color of state law and riders in disguise, placed federal supervisors at the polls, and empowered federal marshals to arrest offenders, with cases tried in federal court.

4. Forrest ordered the Klan dissolved in January 1869 and testified in 1871 that he had not directed its violence or known outrages as its policy. The congressional committee's majority reported its skepticism of the denials and printed his testimony beside the testimony of the men the riders had visited.

5. In October 1871 President Grant proclaimed the armed combinations in nine South Carolina counties an insurrection, ordered them to disperse, and suspended habeas corpus in those counties. Marshals and troops made hundreds of arrests, prosecutors brought hundreds of indictments, and by 1872 the first Klan in South Carolina was broken.

6. Congress built the acts on testimony taken across the South in 1871 and 1872, hundreds of witnesses describing a pattern: raids before elections, orders given under the whipping to abandon the Republican Party, and local prosecution failing because sheriffs would not arrest, witnesses feared to testify, and juries would not convict.

Scaffold

Build the essay in four moves. Open with your judgment in one sentence, naming the terror's aim and the description you choose for the federal answer. Then give the terror at full weight, its organization, its method, and Colby's refusal, before you describe the answer. Then turn to the answer's two forms: the statute in court and the campaign in South Carolina, and show one document working in each form. Close by answering the strongest rival reading and stating what the answer depended on.

Sentence starters you may use: "The terror aimed at ___, and the answer took the form of ___." "The strongest evidence for the law reading is ___, because ___." "The campaign in South Carolina shows ___, and ___ is the record of it." "Against the reading that ___, the record shows ___."

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment choosing one description, civil rights law, counterinsurgency, or both, stated directly and held through the essay. Any of the three can earn full credit if Colby's testimony and the South Carolina campaign are both carried and the rival reading is answered. An essay that retells the terror without judging the answer's form has summarized the lesson rather than answered it. (Blueprint 11, Blueprint 12)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, drawn from at least two different parts of the record, the terror and its testimony, the statute, the denials, or the campaign. An essay built on the South Carolina campaign alone cannot judge the statute's ordinary life in courts and at polls across the South. (Blueprint 12)
  • Colby's testimony accounted for, not decorated with: the two sums and what each was meant to buy, the answer given under the whipping, and the standings of the men he named, since the testimony is both the terror's measure and the factual basis on which Congress built the acts. (Blueprint 11)
  • The South Carolina campaign stated in its steps: the October 1871 proclamation, the nine counties, the suspension of habeas corpus, the hundreds of arrests and indictments, and the Klan broken by 1872. An essay that calls the acts counterinsurgency without the campaign has asserted the description without its evidence. (Blueprint 12)
  • The strongest rival reading stated at full strength and answered. Against a both verdict: the campaign was one state's emergency, or the rights language was a statute's ordinary dress. Against a law verdict: a president proclaiming insurrection and suspending habeas corpus is not ordinary law enforcement by any name. Against a counterinsurgency verdict: the acts created courtroom remedies and poll supervision that ran for years without a soldier present. (Blueprint 11, Blueprint 12)
  • The answer's premise assigned at the close: a president willing to use the act, a Congress willing to fund marshals and courts, and a northern public willing to sustain both, the supports whose later withdrawal the next lesson records. (Blueprint 12)

Model opening for teacher reference. The Enforcement Acts were civil rights law written for an insurgency, and they have to be read as both because the terror they answered was both a crime wave and a campaign. The statute speaks the language of rights: conspiracy against constitutional rights, conduct under color of state law, liability in federal court, supervisors at the polls. The campaign speaks the language of war: a proclaimed insurrection in nine counties, habeas corpus suspended, troops behind the marshals, hundreds arrested in a season. Colby's testimony holds the two together. A legislator was offered money for his seat and then whipped for his ticket, which is a crime against one man and an operation against an electorate in the same night, and the law that answered it had to work in a courtroom and in a county at once.

Common weak answers. A counterinsurgency essay built on bank 5 alone cannot explain why Congress wrote courtroom remedies and poll supervision into the same acts. A law essay built on bank 3 alone cannot explain the proclamation, the suspension, or the troops, and usually avoids South Carolina entirely.
