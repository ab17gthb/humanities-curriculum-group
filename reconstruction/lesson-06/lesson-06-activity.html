Testimony and Answer

Directions: The five stations below are the record of the Klan's terror and the federal answer, 1869 to 1872. Study each station and answer its questions. At every station, state what the record shows terror doing, or law answering. In Part 2, judge whether the Enforcement Acts are better described as civil rights law, counterinsurgency, or both, and defend your judgment. The testimony is stated plainly and is not pictured: no image of the Klan, of its riders, or of any act of violence appears in this activity, by design.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: Abram Colby's Testimony to Congress, 1872

Source: Abram Colby, 'Klansmen Broke My Door Open,' 1872 (Report of the Joint Select Committee on the Late Insurrectionary States, 1872)., as carried in the audited TPSP bank record. Quoted verbatim from the bank record. The record's own cut markers (. . .) are preserved as the record carries them. Colby, a Georgia freedman elected to the state legislature, is answering the committee's questions about the night of October 29, 1869.

"On the 29th of October 1869, [the Klansmen] broke my door open, took me out of bed, took me to the woods and whipped me three hours or more and left me for dead. They said to me, "Do you think you will ever vote another damned Radical ticket?" I said, "If there was an election tomorrow, I would vote the Radical ticket." They set in and whipped me a thousand licks more, with sticks and straps that had buckles on the ends of them. Question: What is the character of those men who were engaged in whipping you? Colby: Some are first-class men in our town. One is a lawyer, one a doctor, and some are farmers. . . . They said I had voted for Grant and had carried the Negroes against them. About two days before they whipped me they offered me $5,000 to go with them and said they would pay me $2,500 in cash if I would let another man go to the legislature in my place. I told them that I would not do it if they would give me all the county was worth. . . . No man can make a free speech in my county. I do not believe it can be done anywhere in Georgia."

State Colby's account in order: the date, what the men did when they came, the question they put to him and the answer he gave, and what followed the answer. Then state the two offers made before the attack, with both sums and what each was meant to buy, and the answer Colby gave to the offers. Colby tells the committee who his attackers were in the town: name the three standings he gives. His last two sentences widen the account from his county to his state. State what condition those sentences describe, and why a congressional committee in 1872 needed a witness to say it on the record rather than in a newspaper.

Station 2: The Enforcement Act of 1871 (the Ku Klux Klan Act)

Source: U.S. Congress, 'Enforcement Act of 1871,' 1871 (Wikisource transcription of the enrolled act)., as carried in the audited TPSP bank record. Quoted verbatim from the bank record. Cuts in the bank record are marked with [...] and are preserved as the record carries them. This is the third Enforcement Act, signed April 20, 1871.

An Act to enforce the Provisions of the Fourteenth Amendment to the Constitution of the United States, and for other Purposes.

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled, That any person who, under color of any law, statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage of any State, shall subject, or cause to be subjected, any person within the jurisdiction of the United States to the deprivation of any rights, privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution of the United States, shall, any such law, statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage of the State to the contrary notwithstanding, be liable to the party injured in any action at law, suit in equity, or other proper proceeding for redress [...]

Sec. 2. [...] shall conspire together, or go in disguise upon the public highway or upon the premises of another for the purpose, either directly or indirectly, of depriving any person or any class of persons of the equal protection of the laws, or of equal privileges or immunities under the laws [...] or by force, intimidation, or threat to prevent any citizen of the United States lawfully entitled to vote from giving his support or advocacy in a lawful manner towards or in favor of the election of any lawfully qualified person as an elector of President or Vice-President of the United States, or as a member of the Congress of the United States, [...] each and every person so offending shall be deemed guilty of a high crime [...]

Work through the act's machinery in its own words. Section 1 reaches a person who acts under color of a state law, custom, or usage: state whose conduct that phrase covers, and what the injured party may do about it. Section 2 reaches two or more persons who conspire together, or go in disguise upon the public highway or upon the premises of another: state the two purposes the section names, protection and voting, in its own terms, and the name it gives the offense. The disguise clause describes a practice from life. Name the practice, and state why Congress wrote the costume into a criminal statute instead of writing violence alone.

Station 3: A Second Witness Before the Committee, 1871 and 1872 (Teaching Summary)

Source: a teaching summary built from the public record of the Joint Select Committee to Inquire into the Condition of Affairs in the Late Insurrectionary States, whose report Congress published in 1872 in thirteen volumes. It is a summary, not a quotation, and it is labeled as one here so that nothing in it is mistaken for a period text.

The committee took testimony in Washington and sent members into the South through 1871, hearing hundreds of witnesses: Black voters and officeholders, teachers, Republican organizers, local sheriffs and judges, and accused Klansmen themselves. Read together, the testimony describes a pattern rather than a set of separate crimes. Disguised bands, sometimes a dozen men and sometimes more than a hundred, visited the houses of Republican voters in the weeks before elections. Men were whipped in counted lashes and ordered to abandon the Republican Party, leave the county, or stop teaching. Warnings were delivered by note and by word of mouth before the visits came. Schools and churches used for Republican meetings were threatened and in places burned. Witness after witness told the committee that local prosecution failed: sheriffs would not arrest, witnesses would not testify for fear of the next visit, and juries drawn from the riders' own counties would not convict. The committee's majority reported that the violence was political in purpose, aimed at the Republican electorate, and beyond the control of the state governments in the counties where it ran strongest.

State the pattern the summary draws from the testimony as a whole: the bands' size and disguise, the timing of the visits, the orders given under the whipping, and the three ways local prosecution failed. Then set this station beside Station 1. Colby is one man describing one night. What does a pattern across hundreds of witnesses give Congress that one testimony, however credible, cannot give it by itself, and what does Colby's single named account give the record that a pattern cannot?

Station 4: The Proclamation and the South Carolina Prosecutions, 1871 and 1872 (Teaching Summary)

Source: a teaching summary built from the public record of President Grant's proclamation of October 17, 1871, and of the federal prosecutions that followed in South Carolina. It is a summary, not a quotation, and it is labeled as one here so that nothing in it is mistaken for a period text.

Under the Ku Klux Klan Act, Grant moved first in the state where the dens were strongest. On October 17, 1871, he proclaimed the armed combinations in nine upcountry South Carolina counties, Spartanburg, York, Union, Chester, Laurens, Newberry, Fairfield, Lancaster, and Chesterfield, to be in insurrection against the government of the United States, commanded their members to disperse and surrender their arms and disguises, and, when the order was defied, suspended habeas corpus in those nine counties. Federal marshals, backed by federal troops, arrested hundreds of men. Federal prosecutors brought hundreds of indictments for conspiracy against citizens' rights. Many of the accused pleaded guilty or confessed and turned evidence against their dens; others were convicted at trial in federal court, and others fled the state. By 1872 organized Klan activity in South Carolina had collapsed. The same machinery was used more lightly elsewhere, and the first Klan as an organization did not survive the campaign.

State the campaign's steps in order: the proclamation's date, what it declared about the nine counties and what it commanded, the measure that followed when the order was defied, and the three outcomes the prosecutions produced among the accused. The campaign ran in federal court, with federal marshals and troops, inside a state whose own courts sat open the whole time. State what that choice of machinery says about the federal government's judgment of the state courts in those counties, using Station 3 as your evidence.

Station 5: Forrest Before the Committee, 1871 (Teaching Summary)

Source: a teaching summary built from the public record of Nathan Bedford Forrest's testimony before the Joint Select Committee in 1871. It is a summary, not a quotation, and it is labeled as one here so that nothing in it is mistaken for a period text.

Forrest came before the committee as the Klan's first Grand Wizard and as the man who had ordered it dissolved. He testified that he had been elected to lead the organization in 1867, that he had found its disguises and name taken up by men beyond his control, and that in January 1869 he had ordered the organization destroyed and its regalia burned because its violence had escaped his purposes. Under questioning he denied directing acts of violence, denied knowledge of outrages as the organization's policy, and said he had advised men across his section to keep the peace and obey the laws. He presented the Klan he had led as a protective association formed when the South's governments had collapsed, and its later crimes as the work of men he could not govern. The committee's majority reported its skepticism of the denials; a minority accepted more of them. Both reports printed the testimony, and the country read the Grand Wizard's account beside the accounts of the men the riders had visited.

State Forrest's account as he gave it: the year of his election, what he says happened to the organization under his name, the order he gave in January 1869, and the two denials he made under questioning. Forrest does not deny the violence itself; he denies directing it and knowing it as policy. State what that choice of ground concedes to the committee, and what it protects. Then name the source problem his testimony poses: he is the best placed witness to the Klan's command and the witness with the most reason to shape the record, in the same chair.

Part 2: The Closing Verdict

The five stations, with the reading, are the evidence for your judgment of what the federal answer was. Sort first, then judge.

The sorting. Enter each station number under what it shows, in one line each, with one quoted phrase from Stations 1 and 2, or one named fact from the summaries, as the evidence: terror as a political instrument, law written against a named practice, a pattern beyond one county, federal force used inside a state, and command denied by the man who held it.

Your verdict. Judge whether the Enforcement Acts are better described as civil rights law, counterinsurgency, or both. Defend the judgment in a paragraph. Your verdict must use at least three stations' evidence, cited by number, and it must account for Station 2, where the law speaks of rights, courts, and crimes, and for Station 4, where the answer arrives as a proclamation, a suspension of habeas corpus, troops, and mass arrests.

The strongest counterclaim. A classmate chooses the single description you did not choose, and argues that a law is what its text says it is, or that an army in a state is an army whatever the statute behind it. State that claim at full strength from the documents, and then answer it: what in the record shows the description you rejected at work, and why does your description still hold the whole of it?

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: Abram Colby's Testimony to Congress

The night, the offers, and the widening. On October 29, 1869, the men broke Colby's door open, took him from his bed to the woods, and whipped him for three hours and more and left him for dead. They asked whether he would ever vote another Radical ticket; he answered that if there was an election tomorrow, he would vote the Radical ticket, and they whipped him a thousand licks more with sticks and straps with buckles on the ends. About two days before, they had offered him $5,000 to go with them and $2,500 in cash to let another man go to the legislature in his place; he told them he would not do it if they gave him all the county was worth. The standings he names are a lawyer, a doctor, and farmers, first class men of the town, and students should be pressed on why he names them: the Klan in his county was not a rabble at its edge but its respectable center in disguise. His close describes a condition, not an incident: no man can make a free speech in his county, and he does not believe it can be done anywhere in Georgia. A committee needed that on the record because sworn testimony, taken under questioning and printed by Congress, is the form in which a legislature builds the factual basis for a statute; a newspaper account can be dismissed as partisan print, and the Enforcement Acts' defense rested on findings exactly of this kind.

Station 2: The Enforcement Act of 1871

The phrase, the disguise, and the offense. Under color of any law, statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage of any State covers the official and the man acting with an official's authority or a community's sanction behind him, the sheriff who joins a raid and the custom that protects him alike. Section 1 makes such a person liable to the party injured in an action at law, a civil suit in federal court, whatever state law says to the contrary. Section 2 reaches conspiracies and disguised riders acting to deprive any person or class of persons of the equal protection of the laws or of equal privileges or immunities, and to prevent, by force, intimidation, or threat, a citizen lawfully entitled to vote from supporting a candidate for President, Vice President, or Congress. The offense is named a high crime. Congress wrote the disguise into the statute because the disguise was the practice's engine: it hid neighbors from neighbors, defeated identification in local courts, and turned a whipping into a visit from nowhere. A statute against violence alone would have been answered by the same identification problem that had already emptied the state courts.

Station 3: A Second Witness Before the Committee (Teaching Summary)

The pattern and the single voice. The summary's pattern: disguised bands of a dozen to more than a hundred men, visits timed to the weeks before elections, whippings in counted lashes with orders to abandon the Republican Party, leave the county, or stop teaching, warnings sent ahead, schools and meeting churches threatened or burned, and local prosecution failing three ways, sheriffs not arresting, witnesses afraid to testify, and juries not convicting. A pattern gives Congress scale and system: it shows a political campaign conducted by terror across states, which is the finding that justifies a federal statute and a president's proclamation, and it answers the defense that each outrage was a private quarrel. Colby's single account gives the record what a pattern cannot: a name, a date, two sums of money, and a refusal spoken under the lash, which is the evidence by which readers judge the pattern's human content and the terror's purpose at the level of one household.

Station 4: The Proclamation and the South Carolina Prosecutions (Teaching Summary)

The steps and the judgment inside them. The proclamation of October 17, 1871, declared the armed combinations in nine named upcountry counties to be in insurrection against the government of the United States and commanded their members to disperse and surrender their arms and disguises. When the order was defied, habeas corpus was suspended in the nine counties. Marshals backed by troops arrested hundreds, prosecutors brought hundreds of indictments, and the accused pleaded guilty or confessed and turned evidence, were convicted at federal trial, or fled the state; by 1872 organized Klan activity in South Carolina had collapsed. Running the campaign through federal machinery while state courts sat open was itself a judgment: on Station 3's evidence, the federal government held that in those counties the local process could not be trusted to arrest, testify against, or convict the riders, because the riders and the local process were too often the same men or the same men's neighbors.

Station 5: Forrest Before the Committee (Teaching Summary)

The ground chosen and the problem posed. Forrest's account: elected to lead the organization in 1867, he found its name and disguises taken up by men beyond his control, ordered the organization destroyed and its regalia burned in January 1869, and under questioning denied directing violence and denied knowledge of outrages as policy, saying he had advised peace and obedience to the laws. Denying direction and policy, rather than denying the violence, concedes the facts the victims testified to and the scale the committee documented; what it protects is his own liability and the claim that the organization he led was protective in purpose. The source problem is the one the question states: the commander is the archive and the interested party at once. Strong answers use him the way the committee did, believing his admissions, which run against his interest, such as his election and his dissolution order, and weighing his denials, which serve his interest, against the testimony of the visited.

Part 2: The Closing Verdict (Grading Guide)

Full credit should be awarded if the verdict:

  • Sorts every station with one quoted phrase from Stations 1 and 2 or one named fact from the summaries for each, and states what each shows, terror as political instrument, law against a named practice, a pattern beyond one county, federal force inside a state, and command denied.
  • Chooses one description, civil rights law, counterinsurgency, or both, and defends the choice from at least three stations, cited by number, rather than from the era's reputation in general.
  • Accounts for Station 2: the acts work through the ordinary forms of civil rights law, defined rights, federal courts, civil liability under color of law, and crimes against voting, and any verdict of pure counterinsurgency must explain why Congress built courts and causes of action if the object was only force.
  • Accounts for Station 4: the acts also armed a president to proclaim insurrection, suspend habeas corpus, and send troops and marshals to make mass arrests in named counties, and any verdict of pure civil rights law must explain why the statute book needed a proclamation in it.
  • States the strongest rival claim at full strength before answering it. For a both verdict: single description answers tend to say the military machinery was incidental or the rights language was a cover. For a law verdict: Station 4's campaign looks like occupation whatever the statute says. For a counterinsurgency verdict: Station 2's text protects a vote and a courtroom remedy, which armies do not usually carry.
  • Answers the rival by naming what it cannot hold, most often the fit between the terror and the answer: the Klan was an insurgency against a constitutional settlement conducted by disguised political violence, so a law against it had to be both a rights statute and a campaign, and each half of the record is the evidence for one half of the description.

Teacher note on the verdicts. The most common failure is a verdict argued from Station 4 alone, in which the South Carolina campaign is treated as the whole of the Enforcement Acts. The acts ran in every southern state as law, in courts and at polls, for years; the campaign was their sharpest edge, not their whole body. The second failure is treating the teaching summaries at Stations 3, 4, and 5 as period quotations. They are labeled summaries on their faces, and their facts may be used, but their sentences may not be quoted as the words of 1871.
