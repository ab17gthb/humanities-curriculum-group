Guided Notes: The Counterrevolution

Lesson question: What did the Klan's terror aim at, and what did it take to break the first Klan?

Unit question: What did Reconstruction settle about freedom, citizenship, and the federal Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Ku Klux Klan: the secret terrorist organization founded at Pulaski, Tennessee, in 1866 and spread across the South as the Invisible Empire, whose disguised night riders whipped, threatened, exiled, and murdered Black voters, Republican organizers, teachers, and officeholders to destroy the Reconstruction governments' electorate

Nathan Bedford Forrest: the former Confederate cavalry general and Memphis slave trader elected the Klan's first Grand Wizard in 1867, who ordered the organization dissolved in 1869 as its violence escaped control and denied directing it when Congress questioned him in 1871

Abram Colby: the Georgia freedman and state legislator whom Klansmen dragged from his house in October 1869 and whipped nearly to death for refusing to abandon Republican politics or sell his vote, and who in 1872 told a congressional committee what was done to him, naming the men and refusing their bribes on the record

night riding: the Klan's characteristic method, mounted men in disguises riding after dark to the houses of their targets, calling them out by name and whipping or shooting them, terror timed to elections, school terms, and contract settlements and aimed at a whole community's conduct, not only its victim

Enforcement Acts: the three laws of 1870 and 1871 by which Congress armed the federal government against the Klan and election violence, making it a federal crime to conspire against citizens' constitutional rights, placing federal supervisors and marshals at the polls, and empowering the president to use troops and suspend habeas corpus against insurrectionary combinations

Ku Klux Klan Act: the third Enforcement Act, of April 1871, which made conspiracy under color of state law to deprive citizens of constitutional rights a federal offense, authorized the president to use the army against such conspiracies, and supplied the authority under which Grant broke the first Klan in South Carolina

federal marshals: the officers of the federal courts, and their deputies, whom the Enforcement Acts empowered to guard polling places, arrest offenders against the election and civil rights laws, and summon civilian posses, the settlement's enforcement arm in counties whose own officers would not act

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher. Teach this lesson sixth in the unit, after Freedom on the Ground and before The Retreat, in one period of about 50 minutes, with the activity in a second period if the schedule allows. It pairs with Lesson 4, whose electorate and governments are the terror's targets, and prepares Lesson 7, on the retreat that followed when federal protection faded. Prior knowledge needed: the Reconstruction Acts and the new electorate from Lessons 3 and 4, and the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments from Lessons 3 and 4. Students will learn the Klan's organization, night riding and its targets, Colby's testimony and refusal, Forrest's dissolution order and denials, the Enforcement Acts' machinery, and the 1871 South Carolina campaign. Teach the violence in testimony and text only; no image of the Klan or of violence is used in this lesson. E. The acts and the crimes: three Enforcement Acts, 1870 and 1871, making it a federal crime to conspire against citizens' constitutional rights, to interfere with voting by force or threat, and to ride in disguise against the rights in the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments. The officers and the courts: federal supervisors at the polls, and federal marshals and deputies empowered to guard polls, arrest offenders, and summon posses, with cases tried in federal court.

F. The act and the proclamation: the Ku Klux Klan Act of April 1871 made conspiracy under color of state law to deprive citizens of constitutional rights a federal offense and authorized the president to use the army against such combinations. In October 1871 President Grant proclaimed the armed combinations in nine South Carolina counties an insurrection, ordered them to disperse, and suspended habeas corpus in those counties. The result by 1872: hundreds of arrests and indictments, and the first Klan in South Carolina broken, its dens dissolved and its night riding stopped.

G. The proof and the premise: the breaking proved that federal power, used at full strength, worked; marshals, troops, prosecutors, and courts moving together collapsed the Invisible Empire within a year. It rested on a president willing to proclaim an insurrection, a Congress willing to fund enforcement, and a northern public willing to sustain both. What returned: the violence returned in the middle seventies in rifle clubs and leagues that rode by daylight.

Terror and Answer at a Glance. The organization, 1866 to 1869: founded at Pulaski in 1866, organized as the Invisible Empire at Nashville in 1867 under Forrest as Grand Wizard, and ordered dissolved by Forrest in 1869 as its violence escaped his control. The method and its targets: night riding by disguised men against Black voters, Republican organizers, teachers, and officeholders, timed to elections, school terms, and contract settlements. The testimony, 1869 to 1872: Colby, whipped for three hours in October 1869 after refusing $5,000 and $2,500, testified to Congress in 1872 and named a lawyer, a doctor, and farmers among his attackers. The federal answer, 1870 to 1872: three Enforcement Acts, federal supervisors and marshals, the Klan Act of April 1871, and the South Carolina campaign that broke the first Klan by 1872.
