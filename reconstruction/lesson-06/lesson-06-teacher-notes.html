Teacher Notes

Reconstruction · Lesson 6 · The Counterrevolution: The Klan and the Enforcement Acts

These are the speaker notes from the slide deck, in slide order, set for printing. The deck itself carries the main ideas in a few fragments per slide; the teaching is here. Print this and keep it beside you while you teach. Each entry is headed by its slide number and shows what is on that slide, so you can find your place without flipping back to the screen. Vocabulary definitions match the student guided notes word for word.

Slide 1 · Title: The Counterrevolution

On the slide: The Counterrevolution · The Klan’s terror, the testimony against it, and the federal answer of 1870 and 1871

The title image is a photograph of the United States Capitol at Washington, taken by Mathew Brady about 1860 to 1865 and held by the National Archives. It is the right opening picture for this lesson, and the only kind this lesson uses. The testimony students will read was given to a committee of this building, and the laws they will read were passed inside it. What happened in Georgia and South Carolina farmhouses at night was decided, in the end, by what this building and the president were willing to do about it. Say the lesson's discipline aloud at the start: the violence is taught in testimony and text, stated plainly, and no picture of the Klan, of its riders, or of any victim appears anywhere in this lesson.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: What did the Klan’s terror aim at, and what did it take to break the first Klan? · Unit question: What did Reconstruction settle about freedom, citizenship, and the federal Union, and what did it leave open?

The lesson question has two halves, and students should keep them separate all period. What the Klan's terror aimed at is a question about purpose: the riders were not random criminals, and the testimony shows purchase attempted first, violence second, and the target always political, a vote, a seat, a school, a contract. What it took to break the first Klan is a question about machinery: statutes, supervisors, marshals, troops, prosecutors, and a presidential proclamation, all of it resting on a northern will to sustain the effort. The unit question stands behind both halves, because this lesson is where the settlement of the earlier lessons meets the organized force raised to destroy it, and where students learn that enforcement is part of a settlement, not an afterthought to it.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: Terror organized, testimony given, law answered · A · Terror organized as politics · Pulaski 1866; the Invisible Empire, 1867; Forrest · B · Night riding and its targets · Voters, organizers, teachers, officeholders · C

The map follows the guided notes section by section. Section A builds the organization, founded at Pulaski in 1866 and refounded as the Invisible Empire in 1867 under Forrest. Section B gives the method, night riding, and its chosen targets and timing. Section C reads Abram Colby's testimony whole, the offers, the attack, and the refusal carried to Congress in 1872. Section D hears Forrest's answer, the dissolution order of 1869 and the denials of 1871. Section E builds the federal machinery of the Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871. Section F watches the Ku Klux Klan Act at work in South Carolina in the fall of 1871. Section G closes on the breaking and on the premise underneath it, the will the statute could not supply.

Slide 4 · Section A divider: Terror Organized as Politics

On the slide: Terror Organized as Politics · Founded at Pulaski in 1866 and organized as the Invisible Empire in 1867, with disguise as its first instrument.

Open the section by slowing down on the founding, because students often imagine the Klan springing up armed and organized in a single night. It began in 1866 at Pulaski, Tennessee, as a club formed by six former Confederate soldiers, and it became dangerous when it was organized: the Nashville meeting of 1867 gave it a name built for awe, the Invisible Empire, a structure of dens, and officers with borrowed romantic titles. Teach the disguise as an instrument, not a costume detail. A disguised rider could not be identified by his victim, could not be named to a sheriff, and could sit in church or court the next day as a respectable neighbor. The organization and the anonymity together are the invention this section names.

Slide 5 · A club becomes an empire

On the slide: A club becomes an empire · ▪ Six former Confederate soldiers, Pulaski, 1866 / ▪ Nashville, 1867: the Invisible Empire / ▪ Dens, rituals, and officers under borrowed titles / ▪ Forrest elected the first Grand Wizard /

Work the fragments in order. The founders are former soldiers, which is why the Klan could organize like an army from its first year. The Nashville meeting is the turning point because it turned local clubs into a single named empire with Forrest at its head as Grand Wizard, and Forrest's standing as the Confederacy's most feared cavalry commander gave the name its threat before a single ride. The dens are the local unit students should picture: a county organization, recruited from the neighborhood, which is why its victims so often knew their attackers' voices and still could not prove a name in court. Close on the last fragment. The sheet and false face did three jobs at once: they hid the man, they frightened the household, and they defeated the legal process that depended on identification.

Slide 6 · Section B divider: Night Riding and Its Targets

On the slide: Night Riding and Its Targets · The method taught in testimony and text, stated plainly. No image of the riders or their work appears in this lesson.

This divider carries no picture, and that is deliberate. Section B teaches the method of the terror, and the unit's discipline holds that the method is taught in words and testimony, never in an image of riders or of violence. Tell students what night riding was with the plainness the reading uses: mounted men, in disguise, after dark, at a named person's house. The terror was scheduled, and the schedule is the section's real content. Raids came before elections, when a vote could still be changed. They came at the opening of school terms, when a teacher could still be driven off. They came at contract settlements, when a family's year of labor was priced. Terror that keeps a calendar is a policy, and students should learn to read it as one.

Slide 7 · The method and the message

On the slide: The method and the message · ▪ Mounted men in disguises, after dark / ▪ Targets called out by name, at their houses / ▪ Black voters and Republican organizers first / ▪ Teachers, officeholders, and their allies / ▪

Teach the method as a system with three parts. The disguise protected the rider. The hour and the visit to a house made the whole family, and the whole neighborhood within earshot, the audience. The selection of the target made the lesson general: whip one organizer and every organizer in the county recalculates; threaten one teacher and every school in the district hears of it. Then name the targets in the reading's order: Black voters and Republican organizers first, because the electorate was the settlement's foundation; teachers, because the schools built the future the terror meant to close; officeholders, because they were the governments in person; and white southerners who worked with those governments, because the coalition had to be broken at every joint. One whipping was addressed to a whole community, and students should be able to say why.

Slide 8 · Stat: three appointments the raids kept

On the slide: 3 · appointments the raids kept · Before elections · at the opening of school terms · at contract settlements

Three is a small number for a giant slide, and it earns its size. The raids kept three appointments on the community's calendar: the election, the school term, and the contract settlement. Each appointment was a moment when the new order became real in daily life, when a man voted, when a child entered a schoolroom, when a family's labor was converted into money or debt. The Klan struck at exactly those moments, which is how students can tell the terror was aimed at the settlement and not at disorder in general. A bandit strikes when the money moves; this terror struck when freedom acted. Have the class say the three moments aloud, in order, before moving on, because the exit ticket and the writing task both return to this timing as evidence of purpose.

Slide 9 · Section C divider: Abram Colby Tells Congress

On the slide: Abram Colby Tells Congress · A Georgia legislator, whipped in 1869 for refusing to sell his seat, carries the account to Congress in 1872.

Section C is the lesson's center, and it should be taught at reading pace, not summary pace. Abram Colby was a freedman elected to the Georgia legislature, an organizer who carried Black voters to the Republican ticket, and the Klan came for him in the sequence the testimony records: first the offers, money for his allegiance and more money for his seat, then, when purchase failed, the visit in the night. Tell students before they read that they will hear the whole account in Colby's own words at the activity's first station, given to a congressional committee in 1872. His testimony is the documentary heart of the lesson: a named man, a date, two sums of money, and a refusal, all sworn and printed by the government the terror was fighting.

Slide 10 · Purchase first, violence second

On the slide: Purchase first, violence second · ▪ $5,000 to change sides; $2,500 for his seat / ▪ Refused at both prices, in his own words / ▪ October 29, 1869: the door broken open / ▪ Three hours in the woods, left for dead / ▪

Walk the sequence slowly, because the order is the argument. Two days before the attack, the offers came: five thousand dollars to go with them, twenty five hundred in cash to let another man take his seat in the legislature. That is not the behavior of a mob; it is a price quoted for a piece of the new government. Colby refused at both prices, in words the class will read verbatim: he would not do it if they gave him all the county was worth. Then came October 29, 1869: the door broken open, the woods, three hours of whipping, and under the whipping the same refusal in political form, that if there was an election tomorrow he would vote the Radical ticket. Purchase first, violence second, refusal throughout: fix that order in memory.

Slide 11 · 1872: the refusal goes on the record

On the slide: 1872: the refusal goes on the record · ▪ A congressional committee hears the account / ▪ Attackers named by standing: lawyer, doctor, farmers / ▪ First class men of the town, in disguise / ▪ No free speech in his cou

The testimony of 1872 did work that the whipping of 1869 could not undo, and students should see Colby as an actor in 1872 and not only a victim in 1869. He named the standing of his attackers to the committee: some are first class men in our town, a lawyer, a doctor, and farmers among them. That sentence dismantles the defense that the Klan was a rabble of the poor and the desperate; in Colby's county it was the respectable center of the community in disguise. He closed by widening the account from his county to his state: no man could make a free speech in his county, and he did not believe it could be done anywhere in Georgia. Congress printed sentences of that kind by the volume, and the Enforcement Acts rest on the record they built.

Slide 12 · Section D divider: Forrest: Dissolution and Denial

On the slide: Forrest: Dissolution and Denial · The Grand Wizard who ordered the Klan dissolved / in 1869, and denied directing it in 1871.

The portrait is Nathan Bedford Forrest as a Confederate general, photographed during the war by E. and H. T. Anthony; it is a wartime portrait, used here because the lesson pictures the man, never his organization at work. Section D is the record's other voice. Forrest had been elected the Klan's first Grand Wizard in 1867, and in January 1869 he ordered the organization dissolved, declaring that its violence had escaped his control. When Congress questioned him in 1871, he denied directing the violence and denied knowing outrages as policy. Teach this section as a source problem, not a verdict: the man best placed to describe the Klan's command was also the man with the most reason to shape its description, and the committee had to weigh his admissions against his denials.

Slide 13 · Admissions and denials

On the slide: Admissions and denials · ▪ Elected Grand Wizard, 1867, by his own account / ▪ January 1869: the organization ordered dissolved / ▪ Its violence, he said, had escaped his control / ▪ 1871: no direction given, no polic

Give Forrest his full account before weighing it, the way the reading does. His admissions run against his interest and are therefore strong evidence: he accepted election as Grand Wizard, he found the name and disguises taken up by men he could not control, and he ordered the organization destroyed and its regalia burned in January 1869 because its violence had escaped his purposes. His denials serve his interest and must be weighed against the testimony: he said he directed no violence, knew no outrages as policy, and had advised peace across his section. Students should notice the shape of the defense. It concedes the terror and disputes only its command, which protects the founder while surrendering the organization. The committee's majority reported its skepticism; the country read both accounts side by side.

Slide 14 · Section E divider: Congress Arms the Federal Government

On the slide: Congress Arms the Federal Government · Three Enforcement Acts, 1870 and 1871: rights given officers, courts, and a reach past the states.

Section E turns from the terror to the answer, and the answer begins as law, not as troops. Congress passed three Enforcement Acts in 1870 and 1871, and students should learn the machinery piece by piece, because the writing task asks them to judge what kind of instrument the acts were. The acts made conspiracy against citizens' constitutional rights a federal crime. They reached the disguised rider by name, in the statute's own words. They placed federal supervisors at the polls to watch registration and counting, and they armed federal marshals to guard polling places, make arrests, and summon posses. Above all, they moved the cases: out of county courts that would not convict, into federal court, before federal judges and juries. A right with a court behind it is a different thing from a right on paper.

Slide 15 · Law built for the emergency

On the slide: Law built for the emergency · ▪ Conspiracy against constitutional rights, a federal crime / ▪ The disguised rider named in the statute / ▪ Federal supervisors at the polls / ▪ Marshals to guard, arrest, and summon po

Work the fragments as a chain of failures and remedies. The state process failed first, at three points the testimony names: sheriffs would not arrest, witnesses would not testify for fear of the next visit, and juries would not convict their neighbors. Each piece of the federal machinery answers one failure. Marshals answer the sheriff. Federal protection and federal venue answer the frightened witness. A federal jury, drawn wider than one county, answers the local jury. The supervisors at the polls answer the election itself, the place where the terror had aimed to decide outcomes before a ballot was cast. Close by asking what this machinery assumed: that the federal government was willing to station its own officers inside southern elections, county by county, for as long as the terror lasted.

Slide 16 · Supervisors watch; marshals act

On the slide: Supervisors watch; marshals act · ▪ Supervisors: registration, polling, the count / ▪ Marshals: guards, arrests, and posses / ▪ Each officer answers a named local failure / ▪ Sheriffs who would not arrest; juries who

Two kinds of federal officer carry this lesson's machinery, and students mix them up, so separate them cleanly. The supervisors watched the process: registration lists, polling places, the counting of ballots, the points where a legal election could be stolen administratively. The marshals carried the force: they guarded the polls, arrested offenders against the election and civil rights laws, and could summon a civilian posse when a county's own officers stood aside. Both kinds of officer were the settlement made visible at ground level, federal authority standing in a county that had its own courthouse and its own sheriff. When students write about the acts as law, these officers are their evidence; when they write about the acts as a campaign, the same officers, backed by troops in South Carolina, are the evidence on that side too.

Slide 17 · Section F divider: The Klan Act and South Carolina

On the slide: The Klan Act and South Carolina · The act of April 1871, used at full strength / in nine South Carolina counties that fall.

The portrait is President Grant, from the Brady-Handy photograph collection at the Library of Congress, taken between 1870 and 1880. Section F is the statute at full stretch. The third Enforcement Act, the Ku Klux Klan Act of April 1871, made conspiracy under color of state law a federal offense and authorized the president to use the army against insurrectionary combinations. Grant chose the ground where the dens were strongest: nine upcountry counties of South Carolina. In October 1871 he proclaimed those combinations an insurrection, ordered them to disperse, and suspended habeas corpus in the nine counties. What followed was the full machinery of Section E, marshals, troops, prosecutors, and federal court, used at once and at strength. Teach the sequence as a deliberate escalation the statute had authorized in advance.

Slide 18 · One act, two reaches

On the slide: One act, two reaches · ▪ Under color of state law, a federal offense / ▪ Section 1: the injured party sues in federal court / ▪ The president may use the army / ▪ Combinations may be proclaimed insurrection / ▪ A ri

Read the act's two reaches carefully, because the verdict task turns on them. The first reach is legal: conspiracy under color of state law to deprive citizens of constitutional rights became a federal offense, and Section 1 of the same act let the injured person sue in federal court. That is the language of civil rights law, rights, liability, and courts. The second reach is military: the president could use the army against combinations he proclaimed insurrectionary, and suspend habeas corpus where the insurrection ran. That is the language of counterinsurgency written into a statute. Students who see only one reach will misjudge the act. Congress knew the terror was both a crime problem and a political war against a constitutional settlement, and it wrote an instrument with a hand for each.

Slide 19 · South Carolina, October 1871 to 1872

On the slide: South Carolina, October 1871 to 1872 · ▪ Proclamation, October 17, 1871: disperse / ▪ Habeas corpus suspended in nine counties / ▪ Marshals and troops: hundreds arrested / ▪ Hundreds indicted; pleas, convictions, fli

The South Carolina campaign is the proof case, and its steps should be recited in order. The proclamation came on October 17, 1871, naming nine counties and ordering the armed combinations to disperse and give up their arms and disguises. When the order was defied, habeas corpus was suspended in those counties, and marshals backed by federal troops arrested hundreds of men. Federal prosecutors brought hundreds of indictments for conspiracy against citizens' rights. The accused pleaded guilty, confessed and turned evidence against their dens, were convicted at trial, or fled the state. By 1872 organized Klan activity in South Carolina had collapsed, and the first Klan as an organization did not survive the campaign. One state received the full dose; students should ask, in the next section, what the dose cost and what it depended on.

Slide 20 · Stat: nine counties

On the slide: 9 · counties under the proclamation · Spartanburg · York · Union · Chester · Laurens · Newberry · Fairfield · Lancaster · Chesterfield

Nine counties is the second giant number of the lesson, and it should be stated precisely. Grant did not proclaim the whole South an insurrection, and he did not suspend habeas corpus generally. He named nine upcountry South Carolina counties, Spartanburg, York, Union, Chester, Laurens, Newberry, Fairfield, Lancaster, and Chesterfield, the counties where the dens were strongest and the testimony worst. The precision is part of the record's meaning: the federal answer was aimed by evidence, county by county, on the findings the congressional investigation had built. It is also the measure of the answer's limits. Nine counties got the full machinery. The rest of the South got the statute, the marshals, and the courts, in doses that varied with local juries and northern attention, which is why the Klan died as an organization while its method survived to be reused.

Slide 21 · Section G divider: Broken, and What the Breaking Depended On

On the slide: Broken, and What the Breaking Depended On · Federal power, used whole, worked. The power rested on a will the statute could not supply.

Section G is the lesson's judgment section, and it should be taught as an open ledger rather than a celebration. What the breaking proved is stated first, and it is a large proof: federal power, used at full strength and in combination, worked. Marshals, troops, prosecutors, and courts moving together dissolved the Invisible Empire in the state where it was strongest, within about a year. No student should leave doubting that the first Klan was breakable, because it was broken. Then the ledger's other column. The power rested on a president willing to proclaim insurrection in an American state, a Congress willing to pay for enforcement, and a northern public willing to sustain both. None of those three wills was written into the statute. The next lesson begins where they start to fail.

Slide 22 · Capacity, and dependence

On the slide: Capacity, and dependence · ▪ Marshals, troops, prosecutors, courts, together / ▪ The Invisible Empire collapsed within a year / ▪ The proof: the first Klan was breakable, and broken / ▪ The premise: a president, a Co

Hold the two findings together and resist letting either swallow the other. The first finding is about capacity: the settlement of 1868 to 1870 included an enforcement arm, and when the arm was used whole, organized terror collapsed. The second finding is about dependence: the arm moved only while a national majority willed it to move, and the men who had ridden in disguise understood that arithmetic as well as anyone in Washington. They did not need to defeat the Enforcement Acts. They needed to outwait them, and to make each year of enforcement cost the North more patience than the year before. The violence also changed its form: the rifle clubs and leagues of the middle seventies would do openly, by daylight, much of what the first Klan had done in disguise, and Lesson 7 takes up that transformation.

Use the glance slide to consolidate before the vocabulary, row by row, in the lesson's order. The organization row fixes the founding, the empire, and the dissolution order. The method row fixes night riding, its targets, and its three appointed moments. The testimony row fixes Colby: the sums, the night, the refusal, and the naming of respectable men. Slide 24 · Vocabulary: the organization and its chief

On the slide: Words for the organization and its chief · Ku Klux Klan · the Invisible Empire, from 1866 · Nathan Bedford Forrest · its first Grand Wizard, 1867

Two terms anchor the organization and its chief, and their full definitions are carried here word for word as the guided notes give them. Ku Klux Klan: the secret terrorist organization founded at Pulaski, Tennessee, in 1866 and spread across the South as the Invisible Empire, whose disguised night riders whipped, threatened, exiled, and murdered Black voters, Republican organizers, teachers, and officeholders to destroy the Reconstruction governments' electorate. Nathan Bedford Forrest: the former Confederate cavalry general and Memphis slave trader elected the Klan's first Grand Wizard in 1867, who ordered the organization dissolved in 1869 as its violence escaped control and denied directing it when Congress questioned him in 1871. Teach the pairing: the definition of the Klan names its victims' categories, and the definition of Forrest names the arc the documents test, command claimed, dissolution ordered, direction denied.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: the testimony and the method

On the slide: Words for the testimony and the method · Abram Colby · the legislator who refused, and testified · night riding · the Klan’s characteristic method

Two terms carry the testimony and the method, and their full definitions are carried here word for word as the guided notes give them. Abram Colby: the Georgia freedman and state legislator whom Klansmen dragged from his house in October 1869 and whipped nearly to death for refusing to abandon Republican politics or sell his vote, and who in 1872 told a congressional committee what was done to him, naming the men and refusing their bribes on the record. night riding: the Klan's characteristic method, mounted men in disguises riding after dark to the houses of their targets, calling them out by name and whipping or shooting them, terror timed to elections, school terms, and contract settlements and aimed at a whole community's conduct, not only its victim. Teach the pairing: Colby's definition is built from his refusal, and night riding's definition ends on the point students must carry into the writing task, that the terror was aimed at a whole community's conduct and not only at its victim.

Slide 26 · Vocabulary: the federal answer

On the slide: Words for the federal answer · Enforcement Acts · the three laws of 1870 and 1871 · Ku Klux Klan Act · the third act, April 1871 · federal marshals · the settlement’s enforcement arm

Three terms carry the federal answer, in the guided notes' words. Enforcement Acts: the three laws of 1870 and 1871 by which Congress armed the federal government against the Klan and election violence, making it a federal crime to conspire against citizens' constitutional rights, placing federal supervisors and marshals at the polls, and empowering the president to use troops and suspend habeas corpus against insurrectionary combinations. Ku Klux Klan Act: the third Enforcement Act, of April 1871, which made conspiracy under color of state law to deprive citizens of constitutional rights a federal offense, authorized the president to use the army against such conspiracies, and supplied the authority under which Grant broke the first Klan in South Carolina. federal marshals: the officers of the federal courts, and their deputies, whom the Enforcement Acts empowered to guard polling places, arrest offenders against the election and civil rights laws, and summon civilian posses, the settlement's enforcement arm in counties whose own officers would not act. The staircase runs acts, act, officers.

Slide 27 · Closing synthesis: two findings, held together

On the slide: Two findings, held together · The terror aimed at the electorate the Reconstruction Acts had made, and it was broken, where the federal government used its full power, within a year. / The breaking rested on a president,

Close the lesson by returning to its question with the whole record in view. The terror aimed at an electorate: at the voters the Reconstruction Acts had enrolled, the teachers who staffed the new schools, the officeholders who staffed the new governments, and the coalition that held them together. It was broken, where it was broken, by the settlement's own machinery used at full strength, and the breaking is the strongest evidence in the unit that the settlement could have been held. What it could not supply was the will to hold it everywhere and indefinitely. Students should leave able to argue either half and required to hold both, because the unit's remaining lessons are the story of the second half overtaking the first.

Slide 28 · Now your turn: answer the question and exit ticket

On the slide: Answer the question · What did the Klan’s terror aim at, and what did it take to break the first Klan? Judge the testimony and the statute, and state what the answer depended on. · Activity · Testimony and Answer: the te

Send students to the work in the lesson's order. The activity, Testimony and Answer, puts Colby's testimony and the Enforcement Act of 1871 in their hands verbatim, beside three labeled teaching summaries, and ends in the verdict this whole lesson has prepared: civil rights law, counterinsurgency, or both. The notebook page charts the terror and the answer stage by stage. The quick check, Testimony and Statute, tests the two quoted records against each other. The writing task asks for the verdict in a full essay, defended against its strongest rival. The exit ticket is below: define night riding and the Enforcement Acts with their years, state the terror's aim in two sentences, and answer the lesson question in two or three, naming what the breaking required and what it depended on.
