## Edition discipline (binding on every document in this unit)
Period texts are public domain, but the edition still matters. Where the bank carries the text, quote the bank record and name the edition its sourceNote names. Where the bank does not carry it (Johnson's Amnesty Proclamation and Civil Rights veto message, the Reconstruction Acts, the Articles of Impeachment, Special Field Orders No. 15, Hayes's First Inaugural), the skeleton names a fallback for each: fetch and verify the text from the named transcription (the American Presidency Project, the Avalon Project, the Statutes at Large, the National Archives or Freedmen and Southern Society transcriptions) during the build and name it in the document's Source line and the lesson's img/SOURCES.md, or write a teaching summary and label it a summary on its face, as the model units do. Spelling stays as the edition carries it; if the edition modernizes spelling, the Source line says so, exactly as the bank records do. Do not reproduce a famous quotation from memory and attach an edition name to it. The bank's sharecropping contract record (sharecropping-contract-1879) is dated 1879, past this unit's 1877 close: do not reprint it in any lesson document; Lesson 5 teaches contract terms through a labeled teaching summary that discloses the 1879 contract as its source, and the manifest records the boundary call.

## Image discipline for Reconstruction (binding)
- The counterrevolution's violence is taught in testimony and text only. Do NOT download or reproduce any photograph or print of Klan violence, of victims, of lynchings, or of the Memphis and New Orleans riots, in a student document, a deck, or a teacher notes document. Name the choice on the document's face where the bank record's presentation calls for it and in SOURCES.md, following the Sectional unit's practice with photographs of the dead.
- The bank's cartoon records (Nast's Emancipation, Waud's First Vote, Nast's Worse Than Slavery and Colored Rule, Frost's Democratic Ticket) are presented by the bank as images described, with captions transcribed. Teach them the way the bank presents them: if a builder verifies a public domain file at the Library of Congress and reproduces it, the file page, holder, and public domain basis go in SOURCES.md; otherwise the station carries the bank's description and transcribed captions, which is the bank's own audited presentation and is complete for teaching.
- Nast's "Colored Rule" carries the era's racist caricature in its imagery. It is taught in Lesson 7's reading and deck only, as evidence of the image war and of its maker's limits, never as decoration, and never as a station image.
- No image of violence is used as decoration anywhere in the unit.

## Vocabulary (use verbatim everywhere: guided notes, decks, study materials)

### Lesson 1 (blueprint 1, 2)
- Reconstruction: the period from 1865 to 1877 in which the United States decided how the seceded states would return to the Union, what freedom would mean for the four million people emancipation had freed, and how much of the settlement the federal government would enforce
- Andrew Johnson: the Tennessee tailor and Unionist Democrat who became vice president under Lincoln in 1864 and president on Lincoln's assassination in April 1865, whose lenient restoration plan and vetoes of Congress's measures set the first terms of Reconstruction and brought him to impeachment in 1868
- presidential Reconstruction: the restoration program Johnson ran from April 1865, offering amnesty and pardons to most former Confederates, letting the southern states organize governments once they abolished slavery, repudiated their secession ordinances and Confederate debts, and left the terms of Black freedom to those governments
- Freedmen's Bureau: the federal agency Congress created in March 1865, formally the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, which fed the hungry of both races, ran hospitals and courts, supervised labor contracts, legalized marriages, and built schools across the South, the first federal relief agency in American history
- Black Codes: the laws the restored southern governments passed in 1865 and 1866 to control Black labor and movement, imposing vagrancy penalties, forced apprenticeships of Black children, and contract rules that tied freedpeople to plantations and denied them equal rights of testimony, arms, and property
- Amnesty Proclamation: Johnson's proclamation of May 29, 1865, which pardoned most former Confederates who swore a loyalty oath and restored their property except in enslaved people, while excepting fourteen classes, including high officers and the wealthy, who had to apply to him personally for pardon
- Jourdon Anderson: the Tennessee freedman who in August 1865 answered his former enslaver's invitation to return with a dictated letter demanding back wages for thirty two years of his own labor and twenty for his wife's, and asking whether his daughters would be safe and his children schooled, freedom's terms stated by a freedman himself

### Lesson 2 (blueprint 3, 4)
- Thirteenth Amendment: the constitutional amendment, passed by Congress on January 31, 1865, and ratified that December, that abolished slavery and involuntary servitude everywhere in the United States, except as punishment for a crime, the first of the three Reconstruction amendments and the only one adopted while the war still ran
- Civil Rights Act of 1866: the law Congress passed over Johnson's veto in April 1866, the first statute enacted over a presidential veto on a major measure, which declared all persons born in the United States citizens and guaranteed citizens of every race the same rights to contract, sue, hold property, and enjoy the law's protection
- Radical Republicans: the faction of the Republican Party, led in the House by Thaddeus Stevens and in the Senate by Charles Sumner, which held that the war had earned a thorough reconstruction of the South on the basis of equal civil and political rights, and that Congress, not the president, held the power to set its terms
- Thaddeus Stevens: the Pennsylvania congressman, the House's most uncompromising Reconstruction leader, who argued that the seceded states were conquered provinces subject to Congress's terms and demanded confiscation of the great planters' estates to give freed families land of their own
- Lyman Trumbull: the Illinois senator who chaired the Judiciary Committee and wrote both the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and the Freedmen's Bureau bill Johnson vetoed, a moderate whose break with the president over the Black Codes carried the Republican center into congressional Reconstruction
- freedmen's conventions: the state conventions freedpeople held across the South in 1865 and 1866, electing delegates, organizing communities, and petitioning for land, fair wages, family protection, schools, and the ballot, the first political assemblies of the emancipated
- freedpeople: the name historians use, following the usage of the time, for the roughly four million men, women, and children freed from slavery by emancipation and the Thirteenth Amendment, whose own demands and institution building drove Reconstruction's agenda from below

### Lesson 3 (blueprint 5, 6)
- Fourteenth Amendment: the amendment Congress proposed in June 1866 and the states ratified in 1868, which wrote birthright citizenship into the Constitution, forbade any state to abridge citizens' privileges or immunities, due process, or equal protection of the laws, cut the representation of states that disfranchised male voters, and barred leading former Confederates from office unless Congress removed the disability
- Reconstruction Acts: the laws of March 1867, passed over Johnson's veto, which set aside the existing governments of the ten unreconstructed states, divided them into five military districts, and required new constitutions guaranteeing Black male suffrage, ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment, and Congress's approval before readmission to representation
- military districts: the five command zones into which the Reconstruction Acts divided the ten unreconstructed southern states, each under a Union general empowered to register voters, supervise elections and conventions, remove officials, and protect persons and property where civil authority would not
- Joint Committee on Reconstruction: the fifteen member committee of senators and representatives formed in December 1865, which took testimony on conditions across the South, drafted the Fourteenth Amendment, and reported that the seceded states had no claim to representation until Congress set the terms of their return
- Tenure of Office Act: the law of March 1867, passed over Johnson's veto, which forbade the president to remove officials confirmed by the Senate without the Senate's consent, the statute Johnson's removal of Secretary of War Stanton defied in the act that triggered his impeachment
- Edwin M. Stanton: Lincoln's secretary of war, who held his post into Johnson's administration, sided with Congress against the president's policy, and whose removal in February 1868 in defiance of the Tenure of Office Act brought the impeachment crisis to its head
- impeachment: the constitutional process by which the House of Representatives charges a federal officer and the Senate tries the charges, used against Andrew Johnson in 1868, when the Senate's vote to convict fell one short of the two thirds the Constitution requires

### Lesson 4 (blueprint 7, 8)
- Fifteenth Amendment: the amendment Congress proposed in February 1869 and the states ratified in February 1870, which forbade the United States and the states to deny or abridge a citizen's right to vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude, the third and last of the Reconstruction amendments
- Hiram Rhodes Revels: the Mississippi minister, freeborn in North Carolina, who had recruited Black regiments and served as a Union army chaplain, elected to the United States Senate in 1870 as the first Black member of Congress, seated in the chamber Jefferson Davis had left, where he argued for desegregating the District of Columbia schools
- Blanche K. Bruce: the Mississippi officeholder born enslaved in Virginia, who rose through tax and education offices to election to the United States Senate in 1874, the second Black senator and the first to serve a full term, seated while the settlement that elected him was already being dismantled
- P. B. S. Pinchback: the Louisiana leader, freeborn in Georgia and a Union officer in the war, who rose through the state senate to serve as acting governor of Louisiana for five weeks in the winter of 1872 and 1873, the first Black man to govern an American state
- carpetbaggers: the hostile name white southern Democrats gave northern men who came South during Reconstruction, teachers, ministers, merchants, investors, Union veterans, and office seekers among them, pictured in Democratic print as adventurers carrying everything they owned in a carpetbag
- scalawags: the hostile name white southern Democrats gave white southerners who joined the Republican Party, upcountry Unionists, former Whigs, and poor farmers among them, whose votes, alongside Black voters and northern newcomers, built the Reconstruction governments
- Union League: the organization of patriotic clubs, spread through the South from 1867 under the Loyal League name, which organized Black voters and white Unionists into the Republican Party, meeting in churches and schoolhouses to register voters, teach the new politics, and turn out the electorate the Reconstruction Acts created

### Lesson 5 (blueprint 9, 10)
- Special Field Orders No. 15: General William T. Sherman's military order of January 16, 1865, which reserved the Sea Islands and a coastal strip of South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida for settlement by freed families in plots of not more than forty acres, under which some forty thousand freedpeople settled before Johnson restored the land to its former owners
- forty acres and a mule: the freedpeople's shorthand for land redistribution, drawn from Special Field Orders No. 15 and the army mules lent to the settlers, the expectation that freedom's material base was land of one's own, whose defeat fixed the land question at the center of Reconstruction's unfinished business
- sharecropping: the labor system that spread across the cotton South after emancipation, in which a family farmed a plot of a landowner's land in return for a share of the crop, commonly half, settling accounts once a year, the compromise between freedpeople's refusal of supervised gang labor and landowners' refusal to sell land or pay steady wages
- crop lien: the credit system that financed sharecropping, in which a furnishing merchant advanced seed, tools, food, and clothing against a legal claim on the crop before it was planted, at prices and interest the merchant set, so that a poor harvest or a disputed account renewed the family's debt at every settlement
- freedmen's schools: the schools freedpeople founded, taxed themselves to sustain, and filled across the South, taught by Black teachers and by northern missionaries under Bureau and church sponsorship, enrolling some quarter of a million students by 1870 and laying the foundation of the South's first public school systems
- African Methodist Episcopal Church: the independent Black denomination organized in Philadelphia in 1816, which carried its churches across the South by the thousands after emancipation, its ministers doubling as organizers of the new politics and its meetinghouses serving as schoolhouses, league halls, and the first property Black communities owned outright
- Freedmen's Savings Bank: the bank Congress chartered in March 1865 to hold the savings of Black soldiers and freedpeople, which took in the deposits of tens of thousands of families before speculation and the Panic of 1873 brought it down in 1874, costing its depositors much of what they had saved

### Lesson 6 (blueprint 11, 12)
- Ku Klux Klan: the secret terrorist organization founded at Pulaski, Tennessee, in 1866 and spread across the South as the Invisible Empire, whose disguised night riders whipped, threatened, exiled, and murdered Black voters, Republican organizers, teachers, and officeholders to destroy the Reconstruction governments' electorate
- Nathan Bedford Forrest: the former Confederate cavalry general and Memphis slave trader elected the Klan's first Grand Wizard in 1867, who ordered the organization dissolved in 1869 as its violence escaped control and denied directing it when Congress questioned him in 1871
- Abram Colby: the Georgia freedman and state legislator whom Klansmen dragged from his house in October 1869 and whipped nearly to death for refusing to abandon Republican politics or sell his vote, and who in 1872 told a congressional committee what was done to him, naming the men and refusing their bribes on the record
- night riding: the Klan's characteristic method, mounted men in disguises riding after dark to the houses of their targets, calling them out by name and whipping or shooting them, terror timed to elections, school terms, and contract settlements and aimed at a whole community's conduct, not only its victim
- Enforcement Acts: the three laws of 1870 and 1871 by which Congress armed the federal government against the Klan and election violence, making it a federal crime to conspire against citizens' constitutional rights, placing federal supervisors and marshals at the polls, and empowering the president to use troops and suspend habeas corpus against insurrectionary combinations
- Ku Klux Klan Act: the third Enforcement Act, of April 1871, which made conspiracy under color of state law to deprive citizens of constitutional rights a federal offense, authorized the president to use the army against such conspiracies, and supplied the authority under which Grant broke the first Klan in South Carolina
- federal marshals: the officers of the federal courts, and their deputies, whom the Enforcement Acts empowered to guard polling places, arrest offenders against the election and civil rights laws, and summon civilian posses, the settlement's enforcement arm in counties whose own officers would not act

### Lesson 7 (blueprint 13, 14)
- Slaughter-House Cases: the Supreme Court's 5 to 4 decision of April 1873, its first reading of the Fourteenth Amendment, which held that the amendment protected only the narrow rights of national citizenship and left the great body of civil rights under state authority, the ruling that began the courts' narrowing of the Reconstruction amendments
- Amnesty Act of 1872: the law of May 1872 by which Congress removed the Fourteenth Amendment's officeholding disability from nearly all former Confederates, restoring the South's old leadership class to eligibility for office some seven years after the war's end
- Liberal Republicans: the reform Republicans who broke with Grant in 1872 over corruption and the costs of Reconstruction, nominating Horace Greeley on a platform of amnesty, civil service reform, and an end to federal protection of the southern governments, and carrying respectability into the retreat
- Panic of 1873: the financial collapse that opened with the failure of Jay Cooke's banking house in September 1873 and settled into a six year depression of closed mills, wage cuts, and unemployment, which turned northern attention from the South's unfinished settlement to hard times at home
- White League: the paramilitary organization formed in Louisiana in 1874, pledged openly to restore white supremacy and drive Republicans from office, whose members murdered Republican officeholders at Coushatta and fought federal troops at Liberty Place in New Orleans that year, terror organized as politics by daylight
- Civil Rights Act of 1875: Charles Sumner's last cause, enacted in March 1875 after his death, which guaranteed all citizens equal enjoyment of inns, public conveyances, and theaters and barred racial exclusion from juries, the last of the Reconstruction civil rights laws, left largely unenforced in its own decade
- Thomas Nast: the Harper's Weekly cartoonist whose pen had prosecuted the war against slavery and the Tweed Ring, whose Reconstruction cartoons pictured the era for northern readers, indicting the Klan and the White League in "Worse Than Slavery" and carrying the era's racial caricature in "Colored Rule," evidence of both the image war and its maker's limits

### Lesson 8 (blueprint 15)
- Compromise of 1877: the informal bargain that settled the disputed election of 1876, by which Democratic leaders accepted the Republican Rutherford B. Hayes as president in return for a cabinet seat for a southerner, support for southern internal improvements, and the withdrawal of the last federal troops protecting the Republican governments of South Carolina and Louisiana, ending federal Reconstruction
- Rutherford B. Hayes: the Ohio Republican, Civil War general and three time governor, who lost the popular vote in 1876, won the presidency through the Electoral Commission's rulings and the Compromise of 1877, and in April 1877 ordered the last federal troops from the statehouses at Columbia and New Orleans
- Samuel J. Tilden: the New York Democratic governor and corruption fighter who won the election of 1876's popular vote by some quarter of a million votes and claimed the presidency on the disputed returns, whose party in the end accepted the bargain that seated Hayes rather than risk a contested inauguration
- Electoral Commission: the fifteen member body Congress created in January 1877, five senators, five representatives, and five Supreme Court justices, which awarded every disputed electoral vote of 1876 to Hayes by votes of eight to seven, giving him the presidency by a single electoral vote, 185 to 184
- Redeemers: the name the South's white Democratic leaders gave themselves as they retook the southern state governments across the 1870s, presenting their return as the rescue of the region from misrule and governing afterward for low taxes, cheap government, retrenched schools, and white supremacy
- home rule: the Redeemers' slogan for ending federal supervision of southern governments and elections, the restoration of the South to the rule of its white Democratic majorities, the condition the Compromise of 1877 delivered and the phrase under which Redemption governed
- Redemption: the name white southern Democrats gave their overthrow of the Reconstruction governments and their own return to power, accomplished state by state across the 1870s by violence, fraud, economic pressure, and northern weariness, and completed when the last federal troops withdrew in the spring of 1877
