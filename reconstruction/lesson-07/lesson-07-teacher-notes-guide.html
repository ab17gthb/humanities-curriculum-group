The Retreat: Courts, Panic, and the Leagues

Reconstruction · Lesson 7 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Reconstruction, Lesson 7 of 8 — teach it seventh in the unit, after The Counterrevolution (Lesson 6) and before 1876 and the End (Lesson 8). Lessons 1 to 6 built the settlement and watched federal power break the first Klan where it was used whole; this lesson traces the retreat from that settlement between 1872 and 1876, step by step — amnesty, a narrowed amendment, a six-year depression, daylight terror in Louisiana, and one last civil rights act passed and left unenforced. The lesson question asks by what steps, legal, economic, and violent, the country retreated, and the lesson ends by asking students to rank those causes from the documents.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 6 (the breaking of the first Klan rested on a will this lesson watches fail) and Lesson 8 (the disputed election of 1876 and the end, which this lesson's closing map sets up). Inside the lesson, the activity, The Retreat in Five Records, works five audited bank records — the Slaughter-House syllabus, the White League platform, Charles Hays's speech, the Civil Rights Act of 1875, and the description of Nast's "Worse Than Slavery" — each quoted or carried as the bank presents it. The writing task, Ranking the Retreat, turns the lesson question into a ranked argument defended from those records.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: the Fourteenth Amendment and the Reconstruction governments from Lessons 3 and 4, the Enforcement Acts and the breaking of the first Klan from Lesson 6, and the settlement's dependence on northern will from Lesson 6's close. Every figure in the lesson is taught as the documents state it — the Amnesty Act of May 1872, the Slaughter-House decision of April 1873 decided 5 to 4, the Panic of September 1873 and the six-year depression, Coushatta and Liberty Place in 1874, the Civil Rights Act of March 1875 with its $500 forfeit, and three southern states still Republican in 1876. No image of the White League, of Coushatta, of Liberty Place, or of any act of violence appears anywhere in this lesson, by design; Nast's two cartoons are taught from the bank's descriptions and captions, never reproduced.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (a court syllabus, a platform, a speech, a statute, and a cartoon description read as one record and weighed by kind), D2.His.16 (Miller, Cooke, Hays, and Nast read as historical actors with stated reasons), D2.Civ.10 (amnesty, a court ruling, and an unenforced statute analyzed as the machinery by which a settlement stands or falls), D2.Civ.14 (rights read with the enforcement that made them effective, and with what happened when it failed); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.8, WHST.9-10.1 (the retreat's causes ranked and defended from the records).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to state how the Amnesty Act of 1872 restored the old leadership class to eligibility and how the Liberal Republican revolt made retreat respectable; explain the Slaughter-House ruling's two moves, concession and line, and its 5 to 4 margin; describe the Panic of 1873, the six-year depression, and the elections of 1874; recount the White League's open platform and its actions at Coushatta and Liberty Place; state the scope of the Civil Rights Act of 1875 and why it went largely unenforced; read Nast's two 1874 cartoons differently, as indictment and as caricature; and rank the retreat's causes — law, money, violence, and weariness — from the documents.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1-3) 5 minutes; amnesty and the Liberal revolt (slides 4-6) 6 minutes; the Court reads the amendment narrowly (slides 7-9) 7 minutes; the Panic of 1873 (slides 10-12) 5 minutes; the White League by daylight (slides 13-15) 6 minutes; the last Civil Rights Act (slides 16-18) 6 minutes; Nast's two mirrors (slides 19-21) 5 minutes; 1876 approaches, synthesis, vocabulary, and exit (slides 22-27) 10 minutes. The activity takes a second period if the schedule allows. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes, and The Retreat in Five Records (five stations). The notebook page charts six pieces of the retreat, classified and evidenced; the quick check is "Two Citizenships: the syllabus read against its background"; the writing task is "Ranking the Retreat: the four causes ordered and defended"; the exit ticket has three parts — the ruling stated, the panic and the elections explained together, and the lesson question answered with one step of each kind.

Key vocabulary

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title "The Retreat: Courts, Panic, and the Leagues"; the standfirst — "Amnesty, a narrowed amendment, hard times, daylight terror, and one last act, 1872 to 1876."

  • Picture: New Orleans from the lower cotton press, an 1852 lithograph by J. W. Hill and Smith, drawn on stone by D. W. Moody and held by the Library of Congress. The view dates to 1852, before the events of this lesson; it is the deck's title backdrop for the Louisiana sections and is not a view of the 1870s.
  • [Open with the lesson's shape.] This lesson covers the retreat, the five years in which the Reconstruction settlement was dismantled by inches rather than repealed at a stroke. Nothing in these years abolished the amendments or repealed the settlement in one act.
  • Instead the old leadership returned to eligibility, the Supreme Court read the Fourteenth Amendment narrowly at its first opportunity, a depression turned northern attention homeward, and a paramilitary league organized terror in daylight in Louisiana. Against that sequence stands one last assertion, the Civil Rights Act of 1875, passed and left largely unenforced.
  • Teach the lesson as a sequence in which each step lowered the cost of the next, because that is the argument students will rank for themselves in the writing task.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "By what steps, legal, economic, and violent, did the country retreat from the settlement between 1872 and 1876?"
  • Read the question slowly, because its three adjectives organize the whole lesson. The legal steps are amnesty in 1872 and the Slaughter-House ruling of 1873.
  • The economic step is the Panic of 1873, the six-year depression, and the elections of 1874 that gave the House to the Democrats. The violent step is the White League's open campaign in Louisiana in 1874. Weariness runs underneath all three as the force that made each step stick.
  • Keep the question in view all hour, and tell students now that the lesson ends by asking them to rank these causes — law, money, violence, and weariness — and defend the ranking from the documents. The unit question stands beneath today's question on the slide, and every section that follows answers to one of those three adjectives.

Slide 4 · Section A: Amnesty and the Liberal Republican Revolt (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "The disability removed in May 1872, and retreat made respectable in the same year."

  • [Open Section A with reconciliation as policy.] The Fourteenth Amendment's third section had barred leading former Confederates from office unless Congress removed the disability by a two-thirds vote, a case-by-case remedy.
  • The Amnesty Act of May 1872 replaced case-by-case mercy with a general removal, restoring nearly the whole leadership class at once. Teach the date plainly: seven years after Appomattox, the men who had governed the South before the war and led it through the war could again sit in its legislatures and in Congress.
  • The settlement's opponents were no longer petitioners outside the system. They were eligible inside it, and they organized accordingly.
  • [Name the pattern for students:] the war's losers returned to the ballot box and the bench faster than the war's promises reached the freedpeople who had waited on them.

Slide 5 · The disability removed at a stroke

On the slide: four bullets — the Fourteenth Amendment barred the old leaders from office; removal once took two thirds of Congress, case by case; in May 1872, nearly all were restored at once; seven years after Appomattox, the old class was eligible again.

  • [Work the machinery of amnesty before its politics.] The disability had been the amendment's one lasting personal consequence for the rebellion's leaders, and Congress kept it in 1868 while removing it from almost no one.
  • By 1872 the political cost of keeping it had risen: southern governments needed experienced administrators, northern opinion wanted the war's accounts closed, and Grant himself supported removal. The Act passed with broad support, which is the point to teach.
  • Amnesty was not a southern trick or a Democratic measure. It was a national decision, taken by the Congress that had written the amendment, and it returned to power the very class whose governments the Reconstruction Acts had set aside five years earlier.
  • Ask: students to state the Act in one sentence before moving on — the disability gone, the leadership restored, the terms set by Congress itself.

Slide 6 · Retreat, dressed as reform

On the slide: the Greeley portrait at right; four bullets — reform Republicans broke with Grant in 1872; the Liberal Republicans nominated Greeley, and the Democrats endorsed him; the platform called for amnesty, civil service reform, and an end to protection; Grant won, and the vocabulary stayed.

  • Picture: Horace Greeley, the New York Tribune editor the Liberal Republicans nominated in 1872, photographed between about 1860 and 1865, in the copy held by the National Archives.
  • The Liberal revolt counts less for its vote — Grant won easily — than for its vocabulary. Reformers who broke with Grant over corruption and the costs of Reconstruction described ending federal protection of the southern governments as good government, economy, and reconciliation.
  • The Democrats endorsed Greeley, their enemy of decades, to ride that vocabulary. When respectable opinion in the president's own party treats retreat as reform, the men doing the retreating no longer have to defend themselves as reactionaries.
  • That is the revolt's lasting work. Greeley's defeat is therefore not the measure of the revolt; the platform's language is.

Slide 7 · Section B: The Court Reads the Amendment Narrowly (divider)

On the slide: the Miller portrait at right; the section label and title; the caption — "Samuel Freeman Miller, who wrote the opinion, by Mathew Brady."

  • Picture: Justice Samuel Freeman Miller, who wrote the Slaughter-House opinion, from the Brady-Handy collection at the Library of Congress, photographed between 1870 and 1880.
  • Section B is the lesson's legal center. Louisiana, rebuilding New Orleans's sanitation after epidemic disease, had chartered one company to run the city's slaughterhouses and ordered the trade concentrated there.
  • White butchers shut out of their own slaughterhouses sued, arguing that the monopoly deprived them of their livelihood under the Fourteenth Amendment. A dispute about butchers became the vehicle for the amendment's first great reading, and Miller, a Lincoln appointee and no friend of the old planter class, wrote the narrowing himself.
  • The case reached the Court from Louisiana, the state that will occupy the lesson's second half.

Slide 8 · A concession, and then a line

On the slide: four bullets — the amendments' purpose was conceded in full; two citizenships, national and state, were held distinct; the amendment protected only the narrow rights of national citizenship; the great body of civil rights was left to the states.

  • [Teach the ruling in its two moves, because students who merge them miss the lesson.] The first move is concession: Miller's syllabus states outright that the main purpose of all three amendments was the freedom of the African race and its protection from the oppressions of the men who had held them in slavery.
  • The second move is the line. Citizenship of the United States and citizenship of a state are distinct, the amendment protects only the privileges or immunities of the first, and those are a narrow list arising from the national government's own character. The great body of civil rights stays with the states.
  • The Court feared becoming a perpetual censor of state legislation, and said so.
  • [Have students mark the two moves in their guided notes with two different marks, concession and line,] because the activity's first station grades exactly that distinction.

Slide 9 · 5 to 4

On the slide: the big figure "5 to 4"; the label — "the Slaughter-House decision, April 1873"; the italic note — "The first reading of the Fourteenth Amendment · The privileges or immunities clause read down to a short list."

  • 5 to 4: the Slaughter-House decision, April 1873.
  • Stay on the margin, because five to four is the sound of a door closing slowly. Four justices dissented, and the dissenters would have read the privileges or immunities clause as the amendment's framers' supporters expected, as a federal protection for the fundamental rights of citizens against state abridgment.
  • The majority's reading won, and it has never been reversed: the clause the amendment's advocates counted on most did its work in almost no later case. Teach the consequence for 1873, not for our century.
  • After Slaughter-House, anyone defending the settlement had to travel through Congress, under the equal protection clause, with statutes, marshals, and appropriations. Every later step in this lesson strikes at exactly that machinery. A closed clause and an open one, and the open one's power runs through politics: that sentence summarizes the ruling's whole legacy for this unit.

Slide 10 · Section C: The Panic of 1873 (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Hard times at home outbid the South for the North's attention, for six years."

  • Section C is the economic step, and it begins in the counting houses, not in the South. The country's postwar boom had run on railroad credit, and the great banking house of Jay Cooke, the financier who had sold the Union's war bonds to ordinary households, had staked itself on the Northern Pacific Railroad.
  • When the loans failed in September 1873, the house closed, banks followed, and the panic became the country's governing fact.
  • [Teach the sequence with care:] the panic did not argue against Reconstruction; it outbid it. A settlement that needed money, troops, and sustained northern attention now had to compete with closed mills and empty tables in the North itself.
  • The North's turn homeward begins here, and it is an economic fact before it is a political one.

Slide 11 · The war's financier falls

On the slide: the Cooke portrait at right; four bullets — Jay Cooke's banking house failed in September 1873; railroad loans failed, banks closed, and mills shut; protection had to compete with empty tables; in 1874, the House went Democratic.

  • Picture: Jay Cooke, by Wenderoth and Taylor, from 1865. The deck crop is enlarged from the file and is correspondingly soft, which is recorded in the lesson's source records rather than hidden.
  • Cooke's fall is a useful face for the panic because he was the war's own financier: the man whose bonds had paid for the Union armies now embodied its credit collapsing.
  • [Work the depression's political arithmetic for students.] Unemployed northern workers did not vote on Louisiana. They voted on wages, prices, and the party in power, and they punished the Republicans in 1874 for hard times the party had not caused but could not cure.
  • Every enforcement appropriation, every regiment kept in the South, now had to be defended to voters who were asking why the government could find money for the South and not for them.

Slide 12 · 6 years

On the slide: the big figure "6 years"; the label — "of depression after the Panic of 1873"; the italic note — "Longer than the war · It covered the League, the act of 1875, and the election of 1876."

  • 6 years of depression after the Panic of 1873.
  • Six years is the number to hold. The depression that opened in 1873 ran until 1879, longer than the war itself and longer than congressional Reconstruction had lasted.
  • It covered the whole remainder of this unit: the White League's campaign, the Civil Rights Act's passage, and the election of 1876 all happened inside it.
  • [Teach duration as a political force.] A single bad winter can be waited out; six years of wage cuts retrain a country's habits of attention. By the time recovery came, the southern governments the depression had starved were gone, the troops were withdrawn, and the settlement the North returned its attention to was the one the Redeemers had already built. Duration also explains the elections: a voter in the sixth year of hard times is not voting about 1873 anymore, but about the whole condition of his household.

Slide 13 · Section D: The White League by Daylight (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "No disguises and no denials: a platform in print, and a parish establishment in arms."

  • Section D turns to Louisiana, and the first fact is the change of style. The Klan of Lesson 6 rode disguised at night and denied itself by daylight.
  • The White League of 1874 published a platform, named its object in print — reestablishing a white man's government — and recruited openly among merchants, planters, and former Confederate officers, the respectable men of their parishes.
  • That openness was itself a measurement of the retreat: by 1874 the counterrevolution no longer believed it needed the dark. Its platform even reversed the story, presenting Black political organization as the original secret league and itself as the defensive answer.
  • [Teach students to read that reversal as evidence, not as confusion.] Its membership lists read like a parish directory, and that was the message: this was the community's establishment in arms.

Slide 14 · Coushatta and Liberty Place

On the slide: four bullets — in Louisiana in 1874, the League stated its object in print; a white man's government was openly pledged; at Coushatta, officeholders were murdered in August 1874; at Liberty Place, the League held the statehouse for three days.

  • [State the year's two actions plainly.] At Coushatta, in the Red River country in August 1874, League members seized Republican officeholders and murdered them, white officials and Black leaders taken from their homes and shot after surrendering on terms.
  • In September the League moved on the state government itself: at Liberty Place in New Orleans its forces defeated the metropolitan police in open street fighting and held the statehouse and the city for three days against Governor Kellogg.
  • Federal troops restored Kellogg, which students should register as the system still working.
  • Ask: what did each rescue cost the North's willingness to perform the next one? Three days in possession of a state capital is not a riot; it is an attempted government, and the League intended it to be read that way.

Slide 15 · Answered, and resented

On the slide: four bullets — federal troops restored the governor; each rescue read worse in the North; the protected were recast as the provocation; weariness was manufactured and spent.

  • [Teach weariness as a mechanism, not a mood.] Each federal rescue of a southern government produced the same northern headlines: troops in Louisiana again, another season of bayonets, another bill for a quarrel that amendments were supposed to have settled.
  • The League understood this arithmetic and counted on it, because its strategists did not need to defeat the United States Army. They needed to make protection feel endless.
  • Northern newspapers across party lines began treating the southern governments themselves as the provocation, as if the existence of a Republican governor in New Orleans were the cause of the fighting rather than its target. When the protected become the problem in the public story, protection is already ending.
  • Weariness, taught this way, is a political resource the League deliberately manufactured and spent.

Slide 16 · Section E: The Last Civil Rights Act (divider)

On the slide: the Sumner portrait at right; the section label and title; the caption — "Charles Sumner of Massachusetts, by Mathew Brady. Library of Congress."

  • Picture: Charles Sumner of Massachusetts, by Mathew Brady, from the Brady-Handy collection at the Library of Congress.
  • Section E is the counterstep, and Sumner is its face because the bill was his life's last work. He had pressed a civil rights bill through session after session, losing, narrowing it, and pressing again, arguing that equality in public life was the completion of the amendments.
  • He died in March 1874 with the bill unpassed, and colleagues took it up as his memorial, which gave the measure a dignity its opponents had to argue against carefully. The act that finally passed in March 1875 carried his preamble's language about the equality of all men before the law.
  • Sumner is named in this lesson's prose and in the act's history, and his biography belongs to the earlier lessons; here he stands as the bill's author and its posthumous argument.

Slide 17 · The broadest statute, the thinnest enforcement

On the slide: four bullets — the act of March 1875 covered inns, conveyances, and theaters; juries were opened, and denial was made a misdemeanor; the person aggrieved could claim $500; prosecutors were few, marshals were exposed, and the act went largely unenforced.

  • [Read the act's scope slowly, because on paper it is the broadest equality statute of the century.] Section 1 guarantees all persons the full and equal enjoyment of inns, public conveyances on land or water, theaters, and other places of public amusement, regardless of previous condition of servitude, and the act bars racial exclusion from juries.
  • Section 2 gives the guarantee teeth: a five hundred dollar forfeit to the person denied, and a misdemeanor punishable by fine or imprisonment.
  • Then teach the gap the reading insists on. A statute enforces itself nowhere. It needs prosecutors to bring cases, marshals to serve them, and juries to convict, and across the South in 1875 those officers were few, exposed, and increasingly unfunded.

Slide 18 · Hays on the floor

On the slide: four bullets — Hays was an Alabama congressman and a former enslaver; he counted his cost before he spoke; he offered freedpeople's conduct as the evidence; he answered "social equality" as a manufactured fancy.

  • Charles Hays is the lesson's most instructive voice, because he cannot be filed as a northern ideologue. He was an Alabama congressman, a former cotton planter who had owned a large number of enslaved people, speaking for the bill in the chamber in 1874.
  • He opens by counting his cost: the hate of the ignorant and the scorn of the untutored will be invoked against him. Then he gives his evidence: freedpeople, cut adrift in ignorance and poverty they did not bring on themselves, have behaved so well that they deserve praise.
  • The terror of social equality raised against the bill is, in his words, a fancy manufactured by newspapers, politicians, and demagogues.
  • [Let students sit with who is speaking before they judge what is said.]

Slide 19 · Section F: Nast's Two Mirrors (divider)

On the slide: the Nast portrait at right; the section label and title; the caption — "Thomas Nast, by Napoleon Sarony, about 1870. National Portrait Gallery."

  • Picture: Thomas Nast, by Napoleon Sarony, about 1870, from the National Portrait Gallery.
  • Section F turns from law to pictures, because the retreat was argued in images as much as in statutes. Nast was the era's most famous political cartoonist: his pen had prosecuted the war against slavery for Harper's Weekly and destroyed the Tweed Ring in New York.
  • Northern families who never read a congressional debate saw Reconstruction through his drawings. That authority is why his two cartoons of 1874 belong in this lesson side by side.
  • The same hand, in the same year, drew the counterrevolution's indictment and the era's caricature of Black officeholding, and students have to hold both facts at once. What a cartoonist chooses to draw, and what he chooses to mock, are both evidence, and this section grades students on reading the two drawings differently.

Slide 20 · Worse Than Slavery, described

On the slide: four bullets — in October 1874, a White Leaguer and a Klansman shook hands over a shield; a Black family stood between a skull and an alphabet book; a schoolhouse burned in the background; Nast named the partnership between daylight politics and night terror.

  • [Teach this cartoon from its description, as the bank presents it, and say on the record why:] the print pictures a man hanging from a tree and a schoolhouse burning, and this unit teaches the counterrevolution's violence in testimony and text, never as a picture on the classroom page.
  • The description is enough. A White Leaguer and a Klansman shake hands over a shield bearing a Black family, a skull and crossbones at the shield's top and an open alphabet book at its base.
  • The title turns the slaveholders' boast around: the Union as it was, the lost cause, worse than slavery.
  • Nast saw the League clearly and named the partnership between daylight politics and night terror in a single frame.

Slide 21 · Colored Rule, described

On the slide: four bullets — in March 1874, Black legislators were drawn in uproar; Columbia scolded them and told them to take back seats; the drawing carried the era's caricature in its faces and mockery; the cartoon taught northern readers to picture Black government as disorder.

  • [Teach this cartoon with the opposite handling, and the same honesty about presentation.] "Colored Rule," published in March 1874, drew Black members of the South Carolina legislature shouting insults at one another while Columbia, the nation's figure, scolded them for disgracing their race and told them to take back seats.
  • The drawing carried the era's racial caricature in its faces and its mockery, and it taught northern readers to picture Black government as disorder.
  • This lesson teaches the cartoon from the bank record's description and its transcribed captions, and does not reproduce the drawing: it enters the lesson as evidence of the image war and of its maker's limits, a man who could indict the League and still see Black legislators through the caricature of his time.

Slide 22 · The map at the opening of 1876

On the slide: four bullets — the old leaders were restored, and the amendment was narrowed; northern attention was spent, and the House was lost; violence was answered late and wearily; one asset was left: the amendments themselves.

  • Section G draws the map at the opening of 1876, and students should be able to read it as four entries. Amnesty had restored the old leadership to eligibility in every southern state.
  • The Court had narrowed the amendments' first reading, and no appeal remained from that reading. The depression had spent the North's attention and the elections of 1874 had taken the House, so the machinery of enforcement had neither money nor a majority.
  • The leagues had demonstrated that organized violence, answered late and wearily, could still move a state government. Against those four entries stands a single asset: the amendments themselves, standing in the Constitution, and one unenforced statute.
  • The question of 1876 is which column the country would pay to defend.

Slide 23 · 3 states

On the slide: the big figure "3"; the label — "southern states still Republican in 1876"; the italic note — "South Carolina, Florida, and Louisiana · Each resting on federal troops near its statehouse."

  • 3 southern states still Republican in 1876: South Carolina, Florida, and Louisiana.
  • Three is the count to carry into the next lesson. By the centennial year, Republican governments survived in only three southern states, South Carolina, Florida, and Louisiana, and each of them stood because federal soldiers still stood near its statehouse.
  • Every other southern state had been redeemed, in the language of the men who took them, by the sequence this lesson has traced: restored leaders, narrowed law, spent northern attention, and violence applied where the other three had weakened the defense.
  • [Teach the number as a cliff edge rather than a conclusion.] A presidential election was coming in which those three states' electoral votes would be disputed, and the settlement's whole remaining weight rested on them. South Carolina, Florida, and Louisiana: students should be able to name the three without prompting by the lesson's end.

Slide 24 · The steps, ranked in the open

On the slide: four bullets — law came first, and the court road closed in 1873; money came second, and the one road left went unfunded; violence came third, through the opening, in daylight; the act of 1875 was passed, and not enforced.

  • [Close the lesson by running the ranking argument once, briefly, before students write it themselves.] The legal step came first and made the rest cheaper: after Slaughter-House, enforcement had only one road left, through Congress.
  • The economic step then closed that road's funding and attention. The violent step walked through the opening, in daylight, in Louisiana. Weariness is the connective tissue, the reason each answer came later and weaker than the last.
  • And the Civil Rights Act of 1875 is the proof text: a Congress could still pass the settlement's broadest statute in 1875, and the country would not enforce it. What had been settled in law by 1870 was being unsettled by 1876 without a single amendment repealed.
  • The ranking itself is the students' work in the writing task; this closing run simply shows them the argument's shape.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: the pardon, the revolt, the panic, the league

On the slide: four terms with their short forms — Amnesty Act of 1872 (the law of May 1872), Liberal Republicans (the reform Republicans who broke with Grant in 1872), Panic of 1873 (the financial collapse that opened in September 1873), White League (the paramilitary organization formed in Louisiana in 1874). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Four terms carry the retreat's first steps — the pardon, the revolt, the panic, and the league — and their full definitions are carried here word for word as the guided notes give them.
  • Say: "Amnesty Act of 1872: the law of May 1872 by which Congress removed the Fourteenth Amendment's officeholding disability from nearly all former Confederates, restoring the South's old leadership class to eligibility for office some seven years after the war's end."
  • Say: "Liberal Republicans: the reform Republicans who broke with Grant in 1872 over corruption and the costs of Reconstruction, nominating Horace Greeley on a platform of amnesty, civil service reform, and an end to federal protection of the southern governments, and carrying respectability into the retreat."
  • Say: "Panic of 1873: the financial collapse that opened with the failure of Jay Cooke's banking house in September 1873 and settled into a six year depression of closed mills, wage cuts, and unemployment, which turned northern attention from the South's unfinished settlement to hard times at home."
  • Say: "White League: the paramilitary organization formed in Louisiana in 1874, pledged openly to restore white supremacy and drive Republicans from office, whose members murdered Republican officeholders at Coushatta and fought federal troops at Liberty Place in New Orleans that year, terror organized as politics by daylight."

Slide 26 · Vocabulary: the case, the act, the cartoonist

On the slide: three terms with their short forms — Slaughter-House Cases (the Supreme Court's 5 to 4 decision of April 1873), Civil Rights Act of 1875 (Charles Sumner's last cause, enacted in March 1875), Thomas Nast (the Harper's Weekly cartoonist). These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Three terms carry the case, the act, and the cartoonist, in the guided notes' words.
  • Say: "Slaughter-House Cases: the Supreme Court's 5 to 4 decision of April 1873, its first reading of the Fourteenth Amendment, which held that the amendment protected only the narrow rights of national citizenship and left the great body of civil rights under state authority, the ruling that began the courts' narrowing of the Reconstruction amendments."
  • Say: "Civil Rights Act of 1875: Charles Sumner's last cause, enacted in March 1875 after his death, which guaranteed all citizens equal enjoyment of inns, public conveyances, and theaters and barred racial exclusion from juries, the last of the Reconstruction civil rights laws, left largely unenforced in its own decade."
  • Say: "Thomas Nast: the Harper's Weekly cartoonist whose pen had prosecuted the war against slavery and the Tweed Ring, whose Reconstruction cartoons pictured the era for northern readers, indicting the Klan and the White League in "Worse Than Slavery" and carrying the era's racial caricature in "Colored Rule," evidence of both the image war and its maker's limits."
