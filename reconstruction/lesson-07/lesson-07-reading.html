The Retreat: Courts, Panic, and the Leagues

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Amnesty and the Liberal Republican Revolt

The retreat began with an act of reconciliation. The Fourteenth Amendment had barred leading former Confederates from office unless Congress removed the disability by a two thirds vote. In May 1872 Congress passed the Amnesty Act, removing the disability from nearly all of them at once, and the South's old leadership class could hold office again. The same year, reform Republicans broke with President Grant over corruption and the costs of southern protection. The Liberal Republicans nominated the editor Horace Greeley on a platform of amnesty, civil service reform, and an end to federal protection of the southern governments, and the Democrats endorsed him. Grant won easily, but respectable opinion in his own party now treated retreat as reform.

B. The Court Reads the Amendment Narrowly

The courts moved next, at the first opportunity. Louisiana had given one company a monopoly on slaughterhouses in New Orleans, and white butchers sued, claiming it violated their Fourteenth Amendment rights. In the Slaughter-House Cases, decided in April 1873 by a vote of 5 to 4, Justice Samuel F. Miller's opinion agreed that the Reconstruction amendments had been written for the freedom of the African race. Then it drew a line. Citizenship of the United States and citizenship of a state were distinct, and the privileges or immunities the amendment protected were only the narrow rights of national citizenship. The great body of civil rights, the rights people used daily, remained under state authority. The Court feared making itself a perpetual censor of state legislation. The consequence was plainer: the clause counted on to protect civil rights in federal court had been read down to a short list, and enforcement now rested almost entirely on Congress and the will behind it.

C. The Panic of 1873

In September 1873 the banking house of Jay Cooke failed under the weight of railroad loans, and the Panic of 1873 followed. Banks closed, mills shut, and wages fell, and the depression ran six years. Northern voters facing unemployment at home turned from the South's unfinished settlement to their own empty tables, and protection had always depended on northern willingness to pay its costs in money, troops, and attention. In the elections of 1874, Democrats won the House of Representatives for the first time since before the war, and a House held by the white South's party would fund no new enforcement.

D. The White League by Daylight

The counterrevolution changed its uniform to match the new weather. The Klan had ridden at night in disguise. The White League, formed in Louisiana in 1874, organized in daylight and stated its purpose in print: to restore white supremacy and reestablish a white man's government in the city and the state. In August 1874 League members murdered Republican officeholders at Coushatta. In September, League forces fought at Liberty Place in New Orleans and held the statehouse against the Republican governor until federal troops restored him. The troops answered the rising. Northern weariness at the answering, the sense that Louisiana required a rescue every season, was the League's second victory.

E. The Last Civil Rights Act

Against the retreat, Congress made one last assertion. Charles Sumner had pressed a civil rights bill for years, died in March 1874 with it unpassed, and Congress took it up as his memorial. Charles Hays, a Republican congressman from Alabama who had himself held enslaved people before the war, defended the bill on the House floor, arguing that freedpeople, cut adrift in poverty they did not bring on themselves, had set an example entitling them to praise, and that the warnings of social equality raised against the bill were fancies, not facts. In March 1875 the Civil Rights Act became law, guaranteeing equal enjoyment of inns, public conveyances, and theaters and barring racial exclusion from juries. It was the broadest equality statute the country had written, and it was left largely unenforced in its own decade, its promise waiting on a will that was already going.

F. Nast's Two Mirrors

The country's argument with itself ran in pictures too. Thomas Nast of Harper's Weekly drew the retreat twice in 1874. In Worse Than Slavery, that October, a White Leaguer and a Klansman shake hands over a shield bearing a Black family, a schoolhouse burning behind them: one work, two organizations, named as partners. In Colored Rule, that March, Nast drew Black members of the South Carolina legislature shouting insults at one another while Columbia scolded them for disgracing their race, and the drawing carried the era's racial caricature in its faces and mockery. The same pen indicted the counterrevolution and pictured Black officeholders as a disorderly show: evidence of the image war the settlement was losing, and of its maker's limits. Nast saw the League clearly and his Black subjects only partly. Colored Rule is taught here from its published description and transcribed captions; the drawing is not reproduced.

G. 1876 Approaches

By the opening of 1876, the retreat could be mapped state by state. Amnesty had restored the old leaders. The Court had narrowed the amendments. The depression had taken the North's attention, and the elections of 1874 had taken the House. The leagues had shown that organized violence, answered late and wearily, could still move a state government. Against the list stood one assertion, a civil rights act on the books, largely unenforced. Republican governments survived in only three southern states, South Carolina, Florida, and Louisiana, each resting on a federal presence the country was tiring of supplying, and a presidential election was coming.

Full credit answers run the steps as one sequence, each step lowering the cost of the next. Law first: amnesty restored the old leaders in 1872, and Slaughter-House in 1873 read the Fourteenth Amendment down to a short list of national rights, leaving civil rights to the states.
