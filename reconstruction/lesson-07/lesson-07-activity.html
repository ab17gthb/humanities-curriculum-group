The Retreat in Five Records

Directions: Work through the five stations in order. Each station holds one record of the retreat between 1873 and 1875: a court syllabus, a paramilitary platform, a congressman's speech, a statute, and a cartoon taught from its published description. For each record, state which pillar of the settlement it raised or struck. At the end, rank the retreat's causes and defend your ranking from the documents.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: The Slaughter-House Cases, Syllabus, 1873

Source: U.S. Supreme Court (Justice Samuel F. Miller), 'Slaughter-House Cases,' 1873 (Justia, 83 U.S. 36), as carried in the audited TPSP bank record. Quoted verbatim from the record, whose own cuts are marked [...] in the record and printed as the record carries them.

An examination of the history of the causes which led to the adoption of those amendments and of the amendments themselves demonstrates that the main purpose of all the three last amendments was the freedom of the African race, the security and perpetuation of that freedom, and their protection from the oppressions of the white men who had formerly held them in slavery.

[...] The first clause of the fourteenth article was primarily intended to confer citizenship on the negro race, and secondly to give definitions of citizenship of the United States and citizenship of the States, and it recognizes the distinction between citizenship of a State and citizenship of the United States by those definitions.

The second clause protects from the hostile legislation of the States the privileges and immunities of citizens of the United States, as distinguished from the privileges and immunities of citizens of the States. These latter, as defined by Justice Washington in Corfield v. Coryell, and by this court in Ward v. Maryland, embrace generally those fundamental civil rights for the security and establishment of which organized society is instituted, and they remain, with certain exceptions mentioned in the Federal Constitution, under the care of the State governments, and of this class are those set up by plaintiffs.

[...] The privileges and immunities of citizens of the United States are those which arise out of the nature and essential character of the national government, the provisions of its Constitution, or its laws and treaties made in pursuance thereof, and it is these which are placed under the protection of Congress by this clause of the Thirteenth amendment.

The clause which forbids a State to deny to any person the equal protection of the laws was clearly intended to prevent the hostile discrimination against the negro race so familiar in the States where he had been a slave, and, for this purpose, the clause confers ample power in Congress to secure his rights and his equality before the law.

State which pillar this record struck. Miller's syllabus concedes the amendments' main purpose in its first paragraph. Quote that concession, and state whose freedom the opinion says the three amendments were adopted to secure.

State the line the syllabus draws. Name the two citizenships it distinguishes, state which one's privileges and immunities the Fourteenth Amendment protects, and state where the fundamental civil rights of the butchers' class remain, naming the authority the syllabus leaves them under.

Station 2: The Louisiana White League Platform, 1874

Source: Louisiana White League, 'Louisiana White League Platform,' 1874 (Facing History & Ourselves), as carried in the audited TPSP bank record. Quoted verbatim from the record, with cuts marked with ellipses in the record and disclosed here.

Disregarding all minor questions of principle or policy, and having solely in view the maintenance of our hereditary civilization and Christianity menaced by a stupid Africanization, we appeal to men of our race, of whatever language or nationality, to unite with us against that supreme danger. A league of whites is the inevitable result of that formidable, oath-bound, and blindly obedient league of the blacks, which, under the command of the most cunning and unscrupulous negroes in the State, may at any moment plunge us into a war of races . . . It is with some hope that a timely and proclaimed union of the whites as a race, and their efficient preparation for any emergency, may arrest the threatened horrors of social war, and teach the blacks to beware of further insolence and aggression, that we call upon the men of our race to leave in abeyance all lesser considerations; to forget all differences of opinions and all race prejudices of the past, and with no object in view but the common good of both races, to unite with us in an earnest effort to re-establish a white man's government in the city and the State.

State which pillar this record struck, and how openly. Quote the platform's stated object at its close, and state what the League asks the men of its race to leave in abeyance and to forget in order to reach it.

State the platform's account of its enemy. Quote the words in which it describes the league it claims to answer, and state what that description is doing in the document: whose organization is presented as the original conspiracy, and what does that presentation license the League's readers to do?

Station 3: Charles Hays Defends the Civil Rights Bill, 1874

Source: Rep. Charles Hays, 'Speech Reaffirming the Rights of African Americans,' 1874 (Congressional Record; reprinted in Gienapp, ed., The Civil War and Reconstruction, 2001), as carried in the audited TPSP bank record. Quoted verbatim from the record, with the record's closing cut marked with ellipses.

I am aware, sir, that the hate of the ignorant and the scorn of the untutored will be invoked against me. All these things are sources of profound regret; but they shall not deter me from the faithful discharge and sacred observance of a duty which God, reason, and conscience tell me is right. Coming from the far South, being once the owner of a large number of slaves, thoroughly conversant with their history in the past and present, I feel that I have some idea of what should be accorded them now. Set free by the strong arm of Federal power, cut adrift upon the cold charities of an inhuman world in ignorance and penury that they did not bring upon themselves, struggling against adverse winds and storms of hate, in my opinion, sir, the colored race have set an example that fairly entitles them to the plaudit of "Well done, ye good and faithful servants." The discussion of this question of "civil rights" has brought about a state of feeling in the South which is to be deplored. Fancies have taken the place of facts. Imagination has been called upon to paint pictures which reality could not do. Newspapers, politicians, demagogues, and inciters of sectional hate preached to the white masses of the south that Congress was upon the verge of enacting a law enforcing "social equality" and blotting out the lines between knowledge and ignorance. You and I know better . . .

State which pillar this record raised. Hays tells the House what he expects his speech to cost him. Quote his opening account of that cost, and state the two facts about himself he puts before the chamber: where he comes from, and what he once was.

State Hays's evidence and his answer to the opposition. Quote his verdict on what the colored race has done since being set free, and state what he says has taken the place of facts in the South's discussion, naming the four kinds of speakers he blames.

Station 4: The Civil Rights Act of 1875, Sections 1 and 2

Source: U.S. Congress, 'Civil Rights Act of 1875,' 1875 (enrolled act, 18 Stat. 335-337), as carried in the audited TPSP bank record. Quoted verbatim from the record, with cuts marked with ellipses in the record and disclosed here.

. . . Whereas it is essential to just government we recognize the equality of all men before the law, and hold that it is the duty of government in its dealings with the people to mete out equal and exact justice to all, of whatever nativity, race, color, or persuasion, religious or political; and it being the appropriate object of legislation to enact great fundamental principles into law: Therefore, Sec. 1. Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled, That all persons within the jurisdiction of the United States shall be entitled to the full and equal enjoyment of the accommodations, advantages, facilities, and privileges of inns, public conveyances on land or water, theaters, and other places of public amusement; subject only to the conditions and limitations established by law, and applicable alike to citizens of every race and color, regardless of any previous condition of servitude. Sec. 2. That any person who shall violate the foregoing section by denying to any citizen, except for reasons by law applicable to citizens of every race and color, and regardless of any previous condition of servitude, the full enjoyment of any of the accommodations, advantages, facilities, or privileges in said section enumerated, or by aiding or inciting such denial, shall, for every such offense, forfeit and pay the sum of five hundred dollars to the person aggrieved thereby, to be recovered in an action of debt, with full costs; and shall also, for every such offense, be deemed guilty of a misdemeanor, and, upon conviction thereof, shall be fined not less than five hundred nor more than one thousand dollars, or shall be imprisoned not less than thirty days nor more than one year . . .

State which pillar this record raised, and on what ground. Quote the preamble's statement of what just government recognizes, and state the four kinds of places Section 1 names whose full and equal enjoyment the act guarantees.

State the act's teeth. Section 2 sets two consequences for a person who denies the enjoyment Section 1 guarantees. Give both figures exactly as printed: the sum forfeited to the person aggrieved, and the range of the fine on conviction as a misdemeanor, with the alternative imprisonment.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: The Slaughter-House Cases, Syllabus

Which pillar struck, and the concession? The amendments' meaning in court. The concession, in the syllabus's first paragraph: an examination of the history demonstrates that the main purpose of all the three last amendments was the freedom of the African race, the security and perpetuation of that freedom, and their protection from the oppressions of the white men who had formerly held them in slavery. The opinion grants the amendments' purpose in full before it narrows their reach, and students should hold those two moves together.

The line drawn. The syllabus distinguishes citizenship of the United States from citizenship of the States, recognized in the first clause's two definitions. The second clause protects from hostile state legislation only the privileges and immunities of citizens of the United States, which arise out of the nature and essential character of the national government. The fundamental civil rights, those defined in Corfield v. Coryell and Ward v. Maryland, remain, with certain exceptions, under the care of the State governments, and the butchers' claimed rights are of that class. Note for the class: the syllabus's last paragraph assigns the equal protection clause ample power in Congress against hostile discrimination, which is the opening later enforcement would have to use, since the privileges or immunities road was closed.

Station 2: The Louisiana White League Platform

Which pillar struck, and how openly? The safety of Republican government, struck in print and by daylight. The stated object at the close: to re-establish a white man's government in the city and the State. The League asks the men of its race to leave in abeyance all lesser considerations and to forget all differences of opinions and all race prejudices of the past, that is, to suspend ordinary politics and old ethnic divisions inside the white electorate for the single work of racial government.

Its account of its enemy. The platform describes its opposite as that formidable, oath-bound, and blindly obedient league of the blacks, under the command of the most cunning and unscrupulous negroes in the State, which may at any moment plunge us into a war of races. The presentation reverses the record: Black political organization, the Union Leagues and the Republican electorate, is drawn as the original secret conspiracy, and the White League as a defensive answer to it. That reversal licenses the League's readers to treat their own open paramilitary organization as self defense and its violence at Coushatta and Liberty Place as prevention of a war the other side was supposedly about to start.

Station 3: Charles Hays Defends the Civil Rights Bill

Which pillar raised, and his cost? The law's promise, raised from the floor of the House. His opening: I am aware, sir, that the hate of the ignorant and the scorn of the untutored will be invoked against me, and none of it shall deter him from the faithful discharge of a duty God, reason, and conscience tell him is right. The two facts about himself: he comes from the far South, and he was once the owner of a large number of slaves, thoroughly conversant with their history in the past and present, which he offers as his credential for knowing what should be accorded them now.

His evidence and his answer. His verdict: set free by the strong arm of Federal power and cut adrift in ignorance and penury they did not bring upon themselves, the colored race have set an example that fairly entitles them to the plaudit of Well done, ye good and faithful servants. His answer to the opposition: fancies have taken the place of facts, and imagination has painted pictures reality could not do. The four kinds of speakers he blames for preaching that Congress was about to enforce social equality: newspapers, politicians, demagogues, and inciters of sectional hate.

Station 4: The Civil Rights Act of 1875, Sections 1 and 2

Which pillar raised, and on what ground? The law's promise, raised as statute. The preamble's ground: it is essential to just government we recognize the equality of all men before the law, and it is the duty of government to mete out equal and exact justice to all, of whatever nativity, race, color, or persuasion. The four kinds of places Section 1 names: inns, public conveyances on land or water, theaters, and other places of public amusement, with full and equal enjoyment guaranteed to all persons within the jurisdiction of the United States, regardless of any previous condition of servitude.

The act's teeth, exactly as printed. The person who denies the enjoyment forfeits and pays the sum of five hundred dollars to the person aggrieved, recovered in an action of debt with full costs. The same offense is a misdemeanor, punished on conviction by a fine of not less than five hundred nor more than one thousand dollars, or imprisonment of not less than thirty days nor more than one year. Set the figures beside the lesson's outcome: penalties this specific still required prosecutors, marshals, and juries willing to use them, and in its own decade the act was left largely unenforced.

Station 5: "Worse Than Slavery," by Thomas Nast (Image Described)

Which pillar raised, and the cartoon rebuilt. The North's will, addressed in its own magazine. A member of the White League shakes hands with a Ku Klux Klan member over a shield showing a Black family; in the background a man hangs from a tree and a schoolhouse burns. The title's claim, in the cartoon's words: The Union as it was, the lost cause, worse than slavery. Nast, the bank's headnote records, turned the slaveholders' old boast on its head: what the counterrevolution was building was worse than the institution it mourned.

The emblems, and the pairing with Station 2. The skull and crossbones at the shield's top says the handshake is a compact of death, aimed at the family pictured between the emblems. The open book with the alphabet at the shield's base names what the compact threatens at its foundation: the freedpeople's schools, the alphabet being the first thing freedom taught, with the burning schoolhouse in the background making the same point as event rather than emblem. Beside Station 2: the platform can state the League's program in its own words, its object, its appeal, its reversed account of Black organization, which no cartoon can quote; the cartoon can show the League and the Klan as one partnership and put the family's peril at the center of the frame, which the platform, written by the League itself, will never concede. One record is the organization's self portrait, the other its indictment, and the lesson needs both.

Part 2: Grading Guide

Rankings earn full credit in more than one order when each rank is tied to station evidence and the closing defense answers the strongest competitor. The violence first case runs on Stations 2 and 5: governments fell to organized force at Coushatta and Liberty Place, and no ruling or depression removed a governor from his statehouse. The law first case runs on Station 1: after Slaughter-House, every later defense of the settlement had to travel the equal protection road with Congress as its only engine, so when money and will failed there was no court road left. The money first case runs on the reading's chain through Stations 3 and 4: the act and the floor defense both existed in 1874 and 1875, and what they lacked was a North willing to fund their enforcement after the panic. The weariness first case earns credit only when it is argued as a mechanism, weariness as the force that converted each League victory into northern policy, rather than as a mood. A defense that merely restates the student's own ranking without stating what the competing cause could not have done alone earns partial credit.
