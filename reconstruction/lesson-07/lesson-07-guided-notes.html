Guided Notes: The Retreat: Courts, Panic, and the Leagues

Lesson question: By what steps, legal, economic, and violent, did the country retreat from the settlement between 1872 and 1876?

Unit question: What did Reconstruction settle about freedom, citizenship, and the federal Union, and what did it leave open?

Key Terms

Slaughter-House Cases: the Supreme Court's 5 to 4 decision of April 1873, its first reading of the Fourteenth Amendment, which held that the amendment protected only the narrow rights of national citizenship and left the great body of civil rights under state authority, the ruling that began the courts' narrowing of the Reconstruction amendments

Amnesty Act of 1872: the law of May 1872 by which Congress removed the Fourteenth Amendment's officeholding disability from nearly all former Confederates, restoring the South's old leadership class to eligibility for office some seven years after the war's end

Liberal Republicans: the reform Republicans who broke with Grant in 1872 over corruption and the costs of Reconstruction, nominating Horace Greeley on a platform of amnesty, civil service reform, and an end to federal protection of the southern governments, and carrying respectability into the retreat

Panic of 1873: the financial collapse that opened with the failure of Jay Cooke's banking house in September 1873 and settled into a six year depression of closed mills, wage cuts, and unemployment, which turned northern attention from the South's unfinished settlement to hard times at home

White League: the paramilitary organization formed in Louisiana in 1874, pledged openly to restore white supremacy and drive Republicans from office, whose members murdered Republican officeholders at Coushatta and fought federal troops at Liberty Place in New Orleans that year, terror organized as politics by daylight

Civil Rights Act of 1875: Charles Sumner's last cause, enacted in March 1875 after his death, which guaranteed all citizens equal enjoyment of inns, public conveyances, and theaters and barred racial exclusion from juries, the last of the Reconstruction civil rights laws, left largely unenforced in its own decade

Thomas Nast: the Harper's Weekly cartoonist whose pen had prosecuted the war against slavery and the Tweed Ring, whose Reconstruction cartoons pictured the era for northern readers, indicting the Klan and the White League in "Worse Than Slavery" and carrying the era's racial caricature in "Colored Rule," evidence of both the image war and its maker's limits

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher

Teach this lesson seventh in the Reconstruction unit, after the Klan and the Enforcement Acts of Lesson 6 and before the closing lesson on 1876 and the Compromise of 1877. It follows the settlement's dismantling between 1872 and 1876: the Amnesty Act and the Liberal Republican revolt, the Slaughter-House Cases, the Panic of 1873, the White League, the Civil Rights Act of 1875, and Nast's two 1874 cartoons. It pairs with the lesson reading and with the activity, The Retreat in Five Records, whose five stations are all audited bank records quoted verbatim, and with the writing task, which asks students to rank the retreat's causes from those records. Prior knowledge: students need the Fourteenth Amendment and its privileges or immunities clause from Lesson 3, Black officeholding from Lesson 4, and the Klan and the Enforcement Acts from Lesson 6, whose federal answer is the high-water mark this lesson measures the retreat against. Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies (cite textual evidence, determine central ideas, and analyze a sequence of events) and the C3 Framework, Dimension 2, History (change and continuity, and the uses of evidence), stated state neutrally for teachers to map to their own frameworks. Students will learn how the courts narrowed the amendments at their first reading, how amnesty and the Liberal revolt made retreat respectable, how the depression and the 1874 elections withdrew the North's money and attention, how the White League organized terror in daylight, and why the Civil Rights Act of 1875 stood largely unenforced. Timing: one period for the reading and these notes, a second period for the activity and writing task. Materials: the reading, these notes, the activity file, and the notebook page.

Summary table. The law narrows: the Amnesty Act of 1872 restored the old leadership to office eligibility, and the Slaughter-House Cases of April 1873, decided 5 to 4, held that the Fourteenth Amendment protected only the narrow rights of national citizenship and left the great body of civil rights under state authority, so enforcement rested on Congress and political will. The money turns: Jay Cooke's failure opened the Panic of 1873, six years of depression turned northern attention home, and the elections of 1874 gave the House to the Democrats for the first time since before the war, ending new enforcement funding. The violence opens: the White League, formed in Louisiana in 1874, pledged itself in print to white supremacy, murdered Republican officeholders at Coushatta, and fought federal troops at Liberty Place, and northern weariness at each rescue compounded its victories. The last assertion: the Civil Rights Act of 1875, Sumner's cause, defended on the floor by Charles Hays, guaranteed equal enjoyment of inns, public conveyances, and theaters and barred racial exclusion from juries, and stood largely unenforced in its own decade.
