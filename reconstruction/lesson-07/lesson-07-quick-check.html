Quick Check: Two Citizenships

Directions: Read the passage and the background, then answer the questions.

[...] The first clause of the fourteenth article was primarily intended to confer citizenship on the negro race, and secondly to give definitions of citizenship of the United States and citizenship of the States, and it recognizes the distinction between citizenship of a State and citizenship of the United States by those definitions. The second clause protects from the hostile legislation of the States the privileges and immunities of citizens of the United States, as distinguished from the privileges and immunities of citizens of the States. These latter, as defined by Justice Washington in Corfield v. Coryell, and by this court in Ward v. Maryland, embrace generally those fundamental civil rights for the security and establishment of which organized society is instituted, and they remain, with certain exceptions mentioned in the Federal Constitution, under the care of the State governments, and of this class are those set up by plaintiffs. The clause which forbids a State to deny to any person the equal protection of the laws was clearly intended to prevent the hostile discrimination against the negro race so familiar in the States where he had been a slave, and, for this purpose, the clause confers ample power in Congress to secure his rights and his equality before the law.

Source: U.S. Supreme Court (Justice Samuel F. Miller), 'Slaughter-House Cases,' 1873 (Justia, 83 U.S. 36), as carried in the audited TPSP bank record. Quoted verbatim from the record. Three paragraphs of the record's excerpt, joined; none is cut in the record.

Background. The syllabus was the Supreme Court's first reading of the Fourteenth Amendment, delivered in April 1873, a year after the Amnesty Act restored the old southern leadership to office eligibility and months before the Panic of 1873 opened six years of depression. In 1874 Democrats won the House, the White League organized in Louisiana, and the bill Hays defended became the Civil Rights Act of 1875, largely unenforced.

1. In the passage, what distinction does the first clause of the Fourteenth Amendment recognize, in the syllabus's own words?

2. State where the passage leaves the fundamental civil rights, naming the authority it assigns them to and the two cases it cites for their definition.

3. Using the background, give two facts: one about politics in 1872 or 1874, one about the Civil Rights Act of 1875. State what each shows about the settlement's position after the ruling.

4. A defender argues that the ruling changed little, because the passage's last paragraph gives Congress ample power under the equal protection clause. State the strongest answer, naming the road the ruling closed and the engine the open road depended on.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What distinction does the first clause recognize? The distinction between citizenship of a State and citizenship of the United States, recognized by the clause's definitions of each; the clause was primarily intended to confer citizenship on the negro race. Credit requires the two citizenships named in the passage's words. (Blueprint 13)

2. Where do the fundamental civil rights remain? Under the care of the State governments, with certain exceptions mentioned in the Federal Constitution. The syllabus defines them by Justice Washington in Corfield v. Coryell and by the Court in Ward v. Maryland, as those fundamental civil rights for the security and establishment of which organized society is instituted, and it holds that the plaintiffs' claimed rights are of that class. (Blueprint 13)

3. Give two facts, one political and one about the act. Political fact, either of: the Amnesty Act of 1872 restored nearly all former Confederates to office eligibility a year before the ruling, or the elections of 1874 gave the House to the Democrats after the Panic of 1873; each shows the political ground moving with the legal ground, the old leadership restored and enforcement's funding withdrawn. (Blueprint 14) Act fact: the Civil Rights Act of 1875, defended by Hays and passed as Sumner's memorial, guaranteed equal enjoyment of public accommodations and stood largely unenforced; it shows Congress still asserting the settlement on paper while the will to enforce it failed. (Blueprint 14)

4. State the strongest answer, naming the closed road and the engine. The ruling closed the privileges or immunities road: after it, the amendment's broadest clause protected only the narrow rights of national citizenship, so a citizen claiming a fundamental civil right against a state had no federal court claim under that clause. The open road, congressional legislation under the equal protection clause, depended on a political engine, a Congress willing to pass enforcement laws and a government willing to fund and press them, and the background shows that engine failing within two years, in the panic, the 1874 House, and the unenforced act of 1875. A defense that quotes the ample power sentence without naming what the power depended on earns partial credit. (Blueprint 13 and Blueprint 14)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Questions 1 and 2 test close reading of the syllabus, the skill Station 1 of the activity grades. Question 3 carries the lesson's northern retreat entry, and Question 4 predicts success on the writing task, because it requires weighing a text's concession against the conditions its enforcement needed.
