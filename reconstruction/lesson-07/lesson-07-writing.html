Writing Task: Ranking the Retreat

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: by what steps, legal, economic, and violent, did the country retreat from the settlement between 1872 and 1876? Rank the retreat's four causes, law, money, violence, and weariness, in the order your evidence supports, and defend the ranking. A strong essay does more than list the steps. It states what Slaughter-House closed, works through what the panic and the elections of 1874 withdrew, and weighs the White League's daylight campaign against the Civil Rights Act of 1875, a law passed and left unenforced. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your ranking.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading, stations, and documents. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank wording, except where a bank item quotes a document.

Evidence Bank

1. The Amnesty Act of May 1872 removed the Fourteenth Amendment's officeholding disability from nearly all former Confederates at once, and the Liberal Republicans the same year nominated Horace Greeley on amnesty, civil service reform, and an end to federal protection of the southern governments. (Lesson reading, section A.)

2. The Slaughter-House Cases, decided 5 to 4 in April 1873, held that the privileges or immunities of United States citizenship were only the narrow rights of national citizenship. Miller's syllabus conceded that the main purpose of all the three last amendments was the freedom of the African race, and left the fundamental civil rights under the care of the State governments. (Audited bank record, quoted in the activity, Station 1.)

3. The Panic of 1873 opened with the failure of Jay Cooke's banking house, the depression ran six years, and the elections of 1874 gave the House of Representatives to the Democrats for the first time since before the war. (Lesson reading, section C.)

4. The White League's 1874 platform appealed to men of its race to unite in an earnest effort to re-establish a white man's government in the city and the State, and that year its members murdered Republican officeholders at Coushatta and fought at Liberty Place in New Orleans. (Audited bank record, quoted in the activity, Station 2, and lesson reading, section D.)

5. Charles Hays, a former enslaver speaking for the civil rights bill in 1874, told the House that the colored race, cut adrift in ignorance and penury they did not bring upon themselves, had set an example entitling them to praise, and the Civil Rights Act of 1875 then guaranteed equal enjoyment of inns, public conveyances, and theaters, with a five hundred dollar forfeit for denial, and stood largely unenforced. (Audited bank records, quoted in the activity, Stations 3 and 4.)

6. Nast's Worse Than Slavery, in October 1874, pictured a White Leaguer and a Klansman shaking hands over a shield bearing a Black family, while his Colored Rule, that March, carried the era's racial caricature of Black legislators, evidence that the image war was being lost on the settlement's own side. (Audited bank records, taught in the activity, Station 5, and lesson reading, section F.)

Plan Before You Write

My ranking (the four causes in your order, with the first ranked cause named as your claim):

My three evidence picks (bank numbers) and what each one proves about the ranking:

The strongest case for a different first cause, and how I will answer it:

Scaffold. Build the essay in this shape. Open with the ranking as your claim. A first body paragraph on your first ranked cause, stated at the level of its mechanism: what Slaughter-House closed, what the panic withdrew, what the League did in the open, or how weariness converted each event into policy. A second body paragraph on the interaction of your second and third causes, showing what each could not have done without the other. A closing move on the Civil Rights Act of 1875: state what its passage and its nonenforcement together prove about the country's will in 1875, and defend your ranking from that proof.

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your ranking, develop it with your three pieces of evidence in the order of the lesson, answer the strongest case for a different first cause in one or two sentences, and end on the act of 1875.

Essay, continued. Keep your evidence in the order of the lesson, and end on the act of 1875.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A ranking stated as a claim, with a mechanism for the first cause, for example that the courts belong first because Slaughter-House closed the federal court road in 1873, leaving enforcement to a Congress whose money and will the panic then withdrew, so the League's violence met a settlement with no second line of defense. A ranking that lists the four causes without ordering them, or orders them without a mechanism, earns partial credit before the evidence is read. (Blueprint 13 and Blueprint 14)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 carries the amnesty and the Liberal revolt. (Blueprint 14) Bank item 2 carries the ruling, its concession, and the rights left to the states. (Blueprint 13) Bank item 3 carries the panic, the depression's length, and the 1874 House. (Blueprint 14) Bank item 4 carries the platform's stated object and the year's violence at Coushatta and Liberty Place. (Blueprint 14) Bank item 5 carries Hays's defense and the act's guarantee, figures, and nonenforcement. (Blueprint 14) Bank item 6 carries the two cartoons as evidence about the North's picture of the settlement. (Blueprint 14)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the ranking. The decisive link is sequence against mechanism: strong essays argue whether the later steps were possible because of the earlier ones, and the closed court road of bank item 2 and the unenforced statute of bank item 5 are the two facts every ranking must place.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest case for a different first cause, such as violence first answered from bank item 4 and the reading: the League's victories were real, and each one was ratified by a northern refusal to answer it again, which returns the argument to the will the panic had already spent.

Model opening for teacher reference. The retreat ran downhill because each step removed the remedy for the next. Amnesty returned the old leaders to eligibility in 1872. Then the Court, in its first reading of the Fourteenth Amendment, closed the road through the federal courts, holding in 1873 that the amendment protected only a short list of national rights and leaving civil rights where the war had found them, under state authority. What remained was Congress, and the panic reached Congress within the year. A full model continues in this pattern, through the depression and the House of 1874 and the League's daylight campaign, and closes on the Civil Rights Act of 1875, passed as a memorial and left unenforced, the proof that by 1875 the country could still write the settlement and would no longer hold it.

Common weak answers. The North got tired (a mood, not a ranking) names weariness without its mechanism and skips every step the prompt requires. An essay that treats the Civil Rights Act of 1875 as proof the settlement was still advancing ignores bank item 5's closing fact, its nonenforcement, and should be sent back to the reading, section E. An essay that ranks violence first on the strength of Coushatta alone, without answering Liberty Place's federal rescue and what the rescuing cost the North's will, has used bank item 4 at half its weight.
