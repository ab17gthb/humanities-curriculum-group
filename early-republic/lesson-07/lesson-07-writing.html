Writing Task: Settled, or Only Postponed

The prompt. What did the settlements of 1820 settle, and what did they only postpone? Answer in a developed essay of about 300 words, argued from the records in The Settlements File and from the reading. A strong answer does more than list the two settlements. It states what each one actually decided, it follows one settlement through both what it settled and what it left open, and it answers the strongest claim on the other side: that a settlement both sections voted for, or a declaration no power dared test, had settled its question for good.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The outward ground, 1819 to 1823: Spain's empire dissolved in revolution, the United States recognized the new republics in 1822, the Adams-Onís Treaty of 1819 took Florida and drew a boundary to the Pacific, and Russia claimed the Pacific coast in 1821.

2. The decision to speak alone: Britain proposed a joint declaration. Jefferson and Madison advised accepting it. Adams argued the United States should declare its principles itself, rather than come in as a cockboat in the wake of the British man of war, and Monroe adopted his course.

3. The Doctrine, December 2, 1823: Monroe declared the continents closed to future European colonization and European interference in the hemisphere dangerous to the peace and safety of the United States, while pledging no interference with existing colonies or European wars.

4. The shelter: in 1823 the United States could not have enforced the declaration alone. The British navy sheltered it, because British merchants wanted the new republics open and rival empires shut out.

5. The crisis: Missouri applied in 1819 as a slave state with the Senate at eleven free and eleven slave states. The Tallmadge Amendment would have barred bringing more enslaved people in and freed children born there at twenty five. The House passed it on sectional lines; the Senate killed it.

6. The settlement: Maine entered as a free state and Missouri as a slave state, and Thomas's line at 36 degrees 30 minutes barred slavery north of it across the Louisiana Purchase lands and permitted it south, with Missouri north of the line as an exception. Monroe signed the act on March 6, 1820.

7. The warning: Jefferson wrote to John Holmes on April 22, 1820 that the question was a fire bell in the night and the knell of the Union, a reprieve only and not a final sentence, and that a geographical line joined to a marked principle would never be obliterated and would deepen with every new irritation.

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your claim, develop it with your evidence, answer the other side in full, and end by restating what 1820 decided and what it left on the table.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that names both a settlement and a postponement, not a claim that the era was confident or that compromise is wise in general. (Blueprint 13, 14)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, drawn from at least three different records, including one Doctrine record (bank 2, 3, or 4) and Jefferson's warning (bank 7). Dates and figures must be right: December 2, 1823; March 6, 1820; eleven and eleven; freed at twenty five; 36 degrees 30 minutes. (Blueprint 13, 14)
  • One settlement followed through both halves of its record: what it decided and what it left open. An essay that treats the Doctrine as American power in 1823, or the line as a ban on slavery in the North generally, cannot earn full credit under this prompt. (Blueprint 13, 14)
  • The other side answered from the record. The strongest form of the answer grants what is true in the claim, that Congress enacted the Compromise with votes from both sections and that no European power tested the Doctrine, and then shows what the records leave open: a declaration sheltered by another nation's fleet, a line whose own text excepts Missouri and stops at the Louisiana Purchase, and a warning from the territory's buyer that the line would deepen with every new irritation.

Model opening for teacher reference. The settlements of 1820 settled the season and postponed the questions. The Compromise decided who would enter the Union in 1820 and on what map, Maine free and Missouri slaveholding with a line at thirty six thirty, and the Doctrine decided the words in which the republic would address Europe. Neither decided the thing beneath it: the Compromise left slavery's expansion to be reargued over every territory the line did not cover, and the Doctrine left its own enforcement to a British fleet and to later generations. A full model continues in this pattern, carries one settlement through its decision and its opening, answers the claim that acceptance or silence closed the questions, and closes on Jefferson's reprieve.

Common weak answers. A confidence claim (America stood tall in 1823) never names the British navy or the Senate balance, and cannot use the bank. A cynicism claim (the settlements were frauds) ignores that the Compromise was enacted law that governed admissions for a generation and that the Doctrine cost its makers a real argument with two former presidents. An essay that quotes the fire bell without Jefferson's line argument, or that prices the line without its Missouri exception, has decorated the settlements without judging them, and the prompt asks for a judgment.
