Guided Notes: The Monroe Doctrine and the Missouri Compromise

Lesson question: What did the settlements of 1820 settle, and what did they only postpone?

Unit question: How did the United States turn its Constitution into a working government, and what did continental expansion do to the republic that government served?

Key Terms

Monroe Doctrine: the policy declared by President Monroe in his annual message of December 2, 1823, holding that the American continents were closed to future European colonization and that European interference in the hemisphere would be viewed as dangerous to the United States

John Quincy Adams: the secretary of state under Monroe, the chief author of the Monroe Doctrine's language and of the Transcontinental Treaty of 1819, who held that the United States should speak for the hemisphere itself rather than appear as a cockboat in the wake of the British man of war

Adams-Onís Treaty: the treaty of 1819, also called the Transcontinental Treaty, by which Spain ceded Florida to the United States and drew the boundary between American and Spanish claims across the continent to the Pacific

sectionalism: loyalty to one's own section of the country above the nation as a whole, the force the Missouri crisis exposed in 1819 and 1820 when the House and Senate divided on slavery's expansion in nearly perfect sectional lines

Tallmadge Amendment: the amendment proposed by Representative James Tallmadge in 1819 that would have barred bringing more enslaved people into Missouri and freed those born there at age twenty five, whose passage in the House and death in the Senate opened the Missouri crisis

Missouri Compromise: the settlement of 1820 that admitted Maine as a free state and Missouri as a slave state and drew a line at 36 degrees 30 minutes across the Louisiana Purchase lands, barring slavery north of it and permitting it south

thirty-six thirty: the parallel of latitude, 36 degrees 30 minutes north, that the Missouri Compromise drew across the Louisiana Purchase territory as the boundary between country closed to slavery and country open to it

Summary table. The Doctrine: Monroe's message of December 2, 1823 declared the continents closed to future European colonization and European interference dangerous to the United States, while pledging no American interference with existing colonies or European wars; it rested on British sea power and British trade interest, since the United States could not have enforced it alone. Maine and Missouri: Maine entered as a free state and Missouri as a slave state, holding the Senate balance at twelve and twelve, while Missouri's bar on free Black citizens was fudged rather than resolved and its admission waited until 1821. The line: Thomas's line at 36 degrees 30 minutes barred slavery north of it across the Louisiana Purchase lands and permitted it south, with Missouri north of the line and slaveholding as an exception from the start. The warning: Jefferson's letter of April 22, 1820 called the question a fire bell and the knell of the Union, and predicted a geographical line joined to a marked principle would deepen with every new irritation.

Exit ticket 1. The Monroe Doctrine held that the American continents were closed to future European colonization and that European interference in the hemisphere would be viewed as dangerous to the United States.

Exit ticket 2. Maine entered as a free state, Missouri entered as a slave state, and the line at 36 degrees 30 minutes barred slavery north of it across the Louisiana Purchase lands while permitting it south. The exception is Missouri itself, which stood north of the line and entered with slavery.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that pairs a Doctrine fact (declared alone at Adams's urging, unenforceable by the United States alone in 1823, sheltered by the British navy) with a Compromise fact (the Senate balance held by pairing Maine and Missouri, the line drawn at thirty six thirty, Jefferson's warning that the line would deepen) and answers the lesson question directly.
