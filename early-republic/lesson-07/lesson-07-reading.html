The Monroe Doctrine and the Missouri Compromise

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Two Settlements in One Season

The second Monroe administration opened with the republic facing outward and inward at once. Abroad, an empire was dissolving and new nations were asking who would stand with them. At home, a territory was asking to enter the Union, and its application forced a question Congress had avoided for a generation. The government answered both pressures the same way. It declared a line and called the declaration a settlement.

B. An Empire Dissolving

Spain's American empire was breaking apart in revolution. Through the 1810s and 1820s, peoples from Mexico to Chile declared republics of their own, and in 1822 the United States recognized the new governments. The Adams-Onís Treaty of 1819 had already taken Florida from Spain and drawn a boundary between American and Spanish claims to the Pacific. In 1821 Russia claimed the Pacific coast as far south as the 51st parallel. Rumors reached Washington that Europe's monarchies might help Spain recover its lost colonies by force. Britain, whose merchants wanted the new republics open to trade and the old empires shut out, proposed through its foreign secretary, George Canning, that Britain and the United States issue a joint declaration. Secretary of State John Quincy Adams argued in cabinet that the United States should declare its principles itself, rather than come in as a cockboat in the wake of the British man of war.

C. The Doctrine Declared

President James Monroe delivered the declaration in his annual message to Congress on December 2, 1823. The American continents, he said, by the free and independent condition they had assumed and maintained, were no longer to be considered subjects for future colonization by any European power. Any attempt by the European powers to extend their political system to any portion of this hemisphere would be viewed as dangerous to the peace and safety of the United States. In the other direction, the United States would not interfere with Europe's existing colonies and would take no part in Europe's own wars. Adams had drafted the central passages and carried the argument for declaring them alone.

D. What the Doctrine Could and Could Not Do

The declaration carried more confidence than force behind it. In 1823 the United States could not have enforced it alone against a determined European power. Its practical shelter was the British navy, whose interest in keeping rival empires out of the new republics' trade ran the same way as the American declaration. The message was addressed to Europe's governments, and it was also addressed to American voters and to the new republics themselves, as a claim about who the United States intended to be in its own hemisphere. It endured because later generations kept choosing to stand on it and give it new meanings, as a later unit will show when a president adds a corollary to it nearly a century on.

E. Missouri Applies

In 1819 Missouri applied for statehood as a slave state. The balance in the Senate stood at eleven free states and eleven slave states, and Missouri's admission alone would tip it. Representative James Tallmadge of New York proposed an amendment to the statehood bill. Missouri would enter only if no more enslaved people were brought into it and children born to enslaved parents there were freed at age twenty five. The House passed the amendment on sectional lines, with northern members voting for restriction and southern members against it. The Senate killed it. Through months of debate, the Congress of the Era of Good Feelings discovered that the question it had compromised in earlier decades had been growing with every bale of cotton the gin had made profitable, and that members now divided by section more reliably than by party.

F. The Settlement of 1820

Congress settled the crisis in three parts, and Monroe signed the central act on March 6, 1820. Maine, which had waited as a district of Massachusetts, entered as a free state. Missouri entered as a slave state, though its admission waited until 1821 while Congress argued over its constitution's bar on free Black citizens entering the state, a question fudged rather than resolved. For the remaining Louisiana Purchase lands, Senator Jesse Thomas of Illinois offered the line at 36 degrees 30 minutes north latitude. North of it, slavery was forever prohibited, except as punishment for a crime. South of it, slavery remained permitted. Missouri itself stood north of the line and entered with slavery anyway, an exception written in from the start. The arrangement held for a generation because it gave both sections a map they could stop arguing over.

G. The Fire Bell

At Monticello, Thomas Jefferson read the settlement as a warning. Writing to John Holmes on April 22, 1820, the former president who had bought the Louisiana territory said the momentous question, like a fire bell in the night, had awakened him and filled him with terror. He considered it, he wrote, the knell of the Union, hushed for the moment but a reprieve only, not a final sentence. A geographical line, he argued, coinciding with a marked principle, moral and political, once conceived would never be obliterated, and every new irritation would mark it deeper and deeper. The republic closed the season having told Europe a hemisphere was closed and told itself a line would hold. Both declarations were acts of confidence by a government that had learned to settle by declaring, and the fire bell had already rung in at least one house in Virginia for anyone who wanted to hear it.
