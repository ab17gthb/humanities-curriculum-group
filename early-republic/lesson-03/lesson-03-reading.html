Jefferson's Republic and the Louisiana Purchase

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. A President Elected to Shrink the Government

Jefferson took office in March 1801 promising a deliberate unwinding. He let the Sedition Act expire and pardoned the men still serving sentences under it. Congress restored the wait for citizenship from fourteen years to five and repealed the excise tax on whiskey. Jefferson cut the army and the navy and treated the national debt as a moral burden to be paid down. At the Treasury, Albert Gallatin ran the government on customs duties and land sales while retiring the debt Hamilton had funded. The whole program rested on strict construction, the reading of the Constitution Jefferson had used against the Bank of the United States. He had argued that the federal government held only the powers the document named. He now had to govern on that ground.

B. The Judges He Could Not Unwind

One inheritance from John Adams could not be repealed. In its last weeks the Federalist Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1801, creating new judgeships, and Adams filled them to his final night in office. The new judges, remembered as the midnight judges, held their places for life. Their commissions were signed and sealed, but one of them, William Marbury's commission as a justice of the peace, sat undelivered in the secretary of state's office when Jefferson took power. Jefferson's secretary of state, James Madison, refused to deliver it. Marbury sued and asked the Supreme Court to order Madison to hand the commission over. The case put Chief Justice John Marshall in a trap of his own party's making.

C. Marbury v. Madison: The Maneuver and the Doctrine

Marshall had been Adams's secretary of state, whose office had failed to deliver the commission, and he now sat as a Federalist judge for life. If the Court ordered Madison to deliver it, Jefferson could refuse, and a refused order would expose how little power the Court held. If the Court surrendered, it would confirm that weakness. In February 1803 Marshall chose a third path. The Court held that Marbury deserved his commission, then held that the section of the Judiciary Act of 1789 on which the suit rested granted power the Constitution did not give and was therefore unconstitutional and void, so no order could issue. The Court surrendered the case and claimed, in the same opinion, the power of judicial review. No president could attack the ruling, because it gave Jefferson the outcome he wanted, an undelivered commission.

D. The Mediterranean Test

Overseas, Jefferson faced the Barbary states of North Africa, whose rulers had long collected tribute from nations whose merchant ships used the Mediterranean. The United States had paid for years. Jefferson refused new payments to Tripoli, whose ruler declared war in 1801, and sent a small navy instead. The war ran on blockade and bombardment for four years. Its most famous episode came after the frigate Philadelphia ran aground and was captured in Tripoli harbor in 1803. Lieutenant Stephen Decatur led a night raid in February 1804 that boarded the captured frigate and burned it where it lay, so Tripoli could not use it. The war closed in 1805 with a treaty that ended tribute to Tripoli. The republic had defended its commerce with frigates instead of bribes.

E. The Offer No Ideology Survived

In 1803 Napoleon offered the United States far more than it had asked for. Jefferson had sent envoys to France to buy New Orleans and keep the Mississippi open to American trade. Napoleon, whose attempt to retake Saint-Domingue in the Caribbean had failed and whose war with Britain was resuming, had lost his use for an American empire. He offered not New Orleans alone but the whole of Louisiana, France's claim to the vast country west of the Mississippi. The treaty was signed in April 1803. The price was fifteen million dollars, including the settlement of American claims against France. In a single signature the national territory roughly doubled, reaching from the Mississippi toward the Rocky Mountains, though no one in Washington could say exactly where its western boundary ran.

F. The Scruple, Stated Honestly

Jefferson stated his objection plainly. The Constitution, he wrote that August, had made no provision for holding foreign territory, still less for incorporating foreign nations into the Union, and the executive had done an act beyond the Constitution. He drafted a constitutional amendment to authorize the purchase and then abandoned it, because delay risked the treaty and the Senate pressed for speed. The parties traded places in the argument. Federalists who had preached a broad reading of federal power now discovered strict construction and warned against the purchase. Jefferson, who had built a career on strictness, signed anyway and asked Congress to ratify and pay, and to trust the nation to approve an act it had not previously authorized. The Senate ratified the treaty in October 1803.

G. The Transfer and the First Governings

The flag was raised at New Orleans in December 1803. The United States took possession of a territory holding about fifty thousand people, many of them enslaved, living under French and Spanish law along the rivers. The treaty promised the inhabitants incorporation into the Union and the rights of citizens as soon as possible under the Constitution. In the meantime Jefferson's government ruled the territory by appointment from Washington, and its people petitioned for the rights the treaty had promised. The purchase opened a question it did not close. Jefferson called Louisiana ground for an empire of liberty. His own theory warned that a republic spread across too much distance could not hold together. The next lesson walks into the country itself, with the expedition Jefferson sent west.
