Writing Task: Against His Principles, or For Them

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: How did a president elected to shrink the government double the nation's size? Take a position on the purchase itself: was it Jefferson acting against his principles, or Jefferson discovering what his principles were for? A strong answer states his principles first from his own words, a wise and frugal government resting on strict construction, then states what he did in 1803 and what he confessed about it in private, and judges the two against each other. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and activity. Write in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The program of 1801: the Sedition Act left to expire and its prisoners pardoned, the citizenship wait restored to five years, the whiskey excise repealed, the army and navy cut, and Gallatin paying down the debt on customs duties and land sales, all resting on strict construction.

2. The First Inaugural's standard, March 4, 1801: a wise and frugal Government, which shall restrain men from injuring one another, shall leave them otherwise free to regulate their own pursuits of industry and improvement, and shall not take from the mouth of labor the bread it has earned.

3. Marbury v. Madison, February 1803: the Court held Marbury deserved his commission, held the Judiciary Act section behind his suit unconstitutional, refused the order, and claimed judicial review in the same opinion. Jefferson, who won the refusal, let the claim stand.

4. The Barbary War, 1801 to 1805: Jefferson refused new tribute to Tripoli, fought with the small navy he was cutting, lost the Philadelphia, saw Decatur burn it in February 1804, and closed the war in 1805 with tribute to Tripoli ended and sixty thousand dollars still paid for the prisoners.

5. Napoleon's position in 1803: his attempt to retake Saint-Domingue had failed and war with Britain was resuming, so he offered the whole of Louisiana when Jefferson's envoys had asked only for New Orleans. The treaty of April 1803 cost fifteen million dollars and roughly doubled the national territory.

6. The Breckinridge letter, August 12, 1803: the constitution has made no provision for our holding foreign territory, still less for incorporating foreign nations into our union, and the executive had done an act beyond the constitution. Jefferson drafted an amendment to cure the defect and abandoned it, and the treaty promised Louisiana's inhabitants incorporation and citizens' rights as soon as possible while his government ruled them by appointment.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that takes one side of the purchase question and answers the lesson question with it: the doubling and the shrinking stated in one argument, not two lists. (Blueprint 5)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 supports the program and its strict construction ground. (Blueprint 5) Bank item 2 supports Jefferson's principles in his own words. (Blueprint 5) Bank item 3 supports the Court enlarging itself while Jefferson stood aside, the limit case of his restraint. (Blueprint 6) Bank item 4 supports the navy used while cut, principles bending under a real refusal to pay. (Blueprint 5) Bank item 5 supports Napoleon's reasons and the treaty's scale and price. (Blueprint 5) Bank item 6 supports the confession, the abandoned cure, and the treaty's promise against the appointed government. (Blueprint 5) A full credit essay must use item 6 or quote the letter's substance; a verdict on the scruple without the scruple's own words is an opinion without its document.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. An essay that recites the unwinding, the treaty, and the price without judging them against the inaugural standard has assembled the lesson's events without its argument, and should earn partial credit.
  • An ending that keeps the governed in view: about fifty thousand people, many of them enslaved, acquired by a signature, promised citizenship as soon as possible, and ruled by appointment in the meantime.

Model opening for teacher reference. Jefferson's principles were never in doubt, because he published them. His sum of good government was a wise and frugal government that restrained injury, left men otherwise free, and took nothing from the mouth of labor, and his party's reading of the Constitution held the federal government to the powers the document names. By that standard the Louisiana Purchase fails in his own hand. Writing to Breckinridge in August 1803, he confessed that the Constitution had made no provision for holding foreign territory and that the executive had done an act beyond the Constitution, and the amendment he drafted to cure the act was abandoned for speed. A full model continues in this pattern, or in the opposite verdict's pattern, in which the same letter shows a hierarchy of principles at work: the good secured would otherwise probably be never again in their power, and a construction rule ranked below the union it was written to serve. Either model must end with the people the treaty acquired.

Common weak answers. A bargain answer (the purchase was cheap land) drops the constitutional question entirely: at three cents an acre it was still, by its buyer's account, an act beyond the Constitution. A hypocrite answer (Jefferson simply broke his rules) drops the letter's reasoning, the guardian comparison and the proposed appeal to the nation, which show a man recording his own breach and planning its cure. A clean triumph answer (the purchase fulfilled the inaugural) ignores the confession and the appointed government ruling the people the treaty had promised citizenship. Credit requires both records held at once: the standard Jefferson announced, and the signature he set against it.
