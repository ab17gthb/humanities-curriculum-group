Guided Notes: Jefferson's Republic and the Louisiana Purchase

Lesson question: How did a president elected to shrink the government double the nation's size?

Unit question: How did the United States turn its Constitution into a working government, and what did continental expansion do to the republic that government served?

Key Terms

strict construction: the reading of the Constitution that holds the federal government to the powers the document names and those necessarily implied by them, the ground on which Jefferson opposed the Bank and doubted the Louisiana Purchase

implied powers: the powers the federal government holds beyond those the Constitution lists, drawn from the clause empowering Congress to make all laws necessary and proper for carrying its listed powers into execution

Louisiana Purchase: the treaty of 1803 by which the United States bought France's claim to Louisiana for fifteen million dollars, doubling the nation's territory in a single signature and testing what a president's treaty power could do

midnight judges: the judges John Adams appointed and the Federalist Congress authorized in his presidency's last days, whose commissions became the subject of Marbury v. Madison when Jefferson's secretary of state refused to deliver them

Marbury v. Madison: the Supreme Court decision of 1803 in which Chief Justice John Marshall held that the Court could not order the delivery of a judicial commission because the law granting it that power was unconstitutional

judicial review: the power of the courts to declare laws and acts of the other branches unconstitutional and void, claimed for the Supreme Court by Marbury v. Madison in 1803 and since become the judiciary's central power

Barbary Wars: the wars of 1801 to 1805 and 1815 in which the United States fought the Barbary states of North Africa rather than keep paying tribute for the safety of American merchant ships in the Mediterranean

The Lesson at a Glance. The program: Jefferson shrank the government at home, letting the Sedition Act expire, pardoning its prisoners, restoring the five year citizenship wait, repealing the whiskey excise, cutting the army and navy, and paying down the debt under Gallatin on customs and land sales; it showed strict construction governing where governing was his choice. The case: Marbury v. Madison left the midnight judges in place for life and gave the Court judicial review while denying Marbury his commission; it showed a Federalist Court enlarging its own power by surrendering the case in front of it. The war: Jefferson refused tribute to Tripoli and fought with a small navy, the Philadelphia burned by Decatur's raid, the war closed in 1805; it showed a president who cut the navy still using it rather than paying. The purchase: for fifteen million dollars Jefferson bought France's claim to Louisiana and roughly doubled the territory; it showed the treaty power doing what his reading of the Constitution had no words for. The scruple: Jefferson wrote that the Constitution had made no provision for holding foreign territory and that the executive had acted beyond it, drafted an amendment, abandoned it for speed, and signed; it showed both parties trading their constructions when power changed hands.

Exit ticket 1. Judicial review is the power of the courts to declare laws and acts of the other branches unconstitutional and void. Marbury v. Madison claimed it in 1803 while refusing to order Madison to deliver Marbury's commission, because the law granting the Court that power was itself unconstitutional.

Exit ticket 2. Jefferson wrote that the Constitution had made no provision for holding foreign territory, still less for incorporating foreign nations into the Union, and that the executive, in seizing the occasion, had done an act beyond the Constitution. He drafted a constitutional amendment authorizing the purchase and abandoned it because delay risked losing the treaty and the Senate pressed for speed.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer pairing the domestic program with the foreign offer. The strongest answers run from Jefferson's unwinding at home to Napoleon's position in 1803: his attempt to retake Saint-Domingue had failed and war with Britain was resuming, so he offered the whole of Louisiana when American envoys had asked only for New Orleans, and Jefferson signed the treaty of April 1803 for fifteen million dollars and doubled the nation's territory in one signature.
