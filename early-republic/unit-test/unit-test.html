Part C: Sources (10 points)

Answer each question in complete sentences, citing the sources by letter.

Source A

George Washington, the Farewell Address, a published letter of September 1796, printed as Washington retired after two terms. From George Washington, 'Washington's Farewell Address,' 1796 (Yale Avalon Project). Cuts are marked with ellipses in brackets.

“The name of American, which belongs to you in your national capacity, must always exalt the just pride of patriotism more than any appellation derived from local discriminations. [...] It serves always to distract the public councils and enfeeble the public administration. It agitates the community with ill-founded jealousies and false alarms, kindles the animosity of one part against another, foments occasionally riot and insurrection. [...] The great rule of conduct for us in regard to foreign nations is in extending our commercial relations, to have with them as little political connection as possible. [...] It is our true policy to steer clear of permanent alliances with any portion of the foreign world; so far, I mean, as we are now at liberty to do it. [...]”

A1. (1 point) Washington writes that the name of American must exalt the just pride of patriotism more than any appellation derived from what?

A2. (2 points) State the great rule of conduct the passage gives for dealings with foreign nations, and state the true policy it names toward permanent alliances.

Source B

President James Monroe, the annual message to Congress of December 2, 1823; the passages known as the Monroe Doctrine. From President James Monroe, 'Monroe Doctrine,' 1823 (Yale Avalon Project). Cuts are marked with ellipses in brackets.

“[...] that the American continents, by the free and independent condition which they have assumed and maintain, are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers. [...] In the wars of the European powers in matters relating to themselves we have never taken any part, nor does it comport with our policy to do so. It is only when our rights are invaded or seriously menaced that we resent injuries or make preparation for our defense. With the movements in this hemisphere we are of necessity more immediately connected, and by causes which must be obvious to all enlightened and impartial observers. [...] We owe it, therefore, to candor and to the amicable relations existing between the United States and those powers to declare that we should consider any attempt on their part to extend their system to any portion of this hemisphere as dangerous to our peace and safety.”

B1. (2 points) State what Monroe declares about the American continents and future colonization by European powers, and state what the United States will consider any attempt by those powers to extend their system to any portion of this hemisphere.

B2. (2 points) State the only condition under which, the passage says, the United States resents injuries or makes preparation for its defense, and state how the passage says the United States stands toward movements in this hemisphere.

Source C

John L. O'Sullivan, the annexation editorial of 1845, written to rally support for the annexation of Texas. From John L. O'Sullivan, 'Annexation,' July-August 1845 (United States Magazine and Democratic Review, 1845). Cuts are marked with ellipses in brackets.

“It is now time for the opposition to the Annexation of Texas to cease. [...] Texas is now ours. Already, before these words are written, her Convention has undoubtedly ratified the acceptance, by her Congress, of our proffered invitation into the Union. [...] Why, were other reasoning wanting, in favor of now elevating this question of the reception of Texas into the Union, out of the lower region of our past party dissensions, up to its proper level of a high and broad nationality, it surely is to be found, found abundantly, in the manner in which other nations have undertaken to intrude themselves into it, between us and the proper parties to the case, in a spirit of hostile interference against us, for the avowed object of thwarting our policy and hampering our power, limiting our greatness and checking the fulfillment of our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions.”

C1. (1 point) O'Sullivan writes that it is now time for the opposition to cease. Opposition to what?

C2. (2 points) State what other nations are accused of checking, in the passage's own phrase for the nation's destiny, and state for what the continent is said to be allotted by Providence.

Part D: Essay

The essay is scored on the six part holistic scale below, whichever prompt the student chose.

Score 5. A clear judgment is stated and held through the essay. At least three pieces of specific evidence from the unit are used accurately, the strongest point on the other side is stated fairly and answered, and the writing is organized in paragraphs with a conclusion that follows from the evidence.

Score 4. A clear judgment is stated and mostly held. Two or three pieces of specific evidence are used accurately, the other side is stated and answered, and the organization holds, with minor lapses in precision or development.

Score 3. A judgment is stated but drifts, or the evidence is specific in places and general in others. The other side is mentioned but answered thinly, or the answer to it is asserted rather than argued.

Score 2. The essay retells the settlements or the expansion story without a judgment that the evidence is made to serve, or the judgment is stated but supported by only one accurate piece of evidence. The other side does not appear.

Score 1. The response is a fragment, a single point restated, or evidence is used inaccurately in ways that change its meaning. Little of the prompt is answered.

Score 0. The response is blank, off the prompt, or copies the prompt without answering it.

For Prompt 1, strong evidence includes the Senate balanced at eleven free and eleven slave states in 1819; the Tallmadge Amendment passing the House on sectional lines and dying in the Senate; Maine admitted free and Missouri admitted slave, with Missouri's constitutional bar on free Black citizens fudged rather than settled; the line at 36 degrees 30 minutes barring slavery from the Louisiana Purchase lands north of it; and Jefferson's letter to John Holmes calling the question a fire bell in the night and the knell of the Union. Evidence on the other side includes the line holding for a generation, the crisis ending in 1820 without disunion, and both sections accepting a map they could stop arguing over, while the cotton gin's economics kept enlarging the question the line had fenced. (Blueprint 14)

For Prompt 2, strong evidence includes O'Sullivan's language of a continent allotted by Providence, which writes the continent's inhabitants and Mexico's sovereignty out of the account; Texas kept outside the Union for nine years because its admission as a slave state threatened the balance, then annexed by joint resolution when a treaty could not pass; Oregon halved at the 49th parallel by treaty with Britain while Mexico was fought; Polk's claim of American blood on American soil in ground both nations claimed, answered by Lincoln's spot resolutions; the cession of some five hundred thousand square miles at Guadalupe Hidalgo for fifteen million dollars; and the Wilmot Proviso passing the House and dying in the Senate. Evidence on the other side includes the sincerity of the expansionist belief, the restraint shown in the Oregon settlement, and the treaty paying Mexico and winning ratification as a peace. Either prompt can earn full credit argued from either side of its question, provided the judgment is earned from the evidence and the other side is answered. (Blueprint 15)
