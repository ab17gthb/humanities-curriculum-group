Washington's Republic: Hamilton, Parties, and the Farewell

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. A Government to Invent

The Constitution named the offices of the new government and listed its powers, but it did not build the machinery that would make those powers work. When George Washington took the oath in April 1789, almost everything his administration did was done for the first time, and each first act stood as a precedent.

Congress supplied the first pieces in 1789. It created the Departments of State, Treasury, and War, and the heads of those departments became the advisers Washington gathered around him as his cabinet. The Judiciary Act of 1789 organized a Supreme Court and lower federal courts. Washington was received as head of state as the visible head of a government most citizens had never seen act.

B. Hamilton's Program in Four Parts

Secretary of the Treasury Alexander Hamilton gave the new government a financial design with four moving parts. First, the United States would fund its Revolutionary War debt at full value, so lenders would trust the word of the new government. Second, the federal government would assume the war debts of the states, so state creditors would look to the union for payment. Third, Congress would charter a national bank to hold the government funds and issue a uniform currency.

Fourth, Congress would lay an excise tax on distilled whiskey in 1791 to help pay for it all. Each part rested on a broad reading of the Constitution. Hamilton argued that powers could be implied as well as listed, and that the clause letting Congress make laws necessary and proper for its powers gave the government room to choose its tools.

C. Strict Reading, Newspapers, and the First Parties

The program built its own opposition. Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson and James Madison read the Constitution strictly and answered that a bank was a tool the document never named. Funding at full value rewarded speculators who had bought soldiers' certificates for a fraction of their worth. Assumption rewarded states that had borrowed heavily and taxed states that had paid their debts down.

The argument left the cabinet room and entered the newspapers, where each side gathered writers and voters behind it. Hamilton supporters called themselves Federalists. Those around Jefferson and Madison called themselves Democratic-Republicans. No one had planned a party system, but the argument over one financial program had sorted the republic into two camps.

D. The Excise Answered on the Frontier

The excise tax met its hardest answer on the frontier. Western farmers grew corn they could not profitably carry over the mountains. Distilled into whiskey, the same corn became a load a horse could carry. The tax fell on the one product their farms could sell, and in western Pennsylvania resistance turned open in 1794. Crowds tarred and feathered tax collectors, robbed the mail, and attacked the home of a federal marshal.

Washington answered with a militia army of some thirteen thousand men called from four states, and he rode west with it part of the way. The rebellion dissolved before the army arrived. Two men were convicted of treason, and Washington pardoned both. The point stood. The new government would enforce its own laws, where the government under the Articles of Confederation had been able only to watch.

E. The Cotton Gin

In the same years a small machine changed what the new government would govern. Eli Whitney patented his cotton gin in 1794. The machine pulled short staple cotton fiber through a grate and left the sticky seeds behind, work hands had done slowly. With the gin, short staple cotton, which grew across the inland South, became a profitable crop for the first time.

Slavery, which many in the founding generation had expected to wither, instead gained a new economic engine. Planters bought land and bought enslaved people to work it, and cotton planting expanded across the South. The expansion became a central fact the new government would spend seventy years failing to settle.

F. Neutrality and Two Treaties

France went to war with Britain in 1793, and American opinion split along the party line the bank fight had drawn. Jefferson sympathized with France. Hamilton leaned toward Britain, whose trade filled American customs houses. Washington held the country out of the war. His Neutrality Proclamation of April 1793 declared a conduct friendly and impartial toward the powers at war and warned citizens who aided either side.

Britain seized American ships trading with French ports and still held frontier posts on American soil. Washington sent Chief Justice John Jay to London, and the Jay Treaty of 1794 won evacuation of the posts and settled old debts, at the price of British limits on American trade. Crowds burned Jay in effigy, and the Senate approved the treaty by the bare two thirds it required. Pinckney's Treaty with Spain in 1795 opened the Mississippi River to American trade.

G. The Farewell

Washington closed his presidency in print. In September 1796 he published his Farewell Address in a Philadelphia newspaper, announcing he would not accept a third term. The refusal set the two term practice presidents observed for nearly a century and a half. In the address he warned against the spirit of party he had watched form inside his own cabinet and against permanent alliances with foreign nations.

The government he handed over had a funded debt, a bank, courts in session, an enforced tax law, and treaties on two frontiers. It also had slavery expanding and two parties campaigning to succeed him. What Washington warned against and what his government had built are the two inheritances the rest of this unit follows.

5. What did Washington's government have to invent, and what did it warn against as it left? Full credit answers hold both halves. The government had to invent the working machinery of a republic the Constitution had only outlined: a cabinet and executive departments, a federal court system, a funded debt and a national bank, an enforceable tax, and a foreign policy of neutrality carried out through treaties. As it left, in the Farewell Address of September 1796, Washington warned against the spirit of party he had watched form inside his own cabinet and against permanent alliances with foreign nations. Strong answers close on the tension the reading leaves standing: the parties the address warned against had been created by the argument over the government the address defended, and slavery was expanding under the cotton gin while the warnings were published. A paragraph that lists inventions without the warnings, or the warnings without the inventions, earns partial credit.
