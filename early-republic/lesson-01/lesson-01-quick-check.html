Quick Check: Hold the Country Out of the War

Directions: Read the passage and the background, then answer the questions.

... the duty and interest of the United States require, that they should with sincerity and good faith adopt and pursue a conduct friendly and impartial toward the belligerent powers: I have therefore thought fit by these presents to declare the disposition of the United States to observe the conduct aforesaid towards those powers respectively; and to exhort and warn the citizens of the United States carefully to avoid all acts and proceedings whatsoever, which may in any manner tend to contravene such disposition. ... whosoever of the citizens of the United States shall render himself liable to punishment or forfeiture under the law of nations, by committing, aiding or abetting hostilities against any of the said powers, or by carrying to any of them those articles, which are deemed contraband by the modern usage of nations, will not receive the protection of the United States, against such punishment or forfeiture.

Source: George Washington, Proclamation of Neutrality, April 22, 1793, Founders Online, National Archives, as carried in the audited TPSP record. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

Background. France declared war on Britain in February 1793. The United States held a treaty of alliance with France from 1778, signed during the Revolution, and American opinion split along the party line formed in the fight over Hamilton's financial program. Jefferson sympathized with France. Hamilton leaned toward Britain, whose trade filled American customs houses. Washington's cabinet agreed the country was too weak to fight. The proclamation never uses the word neutrality. Two years of argument followed over British seizures of American ships, and in 1794 Washington sent Chief Justice John Jay to London. The Jay Treaty won the evacuation of the British frontier posts by June 1796 and required the United States to compensate British creditors for prewar debts. The Senate approved it by the bare two thirds required, and crowds burned Jay in effigy.

1. In the passage, what conduct does Washington declare for the United States toward the powers at war? Quote the two words that describe the conduct.

2. The passage warns citizens twice. State what citizens are warned to avoid doing, and state what a citizen who aids a power at war will lose.

3. Using the background, give two facts: one about why the decision of 1793 was hard for Washington's government, and one about what the Jay Treaty of 1794 settled. State what each fact shows.

4. A reader argues that the proclamation settled American foreign policy, because the country stayed out of the war. State the strongest answer to that argument from the passage and the background.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What conduct does Washington declare, in its two describing words? A conduct friendly and impartial toward the belligerent powers. Credit requires both quoted words. (Blueprint 2)

2. What are citizens warned to avoid, and what will a citizen who aids a power lose? Citizens are exhorted and warned to avoid all acts and proceedings whatsoever which may in any manner tend to contravene the declared conduct. A citizen who commits, aids, or abets hostilities against a power at war, or carries contraband goods to one, will not receive the protection of the United States against punishment or forfeiture under the law of nations. Credit requires the avoidance and the lost protection. (Blueprint 2)

3. Give two facts, one about why the decision was hard and one about what the Jay Treaty settled, and what each shows. For the difficulty, any one: the United States held a 1778 alliance with France; opinion split along the party line formed over Hamilton's program, with Jefferson leaning to France and Hamilton to Britain; or the cabinet judged the country too weak to fight. It shows neutrality was a choice made against an obligation and against half the country's sympathies, not an absence of a question. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2) For the treaty: Britain evacuated its frontier posts by June 1796, and the United States compensated British creditors for prewar debts. It shows the proclamation bought time rather than peace on its own, because holding neutrality still required a negotiation whose price divided the country, as the effigy burnings and the bare two thirds Senate vote show. (Blueprint 2)

4. State the strongest answer to the argument that the proclamation settled foreign policy. Staying out of the war in 1793 settled one season, not the policy. The proclamation itself never uses the word neutrality, it warned American citizens rather than the warring powers, and the seizures and frontier posts that had made the season dangerous were still open, requiring the Jay Treaty of 1794 and its contested price. Washington returned to the question in the Farewell Address of 1796, restating the rule as steering clear of permanent alliances, which is evidence that he treated the posture as a standing argument to be renewed, not a question closed in 1793. (Blueprint 2)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Questions 1 and 2 test whether students can read a proclamation for its declared conduct and its warning, the skill Station 2 of the activity grades. Question 3 carries both of the lesson's blueprint entries, the party split over Hamilton's program for Blueprint 1 and Washington's precedents for Blueprint 2. Question 4 predicts success on the writing task, because it requires a judgment about what a document settled and what it left open.
