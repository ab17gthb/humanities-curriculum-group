Guided Notes: Manifest Destiny and the War with Mexico

Lesson question: What did Americans tell themselves expansion was, and what did the war with Mexico leave unsettled?

Unit question: How did the United States turn its Constitution into a working government, and what did continental expansion do to the republic that government served?

Key Terms

Manifest Destiny: the name given in 1845 to the belief that the United States was destined, by providence and by the superiority of its institutions, to expand across the continent to the Pacific, a belief its holders used to justify annexation and war

annexation: the formal addition of territory to a country, the method by which Texas joined the United States in 1845 by joint resolution of Congress after a treaty of annexation had failed in the Senate

Republic of Texas: the independent nation Texans declared in 1836 after revolting against Mexico, recognized by the United States but kept outside the Union for nine years because its admission as a slave state threatened the sectional balance

Oregon Country: the Pacific Northwest territory jointly occupied by the United States and Britain from 1818, claimed by American expansionists to the line of 54 degrees 40 minutes and divided at the 49th parallel by treaty in 1846

Mexican-American War: the war of 1846 to 1848 between the United States and Mexico, begun over the Texas boundary after American annexation and ended by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo with Mexico ceding half its territory

Wilmot Proviso: the amendment proposed by Representative David Wilmot in 1846, passed repeatedly by the House and blocked in the Senate, that would have barred slavery in any territory taken from Mexico, forcing the slavery question onto the nation's table three years before the gold rush

Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo: the treaty of February 2, 1848, that ended the Mexican-American War, confirmed the Rio Grande as Texas's boundary, and transferred California, New Mexico, and the country between to the United States for fifteen million dollars

Summary table. The story told: in 1845 O'Sullivan named a manifest destiny to overspread a continent allotted by Providence, a story that told expansion as fulfillment of a gift and made the continent's Native nations and its Mexican republic hard to see. It left open whether a story could settle a question of title and of slavery. Texas and Oregon: Texas revolted in 1836, stood outside the Union for nine years as a recognized republic, and entered in 1845 by joint resolution, while Oregon, jointly occupied since 1818, was divided at the 49th parallel by treaty in 1846. Together they left open why one claim ended in negotiation and the other in war. The war and the treaty: Polk ordered troops into ground both nations claimed, Congress recognized a war Polk said Mexico had begun, American armies won in northern Mexico, at Mexico City, and in New Mexico and California, and the treaty of February 2, 1848 confirmed the Rio Grande and ceded California, New Mexico, and the country between for fifteen million dollars. It left open the standing of the Mexican citizens the eighth article promised a choice of citizenship and respected property. The Wilmot Proviso: Wilmot's amendment of 1846 would have barred slavery in any territory taken from Mexico, passed the House repeatedly on sectional votes, and died in the Senate, leaving slavery in the Mexican cession unanswered.

Exit ticket 1. Texas entered by annexation under a joint resolution of Congress, needing a simple majority of both houses. The method that had failed was a treaty of annexation, needing two thirds of the Senate.

Exit ticket 2. Polk claimed that Mexico had passed the boundary of the United States, invaded its territory, and shed American blood upon American soil, and that war existed by Mexico's act. Lincoln demanded that the president name the exact spot where the blood was shed and show whether that spot was or was not American soil at the time.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that names both the story and the open question. Americans told themselves that expansion was a manifest destiny, a continent allotted by Providence for the free development of their institutions. The war left unsettled whether slavery would enter the territory taken from Mexico, the question Wilmot's proviso of 1846 raised in the House, passed there repeatedly, and lost in the Senate.
