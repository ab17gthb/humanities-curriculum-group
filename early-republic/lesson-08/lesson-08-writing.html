Writing Task: Describe or Disguise

The prompt. Answer the closing question of the lesson in a developed essay of about 300 words: did Manifest Destiny describe what the United States did in the 1840s, or did it disguise what the United States did? A strong answer states what the phrase claimed, weighs that claim against the record of annexation, negotiation, and war in this lesson, and answers the strongest claim the other side can make from the same record. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your verdict.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading, stations, and documents. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank wording, except where a bank item quotes a document.

Evidence Bank

1. The unsigned editorial traditionally attributed to John L. O'Sullivan, in 1845, claimed a manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions, and held that Texas was already ours. ("Annexation," United States Magazine and Democratic Review, 1845, audited TPSP record.)

2. Texas revolted from Mexico in 1836 and stood as a recognized republic for nine years, kept outside the Union because admission as a slave state threatened the sectional balance, then entered in 1845 by joint resolution, a simple majority doing what a treaty needing two thirds of the Senate could not. (Lesson reading, section B.)

3. The Oregon Country, jointly occupied with Britain since 1818, was divided at the 49th parallel by treaty in 1846, although the campaign cry of 1844 had demanded the whole country to 54 degrees 40 minutes. (Lesson reading, section D.)

4. Polk ordered troops into the ground between the Nueces and the Rio Grande, and after the Thornton affair of April 1846 told Congress that Mexico had shed American blood upon American soil. Lincoln's spot resolutions of December 1847 demanded the exact spot and whether it was American soil at the time. (Polk, Message to Congress, May 11, 1846, Avalon Project; Lincoln, Spot Resolutions, Papers of Abraham Lincoln.)

5. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo of February 2, 1848 confirmed the Rio Grande and ceded California, New Mexico, and the country between, some five hundred thousand square miles by the usual estimate, for fifteen million dollars. Article VIII gave Mexican citizens in the ceded lands a choice of citizenship within one year, with property to be inviolably respected. (Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, 1848, Yale Avalon Project, audited TPSP record.)

6. The Wilmot Proviso of 1846 would have barred slavery and involuntary servitude in any territory taken from Mexico. It passed the House repeatedly on sectional votes and died in the Senate, leaving slavery in the Mexican cession unanswered when the treaty was signed. (Wilmot Proviso, Congressional Globe, 29th Congress, 1st session, page 1217.)

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A verdict argued from the record, not from the phrase itself. The record has two halves, a continent overspread in three years and a set of choices and disputes the phrase does not name, and a full credit essay weighs both before naming describe or disguise. (Blueprint 15)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 carries the phrase at its strongest, destiny and Providence and a Texas already ours. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 2 carries annexation as a counted political choice, delayed nine years by the slavery balance and carried at last by a simple majority. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 3 carries the Oregon contrast, a claim halved by treaty with Britain in the same years. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 4 carries the war's opening, disputed ground and a presidential claim a congressman demanded be located. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 5 carries the cession and the resident population the treaty assigned and promised to respect. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 6 carries the question left open, slavery in the cession, raised in 1846 and unpassed in 1848. (Blueprint 15)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the verdict. The decisive link is the gap between the telling and the choosing: students should argue that each stage of the expansion was decided by identifiable people in identifiable votes and orders, whichever verdict they name.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest claim of the other side. A describe verdict must answer that the phrase never names Mexico's title, the Native nations, or the disputed spot where the war began. A disguise verdict must answer that the expansion the phrase predicted did occur, Texas in 1845, Oregon divided in 1846, and the cession in 1848, and that millions of Americans acted on the belief as a real motive.

Model opening for teacher reference. Manifest Destiny disguised more than it described, and the record of the choices is the proof. The editorial of 1845 could call Texas already ours only by passing over nine years in which president after president refused admission because a slave state Texas broke the Senate balance, and could call the continent allotted by Providence only by leaving its Mexican and Native holders unnamed. What followed was not a destiny unfolding but a sequence of decisions: a joint resolution substituted for a failed treaty, a claim against Britain halved by negotiation while a claim against Mexico was prosecuted by war, and troops ordered into ground between two rivers whose ownership the war message asserted and the spot resolutions questioned. A full model in this line follows the record to February 1848, where the treaty that closed the war had to assign citizenship to the people already living on the ceded continent, and where the proviso that would have answered the slavery question lay unpassed.

Common weak answers. Manifest Destiny described expansion because the United States did expand (a result, not a weighing) ignores that the question is what the phrase said about how and why the expansion happened. Manifest Destiny was simply a lie its holders did not believe (a motive no document in this file can prove) skips the record that the belief moved voters, editors, and a president. An essay with no answer to the other side's strongest claim earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a weighing, not a verdict alone.
