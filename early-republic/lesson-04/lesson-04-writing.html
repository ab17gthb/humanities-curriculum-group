Writing Task: Explorers, Diplomats, or Surveyors

The prompt. Write a developed essay of about 300 words answering the lesson question from this lesson's documents: what did the Corps of Discovery actually find in the West, and whose country was it? Judge whether the corps should be remembered chiefly as explorers, as diplomats, or as surveyors for settlement, and defend the judgment against the strongest rival label in the record. Your essay must account for the peoples whose country the corps crossed and for York, whose crossing the expedition's rewards never reached. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and The Expedition File. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording. Keep the paired records whole wherever you use them: Jefferson's orders beside the census they required, and the Shoshone council beside the horses it produced.

Evidence Bank

1. By the Treaty of Greenville of 1795, the Western Confederacy, defeated at Fallen Timbers in 1794, ceded most of present day Ohio to the United States and accepted its protection, in a treaty whose first article promised perpetual peace. Purchase, pressure, and cession lines were already the republic's method in the West nine years before the corps set out.

2. Jefferson's instructions of June 20, 1803 ordered the Missouri explored for the most direct and practicable water communication across the continent for commerce, the geography fixed by observation, and the nations on the line learned by name, number, lands, relations, and trade, with all intercourse conducted in the most friendly and conciliatory manner.

3. Clark's journal entry of May 14, 1804 accounts for the first day in miles, weather, boats, and men: four and a half miles under sail in the rain, 22 men in the boat of twenty oars, French boatmen in the pirogues, the men in high spirits. The journals named every river and counted every nation for the government at home.

4. The corps wintered in 1804 and 1805 beside Mandan and Hidatsa towns larger than St. Louis and survived on their hospitality. It engaged Charbonneau because Sacagawea, a Shoshone woman taken captive by the Hidatsa as a girl, could interpret, and because a woman and an infant, her son Jean Baptiste, born in February 1805, read as a party of peace.

5. In August 1805 the corps had to have Shoshone horses to cross the mountains. Sacagawea, called in to interpret, recognized the chief Cameahwait as her brother, and through a chain of interpreters in four languages the captains stated their objects. The horses and the passage west were obtained in the space of that recognition, and the Nez Perce then fed and guided the corps over the Bitterroots.

6. The corps crossed the continental divide and found no single water route beyond it. At Fort Clatsop in November 1805, York and Sacagawea both voted in the poll on the winter camp. The corps returned to St. Louis in September 1806, its maps redrawing the continent and its journals opening a fur trade. York came home enslaved, asked for his freedom, and was refused for years.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment choosing one label, explorers, diplomats, or surveyors for settlement, stated directly and held through the essay. Any of the three can earn full credit if the dependence on Native peoples and York's record are carried and the rival label is answered. An essay that narrates the journey in order without judging what the corps chiefly was has summarized the lesson rather than answered it. (Blueprint 7, Blueprint 8)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, drawn from at least two different parts of the record, the Greenville system and the orders, the first day's journal and the Mandan winter, the Shoshone council, or the crossing, the vote, and the return. An essay built on the mountains alone cannot explain who fed the corps, horsed it, or interpreted for it. (Blueprint 8)
  • The country stated as inhabited wherever the verdict leans toward exploration: Mandan and Hidatsa towns larger than St. Louis, Shoshone horses held by the people who sold them, and Nez Perce guides in the Bitterroots. A discovery essay that empties the country to enlarge the discovering has failed the record. (Blueprint 8)
  • York accounted for, not decorated with: he crossed the continent, hunted, scouted, and voted in November 1805, came home enslaved while the enlisted men received pay and land, asked for his freedom, and was refused for years. An essay that praises the expedition's democracy at the Fort Clatsop vote without the refusal that followed it has read half the record. (Blueprint 8)
  • The strongest rival label stated at full strength and answered. Against a surveyors verdict: the corps really did negotiate its way across a continent and fought once in two years, and its councils were real diplomacy between peoples. Against a diplomats verdict: the councils were also a census, ordered in the same document that ordered the friendly manner, for a government that followed the survey with trade and settlement. Against an explorers verdict: the geography was new to Washington and was home ground to every nation that fed, guided, and interpreted for the men who mapped it. (Blueprint 7, Blueprint 8)
  • The Greenville treaty given its work: the cession of most of Ohio in 1795 places the expedition inside a taking already under way, and an essay that begins with Jefferson's orders alone has accepted the expedition's framing of itself. (Blueprint 7)

Model opening for teacher reference. The Corps of Discovery should be remembered chiefly as surveyors for settlement who could only do their surveying by diplomacy. Jefferson's orders join the two halves in one document: find the water communication for commerce, count the nations and their lands, and treat those nations in the most friendly and conciliatory manner while counting them. The corps that carried out those orders wintered on Mandan and Hidatsa hospitality, crossed the mountains on Shoshone horses obtained through Sacagawea's recognition of her brother, and came home with maps that opened a fur trade, while York, who had voted beside the enlisted men at Fort Clatsop, came home enslaved and was refused the freedom he asked for.

Common weak answers. An explorers essay built on bank 3 and bank 6 alone cannot explain the four language interpreter chain, or why a treaty from 1795 belongs in the file at all.
