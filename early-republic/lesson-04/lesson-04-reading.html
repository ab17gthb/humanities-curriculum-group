Lewis and Clark and the Peoples of the West

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. An Errand of the Purchase

The Corps of Discovery was the Louisiana Purchase sent walking. President Jefferson's instructions to Meriwether Lewis of June 20, 1803 named the work plainly. Lewis was to explore the Missouri River and find the most direct and practicable water communication across the continent for the purposes of commerce. He was to fix the geography by observation, learn the names, numbers, lands, and trade of the Native nations along the line, and treat those nations in the most friendly and conciliatory manner his orders could require. The president wrote of the nations as peoples to be met and known, in the same years his government measured their country for its own uses. In May 1804 the corps set out from near St. Louis, some forty men, soldiers, interpreters, and York, an enslaved man held by William Clark.

B. A River Country Already Inhabited

The river country was not empty, and the corps depended on the peoples who held it. At the Mandan and Hidatsa villages on the upper Missouri, towns larger than St. Louis, farmers and traders fed the expedition and housed it through the winter of 1804 and 1805 at Fort Mandan, and its survival turned on that hospitality. On the lower river the corps had already met the Teton Sioux in a confrontation that stopped at the edge of violence, a reminder that passage was negotiated, not granted by the journey itself. All the while the captains kept the journals as an instrument of possession. Every river was named, every nation counted, every plant and animal described, for a government that meant to use the record.

C. The Winter Hire

That winter the corps engaged Toussaint Charbonneau, a French Canadian trader living among the Hidatsa. He was hired largely because of his young wife. Sacagawea was a Shoshone woman who had been taken captive by the Hidatsa as a girl and carried east, and she could interpret between her own people and the villages. She could also do what no soldier could do. A column traveling with a woman and an infant read to the nations it met as a party of peace rather than a party of war. Her son Jean Baptiste was born at Fort Mandan in February 1805, and mother and child crossed the continent and back with the corps.

D. The Mountains and the Kinship

The Missouri did not carry the corps to the sea. The portage around the Great Falls in present day Montana consumed about a month, and beyond the falls the party worked toward the divide short of food. In August 1805 Lewis met the Shoshone, Sacagawea's people, whose horses the corps had to have to cross the mountains at all. When Clark's party came up, Sacagawea was called in to interpret, and in the chief before her she recognized Cameahwait, her brother. Through a chain of interpreters the captains stated the objects that had brought them into that country, and the Shoshone agreed to the trade. The expedition's horses, and its passage west, were obtained in the space of that recognition.

E. The Crossing and the Winter Vote

Guided by the Shoshone and then fed and guided by the Nez Perce, the corps crossed the Bitterroot Mountains in snow, hungry, and descended the Clearwater and the Columbia to the Pacific. It wintered at Fort Clatsop in the rain near the river's mouth. There, in November 1805, the captains polled the party on where to build the winter camp, and the journals record the votes of York and of Sacagawea alongside the enlisted men's. It was a ballot taken in a nation where neither could vote, recorded in the expedition's own book without comment. The corps had crossed the continental divide, the high ground separating the rivers that flow toward the Atlantic from those that flow toward the Pacific, and it had found no single water route beyond it.

F. The Return and the Uses of the Record

The corps came back to St. Louis in September 1806. It had lost one man, Sergeant Charles Floyd, to illness early in the journey, and on the return its only killing of Native people came in a single violent encounter in which two Blackfeet died. Its maps redrew the continent's geography for the government that had bought a claim on paper, correcting the hope of an easy water passage with ranges of mountains. Its journals opened a fur trade that followed the corps up the Missouri within a few years. The Mandan villages that had housed and fed the expedition stood in the trade's path, and a generation later smallpox destroyed them.

G. The Treaty System Behind the Story

The expedition did not begin the taking of the West. It extended a system already a decade old. By the Treaty of Greenville of 1795, the Western Confederacy, defeated by General Anthony Wayne's army at Fallen Timbers the year before, ceded most of present day Ohio to the United States and accepted its protection, in a treaty whose first article promised that peace would be perpetual. Purchase, pressure, and cession lines were the instruments by which the republic had been moving west since the 1790s. Read whole, the journey holds two facts together. The expedition was a triumph of endurance and record keeping, carried out by men and by the peoples who fed, guided, and interpreted for them. It was also the first page of a dispossession, a survey of other peoples' countries by a republic that had already decided what the survey was for.

Lewis was to fix the geography by observation and to learn the names, numbers, lands, relations, and trade of the Native nations along the line. In all intercourse with those nations he was to treat them in the most friendly and conciliatory manner their own conduct would admit.
