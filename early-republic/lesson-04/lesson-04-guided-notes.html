Guided Notes: Lewis and Clark and the Peoples of the West

Lesson question: What did the Corps of Discovery actually find in the West, and whose country was it?

Unit question: How did the United States turn its Constitution into a working government, and what did continental expansion do to the republic that government served?

Key Terms

Corps of Discovery: the Army expedition of 1804 to 1806, commanded by Meriwether Lewis and William Clark, sent by President Jefferson to explore the Louisiana Purchase, find a water route toward the Pacific, and establish an American presence among the West's peoples

Meriwether Lewis: the Army captain and former secretary to President Jefferson who co-commanded the Corps of Discovery, kept its scientific record, and governed Louisiana Territory after the expedition's return

William Clark: the Army officer who co-commanded the Corps of Discovery with Lewis, drew its maps, and negotiated much of its diplomacy with Native nations, treating the expedition's enslaved member, York, as his property throughout

Sacagawea: the Shoshone woman who joined the Corps of Discovery as an interpreter with her husband in 1804, carried her infant son across the continent, and whose presence and kinship opened doors, including her own people's, that the expedition could not have opened alone

York: the enslaved man held by William Clark who crossed the continent with the Corps of Discovery, hunted, scouted, and voted in the expedition's decisions, and who asked for his freedom on the return and was refused for years

Shoshone: the Native nation of the northern Rockies and Great Basin, Sacagawea's people, whose horses the Corps of Discovery needed to cross the mountains in 1805 and obtained through the meeting between Sacagawea and her brother Cameahwait

continental divide: the high ground of the Rocky Mountains separating the rivers that flow toward the Atlantic from those that flow toward the Pacific, which the Corps of Discovery crossed in 1805 and found no single water route beyond

F. The return, the losses, and the maps: the corps came back to St. Louis in September 1806, having lost one man, Sergeant Charles Floyd, to illness early in the journey, and having killed two Blackfeet in a single violent encounter on the return, its only killing of Native people. Its maps redrew the continent's geography for the government that had bought a claim on paper, correcting the hope of an easy water passage with ranges of mountains. The trade opened, and the villages: its journals opened a fur trade that followed the corps up the Missouri within a few years, and the Mandan villages that had housed and fed the expedition stood in the trade's path; a generation later smallpox destroyed them.

G. Greenville, the cession, and the promise: by the Treaty of Greenville of 1795, the Western Confederacy, defeated by General Anthony Wayne's army at Fallen Timbers the year before, ceded most of present day Ohio to the United States and accepted its protection, in a treaty whose first article promised that peace between the two sides would be perpetual. The two facts held together: the expedition was a triumph of endurance and record keeping, and it was the first page of a dispossession, a survey of other peoples' countries by a republic that had already decided what the survey was for.

The Journey at a Glance. The orders and the river: Jefferson's instructions of June 20, 1803, the Missouri explored for a water communication for commerce, the corps out in May 1804, some forty men including York, and the Teton Sioux confrontation showing passage was negotiated. The peoples who carried it: the Mandan and Hidatsa villages feeding and housing the corps at Fort Mandan in the winter of 1804 and 1805, Charbonneau engaged for Sacagawea's interpreting, and her son Jean Baptiste born in February 1805. The mountains and the vote: the Great Falls portage, the Shoshone horses obtained through Sacagawea's recognition of Cameahwait in August 1805, the Bitterroots crossed in snow with Nez Perce help, and the Fort Clatsop vote of November 1805 in which York and Sacagawea both voted. The return and the treaty system: St. Louis reached in September 1806, one man lost to illness, two Blackfeet killed, the maps redrawing the continent, the fur trade opened, and the Treaty of Greenville of 1795 standing behind the whole story.
