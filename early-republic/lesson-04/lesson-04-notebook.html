The orders and the start, 1803 to 1804Jefferson's instructions of June 20, 1803 ordered the Missouri explored for the most direct and practicable water communication across the continent for commerce, the geography fixed by observation, and the nations on the line learned by name, number, lands, and trade. The corps set out in May 1804, some forty men, and Clark's first journal entry accounts for boats, men, miles, and weather.The country belonged to the Native nations the orders told Lewis to count. Passage had to be negotiated from the start, as the Teton Sioux confrontation on the lower river showed when it stopped at the edge of violence. York, enslaved by Clark, was in the party from the first day and appears in no roster by name.
The Mandan and Hidatsa winter, 1804 to 1805The corps built Fort Mandan and wintered beside the villages, engaging Charbonneau as an interpreter. Sacagawea's son Jean Baptiste was born there in February 1805, and the corps left in the spring rested, fed, and better informed than it had arrived.Mandan and Hidatsa country. The villages, towns larger than St. Louis, fed the expedition and housed it through the winter, and its survival turned on their hospitality. Sacagawea, a Shoshone woman held among the Hidatsa after her capture as a girl, joined here with her husband.
The mountains and the Shoshone, 1805The corps portaged the Great Falls for about a month, met the Shoshone in August 1805, stated its objects through a chain of interpreters, traded for horses, and crossed toward the Columbia with Shoshone guidance.Shoshone country, Sacagawea's own people's. Called in to interpret, she recognized the chief Cameahwait as her brother, and the expedition's horses and passage west were obtained in the space of that recognition. The holders of the crossing set the terms on which it was crossed.
The crossing, the vote, and the Pacific winter, 1805 to 1806The corps crossed the Bitterroots in snow, descended the Clearwater and the Columbia, and wintered at Fort Clatsop in the rain. In November 1805 the captains polled the party on the winter camp site.Nez Perce country on the crossing, Clatsop country at the river's mouth. The Nez Perce fed and guided the strangers through the mountains. At the vote, York and Sacagawea both voted alongside the enlisted men, recorded in the journals in a nation where neither could vote.
The return and what followed, 1806 and afterThe corps reached St. Louis in September 1806, having lost one man to illness and killed two Blackfeet in a single encounter on the return. Its maps redrew the continent, and its journals opened a fur trade up the Missouri within a few years.The Mandan villages stood in the trade's path, and smallpox destroyed them a generation later. Behind the whole journey stood the Treaty of Greenville of 1795, by which the Western Confederacy had ceded most of present day Ohio. York came home to slavery, asked for his freedom, and was refused for years.

The lesson in one paragraph. Strong answers name the found country as inhabited and the rewards as unevenly paid. Example: The corps found no single water route to the Pacific. It found a country held by Native nations, Mandan and Hidatsa towns that fed it through a winter, Shoshone horses obtained through Sacagawea's recognition of her brother Cameahwait, and Nez Perce guides in the mountains, in lands the United States had already begun taking by treaty at Greenville in 1795. Its maps and journals opened the fur trade that later reached the Mandan villages. York, who hunted, scouted, and voted with the corps, came home enslaved and was refused the freedom he asked for.
