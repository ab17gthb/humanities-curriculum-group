Quick Check: The Reach and Its Limits

Directions: Read the two passages and the background, then answer the questions.

Background. In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), Chief Justice Marshall wrote: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional. That the power to tax involves the power to destroy." Source: McCulloch v. Maryland, 1819, as carried in the audited source record (Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law). In his veto message of March 3, 1817, President Madison wrote of the Bonus Bill: "The legislative powers vested in Congress are specified and enumerated in the eighth section of the first article of the Constitution, and it does not appear that the power proposed to be exercised by the bill is among the enumerated powers." Source: Madison, Veto Message, March 3, 1817 (Richardson, Messages and Papers of the Presidents, vol. I). In the same years Congress chartered the Second Bank (1816) and passed the first protective tariff, Madison vetoed federal funding for roads and canals, and New York built the Erie Canal with state money, opening it in 1825.

1. Using Marshall's passage, state the test a claimed implied power must pass, in his own sequence of conditions.

2. Maryland taxed the Baltimore branch of the Second Bank. Using the last sentence of Marshall's passage, explain why the tax could not stand.

3. Using Madison's passage, state exactly where he says Congress's powers are listed, what he says about the roads and canals power there, and the remedy his argument leaves open.

4. A classmate says the nationalism of these years had no limits. Answer with the veto and the Erie Canal from the background, with dates, and state what kind of limit each one shows.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Marshall's test. The end must be legitimate and within the scope of the Constitution. The means must be appropriate and plainly adapted to that end. The means must not be prohibited, and must consist with the letter and spirit of the Constitution. A means meeting all four conditions is constitutional, which is how a bank, nowhere named in the document, stands as an implied power of Congress. (Blueprint 12)

2. Why the tax fell. The power to tax involves the power to destroy. If Maryland could tax the Bank's Baltimore branch at any rate it chose, it could set the rate high enough to destroy an institution Congress had created for the whole nation, and one government would hold the power to defeat the lawful measures of another that is supreme in its sphere. The tax therefore could not stand, whatever its amount in this case. (Blueprint 12)

3. Madison's ground, and the open remedy. The legislative powers vested in Congress are specified and enumerated in the eighth section of the first article of the Constitution. The power to construct roads and canals, the power the Bonus Bill proposed to exercise, does not appear among the enumerated powers, and in the full message Madison adds that it cannot be reached by any just interpretation of the necessary and proper power either. The remedy his argument leaves open is an amendment: a power not granted must be granted, by the mode the Constitution itself provides, however important the roads and canals are. (Blueprint 11)

4. The limits, in two kinds. The veto of March 3, 1817 shows a constitutional limit applied from inside the program's own party: Madison, who had signed the Bank and the tariff in 1816, refused the Bonus Bill because the roads and canals power was nowhere enumerated, and Monroe repeated the veto in 1822. The Erie Canal shows the practical consequence and the second kind of limit: the era's greatest internal improvement was built anyway, by New York State with state money, begun in 1817 and opened in 1825, which means the limit bound the federal government, not the building. Nationalism in these years was wide in banking, tariffs, and the Court's reading of the Constitution, and it stopped where its own presidents read the enumeration strictly. (Blueprint 11)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Question 1 is the diagnostic for the lesson's central doctrine: students who cannot reproduce the test's sequence should be sent back to Station 1 of the activity before the writing task. Question 4 predicts success on the writing task, because it requires the widening and the limit held in one answer.
