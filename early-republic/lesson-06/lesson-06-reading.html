The Era of Good Feelings: The American System and the Marshall Court

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Peace and the Mood It Bought

The war ended and the party that had opposed it collapsed. The Federalists, disgraced by the timing of the Hartford Convention, faded as a national party, and James Monroe, elected in 1816, toured the country in 1817 before crowds that crossed old party lines. A Boston newspaper named the moment the Era of Good Feelings. One party held the field, and the arguments of 1798 seemed buried. Under the calm, the war had taught hard lessons. The government had fought Britain with no national bank, with a currency issued by state banks, with troops delayed on poor roads and governors refusing their militia. After 1815, leaders who had once fought Federalist measures began building the machinery the war had shown missing.

B. The Program of 1816

In 1816 Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States for twenty years, a larger successor to Hamilton's Bank, to hold the government's money and bring order to the currency. The same session passed the Tariff of 1816, the first tariff in the nation's history laid avowedly to protect American manufactures rather than only to raise revenue. President James Madison signed both measures. Madison had argued against the first Bank in 1791 and had built his career on a careful reading of federal power. He signed in 1816 because the war had persuaded him that experience had answered the constitutional question and that the currency's disorder was a danger no theory could excuse. The Republican president was enacting, a generation late, the program the Federalists had first proposed.

C. Clay's American System

Henry Clay of Kentucky, Speaker of the House, gave the program its fullest statement and its name. His American System joined three parts. A national bank would give the country a uniform currency and orderly credit. A protective tariff would shelter American manufactures until they could stand against foreign competition. The revenue the tariff raised would pay for internal improvements, roads and canals built with federal money, binding the country into one market. Clay argued that each section would prosper by the others' demand. Western farmers would feed northern mill towns. Southern cotton would clothe workers in both. Northern manufactures would find buyers at home.

D. The Constitutional Brakes

The program's own friends applied its constitutional brakes. Calhoun's Bonus Bill of 1817 would have set the Bank's bonus and dividends aside as a permanent fund for roads and canals. Madison vetoed it on March 3, 1817, his last full day in office, holding that the Constitution gave Congress no power to build roads and canals and that the power would require an amendment, however much the roads were needed. Monroe vetoed a Cumberland Road bill on the same ground in 1822. The states built what the federal government would not. New York began the Erie Canal in 1817 and paid for it from its own treasury. Opened in 1825, it ran more than 360 miles from Albany to Lake Erie, the era's greatest improvement, built by a state while the constitutional argument stood unresolved.

E. The Panic of 1819

The Panic of 1819 broke the calm. Through the war years and after, buyers had taken up western land on credit at prices floated by high cotton prices, borrowing from state banks that printed notes freely. When cotton prices fell and the Second Bank, under new management in 1819, called in loans and demanded payment in specie to protect itself, the structure collapsed. State banks failed. Western farms were foreclosed, many of them by the national bank itself, and its debtors learned to curse the institution that held their mortgages. City workers lost employment, and state legislatures passed relief laws for debtors. A generation of westerners came out of the panic hearing the word bank as the name of a distant power that had taken their land.

F. McCulloch and the Nationalist Court

In the same years the Supreme Court under Chief Justice John Marshall wrote the nationalist reading of the Constitution into law. Maryland had taxed the Baltimore branch of the Second Bank, and in McCulloch v. Maryland, decided in 1819, the Court struck the tax down. Marshall held that Congress could charter the Bank under powers implied by the Constitution's great ends, because the document was, in his words, a constitution intended to endure for ages to come and to be adapted to the various crises of human affairs. A state could not tax an instrument of the whole nation, because the power to tax involves the power to destroy. In Dartmouth College v. Woodward, also in 1819, the Court protected private charters as contracts a state could not rewrite.

G. Gibbons and the Reach of Commerce

Gibbons v. Ogden, decided in 1824, carried the same reading to commerce. New York had granted a monopoly of steamboat navigation in its waters, held by Aaron Ogden for the route to New Jersey. Thomas Gibbons ran competing boats under a federal coasting license. Marshall held for Gibbons. Commerce, he wrote, is more than traffic; it is intercourse, and it includes navigation. Commerce among the states does not stop at a state's border, and Congress's power over it is complete within its sphere. The monopoly fell. Read against its record, the Era of Good Feelings was real at the top of politics, and the machinery built in those years endured. So did the bank fight, the panic's resentments, and a broad Constitution whose next arguments were already gathering.

3. Clay's system and its market. The three parts are a national bank, to give a uniform currency and orderly credit; a protective tariff, to shelter American manufactures until they could stand against foreign competition; and internal improvements, roads and canals funded from the tariff's revenue, to bind the country into one market. Clay's logic was reciprocal demand: western farmers would feed northern mill towns, southern cotton would clothe workers in both sections, and northern manufactures would find buyers at home. Each section would prosper by the others' demand, and an economic union built by law would hold the political union together. (Blueprint 11)

4. The veto, and who built instead. Calhoun's Bonus Bill would have set the Second Bank's bonus payment and its dividends aside as a permanent fund for roads and canals. Madison vetoed it on March 3, 1817, his last full day in office, holding that the Constitution gave Congress no power to build roads and canals. The remedy he named was an amendment: the power would have to be granted, however much the roads were needed. Monroe vetoed a Cumberland Road bill on the same ground in 1822. The Erie Canal was built by New York State, begun in 1817 and paid for from the state treasury, and it opened in 1825, running more than 360 miles from Albany to Lake Erie. (Blueprint 11)

5. The lesson question, answered. Americans let the national government build far, and then argued about the edges. The Panic of 1819 shows the limit of the calm: land bought on credit at cotton boom prices collapsed when prices fell and the Second Bank, under new management, called in loans and demanded specie; state banks failed, western farms were foreclosed, many by the national bank itself, and city workers lost employment, so the era's signature institution became, to a generation of westerners, the name of a distant power that had taken their land. Maryland (1819), Marshall held the Bank constitutional under implied powers, on a document he described as a constitution intended to endure for ages to come and to be adapted to the various crises of human affairs, and barred Maryland's tax because the power to tax involves the power to destroy. In Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), he defined commerce as intercourse, including navigation, held that commerce among the states does not stop at a state's border, and struck New York's steamboat monopoly where it collided with a federal license.
