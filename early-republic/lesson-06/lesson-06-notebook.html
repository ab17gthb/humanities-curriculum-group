Teacher Answer Key

PartWhat was built or decidedWhose reach grewLimit or cost
The Bank, 1816Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States for twenty years, larger than Hamilton's first Bank, and Madison signed the charter.Congress and the federal government, which gained a fiscal agent and an instrument for ordering a currency issued by hundreds of state banks.The charter ran twenty years only, and the Bank's own contraction in 1819 made it the West's most hated creditor.
The tariff, 1816The Tariff of 1816, the first tariff laid avowedly for protection, sheltering American manufactures and raising the revenue Clay's system counted on.Congress, setting the terms of the home market by national legislation rather than leaving them to foreign demand.Protection raised prices for buyers of imported goods, and the sections that bought more than they manufactured paid for a policy written for the mill towns.
Improvements and the vetoesCalhoun's Bonus Bill would have funded roads and canals from the Bank's bonus and dividends. Madison vetoed it on March 3, 1817, and Monroe vetoed on the same ground in 1822. New York built the Erie Canal with state money, opening it in 1825.The states, New York above all, which built the era's greatest improvement while the federal claim stood unresolved.The federal road stopped at the veto: Madison held the power was nowhere enumerated and would require an amendment, however needed the roads were.
The panic, 1819Land bought on credit at cotton boom prices collapsed when prices fell and the Second Bank called in loans and demanded specie. State banks failed and farms were foreclosed.No one's reach grew. The Bank survived and steadied the currency, but its public standing in the West was spent.The first great peacetime depression: western farmers ruined, city workers unemployed, and a generation taught to hear bank as an enemy's name.
McCulloch, 1819Marshall sustained the Bank under implied powers and struck Maryland's tax on its Baltimore branch, because the power to tax involves the power to destroy.Congress, whose means are constitutional when the end is legitimate and the means are plainly adapted to it, and the Court that announced the test.The states lost the power to tax federal instruments, and the test's breadth left later arguments to fight over what counts as plainly adapted.
Gibbons, 1824Marshall defined commerce as intercourse, including navigation, held that it does not stop at a state border, and struck New York's steamboat monopoly where it collided with a federal license.Congress, whose commerce power the opinion called complete in itself within its sphere, and carriers competing on the nation's waterways.The states lost the monopoly grants they had used to reward builders, and commerce completely internal to one state was the only ground the opinion reserved.

Built by law, widened by interpretation, stopped by veto, in one paragraph. Strong answers keep the three roads distinct. By statute, Congress built the machinery in 1816, chartering the Second Bank and laying the first protective tariff, with Madison signing measures he would once have opposed. By interpretation, the Marshall Court widened what the statutes could rest on: McCulloch in 1819 sustained the Bank under implied powers and barred a state tax on it, and Gibbons in 1824 read commerce as intercourse reaching past state lines. By veto, the third part of the program stopped: Madison's Bonus Bill veto of March 3, 1817 held that roads and canals required an amendment, and the era's greatest improvement, the Erie Canal, opened in 1825 as a work of New York State. The panic of 1819 charged the price: the Bank whose charter the Court sustained foreclosed the farms of the West, and the calm of the Era of Good Feelings never fully recovered its name.
