Guided Notes: The Era of Good Feelings: The American System and the Marshall Court

Lesson question: After the war, how far would Americans let their national government build?

Unit question: How did the United States turn its Constitution into a working government, and what did continental expansion do to the republic that government served?

Key Terms

American System: Henry Clay's program of a national bank, a protective tariff, and federally funded internal improvements, meant to bind the sections into one economy in which western food, southern cotton, and northern manufactures traded with each other

Second Bank of the United States: the national bank chartered in 1816 for twenty years, larger than Hamilton's first Bank and created because the war had exposed the disorder of a currency issued by hundreds of state banks

protective tariff: a tax on imported goods set high enough to shelter American manufacturers from foreign competition rather than only to raise revenue, first enacted as national policy in the Tariff of 1816

internal improvements: roads, canals, and harbors built to move goods and people inside the country, which nearly every president of the era agreed the nation needed and many held the Constitution gave Congress no power to fund

Panic of 1819: the financial collapse of 1819, the first great peacetime depression of the United States, in which the Second Bank's contraction of credit helped burst a land and cotton speculation, throwing western farmers and city workers into ruin

McCulloch v. Maryland: the Supreme Court decision of 1819 in which Chief Justice Marshall held that Congress could charter a bank under its implied powers and that Maryland could not tax it, because the power to tax is the power to destroy

Gibbons v. Ogden: the Supreme Court decision of 1824 in which Chief Justice Marshall read Congress's power to regulate commerce among the states broadly, striking down a state steamboat monopoly and opening the nation's waterways to competition

Summary table. The program of 1816: Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States for twenty years and passed the Tariff of 1816, the first avowedly protective tariff, and Madison signed both, enacting a generation late the program the Federalists had first proposed. Clay's design: the American System joined the bank, the tariff, and federally funded internal improvements into one home market in which western food, southern cotton, and northern manufactures traded with each other. The brakes and the states: Madison vetoed the Bonus Bill on March 3, 1817, holding that a roads and canals power required an amendment, Monroe vetoed on the same ground in 1822, and New York built the Erie Canal with state money, opening it in 1825. The panic: in 1819 land bought on credit collapsed when cotton prices fell and the Second Bank called in loans; state banks failed, farms were foreclosed, and city workers lost employment. The Court: McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) sustained the Bank under implied powers and barred a state tax on it, and Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) read commerce to include navigation and struck a state steamboat monopoly.

Exit ticket 1. The American System joined a national bank for a uniform currency and orderly credit, a protective tariff to shelter American manufactures, and federally funded internal improvements paid from the tariff's revenue, and Clay said the three would bind the sections into one market in which each section prospered by the others' demand.

Exit ticket 2. Widened: the charter of the Second Bank and the Tariff of 1816, or the Court's decisions in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) and Gibbons v. Ogden (1824). Stopped: Madison's veto of the Bonus Bill on March 3, 1817, or Monroe's Cumberland Road veto of 1822, after which New York built the Erie Canal as a state work, opening it in 1825.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that joins the building to its limits and uses two dates. The strongest answers run the program of 1816 (Bank and protective tariff, signed by the Madison who had opposed the first Bank), Clay's American System as its full statement, the Bonus Bill veto of 1817 and the state built Erie Canal as the boundary of federal building, the Panic of 1819 as the cost of the new machinery, and McCulloch (1819) and Gibbons (1824) as the Court widening by interpretation what Congress had built by law.
