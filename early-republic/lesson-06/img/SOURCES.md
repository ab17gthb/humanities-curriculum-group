## Quotation log

### McCulloch v. Maryland (audited bank record mcculloch-v-maryland; edition: Chief Justice John Marshall, "McCulloch v. Maryland," 1819, Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law, as the record's sourceNote names it)
- Quoted in the activity, Station 1: the expounding passage, the enduring constitution passage, and the means test passage as one continuous excerpt of the record's text with the record's own bracketed cuts shown as ellipses; and the power to tax passage entire as the record carries it. Quoted in the quick check background and the writing task evidence bank: the means test passage and the power to tax sentence.
- The reading and guided notes paraphrase the opinion and quote only the two short phrases the bank record itself carries, "intended to endure for ages to come, and consequently, to be adapted to the various crises of human affairs" and "the power to tax involves the power to destroy," presented inside the reading's own sentences as Marshall's words, as the record carries them.

### Gibbons v. Ogden (audited bank record gibbons-v-ogden-1824; edition: U.S. Supreme Court (Chief Justice John Marshall), "Gibbons v. Ogden," 1824, Justia, as the record's sourceNote names it)
- Quoted in the activity, Station 2: the intercourse passage entire as the record carries it; the among the several States passage entire; and the power to regulate passage entire. Quoted in the writing task evidence bank: the intercourse sentence and short passages of the reach and completeness holdings, as the record carries them.
- The reading paraphrases the holding (commerce includes navigation, commerce among the states does not stop at a state's border) without quotation marks around paraphrase, except for the single word intercourse, which is the record's word.

### Henry Clay, speech on the tariff, March 30 and 31, 1824 (named source, fetched during this build)
- Source used: U.S. House of Representatives, History, Art and Archives, "The 1824 American System Speech By Speaker Henry Clay of Kentucky," which quotes the speech as printed in the Annals of Congress and records that the address filled two legislative days and more than 40 pages of print in the Annals of Congress. The builder read the House history page in full during this build.
- Quoted in the activity, Station 3, exactly as the House history carries them: "The object of the bill under consideration is to create this home market, and to lay the foundations of a genuine American policy"; "Are we doomed to behold our industry languish and decay yet more and more?"; and "But there is a remedy, and that remedy consists in modifying our foreign policy, and in adopting a genuine AMERICAN SYSTEM." A fourth sentence quoted on the House page, on naturalizing the arts, continues with a punctuation mark this unit's documents do not use, so the quotation stops at the sentence before it rather than altering the text. Quoted in the writing task evidence bank: the home market sentence.
- This record is a verbatim quotation from a named source, not a teaching summary, and the activity's Source line says so.

### Madison, Veto Message, March 3, 1817 (named edition, fetched during this build)
- Edition used: James Madison, Veto Message, March 3, 1817, in Richardson, A Compilation of the Messages and Papers of the Presidents, vol. I, pp. 584-585, in the transcription read in full during this build. Founders Online, National Archives, was also read during this build: its Madison papers carry the same passages of the message (quoted in the editorial notes to Madison's later correspondence, from Madison, Writings, Hunt ed., vol. 8, p. 388), and its note to Madison to Monroe, December 27, 1817, confirms the bill's passage (House February 8, 1817; Senate February 28, 1817) and its veto on March 3, 1817. The task's instruction to quote the Founders Online transcription is recorded here with this qualification: the full veto message is not carried as a standalone document in the Founders Online Madison papers as searched during this build, so the quotations follow the Richardson edition transcription, with the Founders Online passages agreeing word for word in the passages both carry. The activity's Source line names the Richardson edition and the Founders Online agreement, in that order, so a teacher is not told the class text came from a page it did not come from.
- Quoted in the activity, Station 4: the opening passage entire; the enumerated powers passage, closing cut marked by ellipses; and the great importance passage, with its middle cut and closing cut marked by ellipses. Quoted in the quick check background and the writing task evidence bank: the enumerated powers passage's first two clauses.

### Summary and teaching records, labeled on their faces
- Activity Station 5 (the Panic of 1819) is a teaching summary written for this lesson from the public record, labeled as a summary on its face. No sentence in it is presented as a period quotation. Its facts: high wartime and postwar cotton prices, western land bought on credit, state bank note issues, the Second Bank's early lending under its first president, William Jones, the fall in cotton prices in 1818 and 1819, the contraction under Jones and Langdon Cheves (loans called in, specie demanded, state banks pressed to redeem), bank failures, foreclosures across the West, urban unemployment, state relief laws, and the Bank's survival with its western standing spent. Item 6 of the writing task evidence bank restates the same summary and labels it in summary, as the bank's headnote requires.

## Figures stated with their precision
- Second Bank charter: twenty years, 1816, from the unit vocabulary and the bank records. No capital figure is stated in the student documents.
- Erie Canal: begun by New York in 1817, opened in 1825, more than 360 miles from Albany to Lake Erie. Contemporary accounts read during this build give 362 miles and modern references 363 miles, so the documents state the distance as more than 360 miles rather than choosing between them. Cost figures and freight rate figures are stated in the speaker notes only as orders of magnitude (the canal's cost in the millions on state credit; the Buffalo to New York freight rate falling to near a tenth of its former level), labeled there as what the standard accounts put them at, and are not stated in the student documents.
- Bonus Bill: passed the House on February 8, 1817 and the Senate on February 28, 1817, and vetoed on March 3, 1817, per the Founders Online editorial note read during this build. The student documents state the veto date only.
- Clay's speech: delivered over two legislative days, March 30 and 31, 1824, per the U.S. House of Representatives, History, Art and Archives.

## Gaps recorded honestly
- No portrait of Henry Clay was used. Candidate Commons files for the Matthew Harris Jouett portrait of 1818 could not be verified under those filenames during this build, and the National Portrait Gallery record for the Jouett portrait states that usage conditions apply, so Clay appears in text and in his own quoted words, without a portrait. No image is captioned as Clay anywhere in this lesson.
- No Erie Canal print was used. Candidate period prints (the Lockport views and the canal opening scenes) could not be verified as public domain files under the names tried during this build, so Sections D and the canal content use text and stat slides rather than an unverified image.
- No image of the Second Bank building was used. A photograph downloaded under a Commons filename during this build carried a 2009 copyright notice in its embedded metadata and was deleted from the working set unused; no period print of the building (the Burton engraving held by the Library of Congress) could be verified as a downloadable public domain file during this build, so the Bank is taught in text. The downloaded photograph was not copied into any lesson folder.
- No portrait of James Monroe and no steamboat image was used, for the same reason: no file could be verified public domain during this build. The deck carries one portrait, Marshall, and otherwise uses the text and stat slide forms the deck system provides for subjects without a verified image.
