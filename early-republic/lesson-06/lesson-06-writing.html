Writing Task: Hamilton's Program Fulfilled, or a New Thing?

The prompt. Between 1816 and 1824 the United States chartered a national bank, laid a protective tariff, argued over federally funded roads and canals, and watched its Supreme Court read federal power broadly. Answer this question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Was the nationalism of 1816 to 1824 Hamilton's program fulfilled, or a new thing built from the war's lessons? Argue from the documents in the Nationalism File. A strong answer names who built the program and why they said they built it, weighs the widest widening of federal power against the veto that stopped the building, and ends by explaining what the Panic of 1819 did to the program's standing. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each one supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson documents. Write the history in your own sentences. Quotations from the records belong inside quotation marks, spelled as the records spell them.

Evidence Bank

1. In 1791 Alexander Hamilton defended the first Bank of the United States under Congress's implied powers, against the strict reading of Thomas Jefferson and James Madison. In 1816 Madison, as president, signed the charter of the Second Bank of the United States and the Tariff of 1816, the first avowedly protective tariff, after a war fought with no national bank and a currency issued by hundreds of state banks.

2. Henry Clay, speech on the tariff, March 30 and 31, 1824: "The object of the bill under consideration is to create this home market, and to lay the foundations of a genuine American policy." Clay's American System joined the bank, the protective tariff, and federally funded internal improvements, so that western food, southern cotton, and northern manufactures traded with each other.

3. McCulloch v. Maryland, 1819: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional." The same opinion held that Maryland could not tax the Bank, because "the power to tax involves the power to destroy."

4. Gibbons v. Ogden, 1824: "Commerce, undoubtedly, is traffic, but it is something more: it is intercourse." Marshall held that commerce among the states cannot stop at the external boundary line of each state, and that Congress's power to regulate it is complete in itself within the limits of the Constitution. New York's steamboat monopoly fell.

5. Madison, veto of the Bonus Bill, March 3, 1817: "The legislative powers vested in Congress are specified and enumerated in the eighth section of the first article of the Constitution, and it does not appear that the power proposed to be exercised by the bill is among the enumerated powers." Madison granted the roads' importance and withheld his signature anyway. New York then built the Erie Canal with state money, opening it in 1825.

6. The Panic of 1819, in summary: land bought on credit at cotton boom prices collapsed when prices fell and the Second Bank called in loans and demanded specie. State banks failed, the Bank foreclosed farms across the West, city workers lost employment, and a generation of westerners learned to hear the word bank as an enemy's name.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that chooses one of the two verdicts, or a stated combination, and rests it on who built the program and the reasons they gave, rather than on the era's reputation for good feelings. (Blueprint 11)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Item 1 establishes the fulfilled case in its sharpest form: Hamilton's implied powers argument of 1791, signed into a charter by Madison in 1816. (Blueprint 11) Item 2 supplies the new thing case in the builders' own terms: Clay's home market logic answers an economy of sections trading with each other, a problem stated in 1824, not 1791. (Blueprint 11) Items 3 and 4 establish the judicial widening: Marshall's means test and supremacy holding in McCulloch (1819), and the commerce definition of Gibbons (1824), which reached a steamboat economy Hamilton never addressed. (Blueprint 12) Item 5 establishes the limit from inside the building party: the Bonus Bill veto of 1817 and the state built Erie Canal of 1825. Item 6 establishes the cost. (Blueprint 11)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive distinction is between instruments and reasons: an essay that calls the era simply Hamiltonian, without noting that its builders were Hamilton's opponents acting on a war's lessons, has used half the file.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest rival verdict. For fulfilled: that the instruments were old but the builders, the stated reasons, and the stopping point were new, answered by asking why Madison needed a war to sign. For new thing: that a program is known by its instruments, and bank, tariff, and implied powers are Hamilton's list, answered by noting that the list was completed by the men who had spent a generation opposing it, for reasons they stated on the record.

Model opening for teacher reference. The nationalism of 1816 to 1824 was a new thing built with Hamilton's tools. The tools were his: a national bank defended by implied powers, a tariff that did more than raise revenue, and a Constitution read for its great ends, the reading Marshall wrote into law in McCulloch v. Maryland in 1819. The builders were not his. Madison signed the Second Bank's charter in 1816 after opposing the first Bank for twenty five years, and he signed it because a war fought without a bank, on a currency of state bank notes and roads that delayed armies, had taught him what theory had not. A full model continues in this pattern, weighs Clay's home market speech of 1824 and Gibbons as the new economy's doctrine, sets the Bonus Bill veto of 1817 against them as the limit the builders imposed on themselves, answers the fulfilled verdict directly, and closes on the Panic of 1819, in which the program's first instrument collected its debts from the West and spent the era's good feelings doing it.

Common weak answers. Listing the bank, the tariff, and the two Court decisions as a Hamiltonian triumph, without Madison's 1791 opposition or his stated reasons in 1816, argues from the instrument list and misses the lesson's question. Treating the Bonus Bill veto as a Federalist survival reverses the record: the veto came from the program's own president, on the program's own constitutional scruples. Quoting Clay's home market sentence as if it described an economy already built confuses a speech for a result; the Erie Canal's state funding, item 5, is the corrective evidence.
