# Early Republic and Expansion — Unit Manifest

**Essential question:** How did the United States turn its Constitution into a working government, and what did continental expansion do to the republic that government served?

**Course position:** The third unit of the US History course (courseId u3), built twelfth in the build order. It opens with Washington's administration in 1789, exactly where the Founding unit closed (the government constituted and the Bill of Rights ratified in December 1791; the Farewell Address and the Alien and Sedition Acts, named in that unit only as a closing pointer, are this unit's own ground, the Farewell in Lesson 1 and the acts in Lesson 2), and closes at the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in February 1848 with the question of slavery in the Mexican cession forced onto the table and deliberately left open there: the Jackson and Reform unit opens with the election of 1828, the Missouri Compromise is taught here as the early republic's settlement and warning, and the Compromise of 1850 belongs to the Sectional Crisis unit. The republic is taught with its peoples in the frame throughout, Native nations negotiating and resisting on the record, enslaved Americans including York acting and petitioning on the record, Sacagawea's kinship deciding an expedition's passage, and editors and immigrants in the 1790s learning what the new government's promises were worth to them.

**Built:** 2026-10-22. 51 documents, 247 printed pages. 8 slide decks, 216 slides, 31,705 words of speaker notes, 8 printable teacher notes documents (77 pages).

**Topics (6):** 1 Inventing a government: precedent, finance, and the first parties · 2 The republic tested: dissent, succession, and the courts · 3 A continental republic: purchase, exploration, and the peoples of the West · 4 The second war and the nationalist turn · 5 The Marshall Court and the national economy · 6 Expansion and the question deferred: Monroe, Missouri, and Mexico.

**Test blueprint:** 15 entries, two per lesson except Lesson 8 (one). Entry-to-topic map and per-lesson item assignments are in the skeleton: ~/workspace/curriculum-planner/early-republic-skeleton.md.

**TPSP source plan:** The bank HAS an early republic era file: ~/workspace/primary-source/vite-react-main/public/data/early-republic.json, era "The Early Republic (1800-1828)", 20 audited records, and this unit quotes from it wherever it carries the text. Two further audited records the Founding build deliberately left to this unit live in the founding era file: the Farewell Address (washington-farewell-address) and Whitney's cotton gin patent of 1794 (whitney-cotton-gin-patent-1794); two more that Lesson 8 quotes, O'Sullivan's annexation essay and the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, are filed in the Jackson and reform era file (jackson-reform.json). The premium catalog was checked directly in Firestore (collection psp_premium) for this build on 2026-10-22, because the Colonial build's skeleton had wrongly reported its era's catalog. NO premium DBQ sits wholly on this unit's ground. Two straddle into it, both verified with every docId located in a bank era file: DBQ 9, "Was Westward Expansion a Story of Opportunity or Conquest?" (document dbq9-westward-expansion, catalog code DBQ184, era "Westward Expansion"), 7 documents, of which O'Sullivan's annexation essay and the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo are this unit's Lesson 8 records and the remaining five (Homestead Act, American Progress, Turner, the Dawes Act, Chief Joseph) belong to later units; and DBQ 7, "Did the Constitution Create a Democratic Government?" (dbq7-constitution-democratic, DBQ182, era "Founding and Early Republic"), 7 documents, five in the founding era file plus the Farewell Address (founding era file) and the Missouri Compromise (missouri-compromise-1820, early republic era file). When the planner integration is built, the picker leads with DBQ 9 (DBQ184), whose question is Lesson 8's question, with DBQ 7 (DBQ182) alongside it. This unit's quotations are drawn from the bank's verified records wherever the bank carries the text, and otherwise from the named editions in each lesson's img/SOURCES.md.

**Copyright accounting:** No copyrighted work is quoted anywhere in this unit. Every quotation is a public domain period text quoted from a named edition: the audited bank records, Founders Online, the Avalon Project at Yale, the Library of Congress transcriptions, the 1819 National Register printing of the Tallmadge Amendment, the Annals of Congress text of Clay's 1824 speech as carried by the House of Representatives' History, Art and Archives page, and Richardson's Messages and Papers of the Presidents for the Bonus Bill veto. Where an edition modernizes spelling or punctuation, the Source line says so, following the bank records' own practice. Where no verbatim text from a named edition could be verified, the document carries a labeled teaching summary instead, labeled on its face: the Lyon prosecution (Lesson 2), the Barbary War (Lesson 3), York's record (Lesson 4), Tecumseh's address (Lesson 5), the Panic of 1819 (Lesson 6), and Adams's cabinet argument (Lesson 7).

## Lesson 1 — Washington's Republic: Hamilton, Parties, and the Farewell

- Reading: 893 words, 4 pages. Seven sections matching the skeleton's scope: the invention problem and the cabinet, Hamilton's four measures as one design, the opposition and the forming parties, the excise answered on the frontier, the cotton gin entering the story, the foreign storm and neutrality, and the Farewell.
- Guided notes: 5 pages. Activity: 9 pages, five stations, all audited bank records. Notebook page: 4 pages. Quick check: 3 pages, tagged to blueprint entries 1 and 2. Writing task: 5 pages.
- Deck: 25 slides, speaker notes 121 to 150 words a slide (the tightest band in the unit; every slide inside the 120 to 190 target), giant stat slides (20 years; 13,000; and an 1789 stat slide), teacher notes document 9 pages. Images: the Lansdowne portrait of Washington (National Portrait Gallery; the bank's audited visual record), the cotton gin patent drawing of 1794 in its 1845 restored copy (National Archives), Trumbull's Hamilton of 1806, Stuart's Jay of 1794 (National Gallery of Art), Peale's Jefferson of 1800, and Kemmelmeyer's Washington Reviewing the Western Army at Fort Cumberland, after 1795 (Metropolitan Museum Open Access), the period image of the Whiskey Rebellion's suppression, used as the deck's title image.
- Sources: Hamilton's bank opinion, the Neutrality Proclamation, the Jay Treaty, and the Farewell Address, quoted verbatim from the audited bank records with their named editions, cuts marked and disclosed. The cotton gin patent is a drawing, not a text: its station quotes the bank record's description of the drawing and is labeled as a description on its face.
- Honest flags: Wikimedia Commons file pages returned 403 to the automated reader during this build, so for the Lansdowne portrait the public domain basis recorded in SOURCES.md rests on the painting's 1796 date and authorship as carried in the audited bank record rather than a live read of the Commons file page; that limitation is disclosed in SOURCES.md itself. The Met image was verified directly through the Met Open Access API, and the remaining portraits are works of 1794 to 1806 by artists who died in the 1800s. The Farewell Address is quoted under the bank record's own disclosure that its excerpt modernizes for classroom use. The activity runs 9 pages against the Founding model's 7 because the five verbatim station texts are longer and two stations carry figures; no student page dies with dead space beyond the deliberate writing spaces.
