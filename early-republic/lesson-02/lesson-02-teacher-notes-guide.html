Teacher Notes

Lesson 2 · The Alien and Sedition Acts and the Revolution of 1800

A guide sheet for teaching the deck, in slide order. The slides carry the main ideas in a few sentences each; the teaching is here, in the form you can use while the slide is on the screen. Talking points are the content of the slide in bullets, with the votes and figures pulled out on their own lines so you can find and say them. Picture names what an image shows and what to point at. Ask is a question worth posing aloud, with the shape of a good answer. Note is enrichment: detail beyond the student reading, for the questions students ask you. Bracketed lines are delivery cues. Vocabulary definitions match the student guided notes word for word.

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image, Congressional Pugilists, anonymous American print, Philadelphia, 1798. Metropolitan Museum of Art.

Picture: Congressional Pugilists, an anonymous American print published at Philadelphia in 1798, showing Representative Roger Griswold attacking Representative Matthew Lyon with a cane on the floor of Congress while Lyon defends himself with fireplace tongs.

  • Lyon is the congressman this lesson follows into a jail cell, so introduce him here, in February 1798, months before his prosecution.
  • Place the lesson in the unit. Lesson 1 closed with Washington's Farewell Address warning against parties and foreign entanglements. Within two years the country had both at full strength: an undeclared naval war with France, a party fight carried into statute, an opposition answering from the state legislatures, and an election that deadlocked the House of Representatives for a February week.
  • Students should leave able to argue both sides of the Sedition Act and able to say exactly how the transfer of 1801 was completed.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: When the government called its critics traitors, who answered, and what did the election of 1800 prove? Unit question: How did the United States turn its Constitution into a working government, and what did continental expansion do to the republic that government served?

  • Today's question has two halves, and students will need both for the exit ticket and the writing task. First, who answered when the government treated criticism as treason?
  • The answers came in layers: state legislatures speaking through resolutions their authors would not sign in public, a congressman campaigning from a jail cell, newspaper editors, and finally the voters themselves.
  • Second, what did the election of 1800 prove? It proved the hardest thing a young republic can prove, that a ruling party will count the ballots that remove it and go home.
  • Preview the caution built into the lesson: the states' constitutional answer, the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, failed in its own season, rejected by ten states and joined by none. The answer that worked was slower, electoral, and nearly broke down in the House before it held.

  • Section D covers the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions and the doctrine of nullification. Section E covers the electoral tie of 1800 and the House deadlock. Slide 4 · Section A: A War Scare and a Party Fight (divider)

On the slide: A War Scare and a Party Fight · France demands a bribe, ships fight without a declaration, and each party calls the other a traitor.

  • Set the scene as a foreign crisis arriving inside a domestic party fight, because neither half explains 1798 alone.
  • The French Revolution had become a general European war, France against Britain, and American opinion had sorted itself by party: Federalists leaned toward Britain and order, Democratic-Republicans toward France and popular rights.
  • President Adams had sent envoys to Paris to settle French seizures of American shipping, hoping negotiation would keep the young republic out of the war. In the spring of 1798 he published their dispatches, and the country read that French agents had demanded money before talks could even begin.
  • The rage that followed was genuine and national, and the Federalist majorities in Congress converted it, within months, into armies, taxes, and the four laws of Section B.
  • Teach students to hold both facts: the danger was real, and the use made of the danger was partisan.

Slide 5 · The XYZ Affair, 1798

On the slide: Adams sends envoys to negotiate with France · French agents, named X, Y, and Z, demand a bribe and a loan · The published dispatches enrage the country · An undeclared naval war begins in the Caribbean

Picture: Gilbert Stuart's portrait of John Adams, painted about 1800 to 1815 and held by the National Gallery of Art.

  • Walk the affair in order. The envoys, Charles Cotesworth Pinckney, John Marshall, and Elbridge Gerry, reached Paris in 1797 and were met not by Foreign Minister Talleyrand but by intermediaries who demanded a large loan to France and a personal bribe before negotiations could open.
  • The envoys refused and reported home. Adams printed the dispatches with the agents' names replaced by the letters X, Y, and Z, which gave the scandal its name.
  • Publication was itself a political act: the documents made France look corrupt and grasping, rallied the country behind the Federalist administration, and put Jefferson's party, with its French sympathies, on the defensive in an election season.
  • American and French warships were soon fighting in the Caribbean without any declaration of war.

Slide 6 · The war scare, read through party

On the slide: Federalists: France is revolution and disorder · Democratic-Republicans: France is the republic's old ally · Irish and French immigrants vote overwhelmingly Republican · Each party reads the other as a foreign agent at home

  • The war scare frightened the Federalists twice over, once about France and once about the electorate.
  • Immigrants arriving in the 1790s, Irish fleeing British rule and French fleeing the revolution's turns, settled in American ports and voted, once citizens, overwhelmingly for Jefferson's Democratic-Republicans. Federalist newspapers described the pattern as a foreign vote being imported, and Federalist congressmen described Republican editors, several of them immigrants, as French agents in print.
  • The Republican answer named the Federalists British agents in return, bought by London banks and trade.
  • Teach the vocabulary of the season carefully: in 1798 treason was a word each party threw at the other weekly, in newspapers their readers trusted completely.
  • That atmosphere is the soil of the Sedition Act. A government that believes its critics are the advance guard of an invader will write laws a calmer government would be ashamed of, and it will find juries to enforce them.

Slide 7 · Section B: Four Laws of 1798 (divider)

On the slide: Four Laws of 1798 · The Lyon and Griswold print again: the party fight in Congress, in the season the laws passed.

Picture: the center of the 1798 Congressional Pugilists print, cropped for this divider, Griswold's cane against Lyon's fireplace tongs on the floor of Congress Hall in February 1798.

  • Keep the print in view while the laws are introduced, because it shows the temper in which they were written: the majority and the opposition had already come to blows in the chamber months before the Sedition Act passed.
  • Section B reads the four laws of the summer of 1798 as one program with one logic. The Naturalization Act attacked the opposition's future voters. The two Alien Acts armed the president against immigrants in peace and in war. The Sedition Act attacked the opposition's present voice, its newspapers.
  • Federalists defended the package as war preparation, and parts of it, the Alien Enemies Act above all, followed the ordinary practice of nations at war.
  • Students should learn the four laws separately, with dates and penalties, and then judge the package as a whole in the activity.

Slide 8 · Four laws, one program

On the slide: Naturalization Act: the wait for citizenship, five years to fourteen · Alien Friends Act: the president may remove immigrants he judges dangerous · Alien Enemies Act: enemy subjects may be arrested or removed in a declared war · Sedition Act: false, scandalous, or malicious writing against the government made a crime

  • Take the laws one at a time, as the reading does.
  • The Naturalization Act of June 1798 stretched the residence required for citizenship from five years to fourteen, which postponed the immigrant vote by nearly a decade in one stroke.
  • The Alien Friends Act let the president order out of the country any alien he judged dangerous, on his own finding, with no trial specified. The Alien Enemies Act, the most conventional of the four, applied only after a declared war or invasion and a public proclamation, and reached male enemy subjects of fourteen and upward who were not naturalized.
  • The Sedition Act of July 1798 made it a federal crime to write, print, utter, or publish false, scandalous, or malicious matter against the government, Congress, or the president, with a fine of up to two thousand dollars and up to two years in prison.
  • Note for students what the act protected and what it omitted: the vice president, Thomas Jefferson, leader of the opposition, was not named in it.

Slide 9 · 14 years to citizenship

On the slide: 14 — years an immigrant now waited for citizenship · Up from five years under the old law · The wait decided when an immigrant could vote · Jefferson's Congress restored five years in 1802

  • Hold this number up as the quiet half of the 1798 program, because the Sedition Act's noise has covered it ever since.
  • Naturalization is where immigration policy becomes election policy: a resident who cannot become a citizen cannot vote, and a fourteen-year wait meant that the immigrant arrivals of the 1790s, who leaned heavily Republican, would miss election after election of the new century.
  • Federalists said a long apprenticeship in republican habits made safer citizens, and fourteen years had precedents in the state laws of the Confederation era.
  • Republicans answered that the timing gave the game away, a law passed in a war scare, by the party the immigrants voted against, in the season that party also criminalized criticism of itself.
  • The repeal tells students how the winners judged it: in 1802 the Republican Congress restored the five-year wait, where it has essentially remained.

Slide 10 · Section C: The Sedition Act in Court (divider)

On the slide: The Sedition Act in Court · Some ten convictions, nearly all Republicans, and a congressman reelected from his cell.

  • Laws argue in the abstract, and prosecutions argue in particulars, so Section C teaches the Sedition Act by its cases.
  • About twenty-five people were arrested under the act and some ten convicted, a small number that students should weigh in both directions: small enough that defenders called the act restrained, and pointed enough that every name on the list belonged to the opposition.
  • The defendants were Republican newspaper editors, the men who ran the only national voice Jefferson's party had, and one sitting congressman, Matthew Lyon of Vermont. No Federalist editor was prosecuted for the savage attacks Federalist papers printed on Jefferson in the same years.
  • The section closes with the season's second use of institutional power, the Judiciary Act of 1801, by which the outgoing Federalist Congress created new judgeships and President Adams filled them to his last night in office, setting the problem the next lesson inherits in Marbury v. Madison.

Slide 11 · A congressman in a cell

On the slide: Matthew Lyon of Vermont, jailed for criticizing Adams · A fine of one thousand dollars and four months in jail · Reelected by Vermont voters from his cell, December 1798 · His supporters pay his fine, and the House seats him

Picture: the Congressional Pugilists print cropped to Lyon, who appears in the activity as the subject of a labeled teaching summary built from the public record.

  • Give the case its documented outline. Lyon, an Irish immigrant, a veteran, and the most combative man in the House, was indicted in Vermont for a letter criticizing President Adams and for words of his read aloud by others.
  • A federal court convicted him in October 1798, fined him one thousand dollars, and jailed him for four months at Vergennes.
  • He conducted his reelection campaign from the cell, writing letters his allies printed, and Vermont's voters returned him to Congress in December while he was still serving the sentence. His supporters paid the fine.
  • Teach the political arithmetic carefully: the prosecution meant to remove a voice and instead manufactured a martyr, a campaign story, and proof, repeated in every Republican paper, that the act was a party weapon. The activity asks students to judge exactly that question from the documents.

Slide 12 · Safeguards on paper, and the last use of the season

On the slide: Truth allowed as a defense, and juries judging law and fact · In practice the convictions fall on one party only · The act expires by its own terms on March 3, 1801 · The Judiciary Act of 1801: new judgeships filled to the last night

  • Give the Federalist defense its full strength before judging it, because students will meet this pattern again.
  • The Sedition Act was, on paper, more protective than the English common law of seditious libel it replaced: truth could be pleaded as a defense, and the jury was expressly given the right to judge both the law and the fact of the case.
  • Its authors set it to expire on March 3, 1801, the last day of Adams's term, which they cited as proof it was a war measure and not a permanent gag.
  • The practice is the reply. Editors were convicted for opinion the juries of a Federalist district disliked, and the protected list in the statute named the president and Congress while leaving the vice president exposed.
  • Then, in the administration's final weeks, the Federalist Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1801 creating new circuit judgeships, and Adams signed commissions to his last evening, the midnight judges whose undelivered paperwork opens the next lesson.

Slide 13 · Section D: Virginia and Kentucky Answer (divider)

On the slide: Virginia and Kentucky Answer · Madison, painted by John Vanderlyn, who wrote Virginia's answer in secret.

Picture: John Vanderlyn's portrait of James Madison, held in the White House collection.

  • Madison's portrait belongs on this divider because the answer of Section D was his and Jefferson's, and both men hid their authorship: Jefferson was vice president of the government he was answering, and Madison was its former congressional leader, so the resolutions went to Kentucky and Virginia as the work of state legislatures, not of the opposition's chiefs.
  • Section D teaches the compact theory at the center of both documents: the states created the federal government by an agreement among themselves, delegated definite powers, and kept the right to judge when the agreement was broken.
  • Virginia's verb was interpose. Kentucky's second set of resolutions, in 1799, supplied the stronger noun, nullification.
  • Teach the doctrine's 1799 reception as part of the doctrine's meaning: ten states formally rejected the resolutions, and not one state joined the two that passed them.

Slide 14 · Compact, interposition, nullification

On the slide: The Constitution as a compact among the states · The federal government holds delegated powers only · Virginia: the states are duty bound to interpose · Kentucky, 1799: nullification named as the rightful remedy

  • Work the argument as a chain, because students will quote it in the quick check and writing task.
  • First, origin: the Constitution is a compact, an agreement, and its parties are the states. Second, grant: the agreement delegated certain definite powers to a general government and reserved everything else to the states.
  • Third, judgment: the government the compact created cannot be the final judge of its own powers, since that would make its discretion, not the Constitution, the measure of them, so each party judges infractions for itself.
  • Fourth, remedy: Madison's Virginia Resolutions say the states are in duty bound to interpose, to step between the federal government and their citizens, against a deliberate, palpable, and dangerous exercise of undelegated power.
  • Jefferson's Kentucky draft and Kentucky's 1799 resolutions go the final step and name it: nullification, a state's declaration that the act is void within its borders.
  • Underline the limit in Jefferson's own framing: the claim reaches undelegated powers, powers never given, not any law a state happens to dislike.

Slide 15 · 10 states answered back rejecting the resolutions

On the slide: 10 — states answered back rejecting the resolutions · And no state joined Virginia and Kentucky · The doctrine's authors would not sign it in public · The argument lost in the legislatures of 1799

  • This statistic is the lesson's built-in correction to any romantic reading of the resolutions, and students should write it beside the doctrine in their notes.
  • Virginia and Kentucky asked their fellow states to declare the Alien and Sedition Acts unconstitutional and to stand with them. The other states answered through the winter and spring of 1799, and the answers were refusals: ten states sent resolutions rejecting the compact claim, several of them stating plainly that the federal courts, not state legislatures, judged the constitutionality of federal laws.
  • Not one state joined the protest.
  • Two of the refusals carry an irony worth a full stop in class: the idea that the states could judge federal law was itself judged, by the states, and lost.
  • The doctrine survived anyway, carried forward by its authors' party, and it returned in later decades in hands its 1798 authors did not control, which is why the guided notes definition ends by looking forward.

Slide 16 · Section E: The Deadlock of 1800 (divider)

On the slide: The Deadlock of 1800 · Seventy three votes for Jefferson, seventy three for Burr, and a House that could not move.

  • Section E teaches the election of 1800 as two elections, a victory and a breakdown, in that order.
  • The victory was clear: Jefferson's Democratic-Republicans beat John Adams and took Congress, the verdict the Sedition Act prosecutions had tried to prevent.
  • The breakdown followed from the Constitution's original machinery, under which each elector cast two undifferentiated votes and the runner up became vice president. Every Republican elector voted for both Jefferson and Aaron Burr, as the party intended, and the two men therefore tied at seventy-three votes at the head of the poll, which threw the choice between them into the outgoing House of Representatives, voting by state delegations, where Federalists still held the majority.
  • There the breakdown became a crisis, through thirty-five ballots in February 1801, with rumors of Burr bargains, of Federalist plans to install an officer of their own, and of Republican governors preparing militia.
  • Hold the tension honestly: for a week the republic did not know who its next president was.

Slide 17 · How a victory became a tie

On the slide: Every Republican elector votes for Jefferson and Burr · The Constitution has one ballot, and no separate vote for vice president · Seventy three and seventy three at the head of the poll · The choice falls to the Federalist held House, voting by states

[Explain the mechanical point slowly. The whole crisis is a machine failure before it is a drama.]

  • Under the Constitution as written, an elector voted for two persons without designating which vote was for president and which for vice president; the highest total became president and the second became vice president.
  • Parties, which the framers had not planned for, broke the machine: a party's electors, voting a straight ticket, produced an exact tie between the party's intended president and its intended vice president.
  • Democratic-Republican electors all voted Jefferson and Burr, seventy-three votes each, one elector having failed to withhold a second vote as some earlier tickets had arranged.
  • Adams finished behind them, so the old president was beaten, but the Constitution sent the tie to the House, and the House that received it was the lame-duck Federalist House the voters had just repudiated, voting not by members but by state delegations, nine states needed to choose.

Slide 18 · 36 ballots in the House

On the slide: 36 — ballots in the House before Jefferson was chosen · Thirty five deadlocked ballots in February 1801 · Hamilton argued Jefferson was the lesser danger · Burr never withdrew, and never won

  • Thirty-six ballots across the week of February 11 to 17, 1801, and the first thirty-five changed almost nothing: Jefferson held eight states, one short of the nine required, Burr held six, and two sat divided and silent.
  • The movement, when it came, ran through the Federalists' own divisions. Alexander Hamilton, who despised Jefferson's politics, spent the winter writing to Federalist congressmen that Burr was the dangerous man, unprincipled and hungry, and that Jefferson, whatever his errors, had principles a government could survive.
  • Burr, for his part, refused every invitation to withdraw, which deepened the suspicion that he would take the presidency from his own party's voters if the House handed it to him.
  • On the thirty-sixth ballot, Federalists in the divided and blocking delegations abstained or cast blanks, Maryland and Vermont tipped to Jefferson, and the count reached ten states.
  • The machine had worked at the last moment, operated by men voting against their preference to keep it working.

Slide 19 · Section F: The Transfer of Power (divider)

On the slide: The Transfer of Power · Jefferson, painted by Rembrandt Peale in 1800, the year of the tie and the deadlock.

Picture: Rembrandt Peale's 1800 portrait of Thomas Jefferson, painted in Philadelphia in the year of the election, and held in the White House collection.

  • Section F teaches March 4, 1801 as a choreography of restraint.
  • President Adams left the capital before dawn, riding out of Washington on the morning his successor took the oath, a departure students should read without melodrama: he had also spent his last night signing judicial commissions, which the next lesson examines, but he made no move to hold power, call troops, or dispute the count.
  • Jefferson walked from his lodgings to the Capitol, took the oath from Chief Justice John Marshall, his own political opponent and cousin, and delivered an inaugural address addressed as much to the beaten party as to his own.
  • The reading and the activity both take the address as the settlement's text: majority rule, minority rights, and error of opinion answered by reason rather than prosecution.
  • A government had changed parties, and the day was, by design, almost ordinary.

Slide 20 · Every difference of opinion is not a difference of principle

On the slide: The majority will prevail, and its will must be reasonable · The minority possess equal rights, which equal law must protect · Error of opinion may be tolerated where reason is free to combat it · A wise and frugal government, as the sum of good government

  • Read the address as the anti-Sedition Act, sentence by sentence, because Jefferson wrote it for the men who had passed and enforced that act and for the men it had jailed, who now sat in his audience together.
  • The majority principle comes first and is unconditional, the will of the majority is in all cases to prevail, and then it is bounded in the same breath: that will, to be rightful, must be reasonable, and the minority possess their equal rights, which equal law must protect, and to violate would be oppression.
  • Political intolerance is named as a despotism equal to the religious intolerance the country had escaped.
  • The famous sentence follows: every difference of opinion is not a difference of principle, so the two parties, called by different names, were brethren of the same principle, all Republicans, all Federalists.
  • And the practical program closes the address: a wise and frugal government that restrains men from injuring one another and otherwise leaves them free.
  • On March 3 the Sedition Act had expired. On March 4 its replacement was announced, reason left free to combat error.

Slide 21 · Section G: The Twelfth Amendment and the Precedent (divider)

On the slide: The Twelfth Amendment and the Precedent · The machine is repaired, and the ground every later transfer stands on is cleared.

  • Section G closes the lesson with the repair and the precedent, in that order, and students should learn to distinguish them.
  • The repair was constitutional housekeeping made necessary by the deadlock: the Twelfth Amendment, proposed by the Republican Congress and ratified in June 1804, in time for that year's election. It separated the electoral ballots, one list for president and one for vice president, so that a party ticket could never again tie its own two candidates at the head of the poll, and it set procedures for the House and Senate if no majority emerged.
  • The precedent was larger and could not be amended into existence. In 1801 a party that had prosecuted its opponents counted the votes that removed it, survived a week in which its own House members could have stolen the succession, and went home, while the winners pardoned the prisoners and let the gag law stay expired.
  • Jefferson named his victory a revolution, and Section G asks students to judge the name.

Slide 22 · What 1801 proved, and what it left open

On the slide: Separate ballots for president and vice president from 1804 · Jefferson pardons those still held under the Sedition Act · No soldier in the street, and the government changed hands · Nullification left unanswered, to return in later units

  • Land the lesson's two legacies separately.
  • The settled legacy is the transfer itself: from 1801 forward, American parties that lose presidential elections leave office, a habit so deep now that students must be reminded it was invented here, under strain, by the losing side's restraint and the winning side's inaugural promise to the minority.
  • The naturalization wait went back to five years in 1802, the Sedition Act was never renewed, and its prisoners were pardoned.
  • The unsettled legacy is the doctrine the crisis produced. The Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions were defeated as politics in 1799 and never withdrawn as theory, and their claim that a state may judge and even nullify a federal law sat in the record waiting for later generations to pick it up over tariffs and over slavery.
  • The next lesson turns from words to territory: Jefferson, the strict constructionist who doubted any federal power not named, is offered an empire for fifteen million dollars.

Ask: Jefferson called the transfer a revolution. Was he right? A good answer weighs what changed without violence, a governing party removed, its laws let to expire, its prisoners pardoned, against what did not change, the Constitution itself, the courts, and the unanswered doctrine of nullification.

Slide 23 · The crisis and the transfer at a glance

On the slide: The war scare · The prosecutions · The resolutions · The transfer, four stages

  • This slide mirrors the summary table in the guided notes, The Crisis and the Transfer at a Glance. Students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • The four rows are the lesson's argument in order. The war scare: the XYZ Affair and the undeclared naval war made France an enemy and dissent look like treason.
  • The laws and the prosecutions: Congress criminalized criticism, and the courts jailed the opposition's editors and one of its congressmen.
  • The states answer: Virginia and Kentucky argued compact, interposition, and nullification, and were refused by ten states and joined by none.
  • The transfer: the voters defeated the governing party, the House finished its work on the thirty-sixth ballot, and power changed hands on ballots alone.

Note: grade the table generously on wording and strictly on sequence: the order of the answers is the lesson.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for the crisis

On the slide: XYZ Affair · Alien Acts · Sedition Act · Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions

[Have students copy the definitions exactly as they appear in the guided notes, because the unit test draws on them.]

  • XYZ Affair: the diplomatic scandal of 1798 in which French agents, named X, Y, and Z in the published dispatches, demanded a bribe and a loan before France would negotiate with American envoys, inflaming American opinion against France
  • Alien Acts: the laws of 1798 that lengthened the wait for citizenship from five years to fourteen and let the president deport or imprison immigrants he judged dangerous, aimed at immigrants who leaned toward the Democratic-Republicans
  • Sedition Act: the law of 1798 that made it a crime to publish false, scandalous, or malicious writing against the federal government, its Congress, or its president, under which Republican newspaper editors were tried and jailed
  • Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions: the resolutions of 1798 and 1799, written secretly by Madison for Virginia and Jefferson for Kentucky, which argued that the states, as parties to the Constitution's compact, could judge federal laws and declare them unconstitutional
  • Teach the four in sequence: scandal, fear, laws aimed at voters and at voices, and the states' answer.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for the settlement

On the slide: nullification · Twelfth Amendment · Revolution of 1800

[Have students copy the definitions exactly as they appear in the guided notes.]

  • nullification: the claim, drawn from the Kentucky Resolutions, that a state may declare a federal law void and of no force inside its borders, a doctrine later generations carried much further than its 1798 authors did in print
  • Twelfth Amendment: the amendment ratified in 1804 that required presidential electors to cast separate ballots for president and vice president, written to prevent the tie between running mates that threw the election of 1800 into the House
  • Revolution of 1800: the name Jefferson gave his election, the transfer of power in 1801 from the Federalists to the Democratic-Republicans after a bitter campaign and a deadlocked electoral vote, the first time a ruling party surrendered the government to its opponents by election
  • Keep the first term's warning in class: later generations carried the doctrine far beyond its authors, as students will see in later units.
  • The other two terms belong together: the election changed the government without violence, and the amendment kept the machinery from jamming the same way twice.

Slide 26 · Criticism answered three ways

On the slide: Prosecuted · Answered · Voted · Transferred

[Make students say the four rows aloud in order, because the sequence is the lesson.]

  • Prosecuted: in a war scare the government made criticism a crime and used the law against the opposition's press and one of its congressmen.
  • Answered: the opposition replied with a constitutional doctrine, that the states as parties to a compact could judge federal law, and in 1799 that doctrine was rejected by the states themselves.
  • Voted: the argument was settled instead by the electorate, which removed the party of the prosecutions.
  • Transferred: after a tie and thirty-six ballots, the losers went home, the Sedition Act stayed expired, and the new president promised the minority its equal rights in his first address.
  • The republic had held its enemies inside the law, which is the claim the next lessons test against territory, courts, and war.

Slide 27 · Now your turn: answer the question and exit ticket

On the slide: Answer the question · Activity, notebook, quick check, and writing for the lesson · Exit ticket

  • The activity, The Sedition File, sets out five stations: the Alien and Sedition Acts in Congress's own words, Jefferson's draft of the Kentucky Resolutions, Madison's Virginia Resolutions, a labeled teaching summary of Matthew Lyon's prosecution and reelection, and Jefferson's First Inaugural Address, and it ends with a table sorting the records by the power or the speech they defend and a verdict on whether the act was a wartime necessity or a party weapon.
  • The notebook page charts the five steps of the crisis and asks for the answer that held. The quick check, A Law and Its Answer, tests the Sedition Act's penalties, the compact claim, nullification, and the election's numbers.
  • The writing task turns the verdict into an essay argued from the evidence bank.
  • Exit ticket, three parts: the Sedition Act with its maximum fine and prison term, the compact claim with the remedy Kentucky added in 1799, and the lesson question in two or three sentences. Next lesson opens with the judges Adams signed on his last night.
