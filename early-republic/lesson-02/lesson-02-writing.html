Writing Task: Necessary Law or Party Weapon

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Was the Sedition Act a wartime necessity or a party weapon? A strong answer sets the law in its war scare, weighs how the law worked in court against how it read on paper, brings in the answer the states and the voters gave, and ends by judging what the election of 1800 proved. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. In 1798 French agents named X, Y, and Z demanded a bribe and a loan before France would negotiate, and American and French ships fought an undeclared naval war in the Caribbean. Federalist majorities prepared for a war that might come ashore, and immigrant voters, Irish and French above all, backed Jefferson's party.

2. The Sedition Act of 1798 punished false, scandalous, and malicious writing against the government, Congress, or the president, by a fine of up to two thousand dollars and up to two years in prison. Its defenders cited two safeguards, truth as a defense and juries judging the law and the facts together. The act protected the president and Congress. It did not name the vice president, who was Jefferson.

3. About twenty five people were arrested under the act and some ten convicted, nearly all Republicans. Congressman Matthew Lyon of Vermont was fined one thousand dollars and jailed four months for criticizing President Adams, ran for reelection from his cell, and won. No Federalist editor was prosecuted for attacks on Jefferson.

4. Jefferson's Kentucky Resolutions argued that the states had made the federal government by compact and delegated definite powers only, that undelegated acts were void, and in 1799 that nullification was the rightful remedy. Madison's Virginia Resolutions claimed a state duty to interpose. Ten states rejected the resolutions and no state joined them.

5. In the election of 1800 Jefferson and Burr tied at seventy three electoral votes. The Federalist held House deadlocked through thirty five ballots in February 1801 before choosing Jefferson on the thirty sixth, after Hamilton argued Jefferson was the lesser danger.

6. The Sedition Act expired by its own terms on March 3, 1801. Adams left Washington before dawn on March 4, and Jefferson, in his First Inaugural, said every difference of opinion is not a difference of principle, promised the minority equal rights under equal law, and held that error of opinion may be tolerated where reason is left free to combat it. The Twelfth Amendment separated the ballots in 1804.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the prompt with a verdict. Either verdict earns full credit when weighed against the other side: wartime necessity argued from the war scare and the act's safeguards, or party weapon argued from the act's reach and its prosecutions. (Blueprint 3)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank items 1 and 2 carry the necessity case: the XYZ Affair and the undeclared naval war, and the act's stated safeguards of truth as a defense and jury judgment. Bank items 3 and 4 carry the weapon case and the constitutional answer: the one sided prosecutions and Lyon's reelection from his cell, and the compact argument of the two resolutions with its 1799 limit. (Blueprint 3) Bank items 5 and 6 carry the settlement: the seventy three vote tie and thirty six ballots, the act expiring on March 3, 1801, and the inaugural's promise to the minority. (Blueprint 4) A full credit essay draws on both groups.
  • Figures used as evidence, not decoration. Fourteen years, two thousand dollars, two years, twenty five arrested, seventy three votes, and thirty six ballots each mark a step in the argument, and strong essays say what the step is.
  • A closing that lands on what the election proved. A party that had jailed its opponents surrendered the government to them on ballots alone, and the Twelfth Amendment repaired the machinery that had nearly broken the transfer. An essay that ends in 1798 without the transfer has answered only half the question.

Model opening for teacher reference. The Sedition Act was written for a war and used on a party. The war was real: French agents had demanded a bribe in the XYZ Affair, French and American ships were fighting without a declaration, and a Congress preparing for invasion could plausibly fear enemy sympathizers at home. But the law Congress wrote in that fear protected the president and Congress by name and not the vice president, who happened to lead the opposition, and the courts then used it almost entirely against Republicans, jailing a congressman who answered by winning reelection from his cell. A full model continues in this pattern through the resolutions and their rejection, the deadlock of February 1801, and the transfer of March 4, and judges the act by both records at once.

Common weak answers. The free speech answer that never mentions 1798 treats the act as if it were passed in peacetime, and bank item 1 exists to prevent that. The wartime answer that never mentions Lyon treats the act's text as its whole history, and bank item 3 exists to prevent that. A summary of the resolutions with no election in it misses the lesson's settlement, in bank items 5 and 6, where the argument the states lost in 1799 was won by voters in 1800.
