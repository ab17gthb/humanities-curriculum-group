Guided Notes: The Alien and Sedition Acts and the Revolution of 1800

Lesson question: When the government called its critics traitors, who answered, and what did the election of 1800 prove?

Unit question: How did the United States turn its Constitution into a working government, and what did continental expansion do to the republic that government served?

Key Terms

XYZ Affair: the diplomatic scandal of 1798 in which French agents, named X, Y, and Z in the published dispatches, demanded a bribe and a loan before France would negotiate with American envoys, inflaming American opinion against France

Alien Acts: the laws of 1798 that lengthened the wait for citizenship from five years to fourteen and let the president deport or imprison immigrants he judged dangerous, aimed at immigrants who leaned toward the Democratic-Republicans

Sedition Act: the law of 1798 that made it a crime to publish false, scandalous, or malicious writing against the federal government, its Congress, or its president, under which Republican newspaper editors were tried and jailed

Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions: the resolutions of 1798 and 1799, written secretly by Madison for Virginia and Jefferson for Kentucky, which argued that the states, as parties to the Constitution's compact, could judge federal laws and declare them unconstitutional

nullification: the claim, drawn from the Kentucky Resolutions, that a state may declare a federal law void and of no force inside its borders, a doctrine later generations carried much further than its 1798 authors did in print

Twelfth Amendment: the amendment ratified in 1804 that required presidential electors to cast separate ballots for president and vice president, written to prevent the tie between running mates that threw the election of 1800 into the House

Revolution of 1800: the name Jefferson gave his election, the transfer of power in 1801 from the Federalists to the Democratic-Republicans after a bitter campaign and a deadlocked electoral vote, the first time a ruling party surrendered the government to its opponents by election

Summary table. The war scare: the XYZ Affair of 1798, in which French agents demanded a bribe and a loan, and an undeclared naval war with France pushed Federalist majorities to prepare for war and to fear immigrant voters who backed Jefferson's party; it changed a foreign crisis into a domestic party question. The laws and the prosecutions: the four laws of 1798 lengthened the citizenship wait to fourteen years, armed the president against immigrants, and made criticism of the government a crime, and the Sedition Act's prosecutions jailed some ten people, nearly all Republicans, including Congressman Matthew Lyon; the laws changed opposition from an argument into a prosecutable offense. The states answer: Jefferson's Kentucky Resolutions and Madison's Virginia Resolutions argued that the Constitution was a compact among the states holding only delegated powers, with Kentucky naming nullification in 1799; the answer changed a debate about one law into a doctrine about who judges federal power, even though ten states rejected it and none joined. The transfer: Jefferson and Burr tied at seventy three electoral votes, the House deadlocked through thirty five ballots and chose Jefferson on the thirty sixth, Adams left before dawn on March 4, 1801, and the Twelfth Amendment of 1804 separated the ballots; the transfer changed a jailed opposition into a governing party by ballots alone.

Exit ticket 1. The Sedition Act was the law of 1798 that made it a crime to publish false, scandalous, or malicious writing against the federal government, its Congress, or its president, punishable by a fine of up to two thousand dollars and up to two years in prison.

Exit ticket 2. The resolutions claimed that the Constitution was a compact among the states, that the federal government held only the powers the compact delegated, and that the states retained the right to judge when those powers had been exceeded. The remedy Kentucky named in 1799 was nullification, a state's declaration that a federal law was void inside its borders.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that names an answerer and a proof. The states answered through the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions and the voters answered by defeating the Federalists, and the election proved a ruling party would surrender power by ballots: Jefferson and Burr tied at seventy three, the House took thirty six ballots in all, Adams went home, and Jefferson took office on March 4, 1801 without a soldier in the street.
