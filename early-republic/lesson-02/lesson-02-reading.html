The Alien and Sedition Acts and the Revolution of 1800

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. A War Scare and a Party Fight

By 1798 the French Revolution had become a war between France and Britain, and American politics had split over it. Federalists backed Britain. Democratic-Republicans backed France. In 1798 President John Adams published the dispatches his envoys had sent from Paris. French agents, named X, Y, and Z in the printed papers, had demanded a bribe and a loan before France would even talk. The XYZ Affair enraged the country, American and French ships fought an undeclared naval war in the Caribbean, and Federalist majorities in Congress prepared for a war that might come ashore. They also looked homeward, at immigrant voters, Irish and French above all, who voted overwhelmingly for Jefferson's party.

B. Four Laws of 1798

Congress answered in the summer of 1798 with four laws that worked as one program. The Naturalization Act stretched the wait for citizenship from five years to fourteen, so new immigrants would wait far longer to vote. The Alien Friends Act let the president order any immigrant he judged dangerous out of the country. The Alien Enemies Act let him arrest or remove subjects of a hostile nation in a declared war. The Sedition Act made it a crime to write, print, or publish false, scandalous, or malicious matter against the government, Congress, or the president, punishable by a fine of up to two thousand dollars and up to two years in prison. Its defenders noted two safeguards. Truth could be offered as a defense, and juries would judge both the law and the facts.

C. The Sedition Act in Court

The prosecutions did not stay inside the safeguards. About twenty five people were arrested under the Sedition Act and some ten were convicted, and nearly all of them were Republicans. The best known was Matthew Lyon, a congressman from Vermont, jailed for a letter and a poem criticizing President Adams. Lyon ran for reelection from his cell and won, and his voters paid his fine. Republican editors were tried and jailed, their papers the opposition's only national voice. No Federalist editor was prosecuted for attacks on Jefferson. The act was written to expire on March 3, 1801, the day before the next president took office. In the season's last weeks the Federalist Congress also passed the Judiciary Act of 1801, creating new judgeships, and Adams filled them to his last night in office.

D. Virginia and Kentucky Answer

The answer came from two state legislatures, and its authors hid. Thomas Jefferson, then vice president, secretly drafted the Kentucky Resolutions of 1798. James Madison secretly wrote the Virginia Resolutions that followed. Both argued from one claim. The Constitution was a compact among the states, the federal government held only the powers the compact delegated, and the states kept the right to judge when those powers had been exceeded. Virginia's word was interpose. Kentucky's second set of resolutions, passed in 1799, named a stronger remedy, nullification, the claim that a state could declare a federal law void inside its borders. The doctrine would outgrow its authors and return in later years carrying far heavier loads. In 1799 its weakness was plain. Ten states answered back rejecting the resolutions, and no state joined Virginia and Kentucky.

E. The Deadlock of 1800

The election of 1800 was the crisis the laws had been built, in part, to prevent, and it arrived anyway. Jefferson defeated Adams, but the electoral vote tied Jefferson with his own running mate, Aaron Burr, at seventy three votes each, because every Republican elector had voted for both men and the Constitution gave electors one ballot that did not separate president from vice president. The choice fell to the House of Representatives, still held by Federalists, voting by state. Through thirty five ballots in February 1801 the House stayed deadlocked. Some Federalists schemed to install Burr. Alexander Hamilton argued that Jefferson, his old enemy, was the lesser danger. On the thirty sixth ballot enough Federalists stood aside, and Jefferson won.

F. The Transfer of Power

Adams left Washington before dawn on March 4, 1801. Jefferson walked to the Capitol and took the oath. His First Inaugural Address reached for the voters his party had just beaten. Every difference of opinion, he said, is not a difference of principle, and Americans had called by different names brethren of the same principle. He promised that the majority would rule, and that the minority kept equal rights that equal law must protect, and that error of opinion could be tolerated where reason was left free to combat it. The Sedition Act had expired the day before. Its prisoners would be pardoned. A party that had jailed its opponents had surrendered power to them on the strength of ballots, and the government changed hands without a soldier in the street.

G. The Twelfth Amendment and the Precedent

The settlement was then written into the Constitution. The Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, required electors to cast separate ballots for president and for vice president, so that a tie between running mates could not throw another election into the House. Jefferson called his election a revolution, and the name stuck, the Revolution of 1800, because the change was real and bloodless. The era's largest precedent was not any single law. A government had prosecuted its critics, the critics had answered with a doctrine about the states, the voters had settled the argument at the ballot box, and the losing party went home. Every later transfer of power in the republic stands on the ground this one cleared.

3. State the compact claim, the authors, the remedy, and the limit. Jefferson secretly drafted Kentucky's resolutions and Madison secretly wrote Virginia's. Virginia used the word interpose. The limit is part of the answer: ten states answered back rejecting the resolutions and no state joined them, so in 1799 the doctrine persuaded almost no one beyond its two home states. 4. Explain the tie, the ballots, and Hamilton's part. Every Republican elector voted for both Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr, and the Constitution's single undifferentiated ballot made no distinction between a vote for president and a vote for vice president, so the two men tied at seventy three electoral votes. The election fell to the Federalist held House, voting by state delegation. The House deadlocked through thirty five ballots in February 1801 while some Federalists schemed to install Burr. Hamilton argued for Jefferson, his old opponent, as the lesser danger. On the thirty sixth ballot enough Federalists stood aside and Jefferson won. (Blueprint 4)

5. Who answered, and what did the election prove? Full credit answers name both answers. The states answered first, through Jefferson's Kentucky Resolutions and Madison's Virginia Resolutions, arguing compact, delegated powers, and a state right to judge, with Kentucky naming nullification in 1799, even as ten states rejected the claim. The voters answered second, defeating the party that had passed the acts. The election then proved its point the hard way, through a seventy three vote tie and thirty six House ballots, and the proof held: Adams left, Jefferson took the oath on March 4, 1801, the Sedition Act had expired the day before, and a party that had jailed its opponents surrendered the government to them on ballots alone, without a soldier in the street. Full credit answers close with the precedent and its repair, the Twelfth Amendment of 1804 separating the ballots so the tie could not recur. (Blueprint 3 and 4)
