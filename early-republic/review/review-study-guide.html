Part 3: The Unit as a Whole

The unit opens in 1789 with a government on paper. The Constitution named offices and powers, and Washington's first administration had to invent the machinery, a cabinet the document nowhere creates, federal courts, a funded debt, a national bank, and precedents set by everything done for the first time. Hamilton's program made the federal government real and manufactured its opposition in the same motion, Jefferson and Madison reading the Constitution strictly against his broad construction, until the argument left the cabinet and became the first parties. Washington enforced the excise on the Pennsylvania frontier with a militia army and retired in 1796 warning against the spirit of party and permanent foreign alliances. His successors tested the settlement at once. The Federalists of 1798, frightened by France and by immigrant voters, jailed their critics under the Sedition Act, and the answer came from the states in the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions and then from the voters, who deadlocked the election of 1800 in the House and still transferred power by ballot, the first ruling party in the new republic surrendering the government to its opponents without a soldier in the street.

Jefferson governed as a strict constructionist who doubled the nation. He shrank the government Hamilton had built, pardoned the Sedition Act's prisoners, and paid down the debt, and in the same season he faced Marshall's Court claiming judicial review in Marbury while surrendering the case in front of it, and Napoleon's offer of the whole of Louisiana, which his principles gave him no power to accept and his judgment would not let him refuse. The purchase sent the Corps of Discovery up the Missouri into country that was not empty, Mandan and Hidatsa towns larger than St. Louis, Shoshone horse country, Nez Perce guides, and a ballot at Fort Clatsop where Sacagawea and York both voted in a nation where neither could. The republic that came home from that crossing went to war with Britain in 1812 over impressment, honor, and Canada, fought it divided, and settled it at Ghent with the boundaries of 1811 restored and every stated cause left off the paper, while the war broke the Native confederacies of the West, ruined the Federalist Party, and taught the nationalist generation of 1816 to build the bank, the tariff, and the roads the war had shown wanting.

The settlements of the 1820s declared more than the republic could yet enforce. Clay's American System bound the sections into one market on paper, Madison and Monroe vetoed its roads on constitutional grounds, the Panic of 1819 taught the West to hate the bank that saved itself by ruining its debtors, and the Marshall Court read the Constitution as a frame meant to endure, implied powers in McCulloch and a commerce of intercourse in Gibbons. In December 1823 Monroe declared the hemisphere closed to future European colonization, a declaration that floated on the British navy. Three years earlier the Missouri crisis had drawn the line of thirty-six thirty across the Louisiana lands, Maine against Missouri, slavery barred north of it, and Jefferson heard in the question a fire bell in the night and the knell of the Union. The 1840s closed the unit where the line could not follow. Texas came in by joint resolution, Oregon was halved by treaty, Mexico was beaten in every campaign and ceded half its territory at Guadalupe Hidalgo for fifteen million dollars, and the Wilmot Proviso, barring slavery from the cession, passed the House and died in the Senate until the question every settlement since 1787 had deferred stood alone, unanswered, on the table of the enlarged house.

Teacher Answer Key

Part 1: Key Terms

Lesson 1: Washington's Republic: Hamilton, Parties, and the Farewell

cabinetthe heads of the executive departments who advise the president, an office the Constitution nowhere creates, which Washington formed from the departments Congress established in 1789
assumptionthe federal government's taking over of debts owed by others, the policy by which Hamilton's funding plan had the United States assume the states' Revolutionary War debts
excise taxa tax on the manufacture or sale of a product inside the country, such as the 1791 tax on distilled whiskey that paid part of Hamilton's program and fell hardest on frontier farmers
Bank of the United Statesthe national bank chartered by Congress in 1791 for twenty years, holding the government's money, issuing currency, and lending, which Hamilton defended under Congress's implied powers and Jefferson opposed
Whiskey Rebellionthe uprising of 1794 in western Pennsylvania against the federal excise tax on whiskey, which Washington answered by calling out the militia and leading it west, proving the government could enforce its laws
cotton ginthe machine patented by Eli Whitney in 1794 that separated cotton fiber from seed by machine, which made short-staple cotton profitable across the inland South and deepened the region's dependence on enslaved labor
Farewell AddressWashington's published letter of September 1796 announcing he would not seek a third term, which warned against permanent foreign alliances and against political parties, and set the two-term precedent presidents followed

Lesson 2: The Alien and Sedition Acts and the Revolution of 1800

XYZ Affairthe diplomatic scandal of 1798 in which French agents, named X, Y, and Z in the published dispatches, demanded a bribe and a loan before France would negotiate with American envoys, inflaming American opinion against France
Alien Actsthe laws of 1798 that lengthened the wait for citizenship from five years to fourteen and let the president deport or imprison immigrants he judged dangerous, aimed at immigrants who leaned toward the Democratic-Republicans
Sedition Actthe law of 1798 that made it a crime to publish false, scandalous, or malicious writing against the federal government, its Congress, or its president, under which Republican newspaper editors were tried and jailed
Virginia and Kentucky Resolutionsthe resolutions of 1798 and 1799, written secretly by Madison for Virginia and Jefferson for Kentucky, which argued that the states, as parties to the Constitution's compact, could judge federal laws and declare them unconstitutional
nullificationthe claim, drawn from the Kentucky Resolutions, that a state may declare a federal law void and of no force inside its borders, a doctrine later generations carried much further than its 1798 authors did in print
Twelfth Amendmentthe amendment ratified in 1804 that required presidential electors to cast separate ballots for president and vice president, written to prevent the tie between running mates that threw the election of 1800 into the House
Revolution of 1800the name Jefferson gave his election, the transfer of power in 1801 from the Federalists to the Democratic-Republicans after a bitter campaign and a deadlocked electoral vote, the first time a ruling party surrendered the government to its opponents by election

Lesson 3: Jefferson's Republic and the Louisiana Purchase

strict constructionthe reading of the Constitution that holds the federal government to the powers the document names and those necessarily implied by them, the ground on which Jefferson opposed the Bank and doubted the Louisiana Purchase
implied powersthe powers the federal government holds beyond those the Constitution lists, drawn from the clause empowering Congress to make all laws necessary and proper for carrying its listed powers into execution
Louisiana Purchasethe treaty of 1803 by which the United States bought France's claim to Louisiana for fifteen million dollars, doubling the nation's territory in a single signature and testing what a president's treaty power could do
midnight judgesthe judges John Adams appointed and the Federalist Congress authorized in his presidency's last days, whose commissions became the subject of Marbury v. Madison when Jefferson's secretary of state refused to deliver them
Marbury v. Madisonthe Supreme Court decision of 1803 in which Chief Justice John Marshall held that the Court could not order the delivery of a judicial commission because the law granting it that power was unconstitutional
judicial reviewthe power of the courts to declare laws and acts of the other branches unconstitutional and void, claimed for the Supreme Court by Marbury v. Madison in 1803 and since become the judiciary's central power
Barbary Warsthe wars of 1801 to 1805 and 1815 in which the United States fought the Barbary states of North Africa rather than keep paying tribute for the safety of American merchant ships in the Mediterranean

Lesson 4: Lewis and Clark and the Peoples of the West

Corps of Discoverythe Army expedition of 1804 to 1806, commanded by Meriwether Lewis and William Clark, sent by President Jefferson to explore the Louisiana Purchase, find a water route toward the Pacific, and establish an American presence among the West's peoples
Meriwether Lewisthe Army captain and former secretary to President Jefferson who co-commanded the Corps of Discovery, kept its scientific record, and governed Louisiana Territory after the expedition's return
William Clarkthe Army officer who co-commanded the Corps of Discovery with Lewis, drew its maps, and negotiated much of its diplomacy with Native nations, treating the expedition's enslaved member, York, as his property throughout
Sacagaweathe Shoshone woman who joined the Corps of Discovery as an interpreter with her husband in 1804, carried her infant son across the continent, and whose presence and kinship opened doors, including her own people's, that the expedition could not have opened alone
Yorkthe enslaved man held by William Clark who crossed the continent with the Corps of Discovery, hunted, scouted, and voted in the expedition's decisions, and who asked for his freedom on the return and was refused for years
Shoshonethe Native nation of the northern Rockies and Great Basin, Sacagawea's people, whose horses the Corps of Discovery needed to cross the mountains in 1805 and obtained through the meeting between Sacagawea and her brother Cameahwait
continental dividethe high ground of the Rocky Mountains separating the rivers that flow toward the Atlantic from those that flow toward the Pacific, which the Corps of Discovery crossed in 1805 and found no single water route beyond

Lesson 5: The War of 1812

impressmentthe British practice of stopping American ships at sea and forcing sailors, including American citizens, into service in the Royal Navy, one of the grievances Madison's war message laid before Congress in 1812
embargoa government ban on trade, the policy of the Embargo Act of 1807, which closed American ports to foreign trade entirely in the hope of forcing Britain and France to respect American neutral rights without a war
War Hawksthe young congressmen, Henry Clay and John C. Calhoun among them, who pressed for war with Britain in 1811 and 1812 to end impressment, defend national honor, and conquer Canada
Tecumsehthe Shawnee leader who built a confederacy of Native nations in the West to resist American land cessions, traveled as far south as the Creek country seeking allies, and joined Britain in the War of 1812, dying at the Thames in 1813
Tippecanoethe battle of November 1811 in which William Henry Harrison's army fought Tecumseh's confederacy at Prophetstown while Tecumseh was away, burned the town, and claimed a victory that made Harrison's reputation
Hartford Conventionthe meeting of New England Federalists at Hartford in December 1814 and January 1815, which proposed constitutional amendments and hinted at a separate peace, and whose report reached Washington just as news of peace and New Orleans destroyed the party's standing
Treaty of Ghentthe treaty signed at Ghent on December 24, 1814, that ended the War of 1812 by restoring the boundaries and possessions of 1811, settling none of the questions of impressment or neutral rights over which the war had been declared

Lesson 6: The Era of Good Feelings: The American System and the Marshall Court

American SystemHenry Clay's program of a national bank, a protective tariff, and federally funded internal improvements, meant to bind the sections into one economy in which western food, southern cotton, and northern manufactures traded with each other
Second Bank of the United Statesthe national bank chartered in 1816 for twenty years, larger than Hamilton's first Bank and created because the war had exposed the disorder of a currency issued by hundreds of state banks
protective tariffa tax on imported goods set high enough to shelter American manufacturers from foreign competition rather than only to raise revenue, first enacted as national policy in the Tariff of 1816
internal improvementsroads, canals, and harbors built to move goods and people inside the country, which nearly every president of the era agreed the nation needed and many held the Constitution gave Congress no power to fund
Panic of 1819the financial collapse of 1819, the first great peacetime depression of the United States, in which the Second Bank's contraction of credit helped burst a land and cotton speculation, throwing western farmers and city workers into ruin
McCulloch v. Marylandthe Supreme Court decision of 1819 in which Chief Justice Marshall held that Congress could charter a bank under its implied powers and that Maryland could not tax it, because the power to tax is the power to destroy
Gibbons v. Ogdenthe Supreme Court decision of 1824 in which Chief Justice Marshall read Congress's power to regulate commerce among the states broadly, striking down a state steamboat monopoly and opening the nation's waterways to competition

Lesson 7: The Monroe Doctrine and the Missouri Compromise

Monroe Doctrinethe policy declared by President Monroe in his annual message of December 2, 1823, holding that the American continents were closed to future European colonization and that European interference in the hemisphere would be viewed as dangerous to the United States
John Quincy Adamsthe secretary of state under Monroe, the chief author of the Monroe Doctrine's language and of the Transcontinental Treaty of 1819, who held that the United States should speak for the hemisphere itself rather than appear as a cockboat in the wake of the British man of war
Adams-Onís Treatythe treaty of 1819, also called the Transcontinental Treaty, by which Spain ceded Florida to the United States and drew the boundary between American and Spanish claims across the continent to the Pacific
sectionalismloyalty to one's own section of the country above the nation as a whole, the force the Missouri crisis exposed in 1819 and 1820 when the House and Senate divided on slavery's expansion in nearly perfect sectional lines
Tallmadge Amendmentthe amendment proposed by Representative James Tallmadge in 1819 that would have barred bringing more enslaved people into Missouri and freed those born there at age twenty five, whose passage in the House and death in the Senate opened the Missouri crisis
Missouri Compromisethe settlement of 1820 that admitted Maine as a free state and Missouri as a slave state and drew a line at 36 degrees 30 minutes across the Louisiana Purchase lands, barring slavery north of it and permitting it south
thirty-six thirtythe parallel of latitude, 36 degrees 30 minutes north, that the Missouri Compromise drew across the Louisiana Purchase territory as the boundary between country closed to slavery and country open to it

Lesson 8: Manifest Destiny and the War with Mexico

Manifest Destinythe name given in 1845 to the belief that the United States was destined, by providence and by the superiority of its institutions, to expand across the continent to the Pacific, a belief its holders used to justify annexation and war
annexationthe formal addition of territory to a country, the method by which Texas joined the United States in 1845 by joint resolution of Congress after a treaty of annexation had failed in the Senate
Republic of Texasthe independent nation Texans declared in 1836 after revolting against Mexico, recognized by the United States but kept outside the Union for nine years because its admission as a slave state threatened the sectional balance
Oregon Countrythe Pacific Northwest territory jointly occupied by the United States and Britain from 1818, claimed by American expansionists to the line of 54 degrees 40 minutes and divided at the 49th parallel by treaty in 1846
Mexican-American Warthe war of 1846 to 1848 between the United States and Mexico, begun over the Texas boundary after American annexation and ended by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo with Mexico ceding half its territory
Wilmot Provisothe amendment proposed by Representative David Wilmot in 1846, passed repeatedly by the House and blocked in the Senate, that would have barred slavery in any territory taken from Mexico, forcing the slavery question onto the nation's table three years before the gold rush
Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgothe treaty of February 2, 1848, that ended the Mexican-American War, confirmed the Rio Grande as Texas's boundary, and transferred California, New Mexico, and the country between to the United States for fifteen million dollars

Part 2: Review Questions

1. What did Washington's first administration have to invent, and how did the cabinet and the federal courts take shape? The Constitution named offices and powers but not the machinery for using them, so the first administration built the machinery while the government was already running. Congress created the executive departments in 1789, and Washington formed his cabinet from the heads of those departments, an office the Constitution nowhere creates. Congress organized the federal courts in the same season, the president was received as head of state, and every act stood as a precedent because everything was being done for the first time. The government students meet in this lesson is an invention, assembled piece by piece by the men who had to operate it.

2. State Hamilton's financial program in its four parts, and name the constitutional ground the whole design rested on. Hamilton's program was one design with four moving parts. The national debt would be funded at full value so the government's credit would stand. The federal government would assume the states' war debts so the creditors of the union would look to the union. Congress would charter a national bank to hold the government's money, issue a uniform currency, and make credit move. An excise tax on distilled whiskey would help pay for it all, and it fell hardest on the frontier farmers for whom whiskey was the only form in which their corn could reach a market. Each part rested on a broad reading of the Constitution's powers, the necessary and proper clause above all, and Jefferson and Madison answered with a strict reading, which is how the argument inside the cabinet became the first parties.

3. How did the Whiskey Rebellion test Washington's enforcement precedent, and what did the Farewell Address warn against? Western Pennsylvania farmers resisted the excise in 1794, tarring tax men, robbing the mail, and attacking a federal marshal's house. Washington answered by calling a militia army of some thirteen thousand men from four states and leading it part of the way west himself. The rebellion dissolved before the army arrived, two men were convicted of treason and pardoned, and the point stood that the new government's laws would be enforced by the government itself rather than watched helplessly, as Congress had watched under the Articles. Retiring in 1796 after two terms, a precedent in itself, Washington published his Farewell Address warning against permanent alliances with foreign nations and against the spirit of party he had watched form inside his own cabinet.

4. What war scare produced the laws of 1798, and what did the four laws do as one program? The French Revolution's wars reached American politics as a party question. In the XYZ Affair of 1798, French agents demanded a bribe and a loan before they would negotiate, the published dispatches inflamed opinion, and an undeclared naval war with France followed in the Caribbean while Federalist majorities prepared for a war they half expected to come ashore. The four laws of 1798 read as one program. The Naturalization Act stretched the wait for citizenship from five years to fourteen. The Alien Friends Act and the Alien Enemies Act armed the president against immigrants by decree. The Sedition Act made it a crime to write or publish false, scandalous, or malicious matter against the government, Congress, or the president, and its prosecutions fell on Republican editors and on a sitting congressman, Matthew Lyon of Vermont, who was jailed for criticizing President Adams and reelected from his cell.

5. How did the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions answer the acts, and what limit did their reception show? Jefferson wrote the Kentucky Resolutions and Madison the Virginia Resolutions in secret in 1798 and 1799. They argued that the Constitution was a compact among the states, that the federal government held only delegated powers, and that the states retained the right to judge when those powers had been exceeded. Kentucky's second set of resolutions went further and named the remedy nullification, the claim that a state may declare a federal law void inside its borders. The doctrine would outgrow its authors and return in later years carrying far heavier loads. The limit stands in the record too. Ten states answered back rejecting the resolutions, and no state joined them, so in 1799 the doctrine persuaded almost no one beyond its authors.

6. How did the election of 1800 deadlock, how was the transfer secured, and what did the Twelfth Amendment change? Every Republican elector voted for both Jefferson and Burr, so the two men tied at seventy three electoral votes and the election went to the Federalist House of Representatives. The House deadlocked through thirty five ballots in February 1801 while some Federalists schemed to install Burr, and Hamilton argued for Jefferson as the lesser danger. On the thirty sixth ballot the transfer was secured. Adams left Washington before dawn on inauguration day and Jefferson walked to his swearing in, and in his First Inaugural he said that every difference of opinion is not a difference of principle. A party that had jailed its opponents surrendered power to them on the strength of ballots, with no soldier in the street. The Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, separated the ballots for president and vice president so the tie of 1800 could not happen again.

7. What did Jefferson's program unwind, and what inheritance from Adams could he not unwind? Jefferson let the Sedition Act expire and pardoned its prisoners, restored the naturalization wait to five years, repealed the whiskey excise, and cut the army and the navy. At the Treasury, Gallatin ran the government on customs duties and land sales while paying down the national debt Hamilton had funded, treating the debt as a moral burden. The whole program rested on the strict reading of the Constitution Jefferson had wielded against the Bank. The inheritance he could not unwind was Adams's Judiciary Act of 1801 and its midnight judges, commissions signed and sealed in the presidency's last days. One commission, William Marbury's, sat undelivered in the secretary of state's office, and Marbury's suit over it reached the Supreme Court in 1803.

8. How did Marbury v. Madison surrender the case and claim judicial review in the same opinion? Chief Justice Marshall, Adams's own secretary of state and a Federalist holding office for life, faced a trap. Ordering Secretary of State Madison to deliver the commission would invite refusal and weaken the Court, while surrendering openly would weaken it further. The opinion of February 1803 held that Marbury deserved his commission, then held that the law granting the Supreme Court original jurisdiction in such cases was itself unconstitutional, so the commission could not be ordered after all. The Court gave up the case in front of it and claimed in the same opinion the power of judicial review, the authority to declare acts of Congress void. No president dared attack the claim, because the decision had given Jefferson the only outcome he wanted, which was keeping Marbury off the bench.

9. Why did Jefferson sign the Louisiana Purchase despite his constitutional scruples, and what did the treaty do? Napoleon, his American empire broken in Haiti and war with Britain resuming, offered not New Orleans alone but the whole of Louisiana. Jefferson stated his scruple honestly. The Constitution gave no express power to acquire territory or to govern it, and he drafted an amendment to supply the power, then abandoned the draft as the Senate pressed for speed, because delay risked the offer itself. The treaty was signed in April 1803 for fifteen million dollars and roughly doubled the national territory in a single signature. The parties traded readings as they crossed. The Federalists who had preached broad construction discovered strictness, and Jefferson, who had built a career on strictness, signed anyway. The flag went up at New Orleans in December 1803 over a territory of some fifty thousand people, many of them enslaved, governed by appointment from Washington.

10. What did Jefferson's instructions order the Corps of Discovery to seek, and how were the Native nations described in them? Jefferson's instructions to Lewis of June 1803 named the objects plainly. The Corps was to find a water communication across the continent for commerce, to gain knowledge of the country and its peoples, and to establish relations with the Native nations whose land it all was. The president wrote of those nations as sovereigns to be treated with, in the same years his government measured their country for sale. The Corps of Discovery left St. Louis in May 1804, some forty men, soldiers and interpreters and Clark's enslaved man York among them, ascending the Missouri against the current. Its journals were kept as an instrument of the errand, every river named, every nation counted, every plant and animal described for a government that meant to use the record.

11. How did Sacagawea and York each change what the expedition could do, and how was their service answered? The Corps hired the trader Toussaint Charbonneau largely because his young wife Sacagawea, a Shoshone woman taken captive by the Hidatsa as a girl, could interpret, and because a party traveling with a woman and an infant read to the nations it met as a party of peace rather than war. In August 1805, interpreting at the meeting with the Shoshone, Sacagawea recognized the chief Cameahwait as her brother, and the expedition's horses and its passage west were obtained in the space of that recognition. York hunted, scouted, and labored through the whole crossing, and at the vote on the winter camp at Fort Clatsop both York and Sacagawea voted, a ballot the journals record without comment in a nation where neither could vote. On the return York asked Clark for his freedom and was refused for years.

12. Why does the lesson read the expedition as a survey of other peoples' countries, and what does the Treaty of Greenville show about the decade before it? The river country was inhabited country. The Mandan and Hidatsa villages where the Corps wintered in 1804 and 1805 were towns larger than St. Louis, farmers and traders whose hospitality the expedition's survival depended on, and the Teton Sioux confrontation on the river showed diplomacy running at the edge of violence. Behind the whole story stood the treaty system. By the Treaty of Greenville of 1795, the Western Confederacy, defeated at Fallen Timbers, ceded most of present Ohio, and that instrument, purchase and pressure and cession lines, was how the United States had been taking the West for a decade before the Corps set out. The expedition therefore reads as a survey of other peoples' countries by a republic that had already decided what the survey was for, and its maps redrew the continent's geography for the government that had bought a claim on paper.

13. What grievances made the war party's case, and why did the embargo fail as a peaceful weapon? Britain ruled the seas and France the continent, American ships were seized by both sides, and the Royal Navy practiced impressment, stopping American vessels and carrying off seamen it claimed as British subjects, some of them American citizens, thousands taken across the years. Jefferson's answer was the Embargo Act of 1807, closing every American port in the hope of starving Britain and France into respecting neutral rights without a shot. The exports of the United States collapsed by four fifths, New England's harbors sat idle while its smugglers worked, and the law was enforced with a coercion at home that sat strangely on a republic of limited government. The embargo was repealed in 1809 with the grievances untouched, and its failure is the ground the war argument of 1812 stands on.

14. How did the West's war join the maritime grievances in the declaration of 1812? Settlers pressed into Indiana and Ohio against treaties the Native nations read as frauds. Tecumseh built a confederacy on the claim that no single nation could sell land held by all in common, while his brother Tenskwatawa, the Prophet, preached renewal at Prophetstown. Governor William Henry Harrison marched on the town in Tecumseh's absence and fought the Battle of Tippecanoe in November 1811, after which the confederacy turned openly to the British in Canada. In Congress the War Hawks, young congressmen led by Henry Clay of Kentucky and John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, argued that Canada could be taken by marching and that national honor was itself a cause. Madison's war message of June 1, 1812 laid impressment, the Orders in Council, and the blockades before Congress, and the vote was the closest war vote in the nation's history, every Federalist and the commercial Northeast against.

15. What did the Treaty of Ghent settle, and why did the timing of Hartford and New Orleans ruin the Federalists? The war's invasions of Canada failed year after year, York was burned and Washington was burned in answer, and the fighting at sea and on the lakes went better than the fighting on land. New England's Federalists met at Hartford through December 1814, where the moderates carried amendments instead of secession, and the convention's report traveled south at exactly the wrong moment. The Treaty of Ghent, signed December 24, 1814, restored the boundaries of 1811 and said nothing about impressment or neutral rights, because Napoleon was beaten and the grievances had died with his wars. Andrew Jackson, not knowing of the peace, destroyed a British army below New Orleans on January 8, 1815. Peace and victory arriving together destroyed the Hartford report's authors, and the Federalist Party never recovered its standing.

16. What did the nationalist program of 1816 enact, and what did Clay's American System add to it? The war had taught that a government without a bank, a tariff, or roads had nearly lost a winnable war. In 1816 President Madison, a Republican, signed the charter of the Second Bank of the United States for twenty years and the Tariff of 1816, the first tariff laid avowedly for protection, enacting a generation late the program Hamilton had built. Henry Clay's American System stated the program in full. A national bank would give the currency order. A protective tariff would shelter manufactures until they could stand, and its revenue would be spent on internal improvements, roads and canals binding the sections into one market, the West's food feeding the East's mill towns and the South's cotton clothing both, each section prospering by the others' demand, an economic union offered as the answer to the political union's fragility.

17. How did the presidential vetoes and the Erie Canal together show the constitutional brake on internal improvements? Madison vetoed the Bonus Bill on his last day in office in 1817, holding that the power to build roads and canals was nowhere granted to Congress and would require a constitutional amendment, for all his agreement that the roads were needed. Monroe vetoed in the same line in 1822. The brake came from the program's own friends, and it held at the federal level for the whole era. The states built what the federal government would not. New York broke ground on the Erie Canal in 1817 with the state treasury's money, and the era's greatest internal improvement arrived by state action while the constitutional argument stood unresolved. The lesson's point is the split itself, a nation agreed on the need and divided on which government the Constitution allowed to meet it.

18. What did the Panic of 1819 teach westerners, and how did McCulloch and Gibbons widen the national government's reach? Land had been bought on credit at prices the cotton boom floated, and in 1819 the Second Bank under new management called in loans to save itself. State banks failed, western farms were foreclosed by the national bank its debtors now cursed, and a generation of westerners learned to hear the word bank as an enemy's name. In the same years the Marshall Court built the nationalist reading into law. McCulloch v. Maryland in 1819 struck down Maryland's tax on the Bank's Baltimore branch, held the Bank constitutional under powers implied from the great ends of the document, and ruled that the power to tax involves the power to destroy. Gibbons v. Ogden in 1824 read commerce among the states to include navigation and intercourse, not traffic alone, and struck down a state steamboat monopoly on New York's waters.

19. Why did John Quincy Adams argue that the United States should declare its principles alone rather than jointly with Britain? Spain's American empire was dissolving in revolution, the United States recognized the new republics in 1822, the Adams-Onís Treaty of 1819 had already taken Florida and drawn a line of claims to the Pacific, and Russia's ukase of 1821 claimed the Pacific coast down to the 51st parallel. Britain, whose trade wanted the new republics open and the old empires shut out, proposed through Foreign Secretary Canning a joint declaration with the United States. Adams argued in cabinet that his country should declare its principles itself rather than come in, in his phrase, as a cockboat in the wake of the British man of war. Monroe took the advice, and the declaration went out in the president's annual message of December 2, 1823 as an American statement, not a British partnership.

20. State the Monroe Doctrine's declarations, and state honestly what weight the declaration carried in 1823. Monroe declared that the American continents, by the free and independent condition they had assumed and maintain, were no longer subjects for future colonization by any European power. He declared that any attempt by the European powers to extend their system to any portion of this hemisphere would be viewed as dangerous to the peace and safety of the United States. In the other direction, he declared that the United States would not interfere with Europe's existing colonies or with its internal concerns. The weight in 1823 was smaller than the words. The United States could not have enforced the declaration alone, and it floated on the British navy, whose interest ran the same way. It endured because later generations kept choosing to stand on it.

21. State the Missouri Compromise in its three parts, and give the warning Jefferson sent with it. Missouri applied for statehood in 1819 as a slave state with the Senate balanced at eleven free and eleven slave, and Representative Tallmadge's amendment barring more enslaved people from Missouri and freeing those born there at twenty five passed the House on sectional lines and died in the Senate. The settlement of 1820 had three parts. Maine entered as a free state. Missouri entered as a slave state, its constitution's bar on free Black citizens noted and fudged rather than settled. Thomas's amendment drew the line of thirty-six thirty across the Louisiana Purchase lands, slavery barred north of it and permitted south, and the line held for a generation because it gave both sections a map they could stop arguing over. Jefferson, writing to John Holmes from Monticello in April 1820, called the question a fire bell in the night and the knell of the Union, a geographical line coinciding with a marked principle, moral and political, that once conceived would never be obliterated.

22. Why did Texas stand outside the Union for nine years, and how did annexation finally arrive in 1845? American settlers invited into Mexican Texas in the 1820s increasingly ignored the conditions Mexico had set, slavery among them, and Texas revolted in 1836 and won its independence at San Jacinto. The Republic of Texas then stood outside the Union for nine years, recognized and courted by Britain and France, and unadmitted because every president from Jackson to Tyler could count. A Texas admitted was a slave state admitted, and the Senate's balance and the northern House stood in the way. Annexation arrived in 1845 by joint resolution, a simple majority of both houses doing what a treaty needing two thirds of the Senate could not, in the last days of Tyler's presidency and with Polk elected on expansion. The method is part of the lesson, because it showed how a determined majority could widen the Union around the Constitution's treaty gate.

23. How did Polk's handling of Oregon differ from his handling of Mexico, and what did his war message claim? The Oregon Country had been jointly occupied with Britain since 1818, and American expansionists campaigned on the cry of fifty-four forty or fight. Polk, with a war opening against Mexico, settled Oregon at the 49th parallel by treaty in 1846, halving his claim against Britain while taking half of Mexico by war, and the difference between the two negotiations is part of the lesson. Against Mexico, Polk ordered American troops into the ground between the Nueces and the Rio Grande that both nations claimed, and after the Thornton affair's bloodshed on the Rio Grande in April 1846 his message told Congress that Mexico had shed American blood on American soil. A young congressman named Abraham Lincoln answered later with his spot resolutions, demanding that the president name the exact spot where the blood had been shed.

24. What did the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo settle, and what question did the Wilmot Proviso leave standing? The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo of February 2, 1848 confirmed the Rio Grande as Texas's boundary and transferred California, New Mexico, and the country between to the United States for fifteen million dollars and the assumption of claims, some five hundred thousand square miles added in a signature. The war behind it had been won in every campaign, by Taylor in the north, Scott from Veracruz to Mexico City, and Kearny and Frémont in New Mexico and California. David Wilmot's amendment of 1846, offered while the war still ran, would have barred slavery in any territory taken from Mexico. It passed the House again and again on sectional votes and died in the Senate every time. The question the Missouri line had answered for the Louisiana lands therefore stood unanswered for the Mexican cession, and the unit closes with it standing alone on the table of the enlarged house.
