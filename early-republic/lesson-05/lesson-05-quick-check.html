Quick Check: Madison's War Message, 1812

Directions: Read the war message excerpt and the background, then answer the questions.

Background. On June 1, 1812, President James Madison sent Congress a message laying out the grievances against Great Britain in the order his administration ranked them. Impressment came first. The text is quoted verbatim from the audited TPSP record madison-war-message-1812; the edition named in that record is Founders Online, National Archives, with original spelling preserved. Cuts are marked with ellipses. Congress declared war on June 18, 1812, by the closest war vote in the nation's history.

"British cruisers have been in the continued practice of violating the American flag on the great high way of nations, and of seizing and carrying off persons sailing under it . . ."

"The practice, hence, is so far from affecting British subjects alone, that under the pretext of searching for these, thousands of American Citizens, under the safeguard of public law, and of their national flag, have been torn from their country, and from every thing dear to them; have been dragged on board ships of war of a foreign nation; and exposed, under the siverities of their discipline, to be exiled to the most distant and deadly climes, to risk their lives in the battles of their oppressors, and to be the melancholy instruments of taking away those of their own brethren. . . ."

"We behold, in fine, on the side of Great Britain a state of war against the United States; and on the side of the United States, a state of peace towards Great Britain."

1. Define impressment as the guided notes state it. Then name the claim Britain made for the practice, and the word Madison uses to answer that claim.

2. State, in the message's sequence, three things that happened to the American citizens who were taken. What safeguard does the message say they sailed under?

3. The peaceful weapon had already failed. Name the act, its year, what it did to American exports within a year, and the year Congress repealed it.

4. The war these grievances named ended at Ghent. State the treaty's date, what it restored, and the two questions it said nothing about. Then name the battle fought after the signing, its date, and why it was fought at all.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Define impressment and name the claim and the answer. The definition must match the guided notes: the British practice of stopping American ships at sea and forcing sailors, including American citizens, into service in the Royal Navy, one of the grievances Madison's war message laid before Congress in 1812. Britain's claim was a right to search neutral vessels for British subjects and take them back. Madison's answer is carried in one word, pretext: the searching was the cover, and the practice in fact took thousands of American citizens. Credit requires the definition, the claim, and the word. (Blueprint 9)

2. State three things that happened to the men taken, and their safeguard. In the message's sequence, the men were torn from their country and from everything dear to them, dragged on board ships of war of a foreign nation, and exposed under the severities of naval discipline to exile in distant and deadly climates, to risking their lives in their oppressors' battles, and to killing their own brethren. Any three in order earn the point. The safeguard named is double: public law and their national flag. (Blueprint 9)

3. Name the failed peaceful weapon and its record. The Embargo Act of 1807 closed every American port to foreign trade entirely, to force Britain and France to respect American neutral rights without a war. American exports collapsed by about four fifths within a year, and Congress repealed the act in 1809 with the grievances untouched. All four facts are required, because the item measures the embargo as the war's failed alternative. (Blueprint 9)

4. State the Ghent settlement and the battle after it. The Treaty of Ghent was signed on December 24, 1814. It restored the boundaries and possessions of 1811 and said nothing about impressment or neutral rights, the two questions over which the war had been declared, because Napoleon's defeat had ended the European war behind them. The Battle of New Orleans was fought on January 8, 1815, after the signing, because the treaty was on a ship crossing the Atlantic and neither army below New Orleans knew the war had ended on paper. (Blueprint 10)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Question 1 is the close reading diagnostic: students who cannot find the word pretext have read the message as a list and missed its argument. Questions 1 through 3 predict success on the blueprint 9 half of the unit test, and question 4 on the blueprint 10 half, because each requires the grievance, the failed alternative, and the settlement in the student's own words.
