The War of 1812

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Caught Between Empires at Sea

Europe had been at war since 1803, and the United States tried to trade with both sides of it. Britain ruled the seas and France held much of the continent, and each tried to starve the other by cutting off its commerce. British Orders in Council and French decrees together declared much of American trade illegal, and the warships of both nations seized American ships and cargoes. Britain added a grievance France did not practice. Royal Navy officers stopped American vessels on the high seas and carried off sailors they claimed as British subjects, forcing them into British service. That practice was impressment. Across the years thousands of men were taken, among them American citizens whose papers proved their birth and who were taken anyway.

B. The Peaceful Weapon That Failed

Jefferson answered with a peaceful weapon. The Embargo Act of December 1807 closed every American port to foreign trade, on the wager that Britain and France, cut off from American food and goods, would respect neutral rights without a shot fired. American exports collapsed by about four fifths in a year. New England harbors stood idle, ships rotted at their wharves, and smugglers ran goods across the Canadian line and out of hidden coves. Enforcing the law took federal officers, courts, and troops turned against American citizens, a coercion at home that sat strangely on a republic of limited government. Congress repealed the embargo in 1809. The grievances it was built to cure stood untouched.

C. The West's War Inside the War

On the frontier the coming war had a second cause. Settlers pressed into Ohio and Indiana under treaties that Native nations read as frauds, each cession signed by a few chiefs and paid for once while the land was gone for good. The Shawnee leader Tecumseh built a confederacy on one claim, that the land belonged to all Native nations in common and no single nation could sell what all held together. His brother Tenskwatawa, called the Prophet, preached renewal at Prophetstown on the Tippecanoe River. In November 1811, while Tecumseh traveled south seeking allies, Governor William Henry Harrison marched on Prophetstown and fought the Battle of Tippecanoe, then burned the town. Tecumseh's confederacy turned openly to the British in Canada.

D. The Declaration of 1812

The men who carried the war through Congress were young. The War Hawks, led by Henry Clay of Kentucky and John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, argued that Canada could be taken by marching, that impressment and the Orders in Council had made submission a habit, and that national honor was itself a cause worth an army. On June 1, 1812, President Madison laid the grievances before Congress in the order his administration ranked them, impressment first, then the Orders in Council and the pretended blockades that plundered American commerce. Congress voted for war in the closest war vote in the nation's history. Every Federalist voted no, and the commercial Northeast stood nearly united against a war its own grievances had named.

E. Washington Burned, Baltimore Held

The invasions of Canada failed year after year, at Detroit, on the Niagara, and toward Montreal, while the war at sea and on the lakes went better, in single ship victories by the frigate Constitution and in Oliver Hazard Perry's victory on Lake Erie in 1813. American troops burned York, the capital of Upper Canada, in 1813. In August 1814 a British army answered by burning Washington, and Dolley Madison carried the Lansdowne portrait of George Washington out of the President's House ahead of the fire. In September the fleet bombarded Fort McHenry at Baltimore through a night of rockets and shells, and Francis Scott Key, watching from a British ship, wrote the verses that became the Star-Spangled Banner. In the South, Andrew Jackson defeated the Red Stick Creeks at Horseshoe Bend in 1814, and the treaty that followed took twenty three million acres of Creek land, the war's largest single land transfer, falling on a Native nation some of whose people had fought beside him.

F. Hartford, Ghent, and New Orleans

New England's opposition deepened as the war dragged. Its governors refused federal calls on their militia, its merchants traded with the enemy, and in December 1814 its Federalists met at Hartford. The convention's moderates won amendments to the Constitution instead of secession, and its report started south at the worst possible moment. Peace commissioners signed the Treaty of Ghent on December 24, 1814, restoring the boundaries of 1811 and saying nothing about impressment or neutral rights, because Napoleon was beaten and the grievances had died with his wars. News crossed the Atlantic slowly. On January 8, 1815, not knowing a treaty had been signed, Andrew Jackson destroyed a British army below New Orleans in the war's most lopsided battle.

G. What the War Settled

On paper the war settled none of its stated causes. In fact it settled nearly everything around them. Canada was confirmed beyond American reach. The Native confederacies of the West were broken for good, and the road of settlement stood open across the Old Northwest. The Federalist Party, ruined by the timing of the Hartford report, never recovered as a national party. A generation of officers and voters came home persuaded that the republic had fought Britain to a draw and won its independence a second time, and Andrew Jackson came home the most famous soldier in the country, carrying a political future no one in 1812 had planned for him.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the grievances ranked first and second, and explain each. Madison's war message of June 1, 1812 ranked impressment first. Under impressment, Royal Navy officers stopped American vessels on the high seas and carried off sailors they claimed as British subjects, forcing them into British service; thousands were taken across the years, including American citizens carrying papers that proved their birth. Ranked with it came the Orders in Council, Britain's system of pretended blockades, which declared much of neutral commerce illegal and plundered American trade in every sea, striking the agricultural and maritime interests of the United States. France's decrees seized American ships too, and full credit notes that the grievances ran against both empires while the war was declared against Britain alone.

2. Explain Jefferson's wager behind the Embargo Act. The wager was that Britain and France, cut off from American food, cotton, and goods, would respect American neutral rights without a war, because their colonies and armies needed American supplies more than the United States needed their markets. The record ran the other way. American exports collapsed by about four fifths within a year, New England harbors stood idle, and smuggling across the Canadian line and out of coastal coves made the law a sieve. Enforcing it required federal officers, courts, and troops used against American citizens, a domestic coercion that sat strangely on Jefferson's republic of limited government. Congress repealed the embargo in 1809 with impressment and the Orders in Council untouched.

3. State Tecumseh's claim and the sequence at Prophetstown. Tecumseh's claim was that the land belonged to all Native nations in common, so no single nation, and no few chiefs signing one treaty, could sell what all held together; the separate cessions by which the United States had taken Ohio and Indiana were therefore void in his argument. His brother Tenskwatawa, the Prophet, preached renewal at Prophetstown on the Tippecanoe River, the confederacy's town. In November 1811, while Tecumseh was in the South seeking allies among the Creeks and others, Governor William Henry Harrison marched on Prophetstown. His army fought the Battle of Tippecanoe against the confederacy's warriors and then burned the town, and Harrison claimed a victory that made his reputation. Afterward Tecumseh's confederacy turned openly to the British in Canada, and Tecumseh himself died fighting beside them at the Thames in 1813.

4. Set the two burnings beside Fort McHenry and Horseshoe Bend. American troops burned York, the capital of Upper Canada, in 1813, and in August 1814 a British army burned Washington in answer, firing the Capitol and the President's House. Dolley Madison carried the Lansdowne portrait of George Washington out of the President's House ahead of the fire. At Baltimore in September 1814 the fleet bombarded Fort McHenry through a night of rockets and bursting shells and failed to take it, and Francis Scott Key, watching from a British ship where he had gone to negotiate a prisoner's release, wrote the verses that became the Star-Spangled Banner. At Horseshoe Bend in 1814 Andrew Jackson defeated the Red Stick Creeks, and the Treaty of Fort Jackson that followed took twenty three million acres of Creek land, the war's largest single land transfer, taken from a nation some of whose people had fought beside Jackson against the Red Sticks.

5. Answer the lesson question using Ghent, Hartford, and New Orleans together. Strong answers hold the paper and the fact apart. The United States went to war over impressment, the Orders in Council, and the blockades, with western land hunger and national honor carrying the vote. The Treaty of Ghent, signed December 24, 1814, settled none of those causes in words; it restored the boundaries of 1811 and fell silent on neutral rights because Napoleon's defeat had ended the European war that produced the grievances. The Hartford Convention's report, proposing amendments as its moderates' victory over secession talk, reached the country at the same moment as the peace and Jackson's victory at New Orleans on January 8, 1815, fought after the treaty was signed because the news had not crossed the Atlantic. Together the timing ruined the Federalists, confirmed Canada, left the western confederacies broken, and let a generation believe independence had been won a second time. A paragraph that reports only the treaty's silence, or only the victory's fame, has answered half the question.
