Guided Notes: The War of 1812

Lesson question: Why did the United States go to war with Britain again, and what did the war actually settle?

Unit question: How did the United States turn its Constitution into a working government, and what did continental expansion do to the republic that government served?

Key Terms

impressment: the British practice of stopping American ships at sea and forcing sailors, including American citizens, into service in the Royal Navy, one of the grievances Madison's war message laid before Congress in 1812

embargo: a government ban on trade, the policy of the Embargo Act of 1807, which closed American ports to foreign trade entirely in the hope of forcing Britain and France to respect American neutral rights without a war

War Hawks: the young congressmen, Henry Clay and John C. Calhoun among them, who pressed for war with Britain in 1811 and 1812 to end impressment, defend national honor, and conquer Canada

Tecumseh: the Shawnee leader who built a confederacy of Native nations in the West to resist American land cessions, traveled as far south as the Creek country seeking allies, and joined Britain in the War of 1812, dying at the Thames in 1813

Tippecanoe: the battle of November 1811 in which William Henry Harrison's army fought Tecumseh's confederacy at Prophetstown while Tecumseh was away, burned the town, and claimed a victory that made Harrison's reputation

Hartford Convention: the meeting of New England Federalists at Hartford in December 1814 and January 1815, which proposed constitutional amendments and hinted at a separate peace, and whose report reached Washington just as news of peace and New Orleans destroyed the party's standing

Treaty of Ghent: the treaty signed at Ghent on December 24, 1814, that ended the War of 1812 by restoring the boundaries and possessions of 1811, settling none of the questions of impressment or neutral rights over which the war had been declared

Summary table. The causes at sea: Britain and France, at war since 1803, seized American ships under the Orders in Council and the French decrees, and Britain impressed American sailors into the Royal Navy; the grievances settled nothing before 1812 and were ranked, impressment first, in Madison's message. The embargo: Jefferson closed every port in December 1807 to force respect for neutral rights without a war; exports fell by about four fifths, smuggling spread, enforcement turned federal power on citizens, and Congress repealed the act in 1809 with the grievances untouched. The western war: Tecumseh claimed the land was held by all Native nations in common and built a confederacy to stop the cessions; Harrison fought at Tippecanoe and burned Prophetstown in November 1811, and the confederacy turned to Britain. The war fought: the Canada invasions failed, York and Washington were burned in turn, Fort McHenry held and Key wrote his verses, and Jackson's Horseshoe Bend victory took twenty three million acres of Creek land. The settlement: Ghent restored the boundaries of 1811 and said nothing about impressment or neutral rights, the Hartford report's timing ruined the Federalists, New Orleans made Jackson famous after the peace was signed, and in practice Canada stood confirmed and the western confederacies stood broken.

Exit ticket 1. Impressment is the British practice of stopping American ships at sea and forcing sailors, including American citizens, into service in the Royal Navy. Madison ranked it first among the grievances in his war message of June 1, 1812, ahead of the Orders in Council and the blockades.

Exit ticket 2. Tecumseh claimed the land belonged to all Native nations in common, so no single nation could sell it and the separate cessions were void. In November 1811, while Tecumseh traveled south seeking allies, Governor William Henry Harrison marched on Prophetstown, fought the Battle of Tippecanoe against the confederacy's warriors, and burned the town.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that pairs the paper settlement with a practical one. The strongest answers run from the causes, impressment and the Orders in Council, to the Treaty of Ghent's silence on both, and then to what the war settled in fact, Canada confirmed beyond reach, the western confederacies broken, or the Federalist Party ruined by the Hartford report's timing.
