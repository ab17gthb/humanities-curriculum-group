# Image Sources: Lesson 5, The War of 1812

All images are public domain as stated by the holding institution, the Library of Congress, which places no restrictions on these reproductions of works created in 1814 and about 1816. Deck crops live in slides-build/img/ and were cropped from the downloads listed here. Document copies live in assets/ and img/.

## A View of the Bombardment of Fort McHenry (fort-mchenry-bombardment.jpg; deck crops fortmchenry-port.jpg and title-169.jpg)
- Title: A View of the Bombardment of Fort McHenry, near Baltimore, by the British fleet, taken from the observatory under the command of Admirals Cochrane and Cockburn on the morning of the 13th of September 1814
- Creator: Aquatint by John Bower (the print is signed J. Bower); published in Philadelphia
- Date: Published about 1816 (print undated; the Library of Congress dates the publication to about 1816, with a later re-engraving about 1905 noted in some records; the copy downloaded here is the Library of Congress service image for digital ID LC-DIG-ppmsca-35544)
- Holder: Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division, digital ID LC-DIG-ppmsca-35544
- Source: Downloaded during this build from the Library of Congress storage service image for digital ID ppmsca.35544 (tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/ppmsca/35500/35544v.jpg, 1024 by 811 pixels), opened and verified visually as the Bower bombardment print, and cropped to the plate's image area for use. The full file page is at loc.gov under digital ID LC-DIG-ppmsca-35544.
- Public domain basis: A print published in the United States about 1816. United States works published before January 1, 1929 are in the public domain, and the Library of Congress records no known restrictions on publication for this item.
- Use rule for this lesson: the print appears on the deck's title slide, beside the Star-Spangled Banner station in the activity, and on the Baltimore slide in Section E, captioned with its maker, its date, and what it shows, the British bomb ships at work on the morning of September 13, 1814. It is a near contemporary print, not a view Key himself saw, and captions do not present it as one.

## The United States Capitol after its burning by the British (capitol-after-burning.jpg; deck crop capitol-port.jpg)
- Title: U.S. Capitol after burning by the British (the ruins of the U.S. Capitol following the British burning of August 24, 1814)
- Creator: George Munger (1781 to 1825), ink and watercolor drawing
- Date: 1814
- Holder: Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division, digital ID ppmsca.02160
- Source: Downloaded during this build from the Library of Congress storage service image for digital ID ppmsca.02160 (tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/ppmsca/02100/02160v.jpg, 1024 by 733 pixels), opened and verified visually as Munger's drawing of the fire damaged Capitol, showing damage to the Senate and House wings and the ruined link between them.
- Public domain basis: A drawing made in 1814. Copyright has long expired, the work is in the public domain in the United States, and the Library of Congress records no known restrictions on publication for this item.
- Use rule for this lesson: the drawing appears on the notebook page and on the Section E divider in the deck, captioned with its maker and its 1814 date. It shows damage and repair work, not flames, and it is not used as decoration anywhere else.

## Texts quoted in this lesson, with their editions
- Embargo Act, December 22, 1807: quoted verbatim in the activity and used in the quick check and writing task from the audited TPSP record embargo-act-1807 in early-republic.json; the edition named in that record is the Statutes at Large, by way of Wikisource. Cuts are marked with ellipses in the activity.
- Madison's War Message to Congress, June 1, 1812: quoted verbatim in the activity and the quick check from the audited TPSP record madison-war-message-1812 in early-republic.json; the edition named in that record is Founders Online, National Archives, with original spelling preserved, including the spellings siverities and distructive, which the lesson reproduces as the record carries them. Cuts are marked with ellipses.
- Hartford Convention Resolutions, January 4, 1815: quoted verbatim in the activity from the audited TPSP record hartford-convention-resolutions-1815 in early-republic.json; the edition named in that record is Wikisource. Cuts are marked with ellipses.
- Francis Scott Key, The Star-Spangled Banner, September 1814: quoted whole, all four stanzas, in the activity from the audited TPSP record star-spangled-banner-1814 in early-republic.json; the edition named in that record is the Defence of Fort M'Henry broadside, Library of Congress, by way of LibreTexts and Lumen Learning. No cuts were made.
- Tecumseh's address to Governor Harrison, Vincennes, August 1810: NOT quoted in this lesson. During this build, transcriptions were fetched and compared, including the Indiana Historical Society's digital copy of the council speeches (images.indianahistory.org, collection dc050) and the widely reprinted version carried in Samuel G. Drake's Book of the Indians of North America (1836) as excerpted in later collections. The versions differ in wording, order, and length, because Tecumseh spoke in Shawnee through interpreters and the surviving English texts are reconstructions by the American side. Because no single verbatim text from a named edition could be verified as his words, Station 3 of the activity presents the council as a labeled teaching summary, labeled on its face, stating the occasion, the Fort Wayne demand, the common property argument as the accounts agree in reporting it, and Harrison's refusal, and quoting nothing. The lesson's deck notes and activity key both state the reason for the label.

## Images searched for and not used
- A portrait of Dolley Madison by Gilbert Stuart and art of the frigate USS Constitution in action were searched for in the Library of Congress and Wikimedia Commons during this build. No file for either subject was downloaded and verified as public domain within this build (one Commons download attempt returned empty files for several candidate filenames, which were discarded rather than used), so neither subject is shown. Dolley Madison's rescue of the Lansdowne portrait and the Constitution's victories are taught in words, and no catalog number is claimed for any image not listed above.
- No image of Tecumseh is used. The best known portrait of Tecumseh is a later artist's imagining based on an 1808 sketch of another man, and this lesson does not present an imagining as a likeness. No catalog number is claimed for it, and it was not downloaded.
- No image of battle violence is used as decoration. The two prints listed above show a distant bombardment and a burned building after the fact, each captioned with its maker, date, and subject.
