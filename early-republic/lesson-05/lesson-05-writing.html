Writing Task: Won, Lost, or Survived

The prompt. Answer the question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Did the United States win, lose, or merely survive the War of 1812? Choose one verdict and defend it. A strong answer states what the war was declared for, weighs what the fighting and the treaty actually produced, and answers the strongest evidence for a different verdict. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, drawn from both halves of the bank, and explain how each piece supports your judgment.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the War File activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. In his war message of June 1, 1812, Madison ranked impressment first among the grievances, writing that thousands of American citizens had been torn from their country and dragged on board foreign ships of war. The Orders in Council and the pretended blockades, which plundered American commerce in every sea, stood with it. Congress declared war by the closest war vote in the nation's history, with every Federalist against. (Audited TPSP record madison-war-message-1812.)

2. The peaceful alternative had already failed. The Embargo Act of 1807 closed every American port to foreign trade, American exports collapsed by about four fifths within a year, smuggling spread, and Congress repealed the act in 1809 with the grievances untouched. (Audited TPSP record embargo-act-1807.)

3. In the West, Tecumseh's confederacy claimed the land belonged to all Native nations in common and that no single nation could sell it. In November 1811 Harrison's army fought the Battle of Tippecanoe and burned Prophetstown while Tecumseh was away, and the confederacy turned openly to Britain. Tecumseh died fighting beside the British at the Thames in 1813.

4. The fighting ran both ways. The invasions of Canada failed year after year. American troops burned York in 1813 and a British army burned Washington in August 1814, while Fort McHenry held at Baltimore in September and Key wrote the Star-Spangled Banner. At Horseshoe Bend in 1814 Jackson defeated the Red Stick Creeks, and the treaty that followed took twenty three million acres of Creek land.

5. New England's Federalists met at Hartford in December 1814 and proposed constitutional amendments, among them a sixty day limit on embargoes and a two thirds vote to declare war. Their report reached the country as the peace did, and the Federalist Party never recovered its national standing. (Audited TPSP record hartford-convention-resolutions-1815.)

6. The Treaty of Ghent, signed December 24, 1814, restored the boundaries and possessions of 1811 and said nothing about impressment or neutral rights, because Napoleon was beaten and the grievances had died with his wars. (Terms as stated in the guided notes definition of the Treaty of Ghent.)

7. On January 8, 1815, after the treaty was signed and before its news crossed the Atlantic, Andrew Jackson destroyed a British army below New Orleans in the war's most lopsided battle. Jackson came home the most famous soldier in the country, and a generation of voters believed the republic had won its independence a second time.

Plan Before You Write

My verdict (one sentence that chooses won, lost, or survived and gives the reason in the same sentence):

My evidence picks (bank numbers, at least one from items 1 to 4 and at least one from items 5 to 7) and what each one proves:

The strongest evidence against my verdict, and how I will answer it:

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your verdict, present the case from the causes and the fighting, present the case from the settlement, answer the strongest evidence against your verdict in one or two sentences, and end by restating what the record shows.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A verdict that chooses one of the three judgments, won, lost, or merely survived, and defends the choice rather than narrating the war. (Blueprint 9 and Blueprint 10)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, with at least one from each half. Bank items 1 through 4 carry the road to war and the fighting: Madison's ranked grievances and the divided vote, the embargo's failure, Tecumseh's claim and Tippecanoe, and the burnings, Baltimore, and the Creek cession. (Blueprint 9) Bank items 5 through 7 carry the settlement: Hartford's amendments and the party's ruin, Ghent's restoration of 1811 and its silence on the stated causes, and New Orleans fought after the signing. (Blueprint 10)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the verdict. The strongest essays weigh the paper settlement against the practical one: a treaty that settled nothing in words, beside a war that confirmed Canada, broke the western confederacies, and ended a national party, are different kinds of result, and a full answer says which kind the verdict rests on.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest evidence on another side, answered from the record rather than dismissed. An essay claiming victory must answer Ghent's silence. An essay claiming defeat must answer New Orleans and the Banner. An essay claiming survival must answer the twenty three million acres and the broken confederacies, results no mere survival explains.

Model opening for teacher reference. The United States survived the War of 1812, and the surviving changed the country more than most victories do. A full model continues in this pattern, carries Madison's grievances beside the embargo's failure and the divided vote, carries the burnings and Horseshoe Bend beside Ghent's silence and Hartford's timing, answers the strongest opposing evidence, and closes on what the record shows.

Common weak answers. A verdict with no stated causes (an account of battles with no impressment in it) has not answered the lesson question. A verdict that treats the Treaty of Ghent as an American victory document has misread it, and a verdict that treats New Orleans as the war's result without its date, January 8, 1815, after the December 24 signing, has misread the calendar the lesson is built on.
