# Gilded Age Unit — Assignment Skeleton

_Second unit for the Knoweth curriculum and planner site. Modeled on the Progressive Era pilot. Every lesson has the same working plate so teachers can pick pieces without losing the line of the unit._

## How to read this

- **HAVE** — an audited source or image already exists in the TPSP bank

## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- How did industrial growth transform American life, and who gained, who paid, and who resisted?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's working estimate is 12 instructional days before teacher picks. The unit runs from the completion of the transcontinental railroad in 1869 through the election of 1896 and sets up the Progressive Era pilot.

**The plate in this build**

1. **Reading** — 750 to 900 words with five questions and a teacher key
2. **Guided notes** — keyed to the reading and lesson, with the vocabulary set verbatim and a teacher key
3. **Activity** — stations, jigsaw, map work, simulation, debate, or structured chart work in the locked print design
4. **Notebook page** — a single organizer students keep
5. **Quick check** — a short formative tied to the blueprint
6. **Writing task** — a paragraph or short essay with a scaffold and a teacher key or rubric

TPSP source analyses and photo sets are linked where the audited bank already carries them. They are not rebuilt merely to fill a folder.

**Six state-neutral era topics**

1. Industrial growth and big business
2. Labor and the working class
3. Immigration, migration, and city life
4. The West and Native Americans
5. Race and Jim Crow after Reconstruction
6. Politics, corruption, and protest

## Lesson 1 — The Engines of Industry

**Lesson question:** Why did railroads become the engine of a national industrial economy?

**Reading scope:** Transcontinental railroads, railroad time and national markets, Bessemer steel, oil refining, electricity and other inventions, and the capital and labor needed to build at continental scale. The reading stresses systems rather than inventor biography.

**Activity concept:** Network map stations. Students trace a carload, a steel rail, a barrel of oil, and a telegram across a simplified national map, then judge which link mattered most to building one national market.

**Vocabulary:** transcontinental railroad, Bessemer process, refinery, corporation, capital, telegraph, standard time

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP railroad and industrial photographs — **LINK**.

## Lesson 2 — Big Business and Its Critics

**Lesson question:** Did big business build modern America, capture it, or do both at once?

**Reading scope:** Vertical and horizontal integration, trusts and holding companies, monopoly pricing, Andrew Carnegie and John D. Rockefeller as contrasting organizers, the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 and its weak early enforcement, and the arguments for laissez-faire, Social Darwinism, efficiency, philanthropy, and regulation.

**Activity concept:** Trust hearing simulation. Students take the parts of a small competitor, a consumer, a railroad shipper, a company executive, a worker, and a senator, then question witnesses and issue a hearing finding under the Sherman Act.

**Vocabulary:** trust, monopoly, vertical integration, horizontal integration, laissez-faire, Social Darwinism, Sherman Antitrust Act

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP Standard Oil and antitrust material — **LINK**.

## Lesson 3 — The Workers' World

**Lesson question:** Why did workers organize, and why did collective action so often end in defeat?

**Reading scope:** Factory discipline, mechanization, low wages, child labor, Knights of Labor, the American Federation of Labor, Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman. The reading distinguishes the goals of broad labor reform from craft union bargaining and explains the roles of courts, employers, troops, and public opinion in strike defeats.

**Activity concept:** Strike evidence stations. Students compare union demands, company statements, newspaper treatment, and government action at Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman, then build a verdict on why each strike ended as it did.

**Vocabulary:** union, strike, collective bargaining, injunction, Knights of Labor, American Federation of Labor, child labor

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP labor photographs and strike material — **LINK**.

## Lesson 4 — The New Immigration

**Lesson question:** Who were the new immigrants, and how did the United States decide who belonged?

**Reading scope:** Push and pull factors after 1880, southern and eastern Europe and Asia, steerage and Ellis Island, Angel Island, settlement patterns and ethnic neighborhoods, work, nativism, the Chinese Exclusion Act, contract labor restrictions, and the beginnings of broader restriction.

**Activity concept:** Arrival record jigsaw. Groups work from a passenger manifest, an Ellis Island inspection description, an Angel Island poem in translation, a restriction cartoon, and a settlement map, then assemble one account of arrival that separates inspection, admission, exclusion, and daily settlement.

**Vocabulary:** immigrant, emigrant, nativism, Ellis Island, Angel Island, Chinese Exclusion Act, assimilation

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP immigration photographs and documents — **LINK**.

## Lesson 5 — The City

**Lesson question:** How did rapid urban growth change daily life, health, and city government?

**Reading scope:** Skyscrapers, streetcars and suburbs, tenements and sanitation, water and disease, department stores and leisure, political machines and bosses, and early reform through settlement houses, municipal services, and professional city administration.

**Activity concept:** City systems stations. Students inspect housing, water, transit, and machine politics as connected systems, assign each problem to a cause, and decide whether a machine, a reform administration, or a private company was best placed to fix it in 1890.

**Vocabulary:** tenement, urbanization, political machine, boss, graft, settlement house, sanitation

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. Jacob Riis photographs and TPSP city material — **LINK**.

## Lesson 6 — The West

**Lesson question:** How did railroads and federal land policy remake the West?

**Reading scope:** Mining booms, cattle drives and ranching, commercial farming on the Plains, sod houses and dry farming, barbed wire, the Homestead Act, railroad land grants, and conflict over land, water, and grass. The reading keeps western growth tied to eastern capital and national markets.

**Activity concept:** Land claim map exercise. Students compare a mining camp, a cattle trail, a railroad grant checkerboard, a homestead claim, and a reservation boundary on one western map, then explain how one federal policy could create opportunity and conflict at the same time.

**Vocabulary:** homestead, land grant, cattle drive, mining boom, barbed wire, dry farming, reservation

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP western photographs and maps — **LINK**.

## Lesson 7 — Native Americans and the Closing Frontier

**Lesson question:** Why did the United States pursue assimilation, and what did Native peoples lose, resist, and preserve?

**Reading scope:** Post-Civil War treaty violations and warfare, the reservation system, buffalo destruction, boarding schools, the Dawes Act and allotment, the Ghost Dance, Wounded Knee, and Native resistance, adaptation, and cultural survival. The reading names specific peoples where the evidence supports it rather than treating Native Americans as one group.

**Activity concept:** Policy and consequence stations. Students trace the Dawes Act, a boarding school account, a buffalo hide photograph, and the Ghost Dance to Wounded Knee, then separate stated federal goals from documented outcomes.

**Vocabulary:** assimilation, allotment, Dawes Act, reservation system, boarding school, Ghost Dance, Wounded Knee

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP Native American sources and photographs — **LINK**.

## Lesson 8 — Race, Politics, and Protest

**Lesson question:** How did Jim Crow and economic protest shape the politics that Progressives inherited?

**Reading scope:** The retreat from Reconstruction, segregation statutes, Plessy v. Ferguson, disfranchisement devices, lynching and Ida B. Wells's anti-lynching campaign, Booker T. Washington's Atlanta Compromise and Black criticism of it, Granger laws, farm debt and deflation, the Populist Party and Omaha Platform, free silver, William Jennings Bryan, and the election of 1896. The lesson is the bridge into Progressive Era reform and its limits.

**Activity concept:** Two-front protest jigsaw. Half the class builds the case against Jim Crow from Wells, Washington, and Black newspaper evidence; half builds the farm protest case from Granger, Alliance, and Populist evidence. The whole class then compares whom each movement named as the problem, what remedy it sought, and why both changed the politics of the next era.

**Vocabulary:** Jim Crow, segregation, disfranchisement, Plessy v. Ferguson, Atlanta Compromise, Populist Party, free silver

**Plate:** reading, guided notes, activity, notebook page, quick check, writing task — **BUILD**. TPSP Jim Crow, Washington, Wells, Populist, and 1896 material — **LINK**.

## Changes from the working map

- No lesson was added, dropped, or reordered. Lesson 8 keeps the assigned combination of Jim Crow, Black protest, Populism, and 1896 because those subjects meet in the political crisis of the 1890s. Its activity is a two-front jigsaw so the race and farm protest lines do not collapse into one another.
- The six topics follow the state-neutral topic system already used by the course planner. Topic 5 is used for Native American dispossession only as a secondary tag on entry 11 because the era's federal racial policy links the subjects; the primary topic remains Topic 4.
