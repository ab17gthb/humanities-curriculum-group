The Road in Ten Steps: The Road to War

Directions: Work through the five stations in order. Each station holds one kind of evidence from the years 1935 to 1941: a neutrality law, a Senate charge, a presidential speech, a record of the turn to aid, and a law answered by its critics. Answer the questions at each station. At the end, judge the whole road, step by step, and mark the step you judge decisive.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: The Law That Barred the Door

Source: Neutrality Act of 1935, Section 1 (49 Stat. 1081). Public domain statute text, quoted in full for this provision.

That upon the outbreak or during the progress of war between, or among, two or more foreign states, the President shall proclaim such fact, and it shall thereafter be unlawful to export arms, ammunition, or implements of war from any place in the United States, or possessions of the United States, to any port of such belligerent states, or to any neutral port for transshipment to, or for the use of, a belligerent country.

The resolution that carried this section passed Congress in August 1935, as Italy prepared to invade Ethiopia. Later acts in 1936 and 1937 added a ban on loans to nations at war and barred Americans from traveling on the ships of nations at war. Notice what the 1935 text does not do. It does not name an attacker or a victim, and it gives the president no choice about the embargo once he has proclaimed a state of war.

What exactly does this provision make unlawful, and to which destinations? Answer from the text, naming the goods and both kinds of port.

The provision treats every nation at war alike. What does that design show about the purpose Congress wrote into the law? Answer in two sentences.

Station 2: The Charge Against the Merchants

Source: Teaching summary prepared for this lesson from the record of the Senate Special Committee on Investigation of the Munitions Industry, chaired by Senator Gerald Nye of North Dakota, 1934 to 1936. This station is a summary, not a transcription of the committee's report.

The Nye Committee held hearings on the arms industry and on the banks that had lent money to Britain and France before the United States entered the First World War in 1917. Witnesses described arms makers whose profits rose sharply in wartime and bankers whose loans depended on an Allied victory. From that record the committee and its supporters drew a charge that reached far beyond the hearing room: the United States had not entered the war in 1917 for the reasons its leaders gave in public. It had been pulled in by firms that feared losing their profits and their loans if the Allies lost. Newspapers carried the charge across the country in the middle of the Depression, and it hardened into a lesson. If trade and credit had carried the country into one war, then cutting trade and credit was the way to stay out of the next one. The Neutrality Acts at Station 1 are that lesson written as law.

State the committee's charge in one sentence, naming who was charged and what they were said to have done.

The station text says the charge hardened into a lesson. What was the lesson, and which law at Station 1 carried it out?

Station 3: A Quarantine for Aggressors

Source: Franklin D. Roosevelt, Quarantine Speech, October 5, 1937 (Miller Center transcript), as carried in the audited TPSP record. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

It seems to be unfortunately true that the epidemic of world lawlessness is spreading. When an epidemic of physical disease starts to spread, the community approves and joins in a quarantine of the patients in order to protect the health of the community against the spread of the disease. ... War is a contagion, whether it be declared or undeclared. It can engulf states and peoples remote from the original scene of hostilities.

Roosevelt spoke in Chicago three months after Japan opened its full invasion of China. He named no country, proposed no law, and called for no army or fleet. Newspapers and members of Congress still attacked the speech as a step toward war, isolationist organizations flooded the White House with protests, and the president let the proposal drop. He did not return to it in public.

What comparison does Roosevelt build his argument on, and what action does the comparison quietly propose for peace loving nations?

Roosevelt named no country and proposed no measure, yet the speech failed. What does its reception show about the strength of the neutrality case in 1937?

Station 4: France Falls, and the Door Opens a Crack

Source: Teaching summary prepared for this lesson from the public record of 1939 to 1940. This station is a summary, not a transcription of one document.

In November 1939, two months after Britain and France declared war on Germany, Congress replaced the arms embargo with cash-and-carry. Nations at war could again buy American arms and goods, but only if they paid cash and carried the goods away in their own ships. Because Britain ruled the Atlantic surface, British ships could come and German ships could not, so a law written in neutral words worked for one side. In May and June 1940 Germany overran France in six weeks, and Britain stood alone, short of destroyers for convoy escort and short of money. In September 1940 Roosevelt transferred fifty aging American destroyers to Britain in exchange for leases on British bases from Newfoundland to South America, acting by executive agreement without a vote in Congress. The same month Congress passed the Selective Training and Service Act, the first peacetime draft in American history. The country was still neutral in law. It was now arming one belligerent, training an army, and taking payment in bases on its own side of the Atlantic.

Cash-and-carry was written in neutral words. Why did it work for Britain alone? Name the two conditions and the fact about the Atlantic that decided the result.

Name three steps in this station that moved the United States toward war, and for each one say what changed in practice.

Station 5: Lend-Lease, and the Answer It Drew

Source: Lend-Lease Act, Section 3, March 11, 1941 (Pub. L. 77-11, 55 Stat. 31; GovInfo scan), as carried in the audited TPSP record. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

Notwithstanding the provisions of any other law, the President may, from time to time, when he deems it in the interest of national defense, authorize ... any defense article for the government of any country whose defense the President deems vital to the defense of the United States. ... To sell, transfer title to, exchange, lease, lend, or otherwise dispose of, to any such government any defense article ...

Source: Teaching summary prepared for this lesson of the America First Committee's public case, 1940 to 1941. This paragraph is a summary, not a quotation.

The America First Committee, founded in September 1940, answered that the law at the top of this station was a step toward the war it claimed to prevent. Its case ran as follows. The United States was defended by two oceans and should spend its strength on defense at home. Aid presented as short of war led by its own logic to convoys, convoys to shooting, and shooting to a full war the American people had never voted for. Its best known speaker, Charles Lindbergh, charged that the administration knew where the road led and took the country down it one step at a time. Congress debated the Lend-Lease bill for two months, the longest and sharpest foreign policy debate since 1919, and passed it in March 1941.

Under Section 3, who decides which countries receive American arms, and what test does the law set for that decision? Answer from the quoted text.

State the America First answer to Lend-Lease as a chain of steps, in the order the summary gives it.

Part 2: The Road in Ten Steps

Teaching summary of the steps the stations did not hold. In December 1940 Roosevelt's Arsenal of Democracy fireside chat promised Britain far more material support, and in January 1941 his Four Freedoms address named the aims aid was meant to defend. In August 1941 Roosevelt and Churchill issued the Atlantic Charter, a joint statement of aims signed by a neutral president alongside a nation at war. In the autumn of 1941 American destroyers escorting convoys fought German submarines, and on October 31 a submarine sank the destroyer Reuben James with 115 of her crew.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: The Law That Barred the Door

What exactly does the provision make unlawful, and to which destinations? It makes it unlawful to export arms, ammunition, or implements of war from any place in the United States or its possessions to any port of a belligerent state, or to any neutral port for transshipment to, or for the use of, a belligerent country. Credit requires all three goods and both destinations, including the neutral port clause, which closed the obvious loophole.

What does treating every nation at war alike show about the purpose? It shows the law was written to keep the United States out of war, not to judge or influence the war itself. Congress barred trade with attacker and attacked alike because contact with either side, through ships, goods, or credit, was the danger the law existed to cut off.

Station 2: The Charge Against the Merchants

State the charge in one sentence. The committee charged that American arms makers and bankers, fearing the loss of their wartime profits and their loans to the Allies, had pulled the United States into the First World War in 1917. Accept answers that name both groups and the motive. Students should also be able to say that historians dispute the charge, as the reading states.

What was the lesson, and which law carried it out? The lesson was that cutting trade and credit with nations at war was the way to stay out of the next war. The Neutrality Acts carried it out, barring arms sales first and then loans and travel as well.

Station 3: A Quarantine for Aggressors

What comparison does Roosevelt build on, and what action does it propose? He compares world lawlessness to an epidemic of physical disease, and war to a contagion that can engulf peoples far from the fighting. The comparison proposes that peace loving nations act together to quarantine aggressor nations, isolating them as a community isolates the sick to protect its own health. He named no aggressor and no method, and students should note both silences.

What does the reception show about the neutrality case in 1937? It shows the case was strong enough to defeat a suggestion. A speech that named no country and proposed no measure still drew attacks as a step toward war, organized protest, and a presidential retreat. In 1937 the burden of proof sat on anyone proposing action abroad, which is the political fact the Quarantine episode measures.

Station 4: France Falls, and the Door Opens a Crack

Why did cash-and-carry work for Britain alone? The two conditions were cash payment and carriage in the buyer's own ships. The deciding fact was British command of the Atlantic surface. British merchant ships could reach American ports and carry purchases home, while German ships could not pass the British blockade, so a formally neutral law delivered aid to one side only.

Name three steps toward war, with what changed in practice. Any three. Cash-and-carry restored legal arms sales to belligerents able to pay and carry, in practice Britain. Destroyers for bases put fifty American warships into a belligerent navy and tied American defense to British bases across the Atlantic. The Selective Training and Service Act began building a mass army in peacetime. The fall of France itself belongs in strong answers as the event that made the other three politically possible.

Station 5: Lend-Lease, and the Answer It Drew

Who decides which countries receive arms, and what test does the law set? The president decides. The test in the text is that he deems the country's defense vital to the defense of the United States, acting when he deems it in the interest of national defense. The opening words, notwithstanding the provisions of any other law, lifted the Neutrality Acts' bars for any country the president named, which students should identify as the legal hinge of the whole lesson.

State the America First answer as a chain of steps. Aid presented as short of war leads to convoys to protect the aid, convoys lead to shooting at sea, and shooting leads to a full war the American people never voted for. Credit requires the chain in order and the home defense claim, that two oceans made defense at home the safe course, stated fairly as part of the answer.

Part 2: The Road in Ten Steps (Grading Guide)

Defensible judgments, with the evidence a full credit answer cites. Step 1, H: the acts barred arms, then loans and travel, to all belligerents alike. Step 2, H: the charge taught the country that trade and credit caused entry, building support for the acts. Step 3, B: the speech proposed action against aggressors, yet its failure and retreat showed the country holding back. Step 4, B tending T: neutral in form, British in effect, because of the cash and carriage conditions and the blockade. Step 5, T as an event: the fall of France destroyed the assumption that Britain and France could hold without American help. Step 6, T: warships transferred and a peacetime army raised. Step 7, T: the president defined the war's stakes in American terms and promised the aid to match. Step 8, T: arms by presidential judgment, with the neutrality bars set aside. Step 9, T: a neutral state signed common aims with a belligerent. Step 10, T: American warships fought and an American destroyer was sunk with 115 dead before any declaration. Closing paragraphs earn full credit when the decisive step chosen is defended against a named rival. The strongest cases are usually made for Lend-Lease, because it changed the law itself, and for the Reuben James, because American sailors were dying in an undeclared war. A choice of cash-and-carry can also earn full credit if argued from the point that it first made American industry an Allied supplier in practice.
