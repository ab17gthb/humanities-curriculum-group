The Road to War: Neutrality and Its Critics

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Memory of the First War

The First World War ended in November 1918, and its bill stayed in view for twenty years. The United States sent two million men to France. About 116,000 did not come home, and 200,000 more came home wounded. When new wars opened in the 1930s, that memory stood in front of every argument about what the country should do.

From 1934 to 1936 a Senate committee chaired by Gerald Nye investigated arms makers and bankers. Its hearings charged that such firms, fearing for their profits and their loans to Britain and France, had pulled the country into the war in 1917. Historians dispute the charge, yet it persuaded millions, and Congress drew a plain lesson. Law would cut the ties of trade and credit first.

B. The Neutrality Acts

Congress wrote that lesson into the Neutrality Acts, the laws passed by Congress in the 1930s that barred arms sales and loans to nations at war, written to keep the United States out of another world war. The 1935 act barred arms exports to any nation at war once the president proclaimed a state of war. Later acts banned loans to nations at war and barred American travel on their ships.

The policy behind the acts was isolationism, a policy of avoiding political commitments and alliances with other countries while keeping a country free to act on its own. The acts treated attacker and attacked alike. Their purpose was to keep American ships, goods, and credit out of foreign quarrels.

C. Wars Americans Watched

The wars came anyway, and Americans watched them in newsreels and newspapers. Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931 and China in 1937, bombing Chinese cities and driving hundreds of thousands from their homes. Italy conquered Ethiopia in 1935 and 1936. Civil war in Spain drew German and Italian bombers to the side of Franco. Germany absorbed Austria in 1938, took the Sudetenland that fall, seized the rest of Czechoslovakia in March 1939, and invaded Poland on September 1, 1939. Britain and France declared war two days later.

Because Japan and China never declared war, Roosevelt did not invoke the acts against them, and American goods still reached China. In Europe an embargo falling on both sides fell hardest on the side that needed to buy.

D. A President Pushes Against the Current

Roosevelt doubted the acts, and he tested opinion in Chicago on October 5, 1937. Comparing aggression to disease, he proposed that peace loving nations quarantine aggressors, as a community quarantines the sick. He named no country and proposed no measure, yet newspapers and Congress attacked it as a step toward war, and he let the idea drop.

He pushed in smaller steps instead. In his fireside chats, radio addresses delivered in plain conversational language from the White House, he explained the danger. In December 1940 he told the nation it must become the great arsenal of democracy, arming Britain while staying out of the fighting. In January 1941 he named the aims worth defending, the Four Freedoms.

E. The Fall of France and the Turn to Aid

Events in 1940 did what speeches could not. Germany overran France in six weeks, and Britain stood alone, short of ships and money. In September Roosevelt sent Britain fifty aging destroyers for leases on British bases in the Atlantic, and Congress passed the first peacetime draft. In November 1939 Congress had replaced the arms embargo with cash-and-carry, the policy adopted in 1939 that let nations at war buy American arms and goods if they paid cash and carried them away in their own ships. Only Britain, ruling the Atlantic, could use it.

Cash ran out before the need did. In March 1941 Congress passed Lend-Lease, the law of March 1941 that let the president lend or lease arms and supplies to any country whose defense he judged vital to the defense of the United States.

F. The Case for Staying Out

The opposition had a case, and it deserves a fair hearing. The America First Committee, founded in September 1940 and counting hundreds of thousands of members, argued that the United States was safe behind two oceans, that its duty was defense at home, and that aid was pulling the country into a war its people had not chosen. Its best known speaker, the aviator Charles Lindbergh, charged that the administration moved toward war step by step, each step presented as short of war. Aid, the committee warned, would end in convoys, convoys in shooting, and shooting in a war fought with American lives.

G. The Charter and the Shots at Sea

In August 1941 Roosevelt met Churchill aboard warships off Newfoundland and issued the Atlantic Charter, the joint statement of aims issued by Roosevelt and Churchill in August 1941, pledging self-determination for peoples, freer trade, and freedom of the seas. A neutral nation does not usually sign statements of aims with a nation at war. At sea the fiction wore thinner. American destroyers escorting convoys toward Iceland exchanged fire with German submarines, the Greer in September, the Kearny in October, torpedoed with eleven killed. On October 31, 1941, a submarine sank the destroyer Reuben James, killing 115 of her crew. The United States was arming one side and taking losses, while its laws still called it neutral.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What did the Nye Committee charge, and what lesson did Congress draw? The committee chaired by Senator Gerald Nye investigated arms makers and bankers who had dealt with Britain and France before 1917 and charged that those firms, fearing for their profits and loans, had pulled the United States into the First World War. Historians dispute the charge, and students should say so. The lesson Congress drew was that law should cut the ties of trade and credit in advance, so that no future war could draw the country in through its commerce. That lesson became the Neutrality Acts.

2. Name three things the Neutrality Acts barred, and explain the purpose the acts were written to serve. Any three, stated accurately. The acts barred the export of arms and ammunition to nations at war, barred loans to nations at war, and barred American travel on the ships of nations at war. Their purpose was to keep the United States out of another world war by keeping American ships, goods, and credit out of foreign quarrels. The acts treated attacker and attacked alike, which students should name as a design choice, not an oversight.

3. How did cash-and-carry differ from the arms embargo, and why could only Britain use it? The embargo barred arms sales to nations at war altogether. Cash-and-carry, adopted in 1939, let nations at war buy American arms and goods if they paid cash and carried the goods away in their own ships. Only Britain could make full use of it because Britain ruled the Atlantic surface and had the ships to carry purchases home, while Germany could not send merchant ships through the British blockade to American ports.

4. State the America First case in its strongest form. Full credit answers state the case fairly, as an argument and not a caricature. The United States was protected by two oceans and should build its defense at home. Aid to Britain was pulling the country step by step into a war its people had not chosen, and each step presented as short of war made the next step easier. Aid would end in convoys, convoys in shooting, and shooting in a war fought with American lives. Answers must use at least two of these arguments.

5. By the autumn of 1941, was the United States neutral? Full credit answers argue from the record and may land on either side if the evidence is handled honestly. The strongest answers hold the two records together. In law the country was neutral: no declaration of war had passed, and the neutrality framework still stood. In fact it was arming Britain under Lend-Lease, it had signed the Atlantic Charter with a nation at war, its destroyers were escorting convoys and exchanging fire with German submarines, and the Reuben James had gone down with 115 of her crew. A paragraph that asserts an opinion without three facts from the reading earns partial credit.
