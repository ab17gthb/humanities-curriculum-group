Writing Task: Neutral in Law, at War in Fact?

The prompt. Answer the closing question of the lesson in a developed essay of about 300 words: By the autumn of 1941, was the United States neutral? A strong answer does more than state a side. It works through the road in order, from the neutrality laws to the shots at sea, and it separates what was true in law from what was true in fact. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading, stations, and documents. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording, except where a bank item quotes a document.

Evidence Bank

1. The Neutrality Acts barred arms sales and loans to nations at war, treating attacker and attacked alike. They were written to keep the United States out of another world war, on the lesson Congress drew from the Nye Committee's charge that arms makers and bankers had pulled the country into the war of 1917.

2. Cash-and-carry, adopted in 1939, let nations at war buy American arms and goods if they paid cash and carried them away in their own ships. Because Britain ruled the Atlantic, only Britain could use it in practice.

3. After France fell in 1940, Roosevelt transferred fifty aging destroyers to Britain for leases on British bases, and Congress passed the first peacetime draft in American history.

4. Roosevelt, January 6, 1941: In the future days, which we seek to make secure, we look forward to a world founded upon four essential human freedoms. The first is freedom of speech and expression, everywhere in the world. The second is freedom of every person to worship God in his own way, everywhere in the world. ... The fourth is freedom from fear, which, translated into world terms, means a world-wide reduction of armaments to such a point and in such a thorough fashion that no nation will be in a position to commit an act of physical aggression against any neighbor, anywhere in the world. (Four Freedoms address, January 6, 1941, FDR Presidential Library, as carried in the audited TPSP record. Cuts are marked with ellipses.)

5. Lend-Lease, March 1941, let the president lend or lease arms and supplies to any country whose defense he judged vital to the defense of the United States. Its operative section opens with the words: Notwithstanding the provisions of any other law.

6. In August 1941 the United States signed the Atlantic Charter with Britain. In the autumn its destroyers escorting convoys fought German submarines, and on October 31, 1941, the Reuben James was sunk with 115 of her crew. No declaration of war had passed.

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your claim, develop it with your three pieces of evidence in the order of the road, answer the other side in one or two sentences, and end by restating what the autumn of 1941 shows about the difference between a legal state and a real one.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the question directly and separates the legal record from the record in fact. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 supports the neutral starting point: the acts, their equal treatment of belligerents, and the Nye lesson behind them. (Blueprint 1) Bank items 2, 3, and 5 support the turn to aid in its legal steps: cash-and-carry, destroyers for bases and the draft, and Lend-Lease with its opening words setting other law aside. (Blueprint 2) Bank items 4 and 6 support the aims and the sea war: the Four Freedoms as a statement of what aid defended, the Atlantic Charter, and the Reuben James. (Blueprint 2) A full credit essay draws on at least two of the three groups.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive link is sequence: each step was defended at the time as short of war, and the steps together produced a country arming one side, defining common aims with it, escorting its supplies, and burying its sailors, without a declaration.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest argument on the other side, such as the formal point that Congress never declared war and the neutrality framework was never repealed outright, answered from the record rather than dismissed.

Model opening for teacher reference. In law the United States was neutral in the autumn of 1941, and in fact it was not. No declaration of war had passed, and the framework built in 1935 still stood on paper. But the paper is the weakest part of the record. A government that lends arms to one belligerent under a law opening with the words notwithstanding the provisions of any other law, signs a charter of common aims with that belligerent, and loses a destroyer and 115 sailors escorting its convoys has chosen a side in every way except the formal one. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the cash-and-carry and destroyers evidence in road order, answering the formal neutrality argument from the facts, and closing on the difference between a legal state and a real one.

Common weak answers. Opinion only (Roosevelt tricked the country, or aid was obviously right) asserts a side without working the road. Law only (no declaration was passed) mistakes the formal record for the whole record and ignores the sea war. Fact only (ships were sunk) skips the legal starting point the question asks students to weigh, and a claim with no other side stated earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a judgment between two readings of the same record.
