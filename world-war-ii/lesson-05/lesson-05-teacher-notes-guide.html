The War in the Pacific

World War II · Lesson 5 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: World War II, Lesson 5 of 8. Teach it fifth, after Lesson 4 on the war in Europe and North Africa and before Lesson 6 on the home front reckoning with democracy. The last lesson followed the war in Europe from North Africa to the surrender of Germany. This lesson follows the other ocean, from Japan's empire of spring 1942 to Iwo Jima and Okinawa in 1945.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “The War in the Pacific” and the guided notes, which follow the deck's order exactly — the empire and the distance problem, Coral Sea and Midway, Guadalcanal, island hopping on two roads, the submarine blockade, the Marianas and firebombing, and Iwo Jima and Okinawa, closing with The Pacific War at a Glance table. The activity is “The Stepping Stone Ledger: five battle records, and a verdict on the invasion's expected cost.” The notebook page charts the five steps by what each bought, cost, and taught. The quick check is “The Fleet Under Attack: the kamikaze, Midway, island hopping, and the preview.” The writing task is “Another Way to End the War: whether the cost pattern of 1945 made the search unavoidable.”

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Students need Lesson 2's Pearl Harbor and Germany first strategy, and Lesson 4's close in Europe, since Germany's surrender in May 1945 frames the summer this lesson ends in. No prior knowledge of Coral Sea, Midway, Guadalcanal, or island hopping is assumed; each is taught in sequence, with its dates and figures.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (analyze connections among events — each step of the advance buying the range the next step needed), D2.His.16 (assess options for individual and collective action — bypassing strong garrisons rather than storming them), D2.His.9 (construct arguments using historical evidence — the writing task on whether the cost pattern of 1945 made the search for another way to end the war unavoidable); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from the empire of 1942 through the turn at sea and the advance to the last battles), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task: the ledger's cost pattern argued from the record).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to trace how the Allies crossed the Pacific and state what each step cost: the empire Japan won in six months by spring 1942, the Coral Sea check of May 1942 and the four Japanese fleet carriers sunk at Midway on June 4, 1942, six months at Guadalcanal for one airfield, island hopping on MacArthur's and Nimitz's two roads, the submarine blockade of an island empire, the Marianas taken in June 1944 and the firebombing that followed, an estimated 90,000 or more people killed in one night in Tokyo in March 1945, about 6,800 American dead at Iwo Jima, and about 12,500 American dead at Okinawa against above 100,000 Japanese military dead — and to read that rising ledger as the preview planners used for invading Japan itself.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening, question, and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; the empire and distance (slides 4–5) 5 minutes; the turn at sea (slides 6–8) 7 minutes; Guadalcanal (slides 9–10) 5 minutes; island hopping on two roads (slides 11–13) 6 minutes; the strangling of an island empire (slides 14–15) 5 minutes; fire from the Marianas (slides 16–18) 6 minutes; Iwo Jima and Okinawa (slides 19–23) 7 minutes; glance, vocabulary, and the turn to work (slides 24–27) 4 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes.

Key vocabulary

Guadalcanal · Battle of Midway · island hopping · naval blockade · firebombing · kamikaze · unconditional surrender

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: the title photograph filling the slide (United States Navy Task Group 58.1 underway in the Pacific in July 1944); the unit and lesson line “World War II · Lesson 5”; the title “The War in the Pacific”; the standfirst — “Across the ocean, one island at a time, at a rising price.”

  • Picture: United States Navy Task Group 58.1 underway in the Pacific in July 1944, an official Navy photograph held by the National Archives. Caption it as a Navy task group underway in the Pacific in July 1944.
  • It opens the Pacific half of this unit.
  • The last lesson followed the war in Europe from North Africa to the surrender of Germany.
  • This lesson follows the other ocean.
  • In the spring of 1942 Japan held an empire won in six months, and the United States faced thousands of miles of open water with Japanese garrisons on the islands in between.
  • Students will trace how the Allies crossed that ocean: a turn at sea in 1942, a strategy of island hopping on two roads, a submarine blockade, a bombing campaign flown from the Marianas, and two final battles whose cost figures frame everything the next lessons take up.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the question — “How did the Allies cross the Pacific and close on Japan, and what did each step toward the home islands cost?”; below it, the unit question: “How did the Second World War transform the United States, and how far did the country live up to the democracy it claimed to defend?”

  • [Read both halves of today's question aloud,] because students should keep both in view all hour.
  • The first half asks how the Pacific was crossed, which is a question about method: carrier battles, island hopping, submarines, and bombers based ever closer to Japan.
  • The second half asks what each step cost, which is a question about figures: American dead, Japanese dead, and, from 1944 onward, civilian dead in growing numbers.
  • The lesson is built as a ledger.
  • Every step bought something the advance needed, and every step was paid for, and the price per step rose as the advance closed on the home islands.
  • The unit question runs beneath as the frame.
  • Keep it in reserve today: this lesson stays with the fighting and its cost, and the reckoning with the war's meaning belongs to the lessons that follow.

  • Sections D and E give the method of the advance, island hopping run by two commands, and the strategy underneath it, the submarine blockade that strangled an empire dependent on sea trade.
  • Sections F and G cover the closing years: the Marianas and the firebombing they made possible, then Iwo Jima and Okinawa.
  • Tell students the summary table at the end of their guided notes, The Pacific War at a Glance, asks them to rebuild this map from the record, with what each step bought set beside what it cost.

Slide 4 · Section A: An Empire Won in Six Months (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section A”; the title “An Empire Won in Six Months”; the line — “From Manchuria to the Solomons, and an ocean in the way.”

  • [Open with the speed of it,] because American planning in 1942 began from shock.
  • In the six months after Pearl Harbor, Japanese forces took the Philippines, Malaya, Singapore, and the Dutch East Indies, and pushed south through the Solomon Islands toward Australia.
  • By the spring of 1942 the empire ran from Manchuria in the north to the Solomons in the south, a chain of conquests held together by sea power.
  • Students should also hold the limit inside the achievement.
  • Japan had won territory faster than it could supply or defend all of it, and every garrison it planted on an island depended on ships to feed it.
  • That dependence is the thread this whole lesson pulls.
  • The empire looked solid on a map in 1942.
  • Its supply lines were its weak point from the first day, and the American strategy was built to find them.

Slide 5 · An empire, and thousands of miles of ocean

On the slide: five bullets — Japan took the Philippines, Malaya, Singapore, and the Dutch East Indies.; Japanese forces pushed south through the Solomons toward Australia.; The empire ran from Manchuria to the Solomons by the spring of 1942.; Germany first sent the main American effort to Europe.; Every advance needed a harbor, an airfield, and a supply line.

  • [State the American problem plainly,] because the reading states it in one word: distance.
  • Thousands of miles of ocean lay between Hawaii and Japan, and Japanese garrisons held the islands in between.
  • A land army could not march across that.
  • Every step of any advance would have to be carried by ship, landed on a defended shore, and supplied across open water for as long as it lasted.
  • The Germany first strategy, agreed with Britain and kept through the war, sent the main American effort to Europe first, so Pacific commanders fought most of 1942 with what they had on hand.
  • That shortage shaped the year.
  • The Pacific war in 1942 was a holding war fought by carriers and one division of Marines, and it very nearly was not enough.
  • Students should write the distance problem into section A of the notes, because every later section is an answer to it.

Slide 6 · Section B: The Turn at Sea (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (USS Yorktown burning after the first Japanese attack at Midway, June 4, 1942); the section label “Section B”; the title “The Turn at Sea: Coral Sea and Midway”; the caption lines — “USS Yorktown burning after the first Japanese attack at Midway, June 4, 1942.”

  • Picture: USS Yorktown burning after the first Japanese attack at Midway on June 4, 1942, an official United States Navy photograph, number 80-G-32300, held by the National Archives. Caption it as Yorktown burning at Midway on June 4, 1942.
  • Yorktown survived this attack, was hit again, and sank after the battle, the one American carrier lost.
  • Section B covers the two carrier battles of May and June 1942 that stopped the Japanese advance at sea.
  • [Teach them as a pair.]
  • The Coral Sea was a check, fought entirely by aircraft whose ships never sighted each other.
  • Midway was a decision, made possible by code breaking and paid for in the currency Japan could least afford, fleet carriers and the trained pilots who flew from them.
  • Both battles belong to blueprint entry 9, and both are tested, so students should have the dates and the figures in their notes before the section ends.

Slide 7 · A check in May, a decision in June

On the slide: five bullets — At the Coral Sea in May 1942, the Port Moresby force turned back.; The Coral Sea was the first battle fought entirely by aircraft.; Code breakers named the next target as Midway Island.; Nimitz set an ambush with three carriers.; On June 4, dive bombers caught the Japanese force in the open.

  • [Work the two battles as cause and effect.]
  • In the Coral Sea in May 1942, American and Japanese carrier fleets fought the first naval battle in history conducted entirely by aircraft, in which the opposing ships never sighted each other.
  • The United States lost the carrier Lexington, which students should hear as a real loss, but the Japanese invasion force bound for Port Moresby in New Guinea turned back, the first time a Japanese advance had been stopped.
  • A month later came the decision.
  • Code breakers had read enough of the Japanese naval code to identify Midway Island as the target, and Admiral Nimitz, instead of spreading his weaker force, concentrated his three carriers northeast of the island and waited.
  • On the morning of June 4, American dive bombers found the Japanese carrier force with its decks crowded and its fighter cover drawn low, and in a single day the battle was decided.

Slide 8 · Four carriers in one day

On the slide: the big figure “4”; the label — “Japanese fleet carriers sunk in one day at Midway”; the note — “June 4, 1942 · With them went trained carrier pilots Japan could not replace at American speed.”

  • 4 — Japanese fleet carriers sunk in one day at Midway
  • June 4, 1942
  • [Put the figure together with its meaning,] because the number alone understates the loss.
  • Japan began the Pacific war with the finest carrier aircrews in the world, trained over years.
  • Four fleet carriers went down at Midway, Akagi, Kaga, Soryu, and Hiryu, and with the ships went hundreds of those pilots.
  • A carrier can be built in a shipyard in a year or two, and American yards were already launching ships at a pace Japan could not match.
  • A veteran carrier pilot cannot be built at all.
  • He is accumulated, flight hour by flight hour, over years Japan no longer had.
  • Battle of Midway: the naval battle of June 1942 in which American carrier aircraft sank four Japanese carriers and ended Japan's ability to take the offensive in the Pacific.
  • Japan still had carriers after June 1942.
  • What it no longer had was the combination of ships, pilots, and time that an offensive requires, and it never got that combination back.

Slide 9 · Section C: Guadalcanal (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (Marines on patrol on Guadalcanal, August 1942); the section label “Section C”; the title “Guadalcanal: The First Offensive”; the caption lines — “Marines on patrol on Guadalcanal, August 1942. Marine Corps photograph.”

  • Picture: Marines on patrol on Guadalcanal in August 1942, an official Marine Corps photograph, number 50831 in the Marine Corps record as credited on its file page. Caption it as Marines on patrol on Guadalcanal in August 1942.
  • Section C covers the first major Allied offensive of the Pacific war.
  • Two months after Midway, the United States stopped reacting and chose a place to attack: a small island in the Solomons where Japan was building an airfield that could threaten the sea route to Australia.
  • What followed was not a landing and a victory parade.
  • It was six months of fighting on land, at sea, and in the air, in jungle, in heat, and under nightly naval bombardment, for one island and one airstrip.
  • [Teach the campaign as a supply contest,] because that is what decided it, and because the lesson it taught became the strategy of the whole Pacific war.

Slide 10 · Six months for one island and its airfield

On the slide: the photograph at right (Marines on patrol on Guadalcanal, August 1942); five bullets — Marines landed in August 1942 and took the airfield.; Henderson Field was held against repeated attacks.; The fleets fought offshore at night, again and again.; Japan could not feed or reinforce its troops.; In February 1943, Japan evacuated the survivors.

  • Picture: Marines on patrol on Guadalcanal in August 1942, Marine Corps photograph 50831. Caption it as Marines on patrol on Guadalcanal in August 1942.
  • Guadalcanal: the Solomon Islands campaign of August 1942 to February 1943, the first major Allied offensive of the Pacific war, fought on land, at sea, and in the air around one island and its airfield.
  • [Unpack each part of that definition with students.]
  • On land, Marines and then Army troops held Henderson Field against repeated Japanese attacks delivered through the jungle.
  • At sea, the two fleets fought a series of night battles in the waters offshore so savage that the channel earned a grim nickname among sailors.
  • In the air, the field's aircraft, the Cactus Air Force, flew by day while Japanese bombers came over from Rabaul.
  • What decided the campaign was none of these alone.
  • Japan could not feed or reinforce its troops on the island, and hunger and disease did what American firepower alone had not.
  • In February 1943 Japan evacuated its survivors by night and conceded the island.

Slide 11 · Section D: Island Hopping on Two Roads (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section D”; the title “Island Hopping on Two Roads”; the line — “Take what buys an airfield. Leave the rest to wither.”

  • Guadalcanal taught a lesson that became a strategy, and this section names it.
  • The Pacific held far more Japanese garrisons than the Allies could ever attack one by one, and Guadalcanal had shown what attacking one cost in time, ships, and men.
  • It had also shown something else: a garrison on an island is only as strong as the ships that feed it.
  • Cut the supply and the garrison is a prisoner of its own island, dangerous to anyone who lands on it and harmless to everyone who sails past.
  • Section D covers the strategy built on that insight, island hopping, and the two commands that ran it on two roads across the Pacific, MacArthur in the southwest and Nimitz in the central Pacific.
  • Students should be able to define the strategy in the guided notes wording and to explain, in their own words, why a bypassed garrison counted as defeated.
  • Ask: Why did a bypassed garrison count as defeated without a battle? Students should answer from supply: a garrison that cannot be fed cannot fight, move, or matter.

Slide 12 · Seize the airfield, bypass the fortress

On the slide: five bullets — Selected islands were taken for airfields.; Strong garrisons were bypassed and cut off from supply.; A bypassed garrison could not be fed or moved.; It waited out the war behind the advance.; Strength was spent only where it bought range.

  • island hopping: the Allied Pacific strategy of seizing selected islands for airfields while bypassing strong Japanese garrisons and cutting them off from supply.
  • [Take every clause of that definition in order,] because each one carries weight.
  • Selected islands: the Allies chose islands for what they bought, an airfield within range of the next step, not for their size or fame.
  • Bypassing: strong positions such as the great Japanese base at Rabaul were left alone, neutralized from the air and cut off, rather than stormed.
  • Cutting off from supply: American sea and air power made sure nothing reached them, and a garrison that cannot be fed cannot fight, move, or matter to the outcome.
  • The strategy traded the attacker's old problem, every fortress must be taken, for the defender's new one, every garrison must be fed.
  • Japan had planted its strength across a hundred islands.
  • Island hopping made that strength sit still and starve while the advance walked past it.

Slide 13 · Two commands, two roads, one direction

On the slide: the photograph at right (a Navy task group underway in July 1944); five bullets — MacArthur advanced along New Guinea toward the Philippines.; Nimitz advanced across the central Pacific.; The Gilberts, the Marshalls, and then the Marianas fell in turn.; Each landing put aircraft one step closer to Japan.; Each step was measured in airfield range.

  • Picture: A Navy task group underway in July 1944, the summer the central Pacific drive reached the Marianas. Caption it as a Navy task group underway in July 1944.
  • [Name the two roads clearly,] because students mix them up.
  • In the southwest, General MacArthur advanced along the coast of New Guinea toward the Philippines, the return he had promised when he was driven out in 1942.
  • In the central Pacific, Admiral Nimitz drove straight across the ocean, through the Gilbert Islands and the Marshall Islands toward the Marianas.
  • The two drives supported each other in a way a map makes plain: Japan never knew which road the next blow would come on, and it could not defend both at full strength.
  • Each landing on either road put American aircraft one step closer to Japan.
  • [Insist on the reading's measure for those steps.]
  • They were not counted in miles conquered.
  • They were counted in airfield range, in what the aircraft based there could now reach.
  • That measure explains why some famous islands were taken at great cost and others, larger and stronger, were simply left behind.

Slide 14 · Section E: The Strangling of an Island Empire (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (an American submarine on war patrol); the section label “Section E”; the title “The Strangling of an Island Empire”; the caption lines — “An American submarine on war patrol. National Archives photograph.”

  • Picture: An American submarine on war patrol in the Pacific, a National Archives record, number 520690. Caption it as an American submarine on war patrol.
  • It suits the quietest and in tonnage the deadliest campaign of the Pacific war.
  • Section E steps back from the landings to the strategy running underneath them.
  • Japan was an island empire.
  • It held oil fields, mines, and plantations across Southeast Asia and the East Indies, but none of that wealth sat in Japan itself.
  • All of it had to travel by ship to reach a Japanese refinery, factory, or kitchen.
  • Whoever cut the sea lanes cut the empire off from its own conquests.
  • That is what the submarine campaign, with surface warships and aircraft, set out to do, and by 1944 it was succeeding on a scale Japanese planners had not believed possible.
  • Students tend to remember the landings and forget this campaign.
  • The figures argue otherwise, and the next slide gives them.

Slide 15 · Cut the sea lanes, starve the empire

On the slide: five bullets — A blockade was maintained by warships, submarines, and aircraft.; Submarines did most of the work.; Merchant ships were sunk faster than yards could replace them.; Tankers went down before their oil reached a refinery.; By 1945, aircraft were mining Japan's home harbors.

  • naval blockade: the use of warships, submarines, and aircraft to cut off an enemy's sea trade and supplies.
  • [Apply the definition to Japan's particular weakness.]
  • Japan imported its oil, much of its steel, and part of its food by ship from the territories it had conquered, so the empire's wealth existed at the far end of sea lanes the United States Navy could patrol.
  • American submarines, after early war torpedo failures were fixed, sank Japanese merchant ships faster than Japanese yards could launch replacements.
  • Tankers from the East Indies went down before their oil reached a refinery, which meant the conquest that was supposed to make Japan self sufficient never delivered its promise.
  • In 1945 American aircraft added mines to Japan's home harbors, closing even coastal shipping.
  • An empire won by sea power was being strangled by sea power, and its factories slowed for want of supplies while its armies waited on islands the advance had already passed.

Slide 16 · Section F: Fire from the Marianas (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (a B-29 Superfortress of the 509th Composite Group, 1945); the section label “Section F”; the title “Fire from the Marianas”; the caption lines — “A B-29 Superfortress of the 509th Composite Group, 1945.”

  • Picture: A B-29 Superfortress in 1945, a War Department photograph held by the National Archives. Caption it as a B-29 Superfortress of the 509th Composite Group in 1945.
  • The B-29 was the largest bomber of the war, built to fly farther with a heavier load than anything before it, and for most of the war there was nowhere within its reach of Japan to base it.
  • Section F covers the capture that changed that geometry.
  • In June 1944 Nimitz's central Pacific road reached the Mariana Islands, Saipan, Guam, and Tinian, close enough to the home islands for the B-29 to fly there and return.
  • What followed was first a conventional bombing campaign that disappointed its planners, and then, in March 1945, a change of method, low altitude night attacks with incendiaries against cities built largely of wood.
  • This section carries the heaviest single figure in the lesson, and it should be taught at the pace that figure deserves, with its label kept attached: an estimate.

Slide 17 · Range first, then fire

On the slide: five bullets — Saipan, Guam, and Tinian were taken in June 1944.; The prize was range, because B-29s could reach Japan and return.; In March 1945, the campaign changed form.; Firebombing was flown low at night.; More than sixty Japanese cities burned, in whole or in part.

  • firebombing: the bombing of cities with incendiary weapons designed to start mass fires.
  • [Set the sequence before the definition.]
  • The Marianas were taken in June and July 1944 after hard fighting, Saipan against a garrison that fought nearly to the last man, a fact students should file away for section G.
  • The prize was range.
  • From fields built on those islands, the B-29 could reach Japan's home islands and return, which no base taken earlier could offer.
  • The first raids, flown high by day with explosive bombs, scattered their loads in high winds and did limited damage.
  • In March 1945 the command changed the method: bombers came in low at night, stripped of guns to carry more incendiaries, against cities built largely of wood and paper.
  • The fires did the work the bombs had not.
  • Raid followed raid through the spring and summer, and more than sixty Japanese cities burned in whole or in part before the war ended.

Slide 18 · One night in Tokyo, March 1945

On the slide: the big figure “90,000+”; the label — “people killed in one night in Tokyo, March 9 to 10, 1945, by estimate”; the note — “The record gives a range, on the order of 90,000 to 100,000 dead, and the lesson prints it as an estimate.”

  • 90,000+ — people killed in one night in Tokyo, March 9 to 10, 1945, by estimate
  • The record gives a range, on the order of 90,000 to 100,000 dead
  • [Stop on the labeling before the number,] because how this figure is stated is part of the lesson.
  • Nobody counted the dead of the Tokyo fire raid one by one.
  • The figure in the record is an estimate, a range, on the order of 90,000 to 100,000 people killed in a single night, and students should repeat it with those words attached, as the reading does.
  • What is not in doubt is the scale.
  • On the night of March 9 to 10, 1945, hundreds of B-29s dropped incendiaries on Tokyo in a low altitude attack, and the fires joined into a single storm of flame that burned out a large share of the city.
  • It was the deadliest air raid of the Second World War, deadlier in that one night than either atomic bomb would be in its first count.
  • The atomic decision belongs to lesson 8.
  • Today students need the fact and its label: by March 1945 the bombing of Japan had become a weapon against cities and the people in them, at a cost counted in six figures a night.

Slide 19 · Section G: Iwo Jima and Okinawa (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (the first flag raising on Mount Suribachi, photographed by Sergeant Louis Lowery, USMC); the section label “Section G”; the title “Iwo Jima and Okinawa: The Preview”; the caption lines — “The first flag raising on Mount Suribachi, photographed by Sergeant Louis Lowery, USMC.”

  • Picture: The first flag raising on Mount Suribachi, Iwo Jima, on February 23, 1945, taken by Staff Sergeant Louis Lowery of the Marine Corps. Caption it as the first flag raising on Mount Suribachi on February 23, 1945.
  • [Name the photographer and the service deliberately.]
  • The famous photograph of the second flag raising, later that day, was made by Joe Rosenthal, a civilian Associated Press photographer.
  • It is the better known image and it is not a federal work, so this lesson uses the Marine Corps photograph of the first raising instead, and students should know why the lesson draws the distinction in a course that works from the public record.
  • Section G covers the last two steps of the advance, Iwo Jima in February and March 1945 and Okinawa from April to June.
  • Together they changed the character of the lesson's ledger.
  • The steps stopped buying distance and started previewing the invasion of Japan itself, and planners in Washington read them exactly that way.

Slide 20 · Eight square miles, bought position by position

On the slide: the photograph at right (the first flag raising on Mount Suribachi, February 23, 1945); five bullets — The battle ran from February to March 1945, halfway to Japan.; The island was wanted for a fighter base and an emergency field for B-29s.; The garrison fought from tunnels and caves.; About 6,800 Americans died taking the island.; Nearly the whole Japanese garrison died where it stood.

  • Picture: The first flag raising on Mount Suribachi on February 23, 1945, photographed by Staff Sergeant Louis Lowery, USMC. Caption it as the first flag raising on Mount Suribachi.
  • [Give students the island's purpose first,] because Iwo Jima was taken for the bombers, not for itself.
  • Halfway between the Marianas and Japan, its airfields let American fighters escort the B-29s all the way to the target and gave damaged bombers an emergency field to land on instead of ditching in the ocean, and by the war's end thousands of airmen owed their lives to that strip of volcanic ash.
  • [Then give the cost, and do not rush it.]
  • The Japanese garrison, about 21,000 men, had dug itself into tunnels and caves and had to be destroyed position by position with flame, explosives, and infantry assault.
  • Taking eight square miles cost about 6,800 American dead, and nearly the whole garrison died where it stood rather than surrender.
  • Students should mark that last fact as carefully as the first.
  • A garrison that does not surrender, on an island of no value to anyone but the attacker, is evidence about the next island, and about the home islands behind it.

Slide 21 · 6,800 American dead at Iwo Jima

On the slide: the big figure “6,800”; the label — “American dead for eight square miles at Iwo Jima”; the note — “About 850 dead per square mile · The garrison of about 21,000 was nearly destroyed.”

  • 6,800 — American dead for eight square miles at Iwo Jima
  • About 850 dead per square mile
  • The garrison of about 21,000 was nearly destroyed
  • [Do the arithmetic on the board,] because students should see where the per mile figure comes from and check it themselves: about 6,800 American dead divided by about eight square miles of island is about 850 dead for each square mile taken.
  • Then set the Japanese figure beside it.
  • Of a garrison of about 21,000 men, about 18,000 died and most of the rest were wounded or captured only when they could no longer fight.
  • There was no surrender of the position as a position.
  • Compare this row of the ledger with the first row, Midway, where a decisive victory at sea cost about 300 American dead.
  • The difference is the lesson in one contrast.
  • A war fought by ships and aircraft at a distance and a war fought by infantry against dug in defenders on land are different wars in cost, and by 1945 the Pacific war had become the second kind, with the largest defended land of all still ahead.

Slide 22 · The largest battle of the Pacific war

On the slide: the photograph at right (the carrier Bunker Hill burning after kamikaze hits off Okinawa, May 11, 1945); five bullets — The battle ran from April to June 1945, on a home island sixty miles long.; The island was taken as the base for invading Japan.; Kamikaze attacks came in waves against the fleet.; Japanese military deaths ran above 100,000.; Okinawan civilians died in large numbers.

  • Picture: The carrier Bunker Hill burning after kamikaze hits off Okinawa on May 11, 1945, a Navy photograph held by the National Archives. Caption it as Bunker Hill burning off Okinawa on May 11, 1945.
  • kamikaze: a Japanese pilot who made a deliberate crash attack on an enemy ship; also the attack itself.
  • Okinawa was the first battle fought on a Japanese home island, sixty miles long and about 350 miles from the main islands, taken to serve as the base for the invasion of Japan.
  • Japan defended it with a large army dug into the southern hills and with kamikaze attacks flown in organized waves against the fleet that had to stay offshore and supply the landing.
  • About 12,500 Americans died, on land and at sea together.
  • Japanese military deaths ran above 100,000.
  • And Okinawa was inhabited, which the earlier stepping stones mostly were not: its civilians died in large numbers in the fighting, caught between the armies on their own island.

Slide 23 · 12,500 American dead at Okinawa

On the slide: the big figure “12,500”; the label — “American dead at Okinawa, the preview of invading Japan”; the note — “Above 100,000 Japanese military dead · Heavy civilian deaths on one island sixty miles long.”

  • 12,500 — American dead at Okinawa, the preview of invading Japan
  • Above 100,000 Japanese military dead
  • [Close the ledger with the reading planners did in the summer of 1945,] because it is the bridge to the rest of this unit.
  • Okinawa cost about 12,500 American dead.
  • Set that figure in the ledger's sequence: about 300 at Midway, about 3,400 on Saipan, about 6,800 at Iwo Jima, about 12,500 at Okinawa, each step closer to Japan costing more than the step before it, against defenders who died nearly whole rather than surrender, and, from Saipan onward, among a civilian population.
  • Japan's home islands held a far larger army and a far larger population than Okinawa's, on far larger ground.
  • Planners preparing the invasion, set for the autumn of 1945, read Okinawa as a preview and multiplied accordingly, and their estimates of the invasion's cost ran to figures that shaped every decision of that summer.
  • Add the final fact of the reading and leave it standing: blockade and bombing were starving Japan, and its government still had not surrendered, and the Allied demand remained unconditional surrender.
  • unconditional surrender: the demand that an enemy give up without negotiated conditions, announced as Allied policy for Germany and Japan.

  • The four rows are the lesson's argument in miniature.
  • The turn at sea bought the initiative, at the Coral Sea and above all at Midway.
  • The advance bought airfields, one step of aircraft range at a time, on two roads.
  • The closing bought pressure no island battle could: a blockade that starved the empire and a bombing campaign flown from the Marianas against Japan itself.
  • The last steps bought the invasion's base and its preview at the highest prices in the ledger.
  • Grade the table generously on wording and strictly on pairing: each row must say what happened and what it bought, and the last row must carry a cost figure, because cost is the point of the row.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for the turn and the advance

On the slide: four terms with their definitions — Guadalcanal; Battle of Midway; island hopping; naval blockade.

  • [Have students copy these definitions exactly,] because the unit test draws on them and the guided notes carry the same words.
  • Guadalcanal: the Solomon Islands campaign of August 1942 to February 1943, the first major Allied offensive of the Pacific war, fought on land, at sea, and in the air around one island and its airfield.
  • Battle of Midway: the naval battle of June 1942 in which American carrier aircraft sank four Japanese carriers and ended Japan's ability to take the offensive in the Pacific.
  • island hopping: the Allied Pacific strategy of seizing selected islands for airfields while bypassing strong Japanese garrisons and cutting them off from supply.
  • naval blockade: the use of warships, submarines, and aircraft to cut off an enemy's sea trade and supplies.
  • Note the order: the two battles of 1942 first, then the two strategies those battles made possible.

Slide 26 · Vocabulary: words for the closing

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — firebombing; kamikaze; unconditional surrender.

  • [Keep the definitions exact.]
  • firebombing: the bombing of cities with incendiary weapons designed to start mass fires.
  • The definition names the weapon type because the method, not only the target, is what changed in March 1945.
  • kamikaze: a Japanese pilot who made a deliberate crash attack on an enemy ship; also the attack itself.
  • Note that the definition covers both the man and the act, and that the attacks were organized policy by 1945, flown in waves, not isolated acts of desperation.
  • unconditional surrender: the demand that an enemy give up without negotiated conditions, announced as Allied policy for Germany and Japan.
  • End the vocabulary here on purpose, on the demand itself.
  • Germany had already met it in May 1945.
  • Japan had not, and the gap between the demand and the refusal is where the next lessons begin, with a government in Tokyo that would not surrender and a ledger in Washington that priced the alternative.
