Writing Task: Another Way to End the War

The prompt. By the summer of 1945, American planners were preparing to invade Japan, and they were also searching for another way to end the war. Answer this question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Did the cost pattern of 1945 make that search unavoidable? Argue from the ledger, the record of what each step toward Japan bought and what it cost. A strong answer builds the pattern first, step by step, and then judges what a planner holding that ledger in July 1945 was bound to conclude. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each one supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the Stepping Stone Ledger. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. At Midway in June 1942, American carrier aircraft sank four Japanese fleet carriers at a cost of about 300 American dead. The sea war was won at a distance, by aircraft, before the fleets met.

2. At Guadalcanal, the first landing campaign, about 7,100 Americans and about 31,000 Japanese died across six months. Many Japanese deaths came after supply failed, which taught the Allies to bypass garrisons and starve them instead of storming them.

3. On Saipan in 1944, about 3,400 Americans died, and a Japanese garrison of about 30,000 was nearly destroyed rather than surrender. Many civilians died inside the fighting.

4. Iwo Jima cost about 6,800 American dead and about 18,000 Japanese dead, nearly the whole garrison, for eight square miles of island, taken so fighters could escort bombers and damaged B-29s could land.

5. Okinawa cost about 12,500 American dead, above 100,000 Japanese military dead, and heavy civilian deaths. Japan sent kamikaze attacks in waves against the fleet. Planners read the battle as a preview of invading Japan.

6. Blockade and bombing were already starving Japan by the summer of 1945, and the fire raid on Tokyo of March 9 to 10, 1945 had killed about 90,000 to 100,000 people by estimate. Japan's government still had not surrendered, and the Allied demand remained unconditional surrender.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the question directly, unavoidable or not, and rests on the pattern across steps rather than on one battle. (Blueprint 10)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank items 1 and 2 establish the early pattern, a sea battle won cheaply and a first landing that already cost thousands and taught the bypass lesson. (Blueprint 9) Bank items 3 through 5 establish the 1945 pattern, garrisons dying nearly whole, American dead per step climbing from about 3,400 to about 6,800 to about 12,500, and civilians inside the fighting. (Blueprint 10) Item 6 supplies the other side's strongest ground and its limit at once, blockade and bombing were working on Japan and had still produced no surrender. A full credit essay uses items from both groups.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive link is projection: a planner in July 1945 did not need to imagine the invasion's cost, because the ledger's last three rows, read at the scale of the home islands, supplied it.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest argument on the other side. On one side, that blockade and bombing, given more months, might have ended the war without an invasion or a new weapon, answered from item 6, starvation was already advanced and surrender had not come. On the other side, that no cost pattern can make any particular alternative right, answered by separating the search, which the ledger compelled, from the choice, which the next lesson judges.

Model opening for teacher reference. The search was unavoidable because the ledger left a planner no honest way to expect a cheap invasion. In 1942 the war at sea had cost about 300 American dead at Midway. By 1945 the steps were landings against garrisons that died nearly whole, about 6,800 American dead for eight square miles at Iwo Jima and about 12,500 at Okinawa, with Japanese military deaths above 100,000 and civilians dying in the fighting. Japan itself was larger in every dimension of that arithmetic, and everyone preparing the invasion knew it, because they had written the figures down themselves, battle by battle. A full model continues in this pattern, adds the Saipan or Guadalcanal evidence, answers the blockade argument from item 6, and closes on the distinction between searching for another way, which the pattern compelled, and judging the way that was chosen, which belongs to the next lesson.

Common weak answers. One battle only (Okinawa was terrible) describes a cost without the pattern the prompt asks for. Figures without mechanism (the numbers rose) miss why they rose, dug in garrisons, no surrender, closing distance, and cannot support a judgment. A claim about the atomic bomb itself, defended or condemned, answers the next lesson's question, not this one, and earns partial credit unless it returns to the search the ledger produced.
