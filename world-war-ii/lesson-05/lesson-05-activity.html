The Stepping Stone Ledger: The War in the Pacific

Directions: Work through the five stations in order. Each station holds one battle record from the Pacific advance, with a short description of where the battle sits on the theater map and a dated record table. The records are labeled summaries drawn from the standard operational record, and the casualty figures are the figures of that record, stated as the record states them. At each station, enter three things in your answers: what the step bought the advance, what it cost in American and Japanese dead, and what it taught about the next step. At the end, close the ledger and judge what the invasion of Japan itself was expected to cost.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: Midway, June 1942

Source: Labeled summary prepared for this lesson from the standard operational record of the Battle of Midway, June 4 to 7, 1942. The figures below are the record's figures.

On the theater map, Midway is a small American held atoll about 1,300 miles northwest of Hawaii, at the far edge of the Hawaiian chain. Japan sent its carrier fleet there in June 1942 to seize the atoll and to draw the remaining American carriers into a battle it expected to win. American code breakers had identified the target, and Admiral Nimitz placed his three carriers northeast of the atoll to wait in ambush.

Record itemThe record
Japanese carriers sunk4 fleet carriers in one day, June 4
American carriers lost1, the Yorktown, sunk after the battle
American deadabout 300
Japanese deadabout 3,000, including trained carrier pilots Japan could not replace at American speed

What did Midway buy the advance? Answer in terms of what Japan could no longer do after June 4.

Set the two death figures side by side. Why does the record treat the Japanese figure as the heavier loss, ship for ship and man for man?

What did Midway teach about the next step? Name the advantage the United States carried into every later battle because of this one.

Station 2: Guadalcanal, August 1942 to February 1943

Source: Labeled summary prepared for this lesson from the standard operational record of the Guadalcanal campaign. The figures below are the record's figures for the whole campaign, on land, at sea, and in the air.

On the theater map, Guadalcanal sits in the Solomon Islands, northeast of Australia, along the sea route between the United States and Australia. Japan was building an airfield on the island. Marines landed on August 7, 1942, and took the airfield, named Henderson Field. For six months Japan tried to take it back, on the ground and in the night sea fights offshore, and failed to supply the troops it sent.

Record itemThe record
Length of campaignabout six months, August 1942 to February 1943
American deadabout 7,100 across the whole campaign
Japanese deadabout 31,000, many of them lost to hunger and disease after supply failed
EndingJapan evacuated its surviving troops by night in February 1943

What did Guadalcanal buy the advance? Name the airfield's value and the larger first that this campaign recorded.

What did the campaign cost each side, and what share of the Japanese loss came from supply failure rather than from battle alone?

What did Guadalcanal teach about the next step? State the supply lesson in one sentence a planner could use.

Station 3: The Marianas and the B-29, 1944 to 1945

Source: Labeled summary prepared for this lesson from the standard operational record of the Marianas campaign and the B-29 campaign, with the Tokyo death figure stated as the estimate the record gives. Photograph: United States Air Force, via Wikimedia Commons.

On the theater map, the Mariana Islands, Saipan, Guam, and Tinian, sit about 1,500 miles southeast of Tokyo. That distance is the station. From fields built on Tinian and the other islands, the B-29 could reach Japan's home islands and return, which no base taken earlier could offer. American forces took Saipan in June and July 1944 against a garrison that fought nearly to the last man.

What did the Marianas buy the advance? Answer with the one word the station text builds on, and explain it.

What did this step cost, counting both Saipan itself and the Tokyo night the B-29s made possible? Keep the two kinds of cost separate.

What did Saipan teach about the next steps toward the home islands? Use the garrison's fate in your answer.

Station 4: Iwo Jima, February to March 1945

Source: Labeled summary prepared for this lesson from the standard operational record of the Battle of Iwo Jima, February 19 to March 26, 1945. The figures below are the record's figures. Photograph: United States Marine Corps, via Wikimedia Commons.

On the theater map, Iwo Jima is a small volcanic island about halfway between the Marianas and Japan, eight square miles in all. The United States wanted it for fighter aircraft that could escort the B-29s and as an emergency landing field for bombers damaged over Japan. Its Japanese garrison, about 21,000 men, fought from tunnels and caves cut into the island.

Record itemThe record
Size of the islandabout 8 square miles
American deadabout 6,800
Japanese deadabout 18,000; nearly the whole garrison
What the field then didfighters escorted the bombers, and damaged B-29s landed there for the rest of the war

What did it cost, per side, and what does the cost work out to for each square mile of the island?

What did Iwo Jima teach about the next step? State what the garrison's conduct told planners about a landing on Japan itself.

Station 5: Okinawa, April to June 1945

Source: Labeled summary prepared for this lesson from the standard operational record of the Battle of Okinawa, April 1 to June 22, 1945, with civilian deaths stated as the record's estimates. Photograph: United States Navy, National Archives record 80-G-274266, via Wikimedia Commons.

On the theater map, Okinawa is a Japanese home island, about sixty miles long and about 350 miles from the main Japanese islands. The United States took it as the base from which the invasion of Japan would be launched. Japan defended it with a large army dug into the southern hills and with kamikaze attacks in waves against the fleet offshore, including the attack on the carrier Bunker Hill in the photograph.

Record itemThe record

What did it cost each side, including the civilians who lived on the island? Use all three figures in the record.

What did Okinawa teach about the next step? There was only one step left. State the lesson as the planners of 1945 stated it to themselves.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: Midway, June 1942

What did Midway buy? It ended Japan's ability to take the offensive in the Pacific. After June 4, Japan had lost four fleet carriers and could no longer mount a major carrier offensive; the initiative passed to the United States, which used it two months later at Guadalcanal. (Blueprint 9)

The two death figures. About 300 American dead against about 3,000 Japanese dead. The Japanese figure is heavier because it fell on the trained carrier aircrews who were the hardest men in either navy to replace. Ships could be built in a year or two. Pilots with carrier experience could not, and American yards and training schools were already outbuilding Japan. (Blueprint 9)

What it taught. Code breaking plus carrier air power could decide a battle before the fleets met. The United States carried intelligence, growing carrier strength, and replacement speed into every later step. (Blueprint 9)

Station 2: Guadalcanal, August 1942 to February 1943

What did Guadalcanal buy? Henderson Field, an airfield that guarded the sea route to Australia, and the first major Allied offensive of the Pacific war, proof that the advance could begin while the main American effort was still in Europe. (Blueprint 9)

The cost. About 7,100 American dead across the campaign, and about 31,000 Japanese dead. The record stresses that many Japanese deaths came from hunger and disease after American sea and air power cut the island's supply, so a large share of the Japanese loss was a logistics loss, not a battle loss alone. (Blueprint 9)

The supply lesson. An island garrison that cannot be supplied will lose even if it is never stormed, which is the lesson island hopping was built on. (Blueprint 9)

Station 3: The Marianas and the B-29, 1944 to 1945

What did the Marianas buy? Range. Saipan, Guam, and Tinian put Japan's home islands within round trip reach of the B-29, which no earlier capture had done. (Blueprint 10)

The cost, kept separate. On Saipan, about 3,400 American dead, and a Japanese garrison of about 30,000 nearly destroyed, with many civilians also dead. From the bases the step bought came the firebombing campaign, whose Tokyo raid of March 9 to 10, 1945 killed about 90,000 to 100,000 people by estimate. The first figure is the price of taking the ground. The second is what the ground was then used to do. Students should not merge them. (Blueprint 10)

What Saipan taught. A Japanese garrison close to the home islands fought nearly to the last man rather than surrender, and civilians died inside the fighting. Planners could expect the same conduct, on a far larger scale, closer to Japan. (Blueprint 10)

Station 4: Iwo Jima, February to March 1945

What did Iwo Jima buy? A fighter base, so escorts could fly with the B-29s to Japan, and an emergency landing field where damaged bombers could come down instead of ditching in the ocean. (Blueprint 10)

The cost. About 6,800 American dead and about 18,000 Japanese dead, nearly the whole garrison of about 21,000. On an island of about eight square miles, the American figure alone works out to about 850 dead per square mile. (Blueprint 10)

What it taught. The garrison fought from tunnels and caves and died nearly to a man rather than surrender. A landing on Japan would meet the same defense multiplied by the size of the home islands and their army. (Blueprint 10)

Station 5: Okinawa, April to June 1945

What did Okinawa buy? The base for the invasion of Japan, an island within supporting range of the home islands from which the final landings were to be staged. (Blueprint 10)

The cost. About 12,500 American dead, above 100,000 Japanese military dead, and Okinawan civilian deaths estimated in the tens of thousands and by some counts higher, with kamikaze waves against the fleet across the whole battle. (Blueprint 10)

The lesson as planners stated it. Okinawa was read as a preview. If one island sixty miles long cost these figures, an invasion of the home islands, defended by a far larger army among a far larger civilian population, would cost multiples of them. (Blueprint 10)

Part 2: Close the Ledger (Grading Guide)

Full credit verdicts work from the pattern, not from a single battle. The ledger's pattern has three parts. First, the cost per step rose as the advance closed on Japan, from about 300 American dead at Midway to about 12,500 at Okinawa. Second, Japanese garrisons stopped surrendering and died nearly whole, at Saipan, at Iwo Jima, and at Okinawa. Third, civilians entered the ledger in growing numbers, on Saipan, in the fire raids, and on Okinawa. From that pattern, American planners and commanders in the summer of 1945 expected an invasion of Japan to cost American dead in the hundreds of thousands by the larger staff estimates of the time, Japanese military dead in the millions, and civilian deaths on a scale beyond Okinawa's. Accept a range of stated expectations when the student ties the range to the ledger's pattern and names the expecters as the American planners preparing the invasion. Verdicts that give a figure with no pattern behind it earn partial credit.
