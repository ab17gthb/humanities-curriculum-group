The War in the Pacific

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. An Empire Won in Six Months

In the six months after Pearl Harbor, Japan built an empire faster than any planner in Washington had expected. Japanese forces took the Philippines, Malaya, Singapore, and the Dutch East Indies, and pushed south through the Solomon Islands toward Australia. By the spring of 1942 the empire ran from Manchuria to the Solomons.

The American problem was distance. Thousands of miles of ocean lay between Hawaii and Japan, and Japanese garrisons held the islands in between. The Germany first strategy sent the main American effort to Europe, so Pacific commanders fought most of 1942 with what they had.

B. The Turn at Sea: Coral Sea and Midway

The turn began at sea. In May 1942, American and Japanese carrier fleets met in the Coral Sea, in the first naval battle fought entirely by aircraft whose ships never sighted each other. The United States lost the carrier Lexington, but the Japanese invasion force bound for Port Moresby turned back.

A month later, code breakers learned that the target was Midway Island, and Admiral Nimitz set an ambush with his three carriers. On June 4, 1942, American dive bombers sank four Japanese fleet carriers in a single day. The Battle of Midway, the naval battle of June 1942 in which American carrier aircraft sank four Japanese carriers and ended Japan's ability to take the offensive in the Pacific, cost Japan ships and trained pilots it could not replace at American speed.

C. Guadalcanal: The First Offensive

Two months after Midway, the United States went over to the attack. In August 1942, Marines landed on Guadalcanal in the Solomon Islands, where Japan was building an airfield. Guadalcanal, the Solomon Islands campaign of August 1942 to February 1943, the first major Allied offensive of the Pacific war, fought on land, at sea, and in the air around one island and its airfield. The Marines held the airfield, named Henderson Field, against repeated attacks through the jungle, while the two fleets fought offshore at night. Supply decided the campaign. Japan could not feed or reinforce its troops, and in February 1943 it evacuated the survivors.

D. Island Hopping on Two Roads

Guadalcanal set the pattern. The Allies did not attack every island Japan held. They practiced island hopping, the Allied Pacific strategy of seizing selected islands for airfields while bypassing strong Japanese garrisons and cutting them off from supply. A bypassed garrison could not be fed, moved, or brought into the fight. It waited out the war behind the advance.

Two commands ran the advance on two roads. General MacArthur moved along the coast of New Guinea toward the Philippines, while Admiral Nimitz drove across the central Pacific through the Gilbert and Marshall Islands toward the Marianas. Each landing put American aircraft one step closer to Japan, measured in airfield range rather than miles alone.

E. The Strangling of an Island Empire

While the fleets advanced, a quieter campaign cut Japan off from its empire. The Allies built a naval blockade, the use of warships, submarines, and aircraft to cut off an enemy's sea trade and supplies. American submarines did most of the work. Japan imported its oil, much of its steel, and part of its food by ship from the territories it had conquered. Submarines sank Japanese merchant ships faster than Japanese yards could launch replacements, and by 1944 tankers were going down before their oil reached a refinery. Aircraft later mined Japan's home harbors, and its factories slowed for want of supplies.

F. Fire from the Marianas

In June 1944, Nimitz's road reached the Mariana Islands. American forces took Saipan, Guam, and Tinian after hard fighting, and the prize was range. From airfields there, the new B-29 bomber could reach Japan's home islands and return. In March 1945 the campaign changed form. The Army Air Forces turned to firebombing, the bombing of cities with incendiary weapons designed to start mass fires, flown low at night. On the night of March 9 to 10, 1945, one raid on Tokyo started a fire that killed on the order of 90,000 to 100,000 people, an estimate, in the deadliest air raid of the war. More than sixty Japanese cities burned in whole or in part.

G. Iwo Jima and Okinawa: The Preview

In February and March 1945, Marines took Iwo Jima, eight square miles wanted as a fighter base and emergency field for B-29s. The garrison fought from tunnels and caves. Taking the island cost about 6,800 American dead, and nearly the whole garrison died where it stood.

Okinawa was larger and worse. From April to June 1945, in the largest battle of the Pacific war, American forces fought for an island sixty miles long, meant as the base for invading Japan. Japan answered with the kamikaze, a Japanese pilot who made a deliberate crash attack on an enemy ship; also the attack itself, sent in waves against the fleet. About 12,500 Americans died. Japanese military deaths ran above 100,000, and Okinawan civilians died in large numbers. Planners read Okinawa as a preview of invading Japan, where the defending army and population were far larger. Blockade and bombing were starving Japan, and its government still had not surrendered. The Allied demand remained unconditional surrender, the demand that an enemy give up without negotiated conditions, announced as Allied policy for Germany and Japan.

2. Explain island hopping in your own words, and explain why a bypassed garrison was still defeated even though no one attacked it.

3. Japan had conquered an empire full of oil and raw materials by 1942. Explain how the blockade kept that empire from feeding Japanese industry.

4. What did the Marianas give the United States that no earlier capture had given it, and what change in the bombing campaign followed in March 1945?

Teacher Answer Key

1. Why did Midway end Japan's ability to take the offensive? American dive bombers sank four Japanese fleet carriers in a single day, June 4, 1942. Japan lost the carriers themselves and the trained carrier pilots who went down with them, and Japanese shipyards and training programs could not replace either at the speed American yards and schools were turning out ships and pilots. Code breaking belongs in a full answer as the cause that let Nimitz set the ambush, but the two irreplaceable losses are the carriers and the pilots. (Blueprint 9)

2. Explain island hopping, and why a bypassed garrison was still defeated. Island hopping was the strategy of seizing selected islands for airfields while bypassing strong Japanese garrisons and cutting them off from supply. A bypassed garrison was defeated without a battle because it could not be fed, reinforced, or moved, and it had no way to strike the advance that had passed it by. It spent the rest of the war waiting behind the lines while the Allies spent their strength only on islands that bought airfield range. (Blueprint 9)

3. How did the blockade keep the conquered empire from feeding Japanese industry? Japan imported its oil, much of its steel, and part of its food by ship from its conquered territories. American submarines, with warships and aircraft, sank merchant ships and tankers faster than Japanese yards could replace them, so the oil and materials existed in the empire but never reached Japanese refineries and factories. Aircraft mining of home harbors tightened the cutoff in 1945. (Blueprint 10)

4. What did the Marianas give the United States, and what change followed in March 1945? The Marianas gave range. Saipan, Guam, and Tinian put Japan's home islands within round trip reach of the B-29, which no earlier capture had done. In March 1945 the campaign changed from high altitude daylight bombing to low altitude night firebombing with incendiaries against wood built cities, opening with the Tokyo raid of March 9 to 10, 1945, which killed on the order of 90,000 to 100,000 people by estimate. (Blueprint 10)

5. Why did planners read Iwo Jima and Okinawa as a preview of invading Japan? Full credit answers argue from the figures and the pattern. Iwo Jima cost about 6,800 American dead for eight square miles against a garrison that fought from tunnels and died nearly to a man rather than surrender. Okinawa cost about 12,500 American dead, drew kamikaze attacks in waves against the fleet, and killed above 100,000 Japanese soldiers and large numbers of civilians on one island sixty miles long. Japan itself held a far larger army and population on far larger islands, so a defense fought the same way there would multiply every figure in the ledger. A paragraph that repeats the numbers without the scaling argument earns partial credit. (Blueprint 10)
