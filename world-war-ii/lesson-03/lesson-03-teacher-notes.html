Teacher Notes

World War II · Lesson 3 · Mobilizing the Home Front

These are the speaker notes from the slide deck, in slide order, set for printing. The deck itself carries the main ideas in a few fragments per slide; the teaching is here. Print this and keep it beside you while you teach. Each entry is headed by its slide number and shows what is on that slide, so you can find your place without flipping back to the screen. Vocabulary definitions match the student guided notes word for word.

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: Mobilizing the Home Front · Twelve million in uniform, 300,000 aircraft, and a ration book in every kitchen. How a peacetime economy became an arsenal.

Open with the photograph and the scale it stands for. These are Liberty era cargo ships launching at South Portland, Maine in August 1942, eight months after Pearl Harbor, from yards that had to be built or converted while the war was already running. Lesson 2 ended with an arsenal that existed mostly as a promise and a set of factory orders. This lesson keeps the promise and prices it. Students will follow three conversions at once: a small peacetime army into a force of more than twelve million, a consumer economy into the largest war production system in the world, and a population of the unemployed into drafted soldiers, riveters, welders, and migrants. Hold the closing fact in reserve from the start. The spending and direction in this lesson, not the New Deal programs of the last unit, is what finally ended the Depression, and the lesson ends by proving that claim with the unemployment figure of 1944.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: How did the United States turn a peacetime economy into the arsenal the war required, and who did the work? · Unit question: How did the Second World War transform the United States, and how far did the country live up t

Read the lesson question aloud and separate its two halves, because the exit ticket and the writing task grade them separately. The first half asks for a mechanism: how was the conversion done, by what laws, agencies, controls, and payments. An answer that says the country worked hard has named an attitude, not a mechanism, and should not earn full credit anywhere in this lesson. The second half asks who did the work, and the honest answer is wider than the peacetime workforce of 1941. It includes drafted men, six million women entering paid work, and Black, Mexican American, and Native American migrants who moved across the country to the yards and plants. Connect the question to the unit question beneath it. The transformation half of the unit question is built here: a directed economy, a moved population, and a labor force redrawn in four years.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: An army, a production miracle, and the end of the Depression · A · An Army of Twelve Million · From under 200,000 soldiers to twelve million in uniform · B · The Production Miracle · Cars to tanks, Liberty ships in weeks

This map follows the guided notes section by section. Section A raises the force, section B builds its equipment, and section C names the agencies that directed both, because students often learn the production figures as a kind of natural wonder and miss the direction behind them. Section D pays the bill, and sections E and F widen the workforce, first to the women the factories had excluded and then to the migrants who moved to the work. Section G closes with the consequence the whole unit has been approaching since the Depression lessons: unemployment at about 1.2 percent in 1944, achieved by government direction on a scale the New Deal never attempted. Tell students to collect one figure per section as they go. The writing task pays for figures attached to agencies and workers, not for figures recited alone, and the activity, The Conversion Desk, will hand each group one shortage and ask what the government did about it and who paid.

Slide 4 · Section A: An Army of Twelve Million (divider)

On the slide: An Army of Twelve Million · A force smaller than many nations it would fight became the largest in American history, raised mostly by a draft that began in peacetime.

Teach the draft as a peacetime decision, because its date is the section surprise. Congress passed the Selective Training and Service Act in 1940, while the country was still formally at peace and still arguing about whether it would fight at all, and the law survived the House by a single vote when its extension came due in the summer of 1941. That narrow, early decision is why the expansion after Pearl Harbor could run on rails that already existed: registration rolls, local boards, induction centers, and a training system already a year old. Without it, the volunteers of December 1941 would have met an army with no machinery to receive them. The section also answers a fairness question students raise on their own. Local boards, neighbors deciding neighbors, ran the calls, which made the draft feel local and legitimate to most communities and also left room for the inequalities Lesson 6 will examine in who served, who was deferred, and who was assigned where.

Slide 5 · Raised by law, not by volunteers alone

On the slide: ▪ Army in 1939: fewer than 200,000 soldiers / ▪ In uniform by 1945: more than 12 million / ▪ Served across the war: about 16 million / ▪ The 1940 Act: the first peacetime draft / ▪ Local draft boards chose who went

Carry both definitions exactly as the guided notes print them. Mobilization is this: mobilization: the process of preparing a nation's armed forces, industry, and economy for war. The Selective Training and Service Act is this: Selective Training and Service Act: the law of 1940 that created the first peacetime draft in American history, requiring men of set ages to register for military service as called. Then put the three figures in one line on the board, because their ratios teach the section. Fewer than 200,000 soldiers in 1939. More than twelve million in uniform at the 1945 peak, across the Army, Navy, Marines, and Coast Guard. About sixteen million serving at some point across the war, as men rotated through training, combat, wounds, and discharge. Volunteers mattered, above all in the surge after Pearl Harbor, and the reading says so. But no volunteer system in history has produced sixteen million trained service members on a schedule, and the draft is the difference between enthusiasm and a force. Ask students what the army would have looked like in 1943 on volunteers alone, and let the silence make the point.

Slide 6 · 12 million: in uniform at the 1945 peak

On the slide: Americans in uniform at the 1945 peak · About 16 million served across the whole war, most of them supplied by the draft begun in 1940

Let this number sit against the country that produced it. The United States held about 132 million people in 1940, so the peak force took roughly one American in eleven into uniform at the same moment, and the sixteen million who served across the war took about one in eight. Every one of those people also left a civilian job, a farm, a classroom, or a household, which is why the mobilization of the armed forces and the mobilization of the workforce are one story and not two. The empty chairs left by the draft are the labor shortage that sections E and F fill with women and migrants. The uniforms themselves are also production: cloth, boots, rifles, ships, and aircraft in quantities no procurement officer of 1939 would have believed, which is the bridge to section B. When students ask why the government, rather than the market, ran this expansion, this slide is the first answer. Only a state that could compel service could promise the Allies a force of this size on a date, and the Allies planned the war around that promise.

Slide 7 · Section B: The Production Miracle (divider)

On the slide: The Production Miracle · Car plants to tank lines, cargo ships in weeks, and aircraft output rising past 300,000 across the war years.

Introduce the miracle with its least miraculous fact: it was administered. The photograph shows a woman drilling on a Vengeance dive bomber at the Vultee plant in Nashville, an Office of War Information photograph from February 1943, and it is a good place to kill the idea that output rose by itself. Behind this one drill stood an allocated supply of aluminum, a converted or purpose built plant, a government contract with a delivery schedule, and a worker recruited and trained because the draft had taken the men who once held such jobs. The section runs on three comparisons students should be able to state cold by its end. Cars against tanks, in the same plants. Months against weeks, in the same shipyards. And 2,141 aircraft in 1939 against 96,318 in 1944, in the production table the activity gives them as the record. The miracle is real. Its causes are contracts, materials, and labor, in that order, and each cause has an agency or a section of this lesson attached to it.

Slide 8 · Output, not speeches, armed the Allies

On the slide: ▪ Car production stopped in early 1942 / ▪ Plants turned to tanks, trucks, and aircraft engines / ▪ Liberty ships: building time cut from months to weeks / ▪ One Richmond ship completed in under five days / ▪ About

Walk the figures as a sequence, because each one answers a different skepticism of 1941. The conversion skepticism came first: could a car industry really build war goods? By early 1942 new car production had stopped entirely and the same lines and workers were turning out tanks, trucks, and aircraft engines. The speed skepticism came next: could cargo ships be built faster than submarines sank them? Kaiser yards answered with prefabrication and welding in place of riveting, cutting a Liberty ship from months to weeks, with one Richmond ship completed in under five days in the fall of 1942 as the record run of the program. The scale skepticism lasted longest, and the production table answers it: 2,141 aircraft in 1939, 47,836 in 1942, 96,318 at the 1944 peak, about 300,000 across the war years by the standard accounting. Note the honesty rule with students. These are standard record figures, stated as about where the record itself is an accounting, and no figure in this lesson needs inflating.

Slide 9 · 300,000: aircraft across the war years

On the slide: aircraft produced by American factories across the war years · Annual output rose from 2,141 in 1939 to 96,318 in 1944, by the standard production record

Use this slide to teach what a production figure can and cannot prove. The figure proves capacity: no other belligerent approached American aircraft output, and the margin is why the Allies could lose aircraft over Europe and the Pacific at rates that would have ended a smaller air war and keep expanding their air forces at the same time. The figure also proves conversion, because the industry that built those aircraft in 1944 barely existed as a mass industry in 1939, and much of it ran in converted car plants or in new plants built with government money. What the figure cannot prove by itself is direction. Output this size in the right types, bombers here, transports there, fighters in the numbers the theaters demanded, required allocation decisions made in Washington, which is section C. It also required workers who had not been in the industry at all in 1941, which is sections E and F. Keep the word about in the figure when students repeat it. The standard record supports about 300,000, and precision about the estimate is part of the lesson standard.

Slide 10 · Section C: The Agencies That Ran It (divider)

On the slide: The Agencies That Ran It · The War Production Board allocated the materials. The Office of Price Administration held the prices and ran the ration books.

Frame the two agencies as answers to two different failures the war would otherwise have produced. Leave production to the open market in 1942 and every contractor would have bid against every other for the same steel, rubber, and machine tools, prices would have soared, and the loudest bidder rather than the most urgent order would have taken the materials. Leave civilian prices and scarce goods alone and the same competition would have emptied the shops for anyone without money, while soldiers families watched the cost of living run away from their pay. The War Production Board answered the first failure by allocation. The Office of Price Administration answered the second by price ceilings and rationing. Neither agency was loved. Businessmen fought the Board rulings that closed their civilian lines, and nearly everyone complained about the ration books. The section standard is functional, not affectionate: students should be able to say what each agency controlled, what would have happened without it, and which of the two a citizen met at the kitchen table.

Slide 11 · Direction from Washington

On the slide: ▪ War Production Board, created 1942 / ▪ Decided which factories made what / ▪ Allocated steel, rubber, copper, and aluminum / ▪ Office of Price Administration held price ceilings / ▪ The same office ran the rationi

Carry both definitions exactly. The War Production Board is this: War Production Board: the federal agency created in 1942 to direct the conversion of industry to war production and to allocate steel, rubber, and other scarce materials. The Office of Price Administration is this: Office of Price Administration: the federal agency that set price ceilings and ran the rationing system during the war. Then follow one ton of steel. The steel mill did not sell to the highest bidder. The Board assigned the ton to an order it had ranked, a landing craft program or an aircraft program, and the plant receiving it built what its contract said. On the civilian side, follow one pound of meat. The price office set its ceiling price, and the ration system described on the next slide decided how much of it any one shopper could buy at that price. Business executives staffed many of these agencies and the rulings were argued over daily. The reading sentence students should keep is that the federal government managed the economy more closely than at any time before or since.

Slide 12 · The ration book in every kitchen

On the slide: ▪ Rationing limited scarce goods by coupons and stamps / ▪ Gasoline and tires rationed to save rubber / ▪ Sugar rationed from May 1942 / ▪ Meat and butter under points from March 1943 / ▪ Local boards heard appeals

Carry the definition exactly. Rationing is this: rationing: a system limiting how much of a scarce good each person may buy, enforced with coupons or ration stamps. Then explain the kitchen mechanics from the ration book in the photograph, a 1943 book from Garfield, Washington. Every person, children included, held a book of stamps. For sugar and coffee the stamp itself was the limit. For meat, cheese, and fats a points system let families choose within a monthly total, paying money and points together at the counter. Gasoline rationing, students are always surprised to learn, aimed less at fuel than at rubber: less driving meant tires lasted in a country that had lost its rubber supply when Japan seized the Southeast Asian plantations. Local boards of neighbors ran the system face to face, hearing appeals and policing the black market that grew beside it. The fairness argument is the one to land. Rationing made scarcity shared and visible, and its popularity with the public rested on exactly that: the rich man steak and the poor man steak came out of the same stamp book.

Slide 13 · Section D: Paying for the War (divider)

On the slide: Paying for the War · A war costing more than ten times the First World War, paid by bonds in every schoolroom and a tax in every paycheck.

Set the fiscal problem at its true size before naming the instruments. The war cost the United States more than ten times what the First World War had cost, and the spending ran ahead of any revenue the prewar tax system could raise, because the prewar income tax had touched only a small share of Americans. The government therefore paid in two ways at once, and students should keep them separate, because they asked different things of citizens. Borrowing asked citizens to lend, voluntarily, at interest, and the bond campaigns turned lending into a public act performed in schools, theaters, and factory payroll lines. Taxation asked citizens to pay, compulsorily, and the war widened the income tax into a mass tax and put collection into the paycheck itself through withholding, so the money arrived before the wage was spent. Together the two streams did a third job beyond revenue. Every dollar lent or taxed out of a paycheck was a dollar not bidding for scarce civilian goods, which helped the price ceilings of section C hold.

Slide 14 · Every citizen a lender

On the slide: ▪ War bonds sold in schools, theaters, and factories / ▪ Payroll plans took a share of each wage / ▪ Bonds repaid with interest / ▪ The income tax became a mass tax / ▪ Tax withheld from paychecks

Carry the definition exactly. War bonds are this: war bonds: government bonds sold to the public to raise money for the war, repaid with interest. Then make the bond concrete at the smallest scale, because the campaigns were designed to reach the smallest buyer. A schoolchild could buy stamps for cents at a time, paste them into a book, and trade the filled book for a bond. A factory worker on a payroll plan never handled the share at all; it left the paycheck as a bond purchase before the rest became wages. Eight great bond drives punctuated the war, with rallies, speakers, and quotas by town and plant, and the Treasury kept the campaigns in front of the public for the whole war. Pair the lending with the tax change, which outlasted the war. Withholding and the mass income tax meant that after 1943 the federal government stood inside every paycheck in the country, a transformation of the citizen and state relationship that Lesson 8 will meet again when the veterans come home to the state the war built.

Slide 15 · Section E: The Women Who Built the Arsenal (divider)

On the slide: The Women Who Built the Arsenal · Six million women entered paid work, into shipyards and aircraft plants that had long closed their trades to them.

Name the arithmetic before the icon, because the icon is so familiar that students think they already know this section. The draft took millions of men out of the workforce at exactly the moment war orders demanded millions more workers than the peacetime economy had ever employed. The gap could not be filled from the men who remained, and employers who had barred women from the industrial trades began recruiting them, with the government urging the recruitment on. About six million women entered paid work during the war years. The poster on this slide is the recruitment image students know, J. Howard Miller We Can Do It poster of 1943, and the quick check will complicate it properly: it hung briefly inside one company plants and became famous only decades later. The section should therefore run on the work itself, the drilling, riveting, welding, and scaling in the photographs, and on the terms of the work, lower pay than the men beside them and, at the war end, pressure to leave the trades to the returning veterans.

Slide 16 · Rosie the Riveter at the bench

On the slide: ▪ About six million women entered paid work / ▪ Shipyards, aircraft plants, and munitions lines / ▪ Drilled, riveted, welded, and scaled / ▪ Recruited by posters and songs as Rosie the Riveter / ▪ Often paid less, a

Carry the definition exactly. Rosie the Riveter is this: Rosie the Riveter: the popular name and image for the American women who took factory and shipyard jobs during the war, used in songs and posters to recruit women to war work. The name came out of a popular song and spread across posters, magazines, and newsreels until it stood for the whole female war workforce, most of whom never drove a rivet: they drilled at Vultee in Nashville, welded hulls at Richmond, scaled Liberty ship plates, ran lathes, and inspected parts. Distinguish two groups inside the six million, because the figure hides them. Some women moved from lower paid women work, waitressing, laundries, domestic service, into war wages that doubled their pay. Others entered paid work for the first time, many of them married, many with children, which is why the boomtowns of section F needed nurseries. The terms were unequal while the work was equal, and the lesson keeps both facts: lower pay, segregated lines in many yards, against skills and wages many women carried out of the war.

Slide 17 · Section F: The Migration to War Work (divider)

On the slide: The Migration to War Work · The plants stood where the labor was not, so the labor moved: a second Great Migration redrew the map of the American population.

Teach the migration as an economic decision made family by family, not as a drift. The war plants and yards clustered where deep water, rail, and government contracts put them: Richmond and Los Angeles on the Pacific coast, Seattle, Detroit, the Gulf ports. The workers the plants needed lived somewhere else, above all in the South, where Black workers faced the lowest wages and the tightest Jim Crow labor market in the country. War wages, plus the recruiting trains the yards ran, plus letters home from the first arrivals, set the movement going. Black workers leaving the South formed a second Great Migration, larger in four years than the first had been in decades, joined by Mexican American workers moving to the cities of the Southwest and West and by Native American workers leaving reservations for war industry jobs. The photograph shows a skilled worker at the Richmond yards in 1943, part of the workforce rushing the Liberty ship George Washington Carver to completion. Section F follows the migrants to the cities that were not ready for them, which is where the cost of the migration was paid.

Slide 18 · Richmond: 24,000 to 100,000

On the slide: ▪ Richmond, California: about 24,000 people in 1940 / ▪ About 100,000 within a few years of the yards opening / ▪ Detroit, Los Angeles, and Seattle drew workers the same way / ▪ Crowded housing, doubled school sessio

Price the boomtown honestly, in both currencies. The wage currency was real: yard and plant wages ran far above southern farm and domestic pay, paid weekly, in cash, often with overtime, and migrants sent money home and saved toward houses in the new cities. The housing currency was punishing: Richmond quadrupled faster than any city could build, and families doubled up in trailers, garages, converted storefronts, and the dormitory rows the government built near the yards, with schools on double sessions and clinics queuing around the block. Older residents in several war cities answered the newcomers with hostility that sometimes turned violent, and Lesson 6 takes up the 1943 riots and the discrimination fights inside the war economy directly. For this lesson, hold the demographic ending. The migrants mostly stayed. The West Coast and northern cities the war built kept their new populations after 1945, which makes the migration one of the war permanent transformations of the country, outlasting the ration books and the yards themselves.

Slide 19 · Section G: The Depression Ends in the Shipyards (divider)

On the slide: The Depression Ends in the Shipyards · Unemployment near one in five on the eve of the war fell to about 1.2 percent in 1944. The cause was government direction, at war scale.

Announce this section as the verdict the last two units have been building toward, and make students feel the weight of the question. The Depression and New Deal unit closed with an honest admission: after all the agencies, all the relief, and all the reform, unemployment in the late 1930s still stood in double figures, and the slump of 1937 and 1938 had shown how fragile the recovery was. Historians and citizens have argued ever since about what ended the Depression. This section gives the answer the figures support, and it is not a story about confidence or private demand returning on its own. Between 1941 and 1944 the federal government became the buyer of nearly half the country output, aimed that buying at ships, aircraft, and shells, drafted or hired the workers who built them, and allocated the materials they were built from. The Depression ended inside that system, in the shipyards and aircraft plants, and the next slide puts the closing figure on the screen.

Slide 20 · Direction, not recovery, did it

On the slide: ▪ The government bought the ships, planes, and shells / ▪ It directed the materials and set the prices / ▪ It drafted or hired the workers / ▪ Spending at a scale the New Deal never attempted / ▪ Jobs went begging b

State the mechanism in one chain and make the class repeat it, because the writing task grades exactly this chain. The government placed the orders, which guaranteed the demand the Depression had destroyed. The War Production Board directed the materials to those orders, which guaranteed the supply. The draft and the hiring halls supplied the workers, recruited from groups the peacetime labor market had excluded. Bonds and the mass income tax paid the bills and pulled spending money out of the shops, which let the price ceilings hold. At the end of the chain stands a labor market turned upside down: employers competing for workers, overtime everywhere, and jobs unfilled in 1944 in the same country where jobs had been unfindable in 1933. The comparison with the New Deal must stay exact. The New Deal spent to relieve and prime; the war government spent and directed to produce, at several times the scale, with legal powers of allocation and draft that no peacetime administration had held. That difference in kind, not just in size, is the lesson conclusion.

Slide 21 · 1.2 percent: unemployment in 1944

On the slide: unemployment in 1944, the lowest figure in the national record · Down from near one worker in five on the eve of the war, and near one in four in 1933

End the content on the figure that closes a two unit argument. About 1.2 percent unemployment in 1944 is an estimate, and students should say so: wartime labor statistics are strong but not perfect, and the reading states the figure as about. Even with that caution, the figure stands in a different world from 1933, when about one worker in four was jobless, or from the eve of the war, when the share still stood near one in five after a decade of New Deal effort. Ask the class to name what the figure cost, using the whole lesson as the price list: twelve million drafted, ration books in every kitchen, a tax in every paycheck, boomtown families in trailers, and a huge national debt. The figure also frames the question Lesson 6 will sharpen. The government that could direct an economy to full employment in war chose to do so, which raises the standing question of why full employment had been impossible in peace. Students do not have to settle that argument today. They do have to be able to state it.

Slide 22 · Mobilization at a glance

On the slide: The conversion, part by part · The draft · Registration and local boards from 1940 · Twelve million in uniform by 1945 · Production · Conversion, allocation, and federal orders · 300,000 aircraft; Liberty ships in weeks

This slide mirrors the closing table of the guided notes, and students should complete that table now from memory where they can. The guided notes table, Mobilization at a Glance, groups the lesson into three parts: the draft and the services, the factories and the yards, and the agencies and the kitchen. This screen adds the payment row separately, because the writing task rewards students who can place bonds and the mass tax inside the mechanism rather than treating them as background. Check the table with the two questions that expose shallow answers. First, which row ran on compulsion and which on consent? The draft and the tax ran on law; the bonds and the migration ran on choice inside a directed frame, and the mixture of the two is the system character. Second, which row would a Richmond welder appear in twice? The workforce row for her job, and the production row for her ship, which is the point: the parts interlock, and the table is a map of one machine, not four separate stories.

Slide 23 · Vocabulary: words for the conversion

On the slide: Words for the conversion · mobilization · preparing the armed forces, industry, and economy for war · Selective Training and Service Act · the 1940 law creating the first peacetime draft · War Production Board · the 1942

Four terms, matching the guided notes word for word, and students should copy the definitions because the unit test draws on them. Mobilization: the process of preparing a nation's armed forces, industry, and economy for war. Selective Training and Service Act: the law of 1940 that created the first peacetime draft in American history, requiring men of set ages to register for military service as called. War Production Board: the federal agency created in 1942 to direct the conversion of industry to war production and to allocate steel, rubber, and other scarce materials. War bonds: government bonds sold to the public to raise money for the war, repaid with interest. Press the first term hardest, because it is the lesson title idea: mobilization names a process applied to three things at once, the armed forces, industry, and the economy, and an answer that limits it to raising an army has cut the definition by two thirds. Then press the Act date: a 1940 peacetime law, extended by one vote in 1941, proof the country began mobilizing before it agreed it was at war.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for the home front regime

On the slide: Words for the home front regime · rationing · limits on how much of a scarce good each person may buy, by coupons or stamps · Office of Price Administration · the agency that set price ceilings and ran rationing · Rosie

Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly. Rationing: a system limiting how much of a scarce good each person may buy, enforced with coupons or ration stamps. Office of Price Administration: the federal agency that set price ceilings and ran the rationing system during the war. Rosie the Riveter: the popular name and image for the American women who took factory and shipyard jobs during the war, used in songs and posters to recruit women to war work. Two details in these definitions earn emphasis before the exit ticket. The rationing definition turns on the enforcement, coupons or ration stamps: a limit without the stamp book was a request, and the stamp book is what made the limit equal for every shopper at the counter. The Rosie definition is careful in a way students should notice and copy: it calls Rosie a popular name and image used in songs and posters to recruit. Rosie is not a person and not a single poster, and the quick check on the Miller poster grades exactly that distinction.

Slide 25 · Answering the question

On the slide: ▪ How: draft law, conversion, allocation, ceilings, bonds, and tax / ▪ Who: twelve million drafted, six million women, the migrants / ▪ The proof: about 300,000 aircraft and 1.2 percent unemployment in 1944 / ▪ The k

Run the synthesis as the writing task rehearsal, and build it from the class own answers before summarizing. Strong answers arrive in two columns that meet at the end. The how column is a chain of instruments, each with its date or figure attached: the 1940 draft law, the 1942 production board, price ceilings and ration books, bonds and a mass income tax. The who column is a widening circle of workers: drafted men first, then the women recruited into the trades, then the migrants who rebuilt the war cities. The columns meet in the closing claim, which students should now be able to write unaided: the mobilization ended the Depression, and it did so by government direction of the economy on a scale the New Deal never attempted. Test the claim once against its rival before closing. Private enterprise built the ships and aircraft, yes, as contractor and as worker. But the orders, the materials, the prices, the labor draft, and the money were the government, and the essay that cannot say which parts belonged to which has not yet answered the question.

Slide 26 · Now your turn: answer the question and exit ticket

On the slide: Answer the question · How did the United States turn a peacetime economy into the arsenal the war required, and who did the work? · Activity · The Conversion Desk: five shortages, the agency for each, and who paid · Note

Close by handing the lesson to the work. In the activity, The Conversion Desk, each group receives one shortage file, rubber, shipyard labor, aircraft output, kitchen sugar and meat, or housing in a boomtown, and works out what the government did, which agency acted, who gained work, and who paid the cost, before the class assembles the full conversion chart. The notebook page rebuilds the mobilization as a chart of government action, workers, and civilian cost. The quick check, The Arsenal at Work, sources the Miller poster and tests whether students can place a famous image inside the labor history of 1943 rather than treating it as a decoration. The writing task, The Government That Ended the Depression, requires the mechanism judgment of section G in essay form. The exit ticket has three parts: mobilization defined with the law that began it, the two agencies with what each controlled, and the lesson question answered in two or three sentences. Next lesson leaves the home front for the war in Europe and North Africa, where the arsenal built here goes into action.
