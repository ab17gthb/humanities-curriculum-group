Mobilizing the Home Front

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. An Army of Twelve Million

In 1939 the United States Army held fewer than 200,000 soldiers, a force smaller than the armies of many nations it would soon fight. By the spring of 1945 more than 12 million Americans wore a uniform, and about 16 million served across the whole war. Volunteers filled the first ranks after Pearl Harbor, but volunteers alone could not raise a force of that size. The Selective Training and Service Act of 1940, the first peacetime draft in American history, required men of set ages to register for military service as called. Local draft boards chose who went, and the draft supplied most of the men the armed forces trained and shipped abroad.

B. The Production Miracle

The same country that could not employ its workers in 1939 soon could not build fast enough for them. Car plants stopped making cars in early 1942 and turned to tanks, trucks, and aircraft engines. Shipyards on both coasts and the Great Lakes launched cargo ships by the hundreds. Henry Kaiser and his partners cut the building time of a Liberty ship, the standard cargo vessel of the war, from months to weeks, and one Richmond yard completed a ship in under five days. Aircraft plants spread across the country and raised output year after year. Across the war years American factories produced about 300,000 aircraft, a figure the standard production record supports, alongside tens of thousands of tanks and millions of trucks.

C. The Agencies That Ran It

Conversion on that scale did not run itself, and the federal government built agencies to direct it. The War Production Board, created in 1942, decided which factories made what and allocated scarce materials such as steel, rubber, copper, and aluminum to the orders that counted most. A plant could not simply choose its product or buy its metal on the open market. The Office of Price Administration held price ceilings on most goods so that war demand would not bid the cost of living beyond reach, and it ran the rationing system described below. Business executives filled many agency posts, and the work stayed improvised, but the direction was plain. For the length of the war the federal government managed the economy more closely than at any time before or since.

D. Paying for the War

The war cost the United States more than ten times what the First World War had cost, and no treasury could pay such a bill from ordinary revenue. The government therefore borrowed on a national scale and invited every citizen to hold a share of the debt. War bonds, sold in schools, theaters, factories, and bond rallies, let a buyer lend the government money for the war and receive repayment with interest. Payroll plans took a share of each wage for bonds before the worker spent it. Taxes widened at the same time. The income tax, once paid by a small share of Americans, became a mass tax during the war, withheld from paychecks.

E. The Women Who Built the Arsenal

In 1941 most paid factory work belonged to men, and the draft soon took millions of those men into uniform. The factories still had orders to fill, and the gap opened the industrial trades to women on a scale no one had planned in peacetime. About six million women entered paid work during the war years, many of them taking jobs in shipyards, aircraft plants, and munitions lines that employers had long closed to them. Posters and songs recruited them under the popular name Rosie the Riveter. Women drilled, riveted, and welded beside men, often at lower pay, and when the war ended many were pushed out of the trades for returning veterans.

F. The Migration to War Work

War work also moved the American people. Shipyards and aircraft plants stood where the labor was not, so workers traveled to the jobs. Richmond, California grew from a small city of about 24,000 in 1940 to about 100,000 within a few years around the Kaiser shipyards, and Detroit, Los Angeles, Seattle, and the Gulf ports drew workers in the same pattern. Black workers leaving the South formed a second Great Migration, joined by Mexican American and Native American workers moving to war industry cities. The new arrivals met crowded and makeshift housing, strained schools and clinics, and in some cities open hostility from older residents. They also met wages higher than any they had known, and the war cities kept their new populations after the war.

G. The Depression Ends in the Shipyards

The mobilization closed the question the whole previous decade had argued over. Unemployment, which had stood near one worker in four in 1933 and still near one in five on the eve of the war, fell to about 1.2 percent in 1944, the lowest figure in the national record. The cause was not a recovery of ordinary private demand. The government bought the ships, planes, and shells, directed the materials, set the prices, and drafted or hired the workers. War spending on a scale the New Deal never attempted ended the Depression in the shipyards and the aircraft plants, and it did so by government direction of the economy from Washington.

2. State two production figures, one for ships and one for aircraft. For ships, Kaiser yards cut the building time of a Liberty ship from months to weeks, and one Richmond yard completed a ship in under five days. For aircraft, American factories produced about 300,000 aircraft across the war years by the standard production record. Together the figures show conversion in both speed and scale. Peacetime plants and new yards alike were turned to war orders, and output rose year after year.

3. Contrast the War Production Board and the Office of Price Administration. The War Production Board, created in 1942, decided which factories made what and allocated scarce materials such as steel, rubber, copper, and aluminum. The Office of Price Administration held price ceilings on most goods and ran the rationing system. The war economy needed both because directing production without holding prices would have let war demand bid the cost of living beyond reach, while holding prices without directing materials would have left scarce goods to the highest bidder instead of the most urgent order.

4. Name the two main ways the government paid for the war. Borrowing and taxation. Citizens lent through war bonds, bought in schools, theaters, factories, and payroll plans, and received repayment with interest. Citizens also paid through the income tax, which became a mass tax during the war and was withheld from paychecks, so that borrowing and taxation together paid the bills of a war that cost more than ten times the First World War.
