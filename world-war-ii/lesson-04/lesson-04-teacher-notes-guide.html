The War in Europe and North Africa

World War II · Lesson 4 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: World War II, Lesson 4 of 8. Teach it fourth, after Lesson 3 on mobilizing the home front and before Lesson 5 on the Pacific war. Lesson 3 built the arsenal. This lesson follows that arsenal into action in Europe and North Africa, from the first landing in Africa in 1942 to the surrender of Germany in May 1945.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “The War in Europe and North Africa” and the guided notes, which follow the deck's order exactly — the war the Allies inherited, North Africa, Sicily and Italy, the air war, D-Day, the drive across France and the Bulge, and the Elbe and VE Day, closing with the European War at a Glance table. The activity is “Five Maps, One War: North Africa, Italy, Normandy, the Bulge, and Germany 1945, aim, result, and cost at every station.” The notebook page is the five stages charted by date, aim, result, and cost. The quick check is “The Ramp at Omaha: the Sargent photograph, the Mediterranean sequence, and the road to VE Day.” The writing task is “The Turning Point: one stage defended against its strongest rival, with the Eastern Front weighed.”

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Students need Lesson 2 in full: Pearl Harbor, Germany first, the Grand Alliance, and the second front the Soviet Union demanded from 1942 onward. They also need Lesson 3's mobilization figures, because the armies, shipping, and landing craft in this lesson are the arsenal that lesson built. No knowledge of the individual European campaigns is assumed; North Africa, Italy, the air war, and Normandy are taught in sequence.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (analyze connections among events — each campaign buying the next stage its chance), D2.His.16 (assess options for individual and collective action — the second front argument between the western Allies and the Soviet Union), D2.His.9 (construct arguments using historical evidence — the turning point judgment in the writing task); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's order from North Africa through Italy, the air war, and Normandy to VE Day), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task: one stage defended against its strongest rival).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to tell the European war as one connected road with dates: Operation Torch and the Tunisian surrender, Sicily and Italy leaving the Axis and the mountain war that followed, strategic bombing and its civilian cost, D-Day on June 6, 1944 with about 156,000 troops landed or dropped on the first day, the pursuit across France and the Battle of the Bulge, and the meeting at the Elbe and VE Day on May 8, 1945 — stating for each stage its aim, its result, and its cost, and placing the Eastern Front and the estimated 27 million Soviet deaths inside that account.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening, question, and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; the war the Allies inherited (slides 4–6) 6 minutes; North Africa (slides 7–8) 5 minutes; Sicily, Italy, and the argument (slides 9–10) 6 minutes; the air war (slides 11–12) 5 minutes; D-Day (slides 13–15) 7 minutes; the drive across France and the Bulge (slides 16–18) 6 minutes; the Elbe and VE Day (slides 19–21) 6 minutes; glance, synthesis, vocabulary, and the turn to work (slides 22–26) 4 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes.

Key vocabulary

amphibious landing · Eastern Front · Stalingrad · strategic bombing · D-Day · Battle of the Bulge · VE Day

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: the title photograph filling the slide (landing ships putting cargo ashore on Omaha Beach at low tide in mid-June 1944, with barrage balloons overhead); the unit and lesson line “World War II · Lesson 4”; the title “The War in Europe and North Africa”; the standfirst — “From the first landing in Africa to the surrender of Germany.”

  • Picture: Landing ships putting cargo ashore on Omaha Beach at low tide in mid-June 1944, a few days after the Normandy landings, with barrage balloons overhead against air attack. Caption it as landing ships unloading at Omaha Beach in mid-June 1944.
  • [Open with the photograph and what it stands for.] It is the right opening image for this lesson because the European war was, from first to last, a war of landings and supply.
  • The Allies had to cross water to reach every battlefield in this lesson, and then feed armies across beaches until ports could be taken.
  • Today we follow the road in order: North Africa in 1942, Sicily and Italy in 1943, the air war over Germany, Normandy in June 1944, the drive across France, the last German offensive in the Ardennes, and the meeting at the Elbe in April 1945.
  • At every stage students will be asked the same three questions.
  • What was the aim, what was the result, and what did it cost?
  • Keep those three words in view all hour, because the activity, the notebook page, and the writing task are all built on them.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the question — “How did the Allies carry the war from North Africa to the surrender of Germany, and what did each stage cost?”; below it, the unit question: “How did the Second World War transform the United States, and how far did the country live up to the democracy it claimed to defend?”

  • [Read the lesson question aloud and separate its two halves,] because students should hear both before any map goes up.
  • The first half asks for a route, a sequence of campaigns that carried the war from the Mediterranean to the center of Germany in under three years.
  • The second half asks for cost, stage by stage, and cost in this lesson means more than casualty figures.
  • It means a campaign that consumed a year in Italian mountains, an air offensive that killed civilians in large numbers, and an alliance strained by the argument over when the second front would open.
  • The unit question runs beneath it as the frame for the whole unit.
  • Notice what this lesson does not try to do.
  • The Pacific war belongs to the next lesson, and the Holocaust, which ran on its own chronology from 1933, is taught separately and in full later in the unit.
  • By the end of the hour students should be able to tell the European war as one connected story, with dates, rather than as a list of famous battles.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title “Five landings and one long road east”; seven rows — A, The war the Allies inherited (Germany holds Europe; the east carries the largest war); B, North Africa: the first campaign (Torch, Tunisia, and a hard education); C, Sicily, Italy, and the argument (Italy leaves the Axis; the mountains do not); D, The air war (bombers against industry, transport, and cities); E, D-Day (June 6, 1944, and the second front at last); F, The drive across France and the Bulge (pursuit, then the last German offensive); G, The Elbe and VE Day (two armies meet; Germany surrenders).

  • This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section, so students can use the letters on screen to find their place in their notes all hour.
  • Section A sets the war the Allies inherited in 1942, with Germany holding Europe and the Soviet Union carrying the largest land fighting in the east.
  • Sections B and C cover the Mediterranean road, North Africa and then Sicily and Italy, the campaigns the western Allies fought while the invasion of France was prepared.
  • Section D takes up the air war from England.
  • Section E is D-Day.
  • Section F follows the drive across France and the shock of the Bulge in December 1944.
  • Section G closes at the Elbe and VE Day.
  • Tell students the summary table at the end of their guided notes, The European War at a Glance, asks them to rebuild exactly this map from the record, stage by stage, in their own words.

Slide 4 · Section A: The War the Allies Inherited (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section A”; the title “The War the Allies Inherited”; the line — “Germany held Europe, and the largest war was in the east.”

  • [Set the scene before any western campaign,] because students who begin at D-Day misunderstand the whole war.
  • In December 1941 Germany held most of Europe, from France to deep inside the Soviet Union.
  • Germany had invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, opening the Eastern Front, where the largest land fighting of the war took place along a front of more than a thousand miles.
  • That eastern war is not background to this lesson.
  • It is the reason the alliance argued as it did, the reason the Soviet Union demanded a second front in France from 1942 onward, and the reason any honest accounting of the European war ends where the reading ends, with Soviet losses.
  • Students should write two facts into section A of their notes before we move on: the date of the German invasion of the Soviet Union, and what the Eastern Front was.
  • Everything in the Mediterranean sections that follow is shaped by the western Allies not yet being ready to land in France.

Slide 5 · The largest war was already running

On the slide: five bullets — Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941.; The Eastern Front ran for more than a thousand miles.; A German army was destroyed at Stalingrad in 1942 to 1943.; The Soviet Union demanded a second front in France.; The western Allies chose an easier first campaign.

  • [Give the turning point of the east its full weight here.]
  • Stalingrad, the battle of August 1942 to February 1943, ended with the Soviet army surrounding and destroying a German army, and it happened sixteen months before any Allied soldier landed in France.
  • Hold that date against the western timeline students are about to learn, because the writing task will require them to do exactly that.
  • The Soviet demand for a second front was not a debating point.
  • Every month the invasion of France waited, the land war fell on the Soviet Union with a force the west did not face until 1944.
  • Britain and the United States answered with a strategy they could actually carry out in 1942: land in North Africa, where the enemy was reachable, clear the Mediterranean, and build the armies, shipping, and landing craft that a landing in France would need.
  • Whether that answer was wisdom or delay is one of the questions students should carry through the lesson, and the next slide puts the eastern cost in a single figure.

Slide 6 · 27 million Soviet deaths, estimated

On the slide: the big figure “27 million”; the label — “estimated Soviet military and civilian deaths, across the whole war”; the note — “An estimate for the entire Soviet war, 1941 to 1945 · The largest share of the land war against Germany was fought in the east.”

  • 27 million — estimated Soviet military and civilian deaths, across the whole war
  • An estimate for the entire Soviet war, 1941 to 1945
  • [Handle this figure with care and say its limits out loud.]
  • Soviet military and civilian deaths together are estimated at about 27 million across the whole war, from the German invasion of June 1941 to the surrender of May 1945.
  • It is an estimate, built from records and demographic reconstruction after the fact, and students should repeat it as an estimate.
  • It is also a whole war figure.
  • It does not belong to Stalingrad alone, or to any single campaign, and the activity will test whether students can keep that straight.
  • For scale, set it beside the western figures they will meet today without turning the comparison into a contest.
  • The point is structural.
  • The alliance that won in Europe was an alliance in which one partner did most of the land fighting and most of the dying against Germany, while the western partners brought the sea power, the air power, the production, and, from 1944, a second mass army in France.
  • The argument over the second front, coming in section C, only makes sense with this figure on the wall.

Slide 7 · Section B: North Africa: The First Campaign (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (American tanks advancing in Tunisia, February 1943); the section label “Section B”; the title “North Africa: The First Campaign”; the line — “American tanks advancing in Tunisia, February 1943. Signal Corps photograph.”

  • Picture: American M-3 tanks advancing to strengthen Allied positions in Tunisia on February 20, 1943, United States Army Signal Corps record SC 170092. Caption it as American tanks advancing in Tunisia in February 1943.
  • Note the date, because it places the picture in the hardest week of the campaign, the days of the Kasserine Pass fighting.
  • Section B covers the first American ground campaign against Germany.
  • The strategic setting is simple enough for students to hold.
  • The Allies could not yet invade France, the Soviet Union was demanding action, and North Africa offered a shore the Allies could reach with the shipping they had.
  • What the campaign would teach, about fighting an experienced enemy, about combined operations with British forces, and about supply across an ocean, is the real content of the section.
  • Students should have the three questions ready for this stage: the aim, the result, and the cost, because the notebook chart asks for all three.

Slide 8 · Torch, Kasserine, and the Tunisian surrender

On the slide: five bullets — Operation Torch landed in Morocco and Algeria on November 8, 1942.; It was the first American ground campaign against Germany.; German armor broke through at the Kasserine Pass in February 1943.; The Allies pressed from the west and the east at once.; Axis forces in Tunisia surrendered in May 1943, and about 250,000 prisoners were taken.

  • [Walk the campaign as a first campaign, with a beginner's errors priced in.]
  • Operation Torch, on November 8, 1942, was an amphibious landing on a large scale, and it put American ground troops into the war against Germany for the first time.
  • The race east into Tunisia then slowed, and in February 1943 German armor broke through inexperienced American troops at the Kasserine Pass.
  • Commanders were replaced, training hardened, and the lesson entered American practice: green troops meeting veterans pay for their education in the first battle.
  • The ending deserves its scale stated plainly.
  • Pressed from Algeria by the Americans and British and from Libya by the British Eighth Army, the Axis forces in Tunisia surrendered in May 1943, and about 250,000 soldiers became prisoners.
  • The Allies held the southern shore of the Mediterranean, its sea lanes opened, and the road to Sicily and Italy stood in front of them.
  • That is the result.
  • The cost includes Kasserine, and students should write both into section B.

Slide 9 · Section C: Sicily, Italy, and the Argument (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (American troops moving through a Sicilian town, August 1943); the section label “Section C”; the title “Sicily, Italy, and the Argument”; the line — “American troops moving through a Sicilian town, August 1943. Signal Corps photograph.”

  • Picture: American troops moving through a town in Sicily on August 15, 1943, United States Army Signal Corps record SC 179874. Caption it as American troops moving through a Sicilian town in August 1943.
  • The photograph places the picture two days before the island was fully in Allied hands.
  • Section C is the Mediterranean war's second act, and it carries the lesson's central argument inside it.
  • Sicily was taken quickly, in about five weeks, and Italy then left the Axis altogether, the first of Germany's partners to fall.
  • Yet the Italian campaign that followed lasted until the spring of 1945, longer than any other western campaign of the war.
  • Students should listen for both halves of that sentence all through this section, because a one sided account of Italy, triumph or quagmire, fails the record.
  • The section also returns to the alliance argument from section A.
  • Every month spent in Italy was a month the second front in France had not opened, and the Soviet Union counted those months.

Slide 10 · An ally knocked out, a peninsula held

On the slide: the photograph at right (American troops moving through a Sicilian town, August 1943); five bullets — Sicily fell in about five weeks in the summer of 1943.; Mussolini was removed, and Italy surrendered in September.; Germany occupied northern and central Italy.; Salerno was held under counterattack, and the mountain war began.; Rome fell on June 4, 1944, and fighting ran to the spring of 1945.

  • Picture: American troops moving through a town in Sicily on August 15, 1943, United States Army Signal Corps record SC 179874. Caption it as American troops moving through a Sicilian town in August 1943.
  • [Teach Italy as a campaign of consequences rather than a pause before Normandy.]
  • The landing on Sicily on July 10, 1943 was at the time the largest amphibious operation of the war, and it brought down a government: Mussolini was removed, and Italy signed its surrender in September.
  • Germany answered within hours, disarming Italian forces and occupying the peninsula, so the Allied landing at Salerno on September 9 met German, not Italian, resistance, and the beachhead was nearly driven into the sea.
  • North of Salerno the country itself became the defense: rivers in the valleys, German lines on the ridges, winter mud, and towns built of stone on hilltops.
  • The Allies took Rome on June 4, 1944, two days before D-Day, and still the war in Italy ran on to the spring of 1945.
  • What did the campaign buy? Italy out of the war, German divisions held in the peninsula, airfields, and hard training.
  • What did it cost? Time, and the divisions of two armies, in the slowest fighting of the western war.

Slide 11 · Section D: The Air War (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (B-17 bombers of the 384th Bomb Group in formation, 1944); the section label “Section D”; the title “The Air War”; the line — “B-17 bombers of the 384th Bomb Group in formation, 1944. Army Air Forces photograph.”

  • Picture: A box formation of B-17 Flying Fortress bombers of the 384th Bomb Group, Eighth Air Force, above the clouds in 1944, taken by an Army Air Forces photographer. Caption it as B-17 bombers of the 384th Bomb Group in formation in 1944.
  • Formations like this one are the visual form of the strategy this section teaches.
  • Section D covers strategic bombing, the sustained use of heavy bombers against Germany's industry, transportation, and cities.
  • This was the one campaign against Germany itself that the western Allies could run from 1942 onward, before any landing in France, and it grew into the largest air operation of the war.
  • Students should listen for three threads.
  • The doctrine, daylight precision bombing against named targets, tested against German fighters and flak.
  • The turning of the campaign in 1944, when long range escorts arrived and the target system shifted to oil.
  • And the civilian cost under the bombs, which the reading states as record and which any honest account of the air war has to carry alongside its military results.

Slide 12 · Precision by day, and the price of the doctrine

On the slide: five bullets — The RAF bombed at night, and the Eighth Air Force bombed by day.; Bombers struck named targets such as factories, refineries, and rail yards.; One Schweinfurt mission lost sixty bombers in October 1943.; P-51 Mustang escorts reached deep into Germany in 1944.; By early 1945 German fuel and rail movement were collapsing.

  • [Work the mechanism before the morality, because students need both in order.]
  • The American doctrine held that formations of heavy bombers, armed for self defense, could strike named industrial targets by day with precision.
  • The autumn of 1943 tested that claim hard: on one mission against the ball bearing plants at Schweinfurt in October, sixty American bombers were lost, and deep unescorted missions were paused.
  • The campaign changed when the P-51 Mustang, with drop tanks, could escort the bombers to Berlin and back and fight the German air force on the way in.
  • Through 1944 the combined offensive turned heavily against oil and transportation, and by early 1945 German fuel production and rail movement were collapsing, which students will see again in the final drive, when German armies could barely move by day.
  • Then state the other ledger.
  • A firestorm at Hamburg in July 1943 killed an estimated 37,000 people, and an estimated 25,000 died at Dresden in February 1945.
  • Both figures are estimates.
  • Historians still argue over what the bombing achieved for its cost, and students should hear that argument named as open.

Slide 13 · Section E: D-Day (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (troops wading ashore at Omaha Beach, June 6, 1944); the section label “Section E”; the title “D-Day”; the line — “Troops wading ashore at Omaha Beach, June 6, 1944. Photograph by Robert F. Sargent.”

  • Picture: Troops of the 1st Infantry Division wading ashore at Omaha Beach on the morning of June 6, 1944, photographed from the landing craft by Chief Photographer's Mate Robert F. Sargent of the United States Coast Guard. Its title in the National Archives record is Into the Jaws of Death. Caption it as troops wading ashore at Omaha Beach on June 6, 1944.
  • Teach the camera's position, because the activity and the quick check both test it.
  • Sargent stood on the craft the men are leaving, so we see what the soldiers saw ahead of them and nothing of their faces.
  • Section E covers the operation the whole Mediterranean strategy had been building toward: the cross Channel invasion of France, the second front the Soviet Union had demanded since 1942.
  • It was the largest seaborne invasion ever mounted, and it had been postponed, planned, supplied, and rehearsed for two years.
  • Students should be ready to put it in sequence, before the breakout and after Torch, Sicily, and the air war's long preparation.

Slide 14 · Five beaches, one lodgment

On the slide: the photograph at right (troops wading ashore at Omaha Beach, June 6, 1944); five bullets — Paratroopers dropped behind the beaches overnight.; Troops landed on Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno, and Sword at dawn.; At Omaha, troops crossed open sand under fire from the bluffs.; Artificial Mulberry harbors were towed across the Channel.; The breakout at Saint-Lo followed in late July.

  • Picture: Troops of the 1st Infantry Division wading ashore at Omaha Beach on the morning of June 6, 1944, photographed by Chief Photographer's Mate Robert F. Sargent. Caption it as troops wading ashore at Omaha Beach on June 6, 1944.
  • [Put the day in sequence on the board as you teach it.]
  • During the night, airborne troops dropped behind the beaches to hold bridges and causeways.
  • At dawn, after naval and air bombardment, infantry landed on five beaches across a fifty mile front: Utah and Omaha for the Americans, Gold, Juno, and Sword for British and Canadian forces.
  • The day did not cost the same everywhere, and students should learn the contrast rather than a single story.
  • At Utah the advance moved inland quickly.
  • At Omaha, where the bluffs rose straight from a narrow beach, troops crossed open sand under fire and the heaviest American losses of the day fell there, among them the men in Sargent's photograph.
  • What made the landing a campaign rather than a raid was supply: artificial Mulberry harbors were towed across the Channel and assembled off the beaches, so an army could be fed without a captured port.
  • Through June and July the buildup outran German reinforcement, and in late July American armor broke out at Saint-Lo.

Slide 15 · 156,000 on the first day

On the slide: the big figure “156,000”; the label — “Allied troops landed or dropped in France on the first day”; the note — “June 6, 1944 · Five beaches, airborne drops, and a fleet of some 5,000 ships and landing craft in the standard record.”

  • 156,000 — Allied troops landed or dropped in France on the first day
  • June 6, 1944
  • [Let the figure carry the scale of the preparation.]
  • About 156,000 Allied troops landed or dropped in France on June 6, 1944, and behind them stood years of accumulation: landing craft built by the thousands, divisions trained in Britain until the island strained to hold them, artificial harbors constructed in sections and towed across the Channel, and an air campaign that had spent two years cutting the railways of northern France so German reserves could not move quickly.
  • The figure also marks the alliance argument closing.
  • This is the second front, opened at last, and its size is part of the western answer to the Soviet charge of delay: a landing of this weight could not have been mounted in 1942 with the shipping and craft then available, which is a claim students may test against the smaller landings at Torch and Sicily in their notes.
  • End the slide on the human reading of the number.
  • Every one of those troops crossed water to a defended shore, and the men at Omaha did it under direct fire in the first hours.

Slide 16 · Section F: The Drive Across France and the Bulge (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section F”; the title “The Drive Across France and the Bulge”; the line — “A pursuit across France, then one last German throw in the Ardennes.”

  • [Note the absence of a photograph on this divider.] This lesson uses only images whose public domain status is stated on the record, and no qualifying photograph of the Bulge fighting was used, so the winter offensive is taught here with text and figures rather than an image of uncertain status.
  • Section F covers the fastest and then the most surprising months of the western war.
  • After the breakout at Saint-Lo, the Allied armies crossed France in weeks, a pursuit so fast that supply, still landed over the Normandy beaches, became the binding limit on the advance.
  • In September the attempt to end the war early by an airborne crossing of the Rhine at Arnhem failed.
  • Then, in December, in fog and forest where the Allies believed no offensive was possible, Germany attacked with the last armored reserve it had.
  • The two halves of the section belong together: the pursuit created the overconfidence the Bulge punished.

Slide 17 · A pursuit that outran its supply lines

On the slide: five bullets — The breakout at Saint-Lo opened France in late July 1944.; Paris was liberated on August 25, 1944.; German forces fell back toward their own border.; Fuel still came from the Normandy beaches.; The airborne crossing at Arnhem failed in September 1944.

  • [Teach the pursuit as a logistics story.]
  • Once American armor broke out at Saint-Lo in late July 1944, German resistance in France collapsed faster than anyone had planned for, and Allied armies raced for the German border, liberating Paris on August 25.
  • Then arithmetic caught up.
  • An armored division consumes fuel by the hundreds of tons a day, and every gallon still came ashore over the Normandy beaches or through one damaged port and then forward by truck, burning fuel to deliver fuel.
  • The advance slowed in the autumn mud along the German frontier fortifications.
  • In September, the boldest attempt to break the deadlock, an airborne operation to seize bridges across the Rhine at Arnhem in the Netherlands, failed with heavy losses among the paratroopers dropped farthest forward.
  • The lesson for students is the one the whole war keeps teaching: operations run at the speed of supply.
  • That limit sets up the winter, when a German command reading the same map chose the quiet Ardennes sector, opposite tired and newly arrived American units, for its last attack.

Slide 18 · The last offensive in the west

On the slide: five bullets — Germany attacked through the Ardennes on December 16, 1944.; Surprised American units bent, and Bastogne held while surrounded.; Relief reached Bastogne on December 26.; American casualties were estimated at about 75,000.; By February 1945 the offensive had failed.

  • [Name the aim first, because the size of the aim explains the size of the failure.]
  • Germany's December offensive aimed to drive through the Ardennes, cross the Meuse, and seize Antwerp, the great port through which the Allied armies in the north were supplied, splitting the Allied armies and perhaps forcing a negotiated peace in the west.
  • It achieved complete surprise on December 16, 1944, in weather that grounded Allied aircraft, and the dent it drove into the American line gave the battle its name, the Battle of the Bulge.
  • The road town of Bastogne, held by the 101st Airborne and attachments, refused surrender and held surrounded until relief arrived on December 26, and when the weather cleared Allied aircraft returned in force.
  • American casualties are estimated at about 75,000, the costliest single American battle of the European war by the standard record.
  • By February 1945 the line was restored.
  • Germany had spent its last reserves of armor, fuel, and trained men, which is why this battle, a defensive victory, shortened the war that followed it.

Slide 19 · Section G: The Elbe and VE Day (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (American and Soviet soldiers meet at the Elbe, Torgau, April 25, 1945); the section label “Section G”; the title “The Elbe and VE Day”; the line — “American and Soviet soldiers meet at the Elbe, Torgau, April 25, 1945. National Archives.”

  • Picture: American and Soviet soldiers meeting at the Elbe River near Torgau on April 25, 1945, held by the National Archives. Caption it as American and Soviet soldiers meeting at the Elbe on April 25, 1945.
  • The photograph records the moment the two great land campaigns of the European war joined hands across a beaten Germany.
  • It was photographed for the record and used around the world within days, because both governments understood what it showed: the alliance, embodied in two groups of young soldiers on a broken bridge.
  • Section G closes the lesson's road.
  • In the spring of 1945 the western Allies crossed the Rhine and drove across Germany while Soviet armies fought into Berlin, and within six weeks of the Elbe meeting the war in Europe was over.
  • Students should listen for the accounting at the end of this section, because the reading insists on it and the writing task depends on it: who bore the largest share of this war, and what the figures do and do not prove.
  • The handshake in the photograph is the right place to ask that question, since the alliance it celebrates was already under strain.

Slide 20 · The Rhine crossed, the two fronts meet

On the slide: five bullets — American troops crossed the Rhine at Remagen on March 7, 1945.; American and Soviet soldiers met at the Elbe on April 25.; Hitler killed himself in Berlin on April 30.; Germany signed an unconditional surrender at Reims on May 7.; VE Day followed on May 8, 1945.

  • [Run the last sequence as dates, because the speed of the ending is itself the story.]
  • On March 7, 1945, American troops seized the railway bridge at Remagen before German engineers could destroy it and crossed the Rhine, the barrier on which Germany's western defense rested.
  • Encircled in the Ruhr, German armies in the west began surrendering in large groups.
  • On April 25, American patrols met Soviet troops at the Elbe near Torgau, cutting what remained of Germany in two.
  • In Berlin, under Soviet assault, Hitler killed himself on April 30, and the government that succeeded him sought terms it was not offered: the Allied policy was unconditional surrender, and German representatives signed it at Eisenhower's headquarters at Reims on May 7.
  • VE Day, the day victory in Europe was announced, followed on May 8, 1945.
  • Students should close section G of their notes with those five dates in order.
  • The next slide shows how the day was received in a capital that had been bombed for years and had soldiers on every front of this lesson.

Slide 21 · Victory in Europe, announced

On the slide: the photograph at right (Churchill waves to crowds in Whitehall, London, May 8, 1945); four bullets — Churchill waved to crowds in Whitehall, London.; The news was broadcast on May 8, 1945.; Britain had fought Germany since September 1939.; Celebration and counting of the missing happened at once.

  • Picture: Prime Minister Winston Churchill giving his victory sign to crowds in Whitehall, London, on May 8, 1945, photographed by Major W. G. Horton, a War Office official photographer, and held by the Imperial War Museums as record H 41849. Caption it as Churchill waving to crowds in Whitehall on May 8, 1945.
  • [Use it to slow the ending down.]
  • For Britain the war in Europe had run since September 1939, through the fall of France, the bombing of its cities, and the long Mediterranean and Atlantic campaigns, so the crowd in this picture includes people who had waited nearly six years for this announcement.
  • American students should place their own country on the same day honestly.
  • The United States had been in the European war for three years and five months, its cities untouched, its armies victorious on two fronts, and its war not over: the Pacific war ran on, which is the next lesson's subject.
  • VE Day therefore closes this lesson without closing the unit.
  • What ended on May 8 was the war against Germany, the war this lesson has followed from Torch to the Elbe, and the counting of its cost begins in the synthesis that follows.

Slide 22 · The European war at a glance

On the slide: the title “Five stages, one road”; four rows — North Africa (Torch 1942; Tunisia surrenders May 1943; Kasserine; an army educated in battle); Italy (Sicily falls; Italy surrenders; Rome June 1944; a mountain war to the spring of 1945); Normandy (156,000 ashore on June 6, 1944; Omaha; then the breakout in July); The road east (Bulge beaten back; Rhine and Elbe crossed; Germany surrenders; VE Day, May 8, 1945).

  • This slide mirrors the final table in the guided notes, The European War at a Glance, and students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • The rows compress the road.
  • North Africa runs from Torch in November 1942 to the Tunisian surrender of May 1943, and its cost was an army's education, paid at Kasserine.
  • Italy covers Sicily's quick fall, Italy's surrender, and Rome, against the cost of a mountain war that ran to the spring of 1945.
  • Normandy is the hinge of the western war: about 156,000 troops ashore on the first day, the cost at Omaha, and the July breakout that freed France.
  • The road east closes the table: the Bulge beaten back, the Rhine and Elbe crossed, the surrender, and VE Day on May 8, 1945.
  • Grade the table generously on wording and strictly on pairing: each stage must show what happened and what it cost, and the eastern war's scale, from the stat slide, belongs in the lesson even where the table's rows are western.

Slide 23 · Synthesis: what the road shows

On the slide: five bullets — Every stage bought the next one its chance.; North Africa and Italy taught the armies to land and fight.; The air war cut the fuel the German army moved on.; Normandy opened the front the east had demanded.; The cost rose as the armies closed on Germany itself.

  • [Draw the lesson together before the vocabulary, because synthesis is where the turning point argument becomes possible.]
  • Each stage bought the next one its chance.
  • North Africa gave inexperienced armies a campaign they could win and the Mediterranean's southern shore.
  • Sicily and Italy removed an Axis partner and held German divisions, at the price of the slowest fighting of the western war.
  • The air war, costly to crews and to civilians alike, cut the oil and transport the German army moved on, which is one reason the armies of 1945 could barely maneuver.
  • Normandy opened the second front at a weight no earlier year could have supplied, and the Bulge spent Germany's last reserve months before the Rhine.
  • Underneath all of it ran the eastern war, larger than any western campaign, where a German army had already been destroyed at Stalingrad in the winter of the Tunisian campaign.
  • Students who can hold both scales at once are ready for the writing task, which asks exactly that of them.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for the road into Europe

On the slide: four terms with their definitions — amphibious landing, Eastern Front, Stalingrad, strategic bombing. The full definitions below match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Say: “amphibious landing: an attack from the sea onto a defended shore by forces carried in ships and landing craft”
  • Say: “Eastern Front: the battlefront between Germany and the Soviet Union after June 1941, where the largest land fighting of the war took place”
  • Say: “Stalingrad: the battle of August 1942 to February 1943 in which the Soviet army surrounded and destroyed a German army, the turning point of the war on the Eastern Front”
  • Note that the Stalingrad definition itself makes the turning point claim for the east, which students will have to weigh in the writing task.
  • Say: “strategic bombing: the use of heavy bombers against an enemy's industry, transportation, and cities, aiming to break its capacity and will to make war”
  • The first term covers Torch, Sicily, Salerno, and Normandy alike.
  • The strategic bombing definition's two objects, capacity and will, are the two claims made for the campaign.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for the road to VE Day

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — D-Day, Battle of the Bulge, VE Day. The full definitions below match the guided notes word for word.

  • Say: “D-Day: June 6, 1944, the day Allied forces landed in Normandy, France, in the largest seaborne invasion in history”
  • Students sometimes treat D-Day as a word for any invasion; in this unit it names one date.
  • Say: “Battle of the Bulge: the last major German offensive in the west, launched through the Ardennes in December 1944 and beaten back by February 1945”
  • The Bulge definition carries the battle's strategic meaning, last offensive, which is the fact the notebook chart tests.
  • Say: “VE Day: May 8, 1945, the day victory in Europe was announced after Germany's unconditional surrender”
  • Note the sequence inside the VE Day definition: the surrender came first, signed at Reims on May 7, and the announcement and celebration followed on May 8.
  • With these three terms the full set of seven is complete, and all seven appear in the guided notes, in these slides, and in the review materials for the unit test.
