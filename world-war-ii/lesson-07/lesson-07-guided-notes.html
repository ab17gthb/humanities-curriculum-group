Guided Notes: The Holocaust and the War's Crimes

Lesson question: What did Nazi Germany do to the Jews of Europe and to others it marked for persecution and death, and what did the world know, do, and answer?

Unit question: How did the Second World War transform the United States, and how far did the country live up to the democracy it claimed to defend?

Key Terms

antisemitism: prejudice against, hatred of, or discrimination against Jews as a religious or ethnic group

Nuremberg Laws: the German laws of 1935 that stripped Jews of citizenship and forbade marriage between Jews and non-Jewish Germans

Holocaust: the state-sponsored persecution and murder of six million European Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators, together with the murder of millions of others, including Roma, people with disabilities, and Soviet prisoners of war

ghetto: a section of a city where Jews were forced to live in crowded and starved conditions under German occupation

Final Solution: the Nazi program to murder every Jew in Europe, carried out by shooting units, deportations, and killing centers from 1941 to 1945

death camp: one of the camps built by Nazi Germany in occupied Poland where Jews and others were murdered, most in gas chambers on arrival

war crimes: violations of the laws of war, such as the murder of prisoners and civilians, for which individuals were tried after the war

E. Knowledge and Response

In January 1944 the United States created the (15) ____________, which worked in the war's last year to rescue Jews who could still be reached, including support for Raoul Wallenberg in Budapest, whose papers and safe houses saved thousands.

F. Liberation and the Camera

American forces reached Ohrdruf, the first camp they entered, on April 4, 1945. General Dwight D. Eisenhower visited on April 12 and ordered the camp documented. He brought members of Congress and journalists to see it, and he wrote that he had come deliberately, so that first-hand evidence would exist if the reports were dismissed as propaganda. Buchenwald was liberated on April 11, and American photographers recorded the barracks and the survivors.

G. Judgment at Nuremberg

At Nuremberg in 1945 and 1946 an international tribunal tried the surviving leaders of Nazi Germany for war crimes. Justice Robert H. Jackson, the chief American prosecutor, opened the case by promising proof from the Germans' records. The tribunal heard the evidence, convicted most of the defendants, and sentenced twelve to death. Japanese leaders were also tried, among their crimes the Bataan Death March of 1942.

Summary table. Persecution and refusal: the Nuremberg Laws of 1935 stripped Jews of citizenship and forbade intermarriage, and Kristallnacht in November 1938 brought open violence and about thirty thousand arrests. At Evian in 1938 and with the St. Louis in 1939, the United States and other countries declined to admit the refugees who were trying to escape, and about a quarter of the St. Louis passengers were later murdered. Ghettos and the shooting squads: Jews in occupied Poland were confined in ghettos under crowded and starved conditions, Warsaw holding more than four hundred thousand people, while the Einsatzgruppen shooting units murdered more than one million people, including nearly thirty-four thousand at Babi Yar in two days. The Final Solution: from 1942 Jews were deported by train to killing centers in occupied Poland and murdered in gas chambers; the record supports six million Jews murdered, alongside Roma, people with disabilities, Polish and Soviet civilians, and more than three million Soviet prisoners of war. Liberation and judgment: Allied armies reached the camps in the spring of 1945, Eisenhower ordered Ohrdruf documented and witnessed, and at Nuremberg the surviving leaders were tried for war crimes on the evidence, with twelve sentenced to death.

Exit ticket 2. The St. Louis sailed from Hamburg in May 1939 with 937 Jewish passengers. Cuba refused most of them entry, the United States and Canada turned the ship away, and it returned to Europe. Later research established that about a quarter of its passengers were murdered in the Holocaust.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that covers all three parts with facts from the notes. The strongest answers state that Nazi Germany persecuted Jews in law from 1933 and murdered six million of them, by shooting units and in killing centers, together with millions of others; that Allied governments received reports from 1942 and condemned the extermination that December, while rescue came late and on a small scale through the War Refugee Board of 1944; and that the answer after the war was a trial at Nuremberg, decided on evidence, at which the surviving leaders were tried for war crimes.
