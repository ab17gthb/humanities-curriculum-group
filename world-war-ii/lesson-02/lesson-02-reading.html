Pearl Harbor and the Arsenal of Democracy

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Attack

On Sunday morning, December 7, 1941, Japanese carrier aircraft struck Pearl Harbor, the United States naval base in Hawaii attacked by Japanese carrier aircraft on December 7, 1941, the attack that brought the United States into the Second World War. The planes of six Japanese carriers came in two waves against the battleships beside Ford Island and the airfields at Hickam and Wheeler.

In about two hours, more than 2,400 Americans were killed. All eight battleships in the harbor were damaged, and four were sunk, among them Arizona, which exploded and sank with more than 1,100 of her crew. On the airfields, 188 American aircraft were destroyed, most on the ground. The Pacific Fleet carriers were at sea that morning, and they survived.

B. A Date Which Will Live in Infamy

The attack on Hawaii was one blow in a wider offensive. On the same day, Japanese forces struck the Philippine Islands, Guam, and Wake Island, and British Malaya and Hong Kong. Japanese troops soon landed in the Philippines.

On December 8, President Franklin D. Roosevelt addressed a joint session of Congress. He called December 7 a date which will live in infamy, and asked Congress to declare that a state of war had existed since the attack. The Senate voted 82 to 0. The House voted 388 to 1, the one dissent cast by Representative Jeannette Rankin of Montana.

C. Germany Declares War

The declaration of December 8 covered Japan alone. Nothing required the United States to fight Germany, and the country might have faced a war in one ocean only. On December 11, Germany and Italy removed that choice. Both declared war on the United States, and Congress answered the same day, without a dissenting vote. The Axis powers, the alliance of Germany, Italy, and Japan that fought the Allied nations in the Second World War, had made the war global by their own act.

Hitler was not bound by treaty to follow Japan, because Japan had been the attacker. He declared war anyway, wanting his submarines free against American shipping. The enemy had settled the largest question of December. The United States would fight a two-ocean war.

D. What December Settled

The attack first settled the argument that had divided the country since 1939. The neutrality debate, carried on through years of legislation and speeches, ended in a day. The Allies, the nations allied against the Axis in the Second World War, led by the United States, Great Britain, and the Soviet Union, now included the United States in full.

The country entered united in a way it had not entered in 1917, when the declaration followed months of argument and carried a large minority with it. In December 1941 there was no large minority. The country had decided to fight. How it would fight remained open.

E. The Grand Alliance and Germany First

The answer took shape in the war's first conferences. British and American leaders met in Washington at the Arcadia Conference, opening December 22. Out of it came the Grand Alliance, the formal alliance of the United States, Great Britain, and the Soviet Union against the Axis, joined by many other nations in the Declaration by United Nations of January 1, 1942. Twenty-six nations signed the declaration.

The conference held to Germany first, the Allied strategy of concentrating the main effort on defeating Germany before Japan, agreed by the American and British staffs and kept through the war. Germany was judged the stronger enemy, able to knock Britain or the Soviet Union out of the war. Japan would be held in its theater, a large geographic area of military operations, such as the European theater or the Pacific theater, until Germany fell. The Soviet Union demanded a second front, the Allied invasion of German-held western Europe that the Soviet Union demanded from 1942 onward to draw German strength away from the Eastern Front.

F. The Cost of the First Six Months

The first six months brought defeat after defeat. Japanese forces overran the Philippines despite the defense of Bataan, where American and Filipino troops held out on short rations until April 9, 1942. About 75,000 surrendered, and thousands died on the march their captors forced them to make to prison camps. Corregidor fell on May 6. Japan also took Malaya and Singapore.

One answer came on April 18, 1942. Sixteen army bombers, flown from the carrier Hornet under Lieutenant Colonel James Doolittle, bombed Tokyo and other cities. The damage was light. The value lay in morale at home and shock in Japan, proof the war could reach the enemy capital five months after Pearl Harbor.

G. An Arsenal on Paper

Behind the defeats stood the largest open question. In December 1941 the arsenal of democracy existed mostly as a promise and a set of factory orders. The army was growing under the draft, but it was not yet trained or equipped. The factories that would build its ships, planes, and tanks were still converting from peacetime work.

In his annual message of January 6, 1942, Roosevelt set the targets in public: 60,000 warplanes in 1942, 45,000 tanks, 20,000 antiaircraft guns, and eight million tons of merchant shipping. Whether industry could reach those figures was still unanswered. The attack had settled why the country would fight and, with Germany's help, against whom.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the damage at Pearl Harbor in figures, and name what survived. More than 2,400 Americans were killed. All eight battleships in the harbor were damaged and four were sunk, including Arizona, which sank with more than 1,100 of her crew. On the airfields, 188 American aircraft were destroyed, most of them on the ground. The Pacific Fleet aircraft carriers survived because they were at sea on the morning of the attack. Accept the wounded figure of about 1,178 if a student adds it from the activity record.

2. Explain how the United States came to be at war with Germany. The declaration of December 8, 1941 covered Japan alone, and no law required war with Germany. On December 11, Germany and Italy declared war on the United States, and Congress answered the same day with declarations passed without a dissenting vote. Hitler was not bound by treaty to follow Japan, since Japan had been the attacker. He acted expecting American entry sooner or later and wanting his submarines free against American shipping. The two-ocean war was therefore settled by the enemy's choice, not by Congress's first declaration.

3. What did the attack settle about American opinion, and how did 1941 differ from 1917? It ended the neutrality debate in a day. The argument carried on since the mid 1930s over whether the United States should aid Britain, arm itself, or stay out collapsed once American forces had been attacked. In 1917 the declaration followed months of argument and carried a large minority with it. In 1941 there was no large minority: the Senate voted 82 to 0 and the House 388 to 1 on Japan, and the later declarations against Germany and Italy passed without dissent. The country entered united because the attack, not a presidential argument, had made the decision.

4. Explain Germany first, and name the Soviet demand. Germany first was the Allied strategy of concentrating the main effort on defeating Germany before Japan, agreed by the American and British staffs and kept through the war. The reasoning was that Germany was the stronger enemy and the one that could knock Britain or the Soviet Union out of the war, while Japan could be held in the Pacific theater until Germany fell. The demand pressed against it was the second front, the Allied invasion of German-held western Europe that the Soviet Union demanded from 1942 onward to draw German strength away from the Eastern Front.

5. What did the attack settle, and what did it leave for Americans to decide? Full credit answers hold both halves together. Settled: the neutrality debate ended, the United States entered the war united, and, after Germany and Italy declared war on December 11, the country faced a two-ocean war against three enemies rather than a Pacific war alone. Left open: how to fight that war, answered in part by the Grand Alliance and the Germany first strategy and pressed by the Soviet demand for a second front; whether the alliance would hold; and whether American industry could turn the production targets of January 1942 into the ships, planes, and trained forces the strategy required, a question made sharper by the defeats of the first six months, from Bataan to the fall of Corregidor. Answers that list only the settled side, or only the open side, earn partial credit.
