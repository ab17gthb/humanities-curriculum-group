Writing Task: Did the War Years Advance Freedom at Home?

The prompt. Answer the question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Did the war years advance freedom at home? Argue both sides of the record before you judge it. A strong answer sets out the claims gained, Executive Order 8802, the Double V campaign, and the service record, and sets out the exclusion, the removal and the camps and the court ruling that let them stand, and then weighs which side of the record carries more weight and why. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, drawn from both halves of the bank, and explain how each piece supports your judgment.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the Contradiction File. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. Executive Order 8802, issued June 25, 1941 under the threat of A. Philip Randolph's march on Washington, barred discrimination in defense industries and federal employment and created the Fair Employment Practices Committee. The committee could investigate and publicize complaints but could not compel a firm to hire.

2. The Double V campaign, launched by the Pittsburgh Courier in February 1942, demanded victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home, and claimed full citizenship as a war aim rather than as a reward for service.

3. More than a million Black Americans served in a segregated military. The 332nd Fighter Group escorted bombers from Italy with a loss record better than other escort groups, the 761st Tank Battalion fought across France and Germany, and more than 350,000 women served across the services.

4. Executive Order 9066, signed February 19, 1942, authorized military commanders to exclude any persons from areas they named. The order stated no reason in its text and named no group.

5. About 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry were removed from the West Coast, two thirds of them American citizens. Families sold property in days at heavy losses or lost it outright, and the government held them in ten guarded camps. No charge was filed and no hearing tested any one person's loyalty.

6. In Korematsu v. United States in 1944 the Supreme Court upheld the exclusion by six votes to three as a military necessity, while a dissent charged that the exclusion fell into racism. The 442nd Regimental Combat Team, drawn in part from the camps, became one of the most decorated units of its size and length of service in American history.

Plan Before You Write

My judgment (one sentence that answers the question directly and weighs both sides):

My evidence picks (bank numbers, at least one from items 1 to 3 and at least one from items 4 to 6) and what each one proves:

The strongest evidence against my judgment, and how I will answer it:

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your judgment, present the claims gained, present the exclusion, answer the strongest evidence against your judgment in one or two sentences, and end by restating what the record shows.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment that answers the question directly and weighs both halves of the record rather than listing one side. (Blueprint 11 and Blueprint 12)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, with at least one from each half. Bank items 1 through 3 carry the claims: the 8802 order and its enforcement limit, the Double V demand, and the service record in figures. (Blueprint 11) Bank items 4 through 6 carry the exclusion: the text of Executive Order 9066, the removal figures and the property losses, and the Korematsu holding with its dissent. (Blueprint 12)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the judgment. The strongest essays name a standard for weighing, such as whether a gain reached people as a right they could enforce or as a favor they had to request, and apply that standard to both halves.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest evidence on the other side, answered from the record rather than dismissed. An essay arguing that freedom advanced must answer the camps and the ruling. An essay arguing that it did not must answer the 8802 order and the service record.

Model opening for teacher reference. The war years advanced freedom at home on paper and in service, and they withdrew it in fact from the people who could least answer back. Executive Order 8802 gave Black workers a federal claim against discrimination in war work for the first time, and more than a million Black Americans served, building records such as the 332nd Fighter Group's escort record that no later argument could erase. In the same years the government removed about 120,000 people on ancestry alone, two thirds of them citizens, and the Supreme Court upheld the removal. A full model continues in this pattern, weighs both halves against a stated standard, answers the strongest opposing evidence, and closes on what the record shows.

Common weak answers. Gains only (the order, the campaign, the service record) describes the claims without the exclusion the prompt requires. Exclusion only (the removal and the ruling) makes the same error in reverse. A judgment with no stated standard for weighing earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a weighing, not a tally.
