Guided Notes: A War for Democracy? Exclusion, Internment, and Service

Lesson question: How did a war fought in democracy's name test democracy at home, in who could work, who could serve, and who was imprisoned?

Unit question: How did the Second World War transform the United States, and how far did the country live up to the democracy it claimed to defend?

Key Terms

Executive Order 8802: President Roosevelt's order of June 1941 barring racial discrimination in defense industries and federal employment, issued under the threat of a protest march on Washington

Fair Employment Practices Committee: the federal committee created in 1941 to investigate complaints of job discrimination in war industries

Double V campaign: the campaign led by Black newspapers from 1942 for victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home

Executive Order 9066: President Roosevelt's order of February 19, 1942 authorizing the military to exclude people from areas of the West Coast, the order under which Japanese Americans were removed from their homes

internment: the forced confinement of a group of people; in this unit, the removal of about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, two thirds of them American citizens, to guarded camps for much of the war

Korematsu v. United States: the 1944 Supreme Court decision that upheld the exclusion of Japanese Americans from the West Coast as a military necessity

442nd Regimental Combat Team: the segregated Japanese American army unit that fought in Europe and became one of the most decorated units of its size and length of service in American history

Summary table. War work: Executive Order 8802 barred discrimination in defense industries and federal employment and created a committee to hear complaints, and Black employment in war plants rose; the committee could investigate and publicize but could not compel a firm to hire, and Congress kept its funding short. The press campaign: the Double V campaign claimed victory over fascism abroad and racism at home and refused service first and citizenship later; the claim was a demand, not a grant, and segregation in the services and in war housing stood through the war. Service: more than a million Black Americans served, the Tuskegee Airmen and the 761st Tank Battalion built combat records, and more than 350,000 women served; the military kept its units segregated and assigned most Black servicemen to service and labor units under white officers. Removal and the courts: about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry were removed to ten camps, two thirds of them citizens, and the Supreme Court upheld the exclusion in Korematsu in 1944; Japanese American soldiers of the 442nd fought in Europe while their families remained behind barbed wire.

Exit ticket 1. A. Philip Randolph organized a march on Washington for the summer of 1941 to demand fair hiring in defense work. Days before the march, on June 25, 1941, Roosevelt issued Executive Order 8802, and Randolph called the march off.

Exit ticket 2. About 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry were removed from the West Coast, two thirds of them American citizens, and the government held them in ten guarded camps in the interior.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that pairs a gain with a denial. The strongest answers set Executive Order 8802, the Double V campaign, and the service record against the removal of about 120,000 people and the Korematsu ruling, and conclude that the war widened the claims Americans could make while the government denied the most basic claim of all, freedom from imprisonment without a charge, to citizens chosen by ancestry.
