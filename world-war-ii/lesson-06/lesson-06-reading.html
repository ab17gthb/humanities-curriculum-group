A War for Democracy? Exclusion, Internment, and Service

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The War's Aims and the Country's Facts

In January 1941 President Roosevelt told Congress that the world he wanted rested on four freedoms, speech, worship, freedom from want, and freedom from fear. That August he and Winston Churchill issued the Atlantic Charter, pledging self-determination for peoples. The United States entered the war in December carrying those words as its stated aims. The country that carried them ran a segregated army, barred Black workers from many defense jobs, and held elections that many Black Southerners could not vote in. This lesson holds the aims and the facts side by side and asks how far the war years closed the distance between them.

B. The Fight for War Work

Defense factories hired at speed in 1940 and 1941, and many refused Black applicants outright. A. Philip Randolph, who led the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, organized a march on Washington for the summer of 1941 to demand fair hiring. Days before the march, on June 25, 1941, Roosevelt issued Executive Order 8802, barring discrimination in defense industries and federal employment, and created the Fair Employment Practices Committee to hear complaints. Randolph called the march off. The committee could investigate and publicize, but it could not compel a firm to hire, and Congress kept its funding short. Even so, the order gave workers a federal claim to cite, and Black employment in war plants rose through the war.

C. Double V

In February 1942 the Pittsburgh Courier, a leading Black newspaper, launched the Double V campaign. Its readers had sent in a letter asking whether Black Americans should adopt a double V, victory over enemies abroad and victory over enemies at home. The paper made the question a campaign, and it spread through Black newspapers, churches, and barbershops across the country. Double V named a bargain refused. Black Americans would serve, buy bonds, and work in the war plants, and they would claim full citizenship as a war aim, not as a reward to be granted later. The campaign gave the claims of this lesson their slogan and their standard for judging every gain that followed.

D. Service in a Segregated Military

More than a million Black Americans served in uniform, most of them assigned to service and labor units under white officers in a military that kept its units segregated. The combat record answered the policy that produced it. The Tuskegee Airmen, trained in Alabama, flew escort and combat missions from Italy with the 332nd Fighter Group. The 761st Tank Battalion fought across France and Germany. Benjamin O. Davis Sr. became the first Black general in 1940. More than 350,000 women served across the services, including about 150,000 in the Women's Army Corps, alongside more than 59,000 Army nurses. Mexican Americans served in large numbers, Native American code talkers sent coded radio messages their enemies never broke, and more than half a million Jewish Americans served.

E. Removal: Executive Order 9066 and the Camps

On February 19, 1942, ten weeks after Pearl Harbor, Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066. The order let military commanders exclude any persons from areas they named. The Army used it against one ancestry group. About 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry were ordered out of their West Coast homes, two thirds of them American citizens. Families sold homes, farms, and businesses in days, at heavy losses, or lost them outright. The government held them in ten guarded camps in the interior, behind barbed wire, under a program the reading calls by the lesson word internment, the forced confinement of a group of people. German and Italian ancestry brought no mass removal. No charge was filed against the people removed, and no hearing tested any one person's loyalty.

F. Korematsu and the Courts

Fred Korematsu, an American citizen, refused to leave his California home and was convicted for staying. His case reached the Supreme Court, which decided it in December 1944 as Korematsu v. United States. The Court upheld the exclusion by six votes to three, calling it a military necessity in wartime. Writing for the Court, Justice Black wrote that restrictions on one racial group were suspect and required rigid scrutiny, and then upheld this one. The dissenters answered from the same record. Justice Murphy called the exclusion a fall into the abyss of racism. Justice Jackson warned that the decision would stand as a loaded weapon ready for any authority that could claim urgent need. The ruling stood for the rest of the war.

G. The 442nd and the Claims

From inside the camps and from Hawaii, Japanese American men volunteered and were drafted into a segregated unit, the 442nd Regimental Combat Team. It fought in Italy and France, rescued a trapped Texas battalion in the Vosges in October 1944 at heavy cost, and became one of the most decorated units of its size and length of service in American history. The Military Intelligence Service used Japanese American linguists across the Pacific. The war ended with the claims and the camps standing in the same years. War work had opened under federal order, service had been given in full, and citizens had been imprisoned on ancestry alone with the Supreme Court's approval. What the country owed on each claim was left for later years to argue.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the war aims the reading opens with, and name two facts from section A that stood against them in 1941. The aims were the Four Freedoms of January 1941, speech, worship, freedom from want, and freedom from fear, and the Atlantic Charter's pledge of self-determination for peoples. Against them stood a segregated army, defense jobs closed to Black workers, and elections that many Black Southerners could not vote in. Any two of the three facts earn credit.

2. Explain how Executive Order 8802 came to be issued, what it ordered, and what limit the reading places on the committee it created. A. Philip Randolph organized a march on Washington for the summer of 1941 to demand fair hiring in defense work. Days before the march, on June 25, 1941, Roosevelt issued the order, barring discrimination in defense industries and federal employment and creating the Fair Employment Practices Committee to hear complaints. The limit is enforcement. The committee could investigate and publicize but could not compel a firm to hire, and Congress kept its funding short.

3. What did the Double V campaign demand, and what bargain does the reading say it refused? Launched by the Pittsburgh Courier in February 1942, the campaign demanded victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home, and claimed full citizenship as a war aim. It refused the bargain of service first and citizenship later as a reward. Black Americans would serve, buy bonds, and work in the plants, and the claim on full citizenship ran at the same time.

4. Give the figures for the removal under Executive Order 9066. About 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry were ordered out of their West Coast homes, and two thirds of them were American citizens. Families sold homes, farms, and businesses in days at heavy losses or lost them outright. The government held them in ten guarded camps in the interior. No charge was filed and no hearing tested any one person's loyalty. All three figures, the total, the citizen share, and the ten camps, are required for full credit.

5. Answer the lesson title's question using at least three facts from the reading, drawn from both sides. Full credit answers hold both records. On the claims side, Executive Order 8802 opened war work under federal order, more than a million Black Americans served, and the 442nd built its decorated record. On the exclusion side, about 120,000 people were removed on ancestry alone, two thirds of them citizens, and the Supreme Court upheld the exclusion in Korematsu in 1944. Answers that use only one side, or that assert an opinion without three facts, earn partial credit.
