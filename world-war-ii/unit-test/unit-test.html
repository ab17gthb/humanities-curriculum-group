Part 3: Sources (15 points)

Answer each question in complete sentences, citing the sources by letter.

Source A

President Franklin D. Roosevelt, address to Congress asking for a declaration of war against Japan, December 8, 1941. From the transcript held by the Franklin D. Roosevelt Presidential Library and Museum. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

“The United States was at peace with that Nation and, at the solicitation of Japan, was still in conversation with its Government and its Emperor looking toward the maintenance of peace in the Pacific. . . . It will be recorded that the distance of Hawaii from Japan makes it obvious that the attack was deliberately planned many days or even weeks ago. During the intervening time, the Japanese Government has deliberately sought to deceive the United States by false statements and expressions of hope for continued peace. . . . I ask that the Congress declare that since the unprovoked and dastardly attack by Japan on Sunday, December 7, 1941, a state of war has existed between the United States and the Japanese Empire.”

A1. (2 points) According to Roosevelt, what two facts show that the attack was planned in advance and that the peace talks were used as cover?

A2. (3 points) Roosevelt delivered this address one day after the attack. Using the source and the lesson, explain what the address and the vote that followed settled, and name one question about the war they did not settle.

Source B

Executive Order 9066, February 19, 1942. From the Federal Register, volume 7, page 1407. A cut is marked with an ellipsis.

“I hereby authorize and direct the Secretary of War, and the Military Commanders whom he may from time to time designate, whenever he or any designated Military Commander deems such action necessary or desirable, to prescribe military areas in such places and of such extent as he or the appropriate Military Commander may determine, from which any or all persons may be excluded, and with respect to which, the right of any person to enter, remain in, or leave shall be subject to whatever restrictions the Secretary of War or the appropriate Military Commander may impose in his discretion.”

B1. (2 points) State in your own words what power this order gives to military commanders, and over whom it can be used.

B2. (3 points) The order names no ethnic or national group. Explain how it nonetheless became the legal basis for removing one group from the West Coast, and state two facts about the people removed that the order itself does not mention.

Source C

Robert F. Sargent, United States Coast Guard, photograph of troops of the First Division disembarking from a Coast Guard landing craft at Utah Beach, Normandy, the morning of June 6, 1944. National Archives.

Sargent, a chief photographer's mate, took this picture from the landing craft as the troops went down the ramp into the water. The Coast Guard and the Army Signal Corps photographed the invasion throughout the day, and newspapers printed their pictures across the country within days.

C1. (2 points) What is happening in the photograph, and where and when was it taken? Answer from the caption and the image.

C2. (3 points) This photograph was made by a serviceman on duty and printed in newspapers within days. What does its existence show about how the government documented and presented the war to the home front, and what does the photograph not show about June 6, 1944?

Part 3: Sources

A1. The distance of Hawaii from Japan makes it obvious the attack was planned many days or weeks in advance, since the carriers had to sail that distance in secret. During the same period the Japanese government continued peace conversations and delivered expressions of hope for peace, which Roosevelt names as deliberate deception. Either fact earns a point; both together earn full credit. (Blueprint 3)

A2. The address and the nearly unanimous votes (one dissent in the House) settled that the United States was at war and ended the neutrality debate of 1935 to 1941 in a day; the country entered the war formally and united, unlike 1917. Not settled, any one of: how to fight a two-ocean war against three enemies, whether Germany or Japan came first (settled by agreement and by Germany's own declaration on December 11), or how the arsenal would be built. Answers must tie the settlement to the source's request for a declaration. (Blueprint 3)

B1. The order lets the Secretary of War and designated military commanders create military areas of any size they choose and exclude any or all persons from them, controlling who may enter, remain in, or leave, at the commander's discretion. The power runs over any persons, citizens included; the order sets no limit by citizenship or by charge. (Blueprint 12)

B2. The Western Defense Command used the order to issue exclusion orders directed in practice at all persons of Japanese ancestry on the West Coast, so a facially general power was applied to one ancestry group. Facts the order does not mention, any two: about 120,000 people were removed; two thirds of them were American citizens; they were sent to guarded camps in the interior for much of the war; families lost homes, businesses, and property sold in haste; no individual hearings or charges were held. (Blueprint 12)

C1. American troops of the First Division are disembarking from a Coast Guard landing craft, down its ramp into the sea, at Utah Beach, Normandy, on the morning of June 6, 1944, D-Day. The caption supplies the place and date; the image shows the ramp, the water, and the troops in full kit moving toward the beach. (Blueprint 7)

C2. The government put its own photographers in the first waves and released their pictures to newspapers within days, so documenting the invasion was official business and the home front saw the war largely through government images. The photograph does not show the cost or the scale of the day: the casualties on the beaches, the four other landing beaches, the airborne drops inland, or the thousands of ships behind the one craft in the frame. Full credit requires one point of each kind. (Blueprint 7)
