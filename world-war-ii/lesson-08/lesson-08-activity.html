The Decision File

Directions: The five stations below are the file of July and August 1945, in the order the documents reached the men who had to act on them. Study each station and answer its question. For each document, keep three things separate: what it decided, what it assumed, and what it left open. In Part 2, write the decision memorandum you would have sent on August 1, 1945, and then compare it against the record of what was decided.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: The Potsdam Declaration, July 26, 1945

Source: United States, United Kingdom, and China, Potsdam Declaration, July 26, 1945 (Wikisource). Passages are quoted from the audited text; cuts are marked with ellipses. Paragraph numbers are in the original.

(1) We, the President of the United States, the President of the National Government of the Republic of China and the Prime Minister of Great Britain, representing the hundreds of millions of our countrymen, have conferred and agree that Japan shall be given an opportunity to end this war.

(5) Following are our terms. We will not deviate from them. There are no alternatives. We shall brook no delay.

(10) We do not intend that the Japanese shall be enslaved as a race or destroyed as a nation, but stern justice shall be meted out to all war criminals, including those who have visited cruelties upon our prisoners. ... Freedom of speech, of religion, and of thought, as well as respect for the fundamental human rights shall be established.

(13) We call upon the Government of Japan to proclaim now the unconditional surrender of all the Japanese armed forces, and to provide proper and adequate assurances of their good faith in such action. The alternative for Japan is prompt and utter destruction.

What did this document decide, and what did it deliberately leave open? Answer from the text: name the demand, the promise, and the one subject the warning in paragraph 13 does not name.

Station 2: The Franck Report, June 11, 1945

Source: Committee on Political and Social Problems, Manhattan Project, chaired by James Franck, Report of June 11, 1945 (atomicarchive.com transcription, citing National Archives Record Group 77). Passages are quoted from the audited text; cuts are marked with ellipses.

The only reason to treat nuclear power differently from all the other developments in the field of physics is its staggering possibilities as a means of political pressure in peace and sudden destruction in war. ... The scientists on this Project do not presume to speak authoritatively on problems of national and international policy. However, we found ourselves, by the force of events, the last five years in the position of a small group of citizens cognizant of a grave danger for the safety of this country as well as for the future of all the other nations, of which the rest of mankind is unaware.

... the military advantages and the saving of American lives, achieved by the sudden use of atomic bombs against Japan, may be outweighed by the ensuing loss of confidence and wave of horror and repulsion, sweeping over the rest of the world, and perhaps dividing even the public opinion at home.

From this point of view a demonstration of the new weapon may best be made before the eyes of representatives of all United Nations, on the desert or a barren island. ... After such a demonstration the weapon could be used against Japan if a sanction of the United Nations (and of the public opinion at home) could be obtained, perhaps after a preliminary ultimatum to Japan to surrender or at least to evacuate a certain region as an alternative to the total destruction of this target.

What did this document assume about the future that the men deciding in July could not yet know, and what course did it ask them to take before any bomb fell on a city?

Station 3: The Invasion File

Source: Summary prepared for this lesson from the Allied planning record of 1945, read against the battles of Iwo Jima and Okinawa. This station is a labeled summary, not a reproduced document.

By the summer of 1945, Allied staffs were preparing Operation Downfall, the invasion of Japan itself: landings on Kyushu in November 1945, then landings on Honshu, near Tokyo, in the spring of 1946. The planners read the two most recent battles as previews. Iwo Jima, fought in February and March 1945 for an island of eight square miles, cost about 6,800 American dead, and nearly the whole Japanese garrison died rather than surrender. Okinawa, fought from April to June 1945, cost about 12,500 American dead on land and at sea, where kamikaze aircraft sank and damaged ships in waves; Japanese military deaths ran above 100,000, and Okinawan civilian deaths are estimated in the tens of thousands or more. Estimates of what an invasion would cost varied widely from study to study, because each rested on assumptions about how long Japan would fight and whether its civilians would resist; no single agreed figure existed in 1945. The file also carried the alternatives, continued blockade and bombing, and waiting for Soviet entry into the war.

What did this file assume about Japanese resistance, and which of its numbers is a record of a battle already fought rather than a prediction? Why does that distinction carry weight in the decision?

Station 4: The President's Statement, August 6, 1945

Source: Harry S. Truman, Statement Announcing the Use of the A-Bomb at Hiroshima, August 6, 1945 (Harry S. Truman Library, Public Papers). Passages are quoted from the audited text; cuts are marked with ellipses.

SIXTEEN HOURS AGO an American airplane dropped one bomb on Hiroshima, an important Japanese Army base. That bomb had more power than 20,000 tons of T.N.T. It had more than two thousand times the blast power of the British "Grand Slam" which is the largest bomb ever yet used in the history of warfare.

It is an atomic bomb. It is a harnessing of the basic power of the universe. The force from which the sun draws its power has been loosed against those who brought war to the Far East.

We are now prepared to obliterate more rapidly and completely every productive enterprise the Japanese have above ground in any city. ... Let there be no mistake; we shall completely destroy Japan's power to make war.

It was to spare the Japanese people from utter destruction that the ultimatum of July 26 was issued at Potsdam. Their leaders promptly rejected that ultimatum. If they do not now accept our terms they may expect a rain of ruin from the air, the like of which has never been seen on this earth.

What reason does this statement give for the bombing, stated in its own words, and what does it promise Japan if the terms are still refused? What does it not mention at all?

Station 5: The Surrender, September 2, 1945

Source: Empire of Japan and the Allied Powers, Japanese Instrument of Surrender, September 2, 1945 (National Archives milestone documents transcript; punctuation modernized). Passages are quoted from the audited text; cuts are marked with ellipses. The dated record below the text is drawn from the lesson reading.

We, acting by command of and in behalf of the Emperor of Japan, the Japanese Government, and the Japanese Imperial General Headquarters, hereby accept the provisions set forth in the declaration issued by the heads of the Governments of the United States, China, and Great Britain on 26 July 1945 at Potsdam, and subsequently adhered to by the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics. ...

We hereby proclaim the unconditional surrender to the Allied Powers of the Japanese Imperial General Headquarters and of all Japanese armed forces and all armed forces under Japanese control wherever situated.

We hereby command all Japanese forces wherever situated and the Japanese people to cease hostilities forthwith, to preserve and save from damage all ships, aircraft, and military and civil property. ...

The authority of the Emperor and the Japanese Government to rule the state shall be subject to the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers. ...

The sequence, as a dated record. August 6: Hiroshima bombed. August 8: the Soviet Union declares war on Japan. August 9: Soviet forces invade Manchuria, and a second bomb falls on Nagasaki. Night of August 9 to 10: the emperor breaks the deadlock in the Japanese government in favor of acceptance. August 14: Japan communicates its acceptance of the Potsdam terms. September 2: the Instrument of Surrender is signed aboard USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay.

Which July document does this instrument accept, and what power does its last quoted sentence hand to the Supreme Commander? Then answer from the sequence: name two events between Hiroshima and the signing that the file shows standing between the first bomb and the surrender.

Part 2: The Decision Memorandum

It is August 1, 1945. The Trinity test has succeeded, the Potsdam Declaration has been issued and not accepted, the invasion is being prepared for November, and the Soviet Union is pledged to enter the war. Write the memorandum you would have sent to the President. You may recommend any course the file supports, but your memorandum must rest on the documents in Part 1 and must state the strongest fact against your own recommendation.

Memorandum. Date it August 1, 1945. State your recommendation in the first two sentences, then give your reasons from the file, citing stations by number.

The fact against you. State the strongest fact or document in the file that cuts against your recommendation, and say what you would answer to it.

Against the record. Now compare your memorandum with what was decided. Where does your memorandum agree with the record of August 1945, and where does it part from it? Defend the difference, or state what in the record changes your judgment.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: The Potsdam Declaration

What did it decide, and what did it leave open? It decided the terms and refused negotiation in advance: the unconditional surrender of all Japanese armed forces, on terms the Allies would not deviate from, with no alternatives and no delay allowed. It promised that Japan would not be enslaved as a race or destroyed as a nation, that war criminals would face stern justice, and that freedom of speech, religion, and thought would be established. What it left open is the warning's instrument: paragraph 13 threatens prompt and utter destruction without naming the atomic bomb, which had been tested ten days earlier and was still secret. It also left unstated the future status of the emperor, the silence on which the Japanese government later fastened. Students should be able to say that the open points were not oversights; the declaration was written to be read in Tokyo while its strongest fact stayed hidden.

Station 2: The Franck Report

What did it assume, and what course did it ask for? It assumed a future its authors could not know: that sudden use on a city would bring a wave of horror and repulsion across the world, divide opinion at home, cost America confidence and standing, and, as the report argues in its full text, touch off an arms race that a demonstration might help prevent through international supervision of the weapon. The course it asked for: demonstrate the bomb before representatives of all United Nations on a desert or a barren island, so America could say it had a weapon it did not use; only after such a demonstration, and with international sanction and a further ultimatum, should the weapon be used against Japan. Credit answers that note the authors' position: they had built the weapon, disclaimed authority on policy, and wrote as citizens who knew a danger the rest of the world did not.

Station 3: The Invasion File

What did it assume, and which numbers are record rather than prediction? It assumed that Japanese resistance on the home islands would follow the pattern of Iwo Jima and Okinawa: garrisons fighting to near annihilation, kamikaze attacks in waves, and heavy civilian loss, extended over larger islands and a far larger population. The record numbers are the battle figures: about 6,800 American dead at Iwo Jima and about 12,500 at Okinawa, with Japanese military deaths at Okinawa above 100,000. Everything about the invasion itself was prediction, and the file says so: estimates varied widely because each rested on assumptions, and no single agreed figure existed in 1945. The distinction carries weight because arguments built on the prediction often present it as a counted fact; in the memorandum, students must carry the battle record as evidence and the invasion estimates as estimates.

Station 4: The President's Statement

What reason does it give, and what does it promise and omit? In its own words, the bombing answers the war Japan began at Pearl Harbor, and it serves the ultimatum of July 26, which was issued to spare the Japanese people from utter destruction and which their leaders promptly rejected; the statement's stated purpose is to destroy Japan's power to make war and so end the war on the Potsdam terms. It promises, if the terms are still refused, a rain of ruin from the air, followed by sea and land forces. What it does not mention: the demonstration the scientists had proposed, the coming Soviet entry into the war, the invasion estimates, or any count of the dead at Hiroshima. It calls Hiroshima an important Japanese Army base and gives the weapon's power in comparisons, 20,000 tons of T.N.T. and two thousand times the Grand Slam. Students should read it as both an announcement and an argument, written for Japanese leaders, the American public, and the watching world at once.

Station 5: The Surrender

Which document does it accept, what power does it hand over, and what stands between the first bomb and the signing? The instrument accepts the Potsdam Declaration of July 26, 1945, by name and date. Its last quoted sentence places the authority of the Emperor and the Japanese Government under the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, who may take such steps as he deems proper to carry out the terms; Japan kept its emperor, and kept him subject to an American commander. From the sequence, any two: the Soviet declaration of war on August 8; the Soviet invasion of Manchuria and the Nagasaki bombing on August 9; the emperor breaking the government's deadlock on the night of August 9 to 10; or Japan's communication of acceptance on August 14. The file's point is that the surrender followed a sequence, not a single blow, and any account of the decision has to carry all of it.

Part 2: The Decision Memorandum (Grading Guide)

Full credit should be awarded if the memorandum:

  • Is dated August 1, 1945 in its reasoning as well as its heading: it uses only what was knowable by that date, the Trinity result, the unanswered declaration, the invasion preparations, the Franck proposal, and the pledged Soviet entry, and it does not use Hiroshima, Nagasaki, or the surrender as evidence.
  • States a recommendation in its first two sentences and supports it with reasons cited to stations by number, drawing on at least three stations.
  • Carries the invasion material honestly: Iwo Jima and Okinawa as battle record, the invasion cost as an estimate that varied and was not agreed, whichever recommendation the student defends.
  • States the strongest fact against the recommendation at full strength. Against a recommendation to use the bomb: the Franck Report, written by the weapon's own makers, warning of horror, lost standing, and an arms race, with a workable alternative in a demonstration. Against a recommendation to demonstrate, wait, or rely on blockade: Japan had not accepted the Potsdam terms, its garrisons had fought to annihilation at Iwo Jima and Okinawa, and each week of delay carried its own dead across Asia and the Pacific.
  • In the comparison, names at least one agreement and one difference with the record, or defends full agreement, and defends the difference or states plainly what in the record changes the student's judgment.

Teacher note on the memoranda. The most common failure is hindsight written as foresight, a memorandum that knows August 6 is coming. Send those back to the date line. The second failure is a recommendation with no station citations, which is an opinion about the bomb rather than a judgment from this file; the file is the lesson.
