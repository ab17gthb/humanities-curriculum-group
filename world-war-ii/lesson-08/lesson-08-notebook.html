Teacher Answer Key

Question of 1945What the documents showWhat was decided
What peace would follow victory in Europe?At Yalta in February 1945, Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin planned Germany's division into occupation zones, founded the United Nations, and traded pledges: free elections in eastern Europe and Soviet entry against Japan, against territory for the Soviet Union in Asia. Poland's government and borders were left vague. At Potsdam in July the leaders argued over occupation policy, reparations, and eastern Europe, where Soviet armies stood.The conferences set the occupation of Germany and founded the United Nations. They did not settle Europe's future: each side read Yalta its own way, the argument was open at Potsdam, and the division of Europe carried out of the war unsettled.
On what terms would Japan be made to end the war?The Potsdam Declaration of July 26, 1945 demanded the unconditional surrender of all Japanese armed forces, refused negotiation in advance, promised Japan would not be destroyed as a nation, and warned of prompt and utter destruction without naming the weapon behind the warning.Japan gave no acceptance in July. After Hiroshima, Soviet entry, and Nagasaki, and with the emperor breaking his government's deadlock, Japan accepted the Potsdam terms on August 14 and signed the Instrument of Surrender on September 2, keeping the emperor subject to the Supreme Commander.
Should the new weapon be shown first, or used?The Franck Report of June 11, 1945, written by scientists who had built the bomb, proposed a demonstration on a desert or a barren island before representatives of all nations, and warned that sudden use on a city would bring horror across the world and start an arms race. The Interim Committee recommended use without a demonstration.Truman accepted the Interim Committee's recommendation. No demonstration was held. The bomb was used on Hiroshima on August 6 and on Nagasaki on August 9, and Truman's statement of August 6 announced the weapon to the world as a means of destroying Japan's power to make war.
What would ending the war without the bomb cost?The invasion file, a summary of the planning record, shows landings planned on Kyushu in November 1945 and Honshu in the spring of 1946, read against Iwo Jima, about 6,800 American dead, and Okinawa, about 12,500 American dead and Japanese military deaths above 100,000. Invasion cost estimates varied widely and were never agreed in 1945. Blockade, bombing, and Soviet entry stood in the file as alternatives.The invasion was never launched, so its cost stayed an estimate. The war ended through the sequence of August 1945 instead. In judging the decision, the battle figures are record and the invasion figures are estimates, and a sound answer keeps them separate.
What did victory cost, and who came home?About 405,000 Americans died out of more than 16 million who served. Worldwide deaths are estimated between 60 and 85 million, with Soviet deaths estimated at about 27 million, the largest share. More than 12 million Americans were in uniform at the end.Demobilization brought the forces home, and the GI Bill carried veterans into unemployment pay, college and training tuition, and government-backed home and business loans. Women were pushed toward home as veterans took war jobs, and Black veterans returned to the segregated South, so the transformed country was not transformed equally for everyone who had served it.

The lesson in one paragraph. Strong answers run the sequence and the distinction. Example: The war ended through the conferences that planned the peace, the ultimatum Japan did not answer, the decision to use the bomb without the demonstration its makers had proposed, and the sequence of August 1945, Hiroshima, Soviet entry, Nagasaki, and the emperor's intervention, closing with the signing aboard the Missouri on September 2. The bomb decided the war against Japan on the Potsdam terms. It left undecided the future of Europe, already argued over at Potsdam, and it left the country its reckoning: about 405,000 American dead within worldwide deaths estimated between 60 and 85 million, and more than 12 million veterans to bring home into the GI Bill's transformed country.
