Writing Task: Judging the Atomic Decision

The prompt. Judge the atomic decision from the documents of 1945, in a developed essay of about 300 words. Your verdict may defend the decision to use the bomb, or condemn it, or defend a course that was not taken. Whatever you argue, judge from what the documents of 1945 show: the ultimatum Japan was given, the argument the bomb's makers made, the invasion file the planners read, and the record of August. State the strongest argument against your own verdict, at full strength, and answer it. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and The Decision File. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording. Keep the invasion figures honest as you use them: what happened at Iwo Jima and Okinawa is record, and what an invasion would have cost is an estimate that was not agreed in 1945.

Evidence Bank

1. The Potsdam Declaration of July 26, 1945 demanded the unconditional surrender of all Japanese armed forces, refused negotiation in advance, promised Japan would not be destroyed as a nation, and warned of prompt and utter destruction without naming the atomic bomb behind the warning. Japan gave no acceptance.

2. The Franck Report of June 11, 1945, from scientists who had built the bomb, proposed a demonstration on a desert or a barren island before representatives of all nations, and warned that sudden use on a city would bring a wave of horror and repulsion across the world and start an arms race. The Interim Committee recommended use without a demonstration, and Truman accepted that recommendation.

3. The invasion file: landings were planned on Kyushu in November 1945 and Honshu in the spring of 1946. Iwo Jima had cost about 6,800 American dead for eight square miles, and Okinawa about 12,500 American dead, with Japanese military deaths above 100,000 and kamikaze attacks in waves. Estimates of an invasion's cost varied widely and were not agreed in 1945.

4. The Manhattan Project employed more than 100,000 people at secret sites across the country at a cost of about two billion dollars, and its first bomb was tested successfully at Trinity on July 16, 1945, while Truman was at Potsdam.

5. Truman's statement of August 6, 1945 announced the Hiroshima bombing, gave the weapon's power as more than 20,000 tons of T.N.T., stated the purpose as destroying Japan's power to make war on the Potsdam terms, and promised a rain of ruin from the air if Japan still refused. It did not mention the demonstration proposal, Soviet entry, or the dead at Hiroshima.

6. The sequence after Hiroshima: the Soviet Union declared war on August 8 and invaded Manchuria on August 9, the day a second bomb fell on Nagasaki. Japan's leaders, still split evenly, were brought to acceptance when the emperor broke the deadlock; Japan accepted the Potsdam terms on August 14 and signed the surrender aboard the Missouri on September 2.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A verdict that answers the prompt's question, whether the atomic decision of 1945 was justified, stated early and held to the end. Either verdict, and a defended third course such as demonstration first, can earn full credit; an essay with no verdict has summarized the file rather than judged it. (Blueprint 15)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, drawn from at least two different kinds of document in the file, an ultimatum, a dissenting report, a planning record, a public statement, or the surrender sequence. An essay built on one document alone has not judged from the file. (Blueprint 15)
  • The strongest argument against the verdict stated at full strength and answered. Against a defense of the bombing: the Franck Report, the weapon's own makers warning of horror and an arms race with a workable demonstration available, and a declaration whose warning never named the weapon it threatened. Against a condemnation: Japan unanswering at Potsdam, garrisons fighting to annihilation at Iwo Jima and Okinawa, and a government still split evenly after Hiroshima, so that delay and half measures carried their own dead across Asia and the Pacific. (Blueprint 15)
  • The figures handled as the lesson taught them: Iwo Jima and Okinawa as record, the invasion's cost as an estimate that varied and was not agreed in 1945. An essay that presents any single invasion casualty figure as a counted fact has failed the file's central distinction, whatever its verdict. (Blueprint 15)
  • The sequence kept whole: an essay that credits or blames August 6 alone, and omits Soviet entry, Nagasaki, and the emperor's intervention, has misread how the war actually ended. (Blueprint 15)

Model opening for teacher reference. Judged from the documents of 1945, the decision to use the bomb was the decision the file pushed its makers toward, and its hardest fact remains the one its makers were warned about in advance. Japan had been given terms at Potsdam and had not answered them; its garrisons had fought to annihilation on Iwo Jima and Okinawa; the invasion was being prepared for November. Against that record stands the Franck Report, written by the men who built the weapon, proposing a demonstration and predicting the horror and the arms race that sudden use would bring. A full model continues by weighing those two records against each other, answers the side it did not take at full strength, and closes on a verdict the evidence, and not the verdict's comfort, has chosen.

Common weak answers. A verdict built on hindsight alone, judging 1945 by what no one in the file could know, has left the documents behind. A celebration that omits bank 2 cannot explain why the argument has lasted eighty years. A condemnation that omits bank 1 and bank 3 cannot explain what the men deciding in July believed the alternative to be. Treating the invasion estimate as a counted fact, or the surrender as the work of one bomb on one day, repeats the two errors this lesson was built to correct.
