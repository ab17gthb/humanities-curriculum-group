The Nullification Crisis

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Tariff South Carolina Paid For

South Carolina sold cotton to the world and bought nearly everything else. Its planters shipped their crop to Britain and bought manufactured goods that arrived on foreign ships. A tariff, a tax on imported goods, raised the price of those purchases. Congress passed protective tariffs in 1816, 1824, and 1828 to shelter northern factories from British competition. Southerners also feared Britain would buy less southern cotton in return. By 1828 the newest tariff had a southern name, the Tariff of Abominations, and South Carolina, its economy stagnant, was doing the arithmetic of a state that expected to lose every national vote on the question for years.

B. Calhoun Builds the Doctrine in Secret

The man who built the answer was the vice president. John C. Calhoun of South Carolina had once supported tariffs and a national bank, but by 1828 he was writing in secret for his own state. The South Carolina legislature adopted his pamphlet, the Exposition and Protest, in December 1828 without naming its author. It argued that the Constitution was a compact among sovereign states, and that where no tribunal stood above the parties, each party kept the right to judge when the compact had been broken. A state, acting through a convention, could interpose its sovereignty and declare a federal law void inside its borders. The law would stay suspended until three fourths of the states amended the Constitution to grant the power plainly. He called this a peaceful remedy inside the Union.

C. Webster Answers Hayne in the Senate

In January 1830 the doctrine reached the Senate floor. A debate over western land sales widened when Robert Y. Hayne of South Carolina defended the compact theory and nullification. Daniel Webster of Massachusetts answered in his second reply to Hayne, delivered on January 26 and 27. Webster argued that the Constitution was not a compact among state governments but the people's government, made by the people and answerable to them. Under that reading, no state could judge federal law for itself, because the Constitution named its own judges and its own method of change. Webster closed by refusing to imagine the Union broken, and his final sentence joined liberty and union as one and inseparable. Newspapers printed the speech across the country, and schoolchildren recited its closing words for a century.

D. A Toast, a Breach, and a Resignation

The argument soon had faces. At the Jefferson Day dinner in April 1830, President Andrew Jackson rose and offered a toast to the federal Union, declaring that it must be preserved. Calhoun answered with a toast of his own, placing the Union next to liberty in his affections, and the table understood that president and vice president had taken opposite sides in public. Other quarrels widened the breach. A social war over Peggy Eaton divided the cabinet, and Jackson turned to Martin Van Buren as his heir. In 1831 Calhoun published his Fort Hill letter and owned the doctrine under his own name at last. In December 1832 he resigned the vice presidency and took a seat in the Senate to defend his state's course.

E. The Ordinance and the Deadline

The Tariff of 1832 lowered some duties but kept the protective system, and South Carolina's leaders judged it no answer at all. In November 1832 a state convention, elected for the purpose, adopted the Ordinance of Nullification. The ordinance declared the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 unauthorized by the Constitution, null, void, and no law inside South Carolina. It forbade collection of their duties after February 1, 1833, barred appeals to the federal courts, and required state officers to uphold it. If the federal government collected the duties by force, South Carolina would consider itself absolved from the Union and would organize a separate government. The state began raising volunteers. A doctrine written for a pamphlet in 1828 now had a date, a deadline, and a militia.

F. Jackson Answers with a Proclamation

Jackson answered on December 10, 1832, in a proclamation to the people of South Carolina. He told them that the power to annul a law of the United States, assumed by one state, was incompatible with the existence of the Union and contradicted by the letter of the Constitution itself. The Constitution, he wrote, forms a government, not a league, and it operates directly on the people, not on the states. He warned that disunion by armed force was treason, and he announced that the laws would be executed. The proclamation also asked South Carolinians whether they would destroy the Union their own fathers had fought to create. Jackson asked Congress for new authority to collect the duties, and he quietly strengthened the forts and revenue cutters around Charleston harbor.

G. Force, Compromise, and a Rehearsal

South Carolina stood alone. No other southern state joined the ordinance, while the state's own Unionist minority organized against the nullifiers at home. In Washington, two measures moved together in the winter of 1833. Congress passed the Force Bill, authorizing Jackson to use the army and navy to collect the duties, and Henry Clay's compromise tariff, lowering duties by stages to the levels of 1816 by 1842. Given a way down that surrendered nothing on paper, South Carolina climbed down. Its convention repealed the ordinance in March 1833, then nullified the Force Bill in a final gesture that changed nothing. Both sides claimed victory. The crisis had ended without a shot, but it had rehearsed an argument, a state convention, a legal brief for leaving, and a claim that a state could judge the Union's laws for itself, that the country would meet again in 1861.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Why did protective tariffs fall hardest on South Carolina? South Carolina sold cotton abroad and bought manufactured goods, tools, and cloth that arrived on foreign ships. A tariff raised the price of everything it bought, while the protection sheltered northern factories, and southerners feared Britain would buy less cotton in return. The state paid the duties without receiving the protection.

2. What did the Exposition and Protest claim a state could do, and what had to happen before the federal law could take effect in that state? It claimed that a state, acting through a convention of its people, could interpose its sovereignty and declare a federal law void inside its borders. The law stayed suspended unless three fourths of the states amended the Constitution to grant the disputed power plainly.

3. How did Webster’s answer rest on a different account of who made the Constitution? Webster argued the Constitution was the people's government, made by the people and answerable to the people, not a compact among state governments. It followed that no state could judge federal law for itself, because the Constitution named its own judges and its own method of change.

4. What did the Ordinance of Nullification order, and what did it threaten? It declared the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 null, void, and no law inside the state, forbade collection of their duties after February 1, 1833, barred appeals to the federal courts, and stated that if the federal government collected the duties by force, South Carolina would consider itself absolved from the Union and organize a separate government.

5. What two measures together ended the crisis, and why did South Carolina accept the settlement? Congress passed the Force Bill, authorizing Jackson to use the army and navy to collect the duties, and Clay's compromise tariff, lowering duties by stages to the levels of 1816 by 1842. Facing force with no other state beside it, the state could repeal its ordinance, point to the falling duties as its prize, and claim victory while the Union's authority stood unbroken.
