Nullification: Could a State Veto the Union?

Jackson and Reform · Lesson 3 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Jackson and Reform, Lesson 3 of 8 — after Lesson 2 (Indian removal). That order is the argument of the unit: the same president who declined to enforce a Supreme Court ruling for the Cherokee in Lesson 2 now prepares to enforce a tariff against South Carolina, and students should feel the tension between the two lessons without it being resolved for them. The lesson question is double — could a state veto the Union, and what made South Carolina back down — and the second half is what the writing task grades.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 2 (the Worcester ruling Jackson would not enforce — the same doctrine of state defiance, run in reverse), Lesson 4 (the Bank War — Jackson's veto power against Calhoun's nullification power, two theories of who may say no), and Lesson 8 (democracy's unfinished business, where nullification's filing-away is picked back up). The ordinance and proclamation are the lesson's paired documents; the writing task uses both plus the compromise.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: The tariff idea from Lesson 1's economics and any earlier treatment of protective duties; the Constitution's amendment process (three fourths of the states) at a civics level; and Lesson 2's holding in Worcester, since Jackson's enforcement posture here is the mirror image. No knowledge of the Kentucky Resolutions is assumed — the lesson teaches the pedigree Calhoun claimed as part of the doctrine itself.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.9 (causes of the crisis weighed and ranked), D2.His.13 (documents read against each other — ordinance against proclamation), D2.Civ.3 (the compact-versus-people constitutional argument), D2.Civ.10 (presidential enforcement power); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.8, WHST.9-10.1 (the ranked-causes writing task).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain how a tariff grievance became a constitutional doctrine with a procedure and a deadline; to state the compact theory and the people's-government answer to it from the documents themselves; and to rank the causes of South Carolina's 1833 repeal — isolation, force, and compromise — defending the ranking from the ordinance, the proclamation, and the settlement's chronology.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; the tariff and the doctrine (slides 4–8) 10 minutes; the debate and first vocabulary (slides 9–11) 8 minutes; the toast, the ordinance, and the proclamation (slides 12–20) 14 minutes; the settlement (slides 21–23) 6 minutes; second vocabulary, synthesis, and exit (slides 24–27) 7 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes, and the document packet (the ordinance's operative clauses and the proclamation's central passages). The exit ticket is the three questions at the end of the guided notes; the writing task ranks the causes of the repeal from three documents.

Key vocabulary

Tariff of Abominations · nullification · John C. Calhoun · South Carolina Exposition and Protest · Ordinance of Nullification · Webster-Hayne debate · Force Bill

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image, Andrew Jackson by Thomas Sully (full-bleed, dark overlay); the unit and lesson line; the title; the italic standfirst.

  • Open with the portrait and the standoff it promises.
  • Andrew Jackson, painted by Thomas Sully before 1829, is the president who will tell South Carolina that disunion by armed force is treason.
  • The state facing him is not rebelling in this lesson — it is doing something quieter and, for the Union, harder to answer: passing a law, in a convention, with a constitutional argument attached, claiming that a federal law simply does not exist inside its borders.
  • Set the lesson's shape: first a tariff grievance, then the doctrine built to answer it, then the doctrine debated, personalized, and enacted, then a proclamation, and finally a settlement in which force and compromise arrive in the same week.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the rule; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "Could a state veto the Union, and what made South Carolina back down?"
  • Read the lesson question aloud and split it into its two halves, because the writing task grades both.
  • The first half is constitutional: could one state declare a federal law void inside its borders and stay in the Union while doing it.
  • The second half is practical: whatever the theory said, something made South Carolina repeal its ordinance in March 1833, and students must rank the candidates — isolation, the threat of force, and the offer of lower duties.
  • Warn the class against the easy answer that the state simply lost the argument — South Carolina's leaders believed they had won the argument and repealed anyway, which is exactly why the second half of the question carries the lesson.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "A grievance, a doctrine, a deadline, a settlement"; the seven section rows, A–G.

  • Walk the map once, slowly — it is the lesson's whole argument in seven lines.
  • A tariff grievance comes first, economic before it is constitutional.
  • A doctrine follows, built in secret by the sitting vice president.
  • The doctrine is debated in the Senate, where Webster gives the national answer its lasting form.
  • The argument turns personal at a dinner table and in a resignation; then the doctrine becomes law in one state, with a date attached.
  • The president answers with a proclamation and a request for force; the settlement pairs that force with a compromise tariff.
  • The lesson ends on what the whole episode rehearsed for 1861.
  • Students should copy the seven section titles into their notes now.

Slide 4 · Section A: The Tariff South Carolina Paid For (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Cotton out, manufactures in, and a tax on everything that arrived."

  • Section A is the grievance, and it must land as arithmetic before it lands as politics.
  • South Carolina's economy ran in one direction: cotton sailed out to Britain, and nearly everything the state used — cloth, tools, iron, finished goods — sailed back in on foreign ships.
  • Every duty Congress laid on imports raised the price of what the planting states bought; the protection those duties bought landed in the North, where the sheltered factories stood.
  • Teach students to hold both halves at once, because Calhoun's doctrine in section B only makes sense as an answer to a real bill that arrived every year, in prices a planter could count, from a Congress where his section expected to be outvoted permanently.

Slide 5 · The bill, item by item

On the slide: five bullets — cotton sold to the world; the duties of 1816, 1824, and 1828; protection landing on northern factories; the fear of British retaliation; a permanent minority's arithmetic.

  • Put the key term on the board in its full form, because the name itself is evidence.
  • Say: "Tariff of Abominations: the Tariff of 1828, which raised duties on imported goods to protect northern manufacturers and which southerners denounced by that name because it raised the prices the planting states paid and invited foreign retaliation against their cotton."
  • Notice what the southern name complains about: not taxation itself, but taxation whose benefits flowed one way.
  • The state paid the duties at the Charleston customhouse and watched the protection shelter factories in other states, while its own staple faced a foreign market that American duties could shrink.
  • That arithmetic, repeated across the planting South, is the raw material of the crisis.

Slide 6 · Section B: Calhoun Builds the Doctrine in Secret (divider)

On the slide: the Calhoun portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "The vice president writes for his state, and signs nothing."

  • Introduce the portrait before the doctrine.
  • Picture: John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, painted by George Peter Alexander Healy about 1845.
  • He was no fringe figure in 1828 — a congressman, a secretary of war, and vice president under two presidents — and he had once supported the tariff and the national bank he now opposed.
  • His turn is part of the lesson: as his state's economy stalled and his national ambitions narrowed, he rebuilt his politics around the defense of the planting South.
  • The pamphlet in this section was adopted by the South Carolina legislature in December 1828 with no author's name on it, because the author was still the vice president of the government it argued against.

Slide 7 · The Exposition and Protest, 1828

On the slide: the Calhoun portrait at right; four bullets — the compact of sovereign states; no tribunal above the parties; interposition and nullification by convention; the law sleeping until three fourths amend.

  • Teach the doctrine in its author's own sequence, and put the unit's central definition on the board whole.
  • Say: "nullification: the claim, drawn from the Kentucky Resolutions, that a state may declare a federal law void and of no force inside its borders, a doctrine later generations carried much further than its 1798 authors did in print."
  • Calhoun's steps matter as much as his claim: the nullifying act had to come from a convention of the state's people, the same sovereign body that had ratified the Constitution, not from an ordinary legislature.
  • And the doctrine had an ending other than defiance: if three fourths of the states amended the Constitution to grant the disputed power, the state submitted.
  • Calhoun called this a remedy inside the Union, and students should hear that claim fairly before they hear Webster answer it.

Slide 8 · A remedy, its author said, not a revolution

On the slide: four bullets — interposition claimed from the Kentucky Resolutions of 1798; a veto arresting the law inside one state; the amendment process as final judge; published without a name, owned at Fort Hill in 1831.

  • Slow down on the pedigree Calhoun claimed, because he built his case as inheritance, not invention.
  • The pamphlet's full title belongs in students' notes.
  • Say: "South Carolina Exposition and Protest: the pamphlet adopted by the South Carolina legislature in December 1828, written secretly by Vice President Calhoun, which argued that a state, as a party to the Constitution's compact, could nullify a federal law inside its borders until the Constitution itself was amended."
  • Its argument reached back to the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions of 1798, written against the Alien and Sedition Acts, and read them as a standing grant of state judgment over federal overreach.
  • Opponents answered that 1798 protested and petitioned but never claimed to void a law in force — that gap between protest and nullification is where the next section's debate will be fought.

Slide 9 · Section C: Webster Answers Hayne in the Senate (divider)

On the slide: the Webster portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "A debate over land sales rises to the nature of the Union."

  • Set the Senate scene.
  • Picture: Daniel Webster of Massachusetts, painted by Francis Alexander in 1835 — the North's great constitutional lawyer.
  • Robert Hayne of South Carolina was Calhoun's ally and the nullifiers' voice on the floor.
  • The debate of January 1830 began with a resolution about western land sales, a subject that touched labor, land, and the tariff all at once; Hayne used it to lay out the compact theory in full.
  • Webster's second reply, delivered over two days, January 26 and 27, left land behind entirely and answered the theory at its root — printed across the North, its closing sentence treated for generations as a civic creed.

Slide 10 · The people, not the states

On the slide: the Webster portrait at right; four bullets — Hayne's compact defense; Webster's people's Constitution; answerable to the people; liberty and union, one and inseparable.

  • Give the debate its full name and date in the notes, because students meet it again on the test.
  • Say: "Webster-Hayne debate: the Senate debate of January 1830 in which Robert Hayne of South Carolina defended nullification and Daniel Webster of Massachusetts answered that the Constitution was the people's government, not a compact of states, closing with a defense of liberty and union as one and inseparable."
  • Webster's move was to change the author of the Constitution: if the people of the United States made it, then the federal government answers to them, courts judge its laws, and amendments change it.
  • A state convention is then a party inside the system, not a judge above it.
  • Hayne never lacked an answer on the compact's history, but Webster's version was the one the North memorized.

Slide 11 · Vocabulary: words for the grievance and the doctrine

On the slide: four terms with their short forms — Tariff of Abominations, nullification, John C. Calhoun, South Carolina Exposition and Protest.

  • Run this vocabulary slide as a check on the first half of the lesson, term by term, against the short forms on the slide; the full definitions wait in the guided notes for the written work.
  • Say: "John C. Calhoun: the South Carolina statesman who served as vice president under both Adams and Jackson, secretly wrote the Exposition and Protest, resigned the vice presidency in 1832 to defend nullification in the Senate, and became the leading theorist of the slaveholding South."
  • Ask: Which of these four terms names the grievance, which the doctrine, which the man, and which the document?

Slide 12 · Section D: A Toast, a Breach, and a Resignation (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "The president and his vice president take opposite sides at one table."

  • Section D explains how a constitutional argument became a broken administration.
  • The Jefferson Day dinner of April 1830 was a party ritual, a sequence of toasts to Jefferson's memory, and both men at the top of the government used it as a platform.
  • Around the toasts stood other quarrels to name plainly: the social campaign against Peggy Eaton, the wife of the secretary of war, which Jackson read as a conspiracy by Calhoun's circle; and the contest over who would succeed Jackson, with Martin Van Buren rising as Calhoun fell.
  • Doctrine and ambition cannot be separated in this section, and students judge better when they see both.

Slide 13 · Our federal Union, it must be preserved

On the slide: four bullets — the Jefferson Day dinner, April 1830; Jackson's toast aimed at nullification; Calhoun's answer; the Eaton affair and the Van Buren succession widening the breach.

  • Teach the two toasts as documents, word by word.
  • Jackson's was eleven words of commitment with no qualification, offered while looking, the accounts say, at his vice president.
  • Calhoun's reply placed the Union second — next to liberty — a ranking that sounded loyal and reserved everything, since the whole doctrine claimed to defend liberty against the Union's laws.
  • Everyone at the table understood both toasts as declarations, and the newspapers printed them as such.
  • Ask: Which toast could a nullifier repeat without changing a word — and which could a Unionist?
  • The exercise shows how much of this crisis was fought in sentences short enough to fit on a glass, long before it was fought in ordinances.

Slide 14 · Calhoun owns the doctrine, then resigns

On the slide: four bullets — the Fort Hill letter, July 26, 1831; the doctrine defended as the system's foundation; the vice presidency given up in December 1832; a Senate seat taken to defend the state's course.

  • Close the section on the resignation, an act without precedent.
  • No vice president had ever resigned the office, and Calhoun gave it up in December 1832 to take a seat in the Senate — trading the second office of the government for a platform to defend a doctrine the president of that government had just condemned.
  • He arrived in Washington as the crisis peaked, so the Senate gained the doctrine's author at the moment the doctrine's state defied the law.
  • Students should register the exchange he made: national office surrendered for sectional leadership, a choice that set the pattern of his remaining career and of the politics he built for the slaveholding South.

Slide 15 · Section E: The Ordinance and the Deadline (divider)

On the slide: the Hayne portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "November 24, 1832: the doctrine becomes state law."

  • Introduce Hayne's face for the section his state now dominates.
  • Picture: Robert Y. Hayne, shown in a portrait drawing published in Appletons' Cyclopaedia of American Biography in 1889.
  • Hayne had carried the compact argument in the Senate and now, as governor, would command the state's preparations.
  • The trigger is disappointment: the Tariff of 1832 lowered some duties but kept protection, and South Carolina's nullifiers judged half an answer worse than none.
  • A convention elected for the single purpose met in November 1832 and did what the Exposition had only argued — the doctrine now had legal form, a deadline, and armed men behind it, which changed every calculation in Washington.

Slide 16 · Null, void, and no law

On the slide: the Hayne portrait at right; four bullets — the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 declared void; collection forbidden after February 1, 1833; federal court appeals barred; secession threatened if duties are collected by force.

  • Read the ordinance's operative words aloud, then name the document whole for the notes.
  • Say: "Ordinance of Nullification: the ordinance adopted by a South Carolina convention on November 24, 1832, declaring the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 void within the state, forbidding collection of their duties after February 1, 1833, and threatening secession if the federal government collected them by force."
  • Three features deserve the class's attention. The ordinance spoke through a convention, the sovereign form the doctrine required.
  • It set a date — February 1, 1833 — turning theory into a countdown both governments had to plan against.
  • And it wrote its own consequence into the text: federal force would dissolve the state's obligation to the Union. The threat of secession was not an addition to nullification in 1832 — it was printed inside it.

Slide 17 · Stat: 0 other states joined South Carolina

On the slide: the big figure "0"; the label; the italic note — Georgia, Alabama, and the rest of the planting South condemned or ignored the ordinance, and South Carolina's own Unionists organized against it at home.

  • Linger on the zero, because isolation is the first cause students should weigh for the repeal.
  • Calhoun's doctrine was designed for a section acting together, and in the winter of 1832 and 1833 the section declined.
  • Other southern legislatures denounced the tariff and then denounced nullification in the same breath — Virginia going furthest in sympathy and still refusing the remedy.
  • Inside South Carolina, Unionist meetings in Charleston and the upcountry organized a counterweight the nullifiers had to reckon with.
  • When the convention finally voted to repeal, it voted as a state that had tested its doctrine's market and found no buyers — isolation did not refute the argument, but it priced it.

Slide 18 · Section F: Jackson Answers with a Proclamation (divider)

On the slide: the Jackson portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "December 10, 1832: the Union is a government, not a league."

  • Return to the title portrait, now in its working role.
  • Jackson's proclamation of December 10, 1832, was drafted with his secretary of state, Edward Livingston, and addressed past the convention to the people of South Carolina themselves.
  • Its strategy is the section's subject: it refuses the doctrine completely, in the strongest constitutional language any president had used, and in the same document pleads with South Carolinians as countrymen whose fathers had made the Union.
  • Jackson also acted while he argued — asking Congress for authority to collect the duties by force where the customhouses were blocked, and reinforcing the forts and cutters around Charleston.
  • Students should track argument and preparation together.

Slide 19 · Incompatible with the existence of the Union

On the slide: the Jackson portrait at right; four bullets — the annulment power condemned in one sentence; a government, not a league; laws operating on the people; disunion by armed force named as treason.

  • Teach the proclamation's central sentence as the mirror of Webster's reply.
  • Webster argued from the people's authorship; Jackson argued from consequences and text together — holding the annulment power contradicted expressly by the Constitution's letter, unauthorized by its spirit, and destructive of the object the Constitution was formed to achieve.
  • Note his concession for the sake of argument: even if the Union began as a compact, he wrote, its character now is a government operating on individuals, so the compact road no longer reaches nullification.
  • The proclamation gave Unionists across the South a presidential text to stand on, and several state replies quoted it against their own nullifiers.

Slide 20 · The appeal inside the warning

On the slide: four bullets — addressed to the people, over the convention's head; a shared Revolution invoked; execution of the laws announced as duty; Charleston watched, forts strengthened, cutters moved.

  • End the section on the proclamation's second register, because Jackson was campaigning as well as commanding.
  • The document asks South Carolinians whether they will destroy a Union their own state helped win, and it separates the state's people from the convention's leaders in a way the ordinance had tried to forbid.
  • That appeal had an audience beyond the state: wavering Unionists inside South Carolina, and legislatures in other southern states deciding whether to condemn the ordinance.
  • It worked alongside the military preparations rather than instead of them.
  • When students reach the settlement, they should be able to say which parts of Jackson's answer were words, which were ships and soldiers, and why he believed he needed both.

Slide 21 · Section G: Force, Compromise, and a Rehearsal (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Two bills in one week, and a state climbs down claiming victory."

  • Frame the settlement as a pairing, because neither half works alone in the record.
  • Henry Clay, who had built the protective system the South hated, now brokered its staged dismantling to save the Union he had spent his career knitting together.
  • Jackson, who had asked for force, accepted a compromise that gave South Carolina the falling duties it had demanded since 1828.
  • Each man supplied what the other could not: Clay gave the nullifiers a prize to carry home, and Jackson gave them a reason to take it quickly.
  • The convention that had threatened secession in November voted for repeal in March, and both capitals announced victory in the same week.

Slide 22 · The paired settlement of March 1833

On the slide: four bullets — the Force Bill arming the president; Clay's compromise lowering duties by stages to 1842; the convention repealing the ordinance; the Force Bill then nullified, to no effect.

  • Put the second settlement term into the notes in full, since the test pairs it with the compromise.
  • Say: "Force Bill: the law of March 2, 1833, in which Congress authorized President Jackson to use the army and navy to collect the tariff duties in South Carolina, passed in the same week as the compromise tariff that let the state retreat without surrendering."
  • Sequence is the teaching point: the Force Bill passed first in the Senate's order of business and the compromise beside it, so South Carolina repealed under a drawn weapon it could describe as unnecessary once the duties were falling.
  • Its final gesture — nullifying the Force Bill itself — cost nothing and conceded nothing verbally, which is why both sides could claim the winter.
  • Ask: Which claim survives the chronology better?

Slide 23 · Stat: 1842, the compromise reaches the levels of 1816

On the slide: the big figure "1842"; the label — "the year the compromise duties reach the levels of 1816"; the italic note — nine years of staged reductions, the prize South Carolina carried home.

  • Use the date to test the victory claims students met in section G.
  • The compromise did not repeal protection at a stroke — it stretched the reduction across nine years, in stages, which let northern manufacturers adjust and let the administration call the settlement gradual rather than surrendered.
  • For South Carolina the schedule was still a real concession won under threat, and nullifiers said so for years: no ordinary congressional minority had ever extracted such a schedule at such speed.
  • Unionists answered that a schedule any Congress could revise was a statute, not a right, and that the doctrine behind the demand had been denied by every other government in the country.
  • Both readings use this date, which is why it belongs on a slide of its own.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for the crisis and the settlement

On the slide: three terms with their short forms — Ordinance of Nullification, Webster-Hayne debate, Force Bill.

  • Run the second vocabulary slide backward through the lesson, asking students to attach each term to the section where it did its work.
  • The ordinance belongs to section E, the debate to section C, and the Force Bill to section G — a student who can make those attachments owns the lesson's skeleton.
  • The full definitions wait in the guided notes for the written work.
  • Close by asking which of the three terms names an argument, which names an act, and which names a threat — and let the class hear how the crisis moved through exactly those three forms in exactly that order.

Slide 25 · The lesson at a glance

On the slide: five bullets — a grievance becoming a doctrine; the doctrine debated, personalized, and enacted; the president denying it and preparing force; force and compromise producing repeal; the doctrine surviving its defeat, in print.

  • Synthesize before the exit work, in the lesson's own order.
  • The tariff gave a permanent minority a grievance it could count in prices.
  • Calhoun converted the grievance into a doctrine with a procedure and an ending in amendment.
  • Webster and Jackson answered the doctrine from its two flanks — authorship and consequence — and neither answer moved the state.
  • What moved it was the winter's combination: isolation, an armed president, and a compromise that paid the grievance while denying the doctrine.
  • Hold the last fragment longest: nullification lost in 1833 and was filed away, argued, printed, and remembered — and the filing is part of American history, not a footnote to it.

Slide 26 · Answering the question

On the slide: four bullets — the record's answer to the veto question; the ranking asked for; a defensible order; the ordinance and the proclamation as documents.

  • Model the ranking the writing task asks for, without confiscating it.
  • A defensible order runs isolation, force, compromise: no ally made resistance lonelier than any argument could, the Force Bill made it dangerous, and the compromise made retreat profitable.
  • Students may rank force first if they argue the convention feared war more than loneliness — but they must argue it from the documents, the ordinance's own secession clause and the proclamation's own threats, not from general impressions of Jackson's temper.
  • Remind the class that South Carolina's leaders left claiming victory, and that a good answer explains a repeal its authors could celebrate, not a surrender they admitted.

Slide 27 · Now your turn: answer the question and exit ticket

On the slide: the closing title; the two lines — exit ticket (the three questions at the end of the guided notes); writing task (rank the causes of the repeal, from three documents).

  • Close by sending students to the two pieces of written work and naming what each one checks.
  • The exit ticket checks the definition whole, the settlement's two measures, and the victory claim judged from both sides.
  • The writing task checks the ranking, and it should be introduced now, while the documents are fresh: claim first, three pieces of evidence, the strongest fact against the ranking faced directly, and an ending on what the settlement left unsettled.
  • Tell the class the standard plainly: a verdict on 1833 that never quotes the ordinance or the proclamation is an opinion about documents it has not used, and the rubric will grade it as one.
