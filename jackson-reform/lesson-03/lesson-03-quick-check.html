Quick Check: The Veto and the Answer

Directions: Read both passages, then answer the questions.

Passage A, Ordinance of Nullification, November 24, 1832

Source: South Carolina Convention, Ordinance of Nullification, 1832 (Paul Leicester Ford, ed., The Federalist, 1898; via the Yale Avalon Project; capitalization modernized). Audited TPSP bank record, south-carolina-nullification-1832. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

[...] We, therefore, the people of the State of South Carolina, in Convention assembled, do declare and ordain [...] that the several acts and parts of acts of the Congress of the United States, purporting to be laws for the imposing of duties and imposts on the importation of foreign commodities [...] are unauthorized by the Constitution of the United States, and violate the true meaning and intent thereof and are null, void, and no law, nor binding upon this State, its officers or citizens. [...]

Passage B, Jackson's Proclamation to the People of South Carolina, December 10, 1832

Source: Andrew Jackson, Proclamation Regarding Nullification, December 10, 1832 (Avalon Project, Yale Law School; from James D. Richardson, A Compilation of the Messages and Papers of the Presidents). Cuts are marked with ellipses.

I consider, then, the power to annul a law of the United States, assumed by one State, incompatible with the existence of the Union, contradicted expressly by the letter of the Constitution, unauthorized by its spirit, inconsistent with every principle on which It was founded, and destructive of the great object for which it was formed. [...] The Constitution of the United States, then, forms a government, not a league [...] It is a government in which all the people are represented, which operates directly on the people individually, not upon the States.

1. Work Passage A. State what the convention declares about the tariff acts, and state who it says those acts do not bind.

2. Passage A speaks in the name of the people of one state in convention. State why the convention form, rather than an act of the legislature alone, was central to the doctrine behind the ordinance.

3. Work Passage B. State Jackson's verdict on the power to annul in his own words, and state the ground he gives in the second passage about what the Constitution forms.

4. Set the two passages side by side. Each claims to speak for a people. Name the people in each passage, and state what each claim would mean for a federal law both peoples disliked.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Work Passage A. The convention declares the tariff acts unauthorized by the Constitution, in violation of its true meaning and intent, and null, void, and no law. It says they bind neither the state, its officers, nor its citizens. (Blueprint 5)

2. Why a convention. Under the compact doctrine, the sovereign people of a state, the party that ratified the Constitution, had to act in their sovereign capacity, and a convention spoke for them as the ratifying conventions had. A legislature was an agent of that sovereignty, not the sovereign itself. (Blueprint 5)

3. Work Passage B. Jackson holds the power to annul a law of the United States, assumed by one state, incompatible with the existence of the Union, contradicted expressly by the letter of the Constitution, unauthorized by its spirit, inconsistent with every principle on which it was founded, and destructive of the great object for which it was formed. His ground is that the Constitution forms a government, not a league, operating directly on the people individually rather than on the states. (Blueprint 6)

4. Set the passages side by side. Passage A speaks for the people of South Carolina in convention, claiming they may void a federal law inside their state. Passage B speaks for the people of the United States, whose government operates on individuals in every state. Under the first claim, a disliked federal law dies at the state line. Under the second, it stands until the nation's own courts or the amendment process remove it. (Blueprint 5 and Blueprint 6)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Question 4 is the lesson diagnostic. A student who can state the ordinance's claim but not Jackson's ground has the crisis without its constitutional argument, and should redo sections B and F of the guided notes before the writing task.
