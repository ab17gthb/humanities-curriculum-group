Teacher Answer Key

Part of the changeWhat changedHow work and daily life changedWho gained, and who paid
Households and marketsBetween about 1815 and 1848 the cost and time of moving goods and news fell so sharply that households stopped making most of what they used and began producing for distant markets and buying from them.A farm family's work now aimed at a buyer it would never see, and its table and clothing came from places it would never visit.Households gained cheaper and more various goods, and paid in dependence on markets they could not see or control.
Canals and steamSteamboats moved freight upstream, and the Erie Canal of 1825 cut Buffalo to New York freight from about one hundred dollars a ton to under ten, and from about twenty days to about eight.Western wheat and flour poured east and eastern manufactures west, and New York City became the nation's first port.Western farmers and eastern merchants gained a cheap road to each other; older routes and river towns the canal bypassed lost their trade.
Roads of ironRailroads grew from about twenty three miles of track in 1830 to more than nine thousand in 1850, beginning with the Baltimore and Ohio in 1827 and the DeWitt Clinton's run in 1831.Travel and freight ran in winter and across country canals could not climb, at speeds no horse or boat could match, and towns rose and fell by the lines.Towns and farms on the lines gained near markets year round; canal and turnpike interests paid as traffic left them.
The factory and LowellThe factory system gathered workers at the employer's machines for set hours under a bell, and at Lowell the Boston Associates added boardinghouses and churches, recruiting farm women under rules guarding their morals and hours.Work left the household's pace for the machine's, paid in cash wages high for women's work, and the women answered speedups and cuts with turn outs in 1834 and 1836 and a union of their own.The women gained wages, savings, and a printed voice, and paid in hours, pace, and company rule over their evenings; the looms ran on cotton picked by enslaved people.
News by wireMorse's telegraph line opened between Washington and Baltimore on May 24, 1844, with the message What hath God wrought, separating the speed of news from the speed of travel.Prices, orders, and newspaper news moved in hours instead of weeks, and railroad dispatchers soon ran trains by wire.Merchants, newspapers, and railroads gained same day knowledge; everyone gained a world in which distant events arrived before anyone had weighed them.

The change in one paragraph. Strong answers run the chart as one account. Americans lived and worked, before about 1815, mostly inside the household and the neighborhood, making what they used because moving anything was slow and dear. Cheap water, then iron roads, then the factory moved production out of the household into the employer's building, set its hours by a bell and its pace by a machine, paid young women cash wages their mothers had never commanded, and bound every household to distant markets, distant prices, and, after 1844, distant news arriving the day it happened. The paragraph should end on the lesson's open edge: the gain in goods, wages, and reach was real, and so were the bell, the speedup, the cotton picked by enslaved people, and the dependence.
