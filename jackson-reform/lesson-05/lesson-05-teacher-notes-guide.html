Factories, Canals, and Wires

Jackson and Reform · Lesson 5 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Jackson and Reform, Lesson 5 of 8 — after Lesson 4 (the Bank War). The first four lessons were political; this one goes underneath the politics to the economy those men were arguing inside, rebuilt between about 1815 and 1848 by canals, steamboats, railroads, factories, and one wire. It also deliberately re-reads Lesson 4's panic from below: the collapse that broke banks there breaks the young trade unions here. Lessons 6–8 then build reform movements on the ground this lesson lays — wage labor, corporations, and instant news.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 4 (the Panic of 1837 returning as a union-breaking event; canal borrowing as one road into the panic), Lesson 3 (the tariff's protected manufactures now visible as actual mills), and Lesson 6 (the reform movements organized by the people this economy made). The activity, The Market Revolution File, pairs the mill woman's own defense of the mills with Morse's first message — the lesson's two best documents for its two-sided verdict.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Household production as a lived pattern (most students need the baseline taught explicitly, which section A does); the tariff idea from Lesson 3; and the Panic of 1837 from Lesson 4, which this lesson assumes when it reaches the trade unions. No technical knowledge of canals, looms, or telegraphy is assumed — each machine is introduced by what it did to prices, time, or work.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.9 (the change explained as causes and consequences, not invention lists), D2.Eco.6 and D2.Eco.8 (transport costs and market integration; what the factory system changed about production), D2.His.13 (the mill woman's defense and the corporation's rules read against each other), D2.Geo.8 (canals and railroads reordering cities and regions); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.6, WHST.9-10.1 (the defended verdict).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to describe the household economy the change replaced; to state the Erie Canal's numbers and the railroad mileage as the transport revolution in figures; to define the factory system as a contrast with household production and explain interchangeable parts; to state the Lowell bargain whole — wages and voice against bell, speedup, and blacklist — including the looms' dependence on slave-grown cotton; and to explain what the telegraph separated, defending one judgment on whether ordinary Americans gained or lost control of their lives.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; households and water (slides 4–8) 9 minutes; iron (slides 9–11) 6 minutes; the factory and its parts (slides 12–14) 8 minutes; Lowell (slides 15–18) 9 minutes; wires (slides 19–21) 6 minutes; gain and cost, glance, vocabulary, synthesis, and exit (slides 22–28) 7 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes, and The Market Revolution File (five stations). The notebook page charts the change in five rows; the quick check is "The Mill Woman and the Wire"; the exit ticket has three parts — the factory system defined, the canal numbers stated, and the lesson question in two or three sentences.

Key vocabulary

market revolution · factory system · Lowell system · interchangeable parts · telegraph · Samuel F. B. Morse · trade union

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Title: the Lowell mills print opens the lesson

On the slide: the Lowell view at right; the unit and lesson line; the title "Factories, Canals, and Wires"; the standfirst — "The market revolution, and what the factory and the telegraph did to the way Americans lived and worked."

  • Picture: a period print of Lowell, Massachusetts, its mills in a row along the Merrimack River, published between 1840 and 1860 and held by the Library of Congress.
  • Open with the town in the picture, because Lowell is this lesson built in brick: a place that was farmland in 1820 and a factory city within the decade.
  • Set the frame before the detail: the last four lessons were political — the vote, removal, nullification, and the bank. This lesson goes underneath the politics to the economy those men were arguing inside.
  • Students will end by judging whether the rebuilding handed ordinary Americans more control over their lives, or less.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the rule; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "What did the factory and the telegraph do to the way Americans lived and worked?"
  • The question names two inventions, but it asks about lives, and students should hear that from the first minute.
  • The factory is a way of organizing people: work moved out of the household, hours set by someone else's bell, wages paid in cash.
  • The telegraph is a way of organizing knowledge: news and prices moving faster than any person could travel.
  • Between them stands the transport that made both pay — the canals and railroads that let a factory sell to a continent and a merchant trade on same-day prices.
  • The writing task will ask for one judgment, more control or less, defended from the documents — and the best evidence for both judgments is in this lesson, including a mill woman's own defense of the mills.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "A household economy becomes a market economy"; the seven section rows, A–G.

  • The map follows the guided notes section by section, so students can use the letters on screen to find their place in their notes all hour.
  • Section A sets the baseline, a household economy that made most of what it used; sections B and C move goods — water first, then iron.
  • Section D changes how things are made, gathering workers at an employer's machines under a bell, with interchangeable parts in the armories.
  • Section E fills the factory with people — the young women of Lowell, their rules, their magazine, and their turn outs.
  • Section F moves news off the road entirely; section G weighs the whole change.
  • Tell students the summary table at the end of their guided notes asks them to rebuild exactly this map.

Slide 4 · Section A: A Country of Households (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "In 1815 most American households made most of what they used, because moving goods was slow and dear."

  • Begin with the baseline, and make students feel it before breaking it.
  • A farm family in 1815 grew its food, made or bartered for its cloth, built and mended its own tools, and sold a small surplus in the nearest town.
  • That was not backwardness; it was arithmetic — where a wagon haul could double the price of a barrel of flour in a hundred miles, producing for a distant market made no sense, and buying from one made less.
  • The household was therefore a workshop, a school of trades, and a welfare system at once.
  • Everything after this section — canal, railroad, factory, and wire — is measured against it. The change, when it came, did not move the household; it widened the household's world past the horizon.

Slide 5 · Making most of what they used

On the slide: five bullets — food grown and cloth made or bartered; surplus traded close to home; transport slow and dearer than the goods; the pattern breaking, about 1815 to 1848; producing for distant markets.

  • Work the arithmetic of distance, because it explains the whole section: freight on bad roads cost so much per mile that low-value goods could not travel far and live.
  • Wheat was worth grinding into flour, and flour into whiskey, partly because each step made the product worth more per pound it cost to haul.
  • So the household economy was local by necessity, and its members' skills were general by necessity too — a farmer was also a carpenter, a cooper, and a mender of everything.
  • Then, between about 1815 and 1848, canals, steamboats, railroads, and the telegraph cut the cost and time of moving goods and news so sharply that the arithmetic inverted.
  • Put the term on the board in full.
  • Say: "market revolution: the transformation of the American economy between about 1815 and 1848, when canals, steamboats, railroads, and the telegraph cut the cost and time of moving goods and news, and households that had made most of what they used began producing for distant markets and buying from them."
  • The Ohio farmer of the reading — selling wheat to Rochester and wearing Massachusetts cloth woven from southern cotton — is not three stories; he is one household, rewired.

Slide 6 · Section B: Water First (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Steamboats ran against the current, and one canal cut the price of a ton of freight by nine tenths."

  • Water moved first because water was cheap, and the two machines of this section answer two different rivers.
  • The steamboat answered the western rivers, where flatboats had floated produce down to New Orleans and then been broken up for lumber because nothing could haul freight back up against the Mississippi current; steam could, from 1811 on the western waters, and the valley was tied two ways to its port.
  • The canal answered the East's geography — the Appalachian wall between the seaboard and the lakes.
  • The section asks students to learn the canal's numbers cold, because the numbers are the revolution in one line: what a ton of freight cost, and how long it took, before and after 1825.

Slide 7 · The Erie Canal, finished 1825

On the slide: five bullets — 363 miles, Albany to Buffalo; a ton of freight from about $100 to under $10; the trip from about twenty days to about eight; western wheat east and eastern goods west; New York City becoming the nation's first port.

  • Teach the canal as a price collapse with a ditch attached.
  • Before 1825, moving a ton of goods between Buffalo and New York City cost about one hundred dollars and took about twenty days by road and lake.
  • The canal brought the charge under ten dollars and the time to about eight days, and every farmer, miller, and merchant between the lakes and the Hudson redid his sums at once.
  • Western wheat and flour poured east; eastern manufactures, salt, and immigrants poured west; Rochester milled the wheat, Buffalo shipped it, and New York City, collecting the ocean end of the trade, passed Philadelphia and Boston to become the nation's first port.
  • Other states rushed to copy New York and mostly borrowed themselves into trouble doing it — a fact Lesson 4's panic makes relevant.
  • The canal did not just move goods; it decided which cities would matter.

Slide 8 · 363 miles: the Erie Canal

On the slide: the big figure "363"; the label — "miles of Erie Canal, from the Hudson River at Albany to Buffalo on Lake Erie, finished in 1825"; the italic note — built by the State of New York in eight years, and paid for by its tolls within the decade.

  • Give the giant number its setting, because 363 miles of hand-dug canal is easy to say and hard to picture.
  • Work began in 1817 — Governor DeWitt Clinton's project, derided as a ditch and a folly — and the canal opened whole in October 1825, with Clinton pouring a keg of Lake Erie water into New York harbor to marry the waters.
  • The engineering was American and improvised: locks lifting boats over the escarpment at Lockport, an aqueduct carrying the canal itself across the Genesee River at Rochester.
  • Tolls repaid the state's borrowing faster than its friends had promised.
  • Students should also register who dug it: contractors' gangs, many of them Irish immigrants, at wages and in conditions the celebration prints leave out — the canal is this lesson's first proof that the market revolution was built by labor as well as by invention.

Slide 9 · Section C: Roads of Iron (divider)

On the slide: the first-train print at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Railroads began as canal feeders, ran in winter, climbed new country, and within twenty years outran the canals entirely."

  • Picture: the first passenger train in New York State in 1831 — the locomotive DeWitt Clinton hauling cars that are stagecoach bodies set on rails, as reproduced in a 1921 railroad history.
  • Introduce the railroad as the canal's child and then its executioner: the first lines were built to feed canals and rivers with freight from the country behind them.
  • Within a few years promoters saw that a road of iron was not a feeder but a rival — it ran when canals froze, it climbed where canals could not be dug, and it moved passengers at speeds that made the packet boat feel like a ferry.
  • The section follows the first runs, which students read as a labeled teaching summary in the activity, and then the mileage, which is the section's real text.

Slide 10 · First runs, and a race with a horse

On the slide: the first-train print at right; five bullets — the B&O chartered in 1827; Tom Thumb hauling the directors in 1830; the DeWitt Clinton in 1831; stagecoach bodies on rails; runs made to prove steam to investors and riders.

  • Slow down on the demonstrations, because in 1830 a railroad was a claim, not a fact, and claims need audiences.
  • Peter Cooper built the little Tom Thumb to show the Baltimore and Ohio's directors that a steam engine could haul a car around the road's sharp curves; the famous race with a horse-drawn car, lost when a belt slipped, still proved the point his hosts cared about.
  • A year later the DeWitt Clinton made the first passenger run in New York State, Albany to Schenectady, with respectable citizens in coach-bodied cars being singed by sparks and thrilled by speed in about equal measure.
  • What was being sold in both runs was confidence: to legislators asked for charters, to investors asked for money, and to a public asked to ride behind a boiler.
  • The mileage in the next slide is what that confidence bought.

Slide 11 · 9,000 plus: railroad mileage by 1850

On the slide: the big figure "9,000+"; the label — "miles of railroad track in the United States by 1850, from about twenty three miles in 1830"; the italic note — more track than any other nation on earth, laid in a single generation.

  • Put the two numbers side by side and let the class sit with them: about twenty-three miles of track in all the United States in 1830, some twenty-eight hundred in 1840, more than nine thousand in 1850 — no other nation had laid as much.
  • The growth was not smooth or public-spirited by nature: companies fought for charters, towns bid against each other with subscriptions and bonds to be put on a line, and a town the railroad passed by could wither in a decade.
  • Gauges differed from road to road, so freight still changed cars at breaks, and the whole web ran on borrowed money, much of it British — which is one road by which a panic in London reached an Ohio farm.
  • For the lesson's question, note what the railroad did to time: it was the first land transport faster than a horse, and it made the timetable, and eventually standard time itself, a fact of ordinary life.

Slide 12 · Section D: The Factory System (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Work leaves the household's pace for the employer's building, the employer's machines, and the employer's bell."

  • Now change the subject from moving things to making them, and state the section's contrast exactly as the guided notes carry it.
  • The older pattern: artisans and farm households making goods in their own shops and homes, at their own pace, with their own tools, selling what they finished.
  • The new pattern: workers gathered in one building, working set hours at machines they do not own, owned by an employer who sells what they make and pays them wages for their time.
  • Put the term on the board in full.
  • Say: "factory system: the organization of production in which workers gather in one building to work set hours at machines owned by their employer, replacing the older pattern of artisans and households making goods at their own pace in their own shops and homes."
  • Cheap transport is what made it pay, because a factory that can sell to a continent can keep its looms running and its people hired in a way a village workshop never could.
  • The section builds the model at Waltham and Lowell, then turns to the armories, where the same logic was applied to the parts of a gun.

Slide 13 · One building, one bell, one owner

On the slide: five bullets — workers gathered, set hours; machines owned by the employer, driven by water; Waltham 1814, then Lowell; operatives tending looms they do not own; the product sold by the company, not the maker.

  • Walk the contrast point by point, because the unit test will ask for it as a contrast.
  • Place: the employer's building, not the worker's home or shop.
  • Tools: the company's power looms, driven by water through belts and shafts, not the weaver's own loom.
  • Time: set hours, announced by bells, the same for hundreds of people at once, not the task-based rhythm of a household.
  • Pay: wages for time, not the price of a finished piece the maker owned and sold.
  • Francis Cabot Lowell and his partners, the Boston Associates, proved the system at Waltham in 1814 with spinning and weaving under one roof, then built it at scale at the Pawtucket Falls of the Merrimack in the 1820s, in the town that took Lowell's name.
  • Everything in section E — the boardinghouses, the rules, the magazine, and the strikes — follows from this section's five points.

Slide 14 · Interchangeable parts: any part fits any unit

On the slide: five bullets — perfected in the federal armories; every part filed and machined to a pattern; a broken part replaced, not refitted; spread through New England machine shops; skill moving from the hand into the pattern.

  • Use a musket to teach this, because the federal armories at Springfield and Harpers Ferry were its school.
  • The old way: a gunsmith files each lock's parts until they fit that one gun, and a broken part in the field means a craftsman, time, and luck.
  • The new way: gauges, jigs, and milling machines cut every part to a standard pattern so closely that any part fits any unit of the model, and a soldier or a mill mechanic swaps the broken part for a sound one in minutes.
  • Put the term on the board in full.
  • Say: "interchangeable parts: the manufacturing method, perfected in the federal armories and spread through New England shops, of filing and machining every part of a product to a standard pattern so that any part fits any unit and a broken one can be replaced without a craftsman's hand fitting."
  • The economic meaning is the section's point: skill that lived in one man's hands now lives in the pattern and the machine, which means the work can be divided, supervised, and hired by the hour — the factory system's logic carried into metal.
  • New England's machine shops, many of them founded to build textile machinery, spread the method to clocks, tools, sewing machines, and everything after.

Slide 15 · Section E: The Lowell System and the Mill Women (divider)

On the slide: the Lowell print at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Factories, boardinghouses, and churches built together, and a workforce of young women recruited from New England farms."

  • The print is Lowell's mills along the Merrimack again, the title slide's image, and the section now fills those buildings with people.
  • Lowell's problem as its founders saw it was labor and reputation together: New England had no surplus of factory hands, and no respectable farm family would send a daughter to the kind of mill town the English industrial cities had made infamous.
  • The Boston Associates' answer was a system: build the boardinghouses and churches with the factories, recruit young women from the farms, house them under keepers and rules, and pay cash wages high enough to make the offer worth a family's while.
  • Put the term on the board in full.
  • Say: "Lowell system: the labor system of the mills at Lowell, Massachusetts, where the Boston Associates built factories, boardinghouses, and churches together and recruited young women from New England farms to work the looms under rules meant to guard their morals and their hours."
  • The section teaches the bargain whole: what the women got, what it cost them, what they wrote, and what they did when the companies tightened the terms in the 1830s.

Slide 16 · The bargain at Lowell

On the slide: five bullets — boardinghouses under keepers beside the mills; a ten o'clock curfew, church expected; cash wages high for work open to women; savings, money sent home, a brother schooled; more than 8,000 at Lowell by 1840.

  • State the bargain as both sides understood it in the 1820s and 1830s, because students judge it better whole.
  • The company offered cash wages that beat domestic service, sewing, and school teaching — the employments open to women — paid to the woman herself.
  • It offered housing in company boardinghouses under a keeper, meals, a curfew, and churches built with the mills — the whole apparatus certifying to a farm family in Vermont or New Hampshire that a daughter's good name was safe in a factory town.
  • The woman offered her labor at the loom for long days at the machine's pace, and her conduct, in the house as well as the mill, to the company's judgment.
  • Many women came for a few years, saved, helped their families, read and borrowed books, and left.
  • The system's defenders, including the women in the activity's mill woman's defense, made exactly this case, in print, and meant it.

Slide 17 · Their magazine, and their turn outs

On the slide: five bullets — the Lowell Offering from 1840; Farley answering Brownson in its pages; speedups, wage cuts, and board charges; turn outs in 1834 and 1836; a Factory Girls' Association of their own.

  • Give the women the section's last word in both of their registers — print and combination.
  • The Lowell Offering, a magazine written by the operatives themselves and edited by Harriet Farley among others, carried their essays and poems across the country and answered the reformer Orestes Brownson's 1840 charge that factory work disgraced them; students read that answer whole in the activity, from the audited record.
  • The other register is the turn out: when the corporations cut wages in 1834, about eight hundred women struck; when they raised the board charge in 1836, well over a thousand struck, and out of that defeat built the Lowell Factory Girls' Association, some two thousand five hundred strong.
  • Neither strike won — together they show the factory town teaching its workforce the skills of association the next lessons' reform movements run on.
  • Put the term toward the board.
  • Say: "trade union: an organization of workers in a trade formed to bargain over wages and hours and to strike when bargaining failed, which spread through the cities in the 1830s until the courts, the panic of 1837, and employer resistance broke most of them."

Slide 18 · 8,000 plus: Lowell's workforce by 1840

On the slide: the big figure "8,000+"; the label — "workers in the Lowell mills by 1840, most of them young women from New England farms"; the italic note — the largest industrial workforce in the country, gathered in a town that had been farmland twenty years before.

  • Anchor the section in the record's own figure, from the headnote of the Lowell mill girls record students read in the activity: by 1840 more than eight thousand workers tended Lowell's textile machinery, most of them young women from New England farms.
  • Consider what concentration means: eight thousand workers eating in company houses, worshiping in company-aided churches, reading the same magazine, and discovering the same grievances in the same week is a new kind of public, one no household economy could have produced.
  • It is also a workforce the corporations had assembled for their own purposes and could not disassemble when it began to act for its own.
  • Keep the number beside the turn outs of the last slide: strikes of eight hundred and of well over a thousand are what a concentrated workforce makes possible, and the companies' blacklist of 1834 is what they used to answer it.

Slide 19 · Section F: Wires (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "On May 24, 1844, a message crossed forty miles without a horse, a boat, or a train under it."

  • Slow the class down for four words, because this section's event is small enough to hold in one hand and large enough to end the old world of news.
  • Until 1844, information could move no faster than the body carrying it: the fastest dispatch was a relay of horses, a steamboat, or the new locomotive.
  • Every price, every election return, every declaration of war in this course so far traveled at the speed of travel.
  • The section follows Samuel F. B. Morse, a painter turned inventor, his electromagnetic telegraph, the federal money that built his first line, and the demonstration message students read in the activity from the audited record, tape and all.
  • Then it asks what work changed when news and travel parted company.

Slide 20 · What hath God wrought

On the slide: five bullets — Morse, painter turned inventor; a federal appropriation building the line; Washington to Baltimore, May 24, 1844; four words from the Bible returning by wire; the message proving the system, not the news.

  • Tell the demonstration as theatre with a purpose, because it was one.
  • Congress had voted thirty thousand dollars for an experimental line between Washington and Baltimore, strung beside the railroad, and Morse sent the first message from a room in the Capitol on May 24, 1844, during the Democratic convention's season.
  • The words — What hath God wrought, from the book of Numbers — were suggested by Annie Ellsworth, a family friend.
  • Alfred Vail in Baltimore received the dots and dashes on paper tape, the tape students see in the activity, and sent the message back.
  • Note what the four words do not do: they report nothing, order nothing, and price nothing — their whole content was the fact of their arrival, which was the information Congress had paid to test, and every newspaper reader in the country understood it at once.
  • Two of the unit terms belong to this slide's subject.
  • Say: "Samuel F. B. Morse: the painter turned inventor who developed the electromagnetic telegraph and the code of dots and dashes named for him, and whose first line, built with a federal appropriation, carried its first message on May 24, 1844."

Slide 21 · What the wire changed first

On the slide: five bullets — prices learned the day they moved; newspapers printing hours-old news; wire services gathering news once and selling it to all; railroad dispatchers running trains by wire; news and travel never moving together again.

  • Follow the wire into work, in the order the changes came.
  • Commerce first: a merchant or a cotton factor who knows today's price in New York or Liverpool acts on knowledge that used to be a week old and a guess, and markets that had moved at the speed of ships began moving at the speed of signals.
  • News second: papers organized cooperative wire services to share the cost of telegraphic dispatches — the Associated Press among them by 1846 — and an event hundreds of miles away entered the evening edition the day it happened.
  • Railroads third: dispatchers running single-track lines by telegraphic train order made the two networks partners, wires on poles beside the rails.
  • Underneath all three stands the section's definition, the one students must carry out in substance.
  • Say: "telegraph: the electromagnetic signaling system Samuel Morse demonstrated to Congress and opened between Washington and Baltimore in 1844, which separated the speed of news from the speed of travel for the first time in human history."

Slide 22 · Section G: The Gain and the Cost Together (divider)

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Cheaper goods, cash wages, and same day news, against the bell, the speedup, and the panic that broke the unions."

  • End the narrative where the reading ends, holding the gain and the cost in one frame, because holding them together is the lesson's main discipline and the writing task's whole demand.
  • The gain is not propaganda: goods really did grow cheaper and more various, a farm daughter really could earn and keep cash wages, and a merchant's or a reader's world really did widen past the horizon.
  • The cost is not rhetoric either: the working day was set by a bell and its pace by a speedup, households were bound to markets they could not see, the looms were fed by cotton picked by enslaved people, and the Panic of 1837 — which students met in Lesson 4 as a banking collapse — here reappears breaking the young trade unions.
  • The section also hands the course forward: an economy of wage labor, corporations, and instant news is the ground the reformers of Lesson 6 are about to organize on.

Slide 23 · What the change settled, and left open

On the slide: five bullets — production out of the household for good; time set by bell, clock, and timetable; wage labor and combination growing together; the market's reach exceeding anyone's control; who the remaking served, still argued.

  • Take the five results one by one and ask, of each, who decided it.
  • Production: once a factory could sell to a continent, household making for home use could not compete on price, and the change ran one way.
  • Time: bells, timetables, and telegraphic dispatches taught a whole people to live by the clock in place of the sun and the season.
  • Combination: the same gathering that disciplined workers taught them to act together, in turn outs, unions, and magazines.
  • Reach: by 1848 an Ohio farmer's income could be decided by a cotton price wired from New York and a credit decision made in London — powers no vote of his could touch.
  • And the open question, the one the reading leaves standing and the writing task takes up: whether ordinary Americans, inside this new machinery, held more control over their lives, or less.

Slide 24 · The market revolution at a glance

On the slide: the title "Movement, making, and news"; the five-row table — moving goods; roads of iron; making things; the mill women; news by wire.

  • This slide mirrors the summary table at the end of the guided notes, and students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • Read the rows as one account rather than five summaries: movement made the market national — a canal that cut freight by nine tenths, and railroads that multiplied track four hundred fold in twenty years.
  • Making followed movement into the factory, where the employer owned the machines and the bell owned the hours.
  • The mill women row is the account in persons: wages, a magazine, and two strikes.
  • The last row is the revolution turned inward on knowledge itself, news arriving faster than any traveler — which is why 1844, and not a battle or an election, is this lesson's closing date.

Slide 27 · Judging the change whole

On the slide: five bullets — goods cheaper, markets near; wages in cash and a printed voice; the bell, the speedup, and the blacklist; cotton picked by enslaved people feeding the looms; the writing task asking for one judgment, defended.

  • Close the teaching here, before the work slide, and model the judgment the writing task will ask for — each verdict has a record.
  • More control: Farley's wages and her liberty to withdraw, markets brought within a farm's reach, a magazine and a union built by the workers themselves.
  • Less control: the corporation's rules reaching into the evening, the leaders of 1834 dismissed and blacklisted, households exposed to prices and panics arriving from beyond any horizon they could see, and looms fed by slave-grown cotton tying Lowell's freedom to the South's bondage.
  • The strongest student writing picks one verdict, builds it from at least three of the activity's records, and answers the best record against it instead of pretending it is not there.
  • Note for the class that the women of Lowell are themselves the finest arguers on both sides of this question, sometimes in the same document.
