Guided Notes: The Market Revolution

Lesson question: What did the factory and the telegraph do to the way Americans lived and worked?

Unit question: What did democracy mean in Jackson's America, who was it for, and what did the era's reformers claim it still owed?

Key Terms

market revolution: the transformation of the American economy between about 1815 and 1848, when canals, steamboats, railroads, and the telegraph cut the cost and time of moving goods and news, and households that had made most of what they used began producing for distant markets and buying from them

factory system: the organization of production in which workers gather in one building to work set hours at machines owned by their employer, replacing the older pattern of artisans and households making goods at their own pace in their own shops and homes

Lowell system: the labor system of the mills at Lowell, Massachusetts, where the Boston Associates built factories, boardinghouses, and churches together and recruited young women from New England farms to work the looms under rules meant to guard their morals and their hours

interchangeable parts: the manufacturing method, perfected in the federal armories and spread through New England shops, of filing and machining every part of a product to a standard pattern so that any part fits any unit and a broken one can be replaced without a craftsman's hand fitting

telegraph: the electromagnetic signaling system Samuel Morse demonstrated to Congress and opened between Washington and Baltimore in 1844, which separated the speed of news from the speed of travel for the first time in human history

Samuel F. B. Morse: the painter turned inventor who developed the electromagnetic telegraph and the code of dots and dashes named for him, and whose first line, built with a federal appropriation, carried its first message on May 24, 1844

trade union: an organization of workers in a trade formed to bargain over wages and hours and to strike when bargaining failed, which spread through the cities in the 1830s until the courts, the panic of 1837, and employer resistance broke most of them

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher

Teach this lesson fifth in the unit, after the Bank War and the Panic of 1837 in Lesson 4 and before the Second Great Awakening and reform in Lesson 6; the panic the students met as a banking collapse returns here as the event that broke the young trade unions, and Lowell's mill women carry the unit toward the reformers and writers of Lessons 6 through 8. It pairs with the lesson reading, the slide deck, and the Market Revolution File activity, whose stations quote two audited bank records, the Lowell mill girls' defense of 1840 and Morse's first telegraph message of 1844. Students need the market revolution's ground from the Early Republic unit, canals and early factories, and Lesson 4's panic; no other prior knowledge is assumed. The lesson matches the Common Core literacy standards for history and social studies, reading and citing informational text and writing evidence based arguments, and the C3 Framework, Dimension 2, History and Economics indicators on change over time and on how economic systems shape work. Students will learn how cheap transport and the factory together moved production out of the household, how the Lowell system organized young women's labor, and how the telegraph separated news from travel. Timing: one class period of about fifty minutes for the reading and notes, with the activity in a second period. Materials: the reading, these notes, the activity, and the deck or its printed teacher notes.

Summary table. Moving goods: steamboats moved freight upstream on the western rivers, and the Erie Canal of 1825 cut Buffalo to New York freight from about one hundred dollars a ton to under ten and from about twenty days to about eight, binding western farms to eastern and ocean markets. Roads of iron: railroads, begun with the Baltimore and Ohio in 1827 and the DeWitt Clinton's run of 1831, ran in winter, climbed new country, and grew from about twenty three miles of track in 1830 to more than nine thousand in 1850. Making things: the factory system gathered workers in one building at the employer's machines for set hours under a bell, and interchangeable parts let any standard part fit any unit. The mill women: the Lowell system housed recruited farm women in company boardinghouses under rules guarding their morals and hours, paid cash wages high for women's work, published their writing in the Lowell Offering, and met turn outs in 1834 and 1836 when the bargain tightened. News by wire: Morse's line of May 24, 1844 sent its first message between Washington and Baltimore, and news, prices, and orders thereafter moved at the speed of the signal rather than the speed of travel.

The strongest answers run from the Lowell system, work moved into the employer's building under a bell, with cash wages and company rules, to the telegraph, which separated the speed of news from the speed of travel in 1844, and close on the lesson's point: Americans now worked and traded inside systems that reached far past anything they could see from home.
