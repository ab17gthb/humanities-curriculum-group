Quick Check: The Mill Woman and the Wire, 1840 to 1844

Directions: Read the two records and the background, then answer the questions.

Background. In December 1840 the Lowell Offering, a magazine written by the mill women of Lowell, Massachusetts, printed a defense of factory life, probably by Harriet Jane Farley, answering Orestes Brownson's charge that factory work disgraced the women who did it. The text is quoted verbatim from the audited TPSP record lowell-mill-girls-1840 (edition named in that record: the Gilder Lehrman Institute). Morse's first telegraph message, which follows it, is quoted whole from the audited TPSP record morse-first-telegraph-message-1844 (edition named in that record: the Library of Congress).

"We are under restraints, but they are voluntarily assumed; and we are at liberty to withdraw from them, whenever they become galling or irksome. Neither have I ever discovered that any restraints were imposed upon us but those which were necessary for the peace and comfort of the whole, and for the promotion of the design for which we are collected, namely, to get money, as much of it and as fast as we can; and it is because our toil is so unremitting, that the wages of factory girls are higher than those of females engaged in most other occupations. It is these wages which, in spite of toil, restraint, discomfort, and prejudice, have drawn so many worthy, virtuous, intelligent, and well-educated girls to Lowell, and other factories; and it is the wages which are in great degree to decide the characters of the factory girls as a class."

"What hath God wrought."

1. Define the factory system as the guided notes state it. Then state, from the first record, the two reasons the writer gives for accepting its restraints.

2. The writer says the women's toil is "so unremitting" and connects that fact to their wages. Explain the connection she is making, and name the system, from the Key Terms, whose rules she is defending.

3. State what the Erie Canal did to the cost and the time of a ton of freight between Buffalo and New York City, and give the railroad mileage for 1830 and 1850. These are the numbers the factory's markets ran on.

4. Define the telegraph as the guided notes state it, word for word in substance. Then state who sent the second record, on what date, between which two cities, and the four words it carries.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Define the factory system, and state her two reasons. The definition must match the guided notes: the organization of production in which workers gather in one building to work set hours at machines owned by their employer, replacing the older pattern of artisans and households making goods at their own pace in their own shops and homes. Her two reasons are consent and purpose: the restraints were voluntarily assumed, with liberty to withdraw whenever they became galling or irksome, and they were imposed only as necessary for the peace and comfort of the whole and for the design for which the women were collected, to get money, as much of it and as fast as they could. (Blueprint 9)

2. Explain the wage connection, and name the system. Her argument runs from effort to price: because factory toil never lets up, unremitting, employers must pay more for it than women can earn in most other occupations, so the hardness of the work is itself the ground of the high wage, and the wage is her evidence that the bargain is worth keeping. The system is the Lowell system, the labor system of the mills at Lowell, Massachusetts, where the Boston Associates built factories, boardinghouses, and churches together and recruited young women from New England farms to work the looms under rules meant to guard their morals and their hours. (Blueprint 9)

3. State the canal numbers and the mileage. The Erie Canal cut the cost of shipping a ton of freight between Buffalo and New York City from about one hundred dollars to under ten dollars, and the time from about twenty days to about eight days. Railroad track stood at about twenty three miles in 1830 and more than nine thousand miles in 1850. All four figures are required, because the item measures the transport numbers as one set. (Blueprint 9)

4. Define the telegraph, and source the message. The telegraph is the electromagnetic signaling system Samuel Morse demonstrated to Congress and opened between Washington and Baltimore in 1844, which separated the speed of news from the speed of travel for the first time in human history. Samuel F. B. Morse sent the message on May 24, 1844, from Washington to Baltimore, over the line built with a federal appropriation, and its four words are What hath God wrought, a phrase from the Bible suggested by Annie Ellsworth. (Blueprint 10)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Question 1 is the sourcing diagnostic: students who report the restraints without the consent claim have read the defense as a description. Questions 1 through 3 predict success on the blueprint 9 half of the unit test, and question 4 on the blueprint 10 half, because each requires the definition and the record in the student's own handling.
