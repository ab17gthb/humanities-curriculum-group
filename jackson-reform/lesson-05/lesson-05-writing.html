Writing Task: More Control, or Less

The prompt. Answer the question in a developed essay of about 300 words: What did the factory and the telegraph do to the way Americans lived and worked? Argue one judgment: taken together, the factory and the wire handed ordinary Americans more control over their own lives, or less. A strong answer states what life and work were like before the change, weighs what the factory and the wire each did, and answers the strongest evidence for the other judgment. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, drawn from both halves of the bank, and explain how each piece supports your judgment.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the Market Revolution File activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording. Records the activity labels as teaching summaries may be used for their facts and never quoted.

Evidence Bank

1. Before about 1815 most American households made most of what they used, because moving goods was slow and dear. Between 1815 and 1848 canals, steamboats, railroads, and the telegraph cut the cost and time of moving goods and news, and households began producing for distant markets and buying from them instead.

2. The Erie Canal, finished in 1825, cut the cost of shipping a ton of freight between Buffalo and New York City from about one hundred dollars to under ten, and the time from about twenty days to about eight. Railroad track grew from about twenty three miles in 1830 to more than nine thousand in 1850.

3. Under the factory system, workers gather in one building to work set hours at machines owned by their employer. At Lowell the Boston Associates built factories, boardinghouses, and churches together, set the day by bells, and governed the women's evenings by curfew and rule as a condition of the job. (Factory rules as stated in the activity's labeled teaching summary.)

4. The mill women were paid cash wages higher than those of most work open to women. Writing in the Lowell Offering in December 1840, a mill woman answered the charge that factory work disgraced them: the restraints, she wrote, were voluntarily assumed, the women were at liberty to withdraw, and the design of their gathering was to get money, as much of it and as fast as they could. (Audited TPSP record lowell-mill-girls-1840.)

5. When the corporations cut wages in 1834 and raised the board charge in 1836, the women turned out together, some eight hundred the first time and well over a thousand the second. The leaders of 1834 were dismissed and no other mill would hire them. Out of 1836 the women built the Lowell Factory Girls' Association, some two thousand five hundred members strong. (Turn outs as stated in the activity's labeled teaching summary.)

6. On May 24, 1844, Samuel F. B. Morse sent the first message over his telegraph line between Washington and Baltimore, built with a federal appropriation: What hath God wrought. The telegraph separated the speed of news from the speed of travel for the first time in human history. (Audited TPSP record morse-first-telegraph-message-1844.)

7. The new dependence cut both ways. The mills' looms ran on cotton picked by enslaved people in the South, and when the Panic of 1837 broke credit and closed mills, it broke most of the young trade unions with them, the organizations workers in a trade had formed to bargain over wages and hours.

Plan Before You Write

My judgment (one sentence that chooses more control or less and gives the reason in the same sentence):

My evidence picks (bank numbers, at least one from items 1 to 4 and at least one from items 5 to 7) and what each one proves:

The strongest evidence against my judgment, and how I will answer it:

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your judgment, present the case from the factory, present the case from the wire and the markets, answer the strongest evidence against your judgment in one or two sentences, and end by restating what the record shows.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment that chooses one of the two verdicts, more control or less, and defends the choice rather than narrating the inventions. (Blueprint 9 and Blueprint 10)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, with at least one from each half. Bank items 1 through 4 carry the ground and the factory: the household economy before 1815 and the turn to distant markets, the canal and railroad numbers, the factory system with Lowell's bells and boardinghouse rule, and the mill woman's defense of wages and consent in her own magazine. (Blueprint 9) Bank items 5 through 7 carry the answer and the wire: the turn outs, the blacklist, and the union built out of a lost strike, Morse's message of May 24, 1844 and what the wire separated, and the dependence on enslaved grown cotton and on a credit system whose panic broke the unions. (Blueprint 10)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the judgment. The strongest essays weigh the wage against the bell as two sides of one bargain, and the wire's speed against the household's new exposure to distant prices and orders, and say which side of each pair their verdict rests on.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest evidence on the other side, answered from the record rather than dismissed. An essay claiming more control must answer the curfew, the blacklist of 1834, or the panic's breaking of the unions. An essay claiming less control must answer Farley's wages and her stated liberty to withdraw, or the union the women built.

Model opening for teacher reference. The factory and the telegraph handed ordinary Americans less control over their own lives, and the loss came wearing the dress of gain. A full model continues in this pattern, carries the household baseline beside Lowell's bells and rules, carries the wire and the mileage beside the turn outs and the panic, answers the strongest opposing evidence, and closes on what the record shows.

Common weak answers. A verdict with no baseline (an account of inventions with no household economy before them) has not answered the lesson question. A verdict that quotes a teaching summary as if it were a period voice has misused the activity's labels, and a verdict that treats Farley's defense as the corporations speaking, rather than a worker arguing in her own magazine, has missed the record's sourcing.
