## View of Lowell, Massachusetts (lowell-view.jpg; deck crops lowell-port.jpg and title-169.jpg)
- Title: View of Lowell, Massachusetts at the confluence of the Merrimack and Concord rivers, with a row of textile mills or factories mainly along the Merrimack River
- Creator: Popular Graphic Arts (publisher credit as given on the Wikimedia Commons file page)
- Date: Between 1840 and 1860 (print, hand colored)
- Holder: Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division, LCCN 94515606, digital ID pga.07376
- Source: Downloaded during this build from the Library of Congress service image at cdn.loc.gov/service/pnp/pga/07300/07376v.jpg (1024 by 669 pixels), by way of the Wikimedia Commons file page for LCCN94515606, opened and verified visually as the period view of the Lowell mills along the river.
- Public domain basis: A print published in the United States between 1840 and 1860. United States works published before January 1, 1929 are in the public domain, and the Commons file page marks the work public domain.
- Use rule for this lesson: the print appears on the deck title slide, on the Section E divider, in the activity at Station 1, and on the notebook page, captioned with its date range and holder. It is a period view of the mills, not a view of any single year of the lesson, and captions do not present it as one.

## The first telegraph message tape, May 24, 1844 (telegraph-tape.jpg)
- Title: The First Telegraph (the paper tape of the first telegraphic message, with the message written out)
- Creator: Samuel Finley Breese Morse
- Date: May 24, 1844, 8:45 A.M. as the file page records
- Holder: Library of Congress, Samuel F. B. Morse Papers (file page source image rs6.loc.gov/mss/mcc/019/0001.jpg)
- Source: Downloaded during this build from Wikimedia Commons (The First Telegraph.jpg, 1280 pixel wide service image, 1280 by 63 pixels), opened and verified visually as the narrow paper tape with the message written out in words.
- Public domain basis: A manuscript record created in 1844 by a man who died in 1872. Copyright has long expired, the work is in the public domain in the United States, and the Commons file page marks it public domain.
- Use rule for this lesson: the tape appears at Station 2 of the activity, captioned as the paper tape of the message of May 24, 1844. Its great width and small height are the artifact's own proportions and are not altered.

## Texts quoted in this lesson, with their editions
- Lowell mill girls' defense, December 1840: quoted verbatim in the activity (Station 1), the quick check, and the writing task evidence bank's summary of it, from the audited TPSP record lowell-mill-girls-1840 in jackson-reform.json; the edition named in that record is Probably Harriet Jane Farley ("A Factory Girl"), 'Lowell Mill Girls and the Factory System,' December 1840 (Gilder Lehrman Institute). Cuts are marked with ellipses, as the record carries them.
- Lowell's Lowell Offering piece, stated choice: the unit spec allows the required Lowell Offering piece by a mill woman to be carried inside the bank record's coverage, with the choice stated here. That is the choice made. Station 1 of the activity IS the Lowell Offering piece: Farley's defense appeared in the Lowell Offering, the magazine written and edited by the mill women themselves, in December 1840, and the activity's source line and this record both say so. No second Offering piece is quoted, and none is paraphrased as a quotation.
- Morse's first telegraph message, May 24, 1844: quoted whole, all four words, in the activity (Station 2), the quick check, and the writing task, from the audited TPSP record morse-first-telegraph-message-1844 in jackson-reform.json; the edition named in that record is Samuel F. B. Morse, 'First Telegraphic Message,' May 24, 1844 (Library of Congress). No cuts were made.
- The railroad record (activity Station 3): a labeled teaching summary, labeled on its face, built from the public record (the Baltimore and Ohio charter of 1827, the Tom Thumb demonstration of 1830, the DeWitt Clinton's first New York passenger run of August 1831 as the Smithsonian's collection record for the DeWitt Clinton print states it, and the standard national track mileage figures of about 23 miles in 1830, about 2,800 in 1840, and more than 9,000 in 1850). Nothing in it is quoted.
- The corporation's factory rules at Lowell (activity Station 4): a labeled teaching summary, labeled on its face, built from the public record of the Lowell corporations' printed regulations and boardinghouse rules (bells ordering the day, the ten o'clock curfew, the ban on spirits, expected church attendance, and the keeper's reporting duty). No verbatim regulations text from a named transcription was verified during this build, so nothing is quoted.
- The turn outs of 1834 and 1836 (activity Station 5): a labeled teaching summary, labeled on its face, built from the public record (the wage cut and turn out of about 800 women in February 1834 and the dismissal of its leaders; the board charge increase and larger turn out of October 1836; the Lowell Factory Girls' Association and its membership of some 2,500). No verbatim turn out text from a named transcription was verified during this build, so nothing is quoted.

- No image of factory labor in motion from the period exists in the sources searched, and none is simulated. The mill women appear in this lesson through their print, their magazine, and the period view of their town.
