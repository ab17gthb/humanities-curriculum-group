The Market Revolution: Factories, Canals, and Wires

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. A Country of Households

In 1815 most American households made most of what they used. A farm family grew its food, spun and wove or bought cloth from a neighbor, and traded its surplus close to home, because moving goods was slow and dear. Within a generation that pattern broke. Between about 1815 and 1848 canals, steamboats, railroads, and the telegraph cut the cost and time of moving goods and news so sharply that households began producing for distant markets and buying from them instead. Historians call the change the market revolution. A farmer in Ohio could sell wheat to a miller in Rochester and buy cloth woven in Massachusetts from southern cotton. The household had not moved. Its world had widened past the horizon.

B. Water First: Steamboats and the Erie Canal

Water carried the first revolution. Steamboats, on the western rivers from 1811, moved freight upstream against the current, tying the Mississippi valley to New Orleans and then, by portage and lake, to the East. Canals did heavier work still. The Erie Canal, finished in 1825, ran 363 miles from the Hudson River at Albany to Buffalo on Lake Erie, and it cut the cost of shipping a ton of freight between Buffalo and New York City from about one hundred dollars to under ten, and the time from about twenty days to about eight. Western wheat and flour poured east, eastern manufactures poured west, and New York City, at the canal's ocean end, became the nation's first port.

C. Roads of Iron

Railroads began as canal feeders and became their rivals. The Baltimore and Ohio, chartered in 1827, was the first American railroad built to carry both freight and passengers. In August 1831 the little locomotive DeWitt Clinton hauled its first train between Albany and Schenectady, its passenger cars built like stagecoaches on rails. Iron roads did what canals could not. They ran in winter when canals froze, climbed country canals could not cross, and moved at speeds no horse or boat could match. The mileage tells the pace of the change. The United States had about twenty three miles of track in 1830, some twenty eight hundred in 1840, and more than nine thousand in 1850.

D. The Factory System

Cheap transport made a new way of making things pay. Under the factory system, workers gather in one building to work set hours at machines owned by their employer, replacing artisans and households who had made goods at their own pace in their own shops and homes. Francis Cabot Lowell and his partners built the model at Waltham in 1814 and then at the falls of the Merrimack, where the town named Lowell rose in the 1820s. Water turned the wheels, belts turned the power looms, and every operative tended machines she did not own through hours set by a bell. The armories taught the same lesson in metal. Machinists perfected interchangeable parts, every part filed and machined to a standard pattern, so any part fit any unit and a broken part could be replaced without a craftsman's hand fitting.

E. The Lowell System and the Mill Women

Lowell's mills ran largely on young women. The Boston Associates built factories, boardinghouses, and churches together, and recruited women from New England farms to work the looms under rules meant to guard their morals and their hours. The women lived in company boardinghouses under keepers, kept a ten o'clock curfew, and were expected at church. Their wages, paid in cash, ran higher than most work open to women, and many saved, sent money home, or paid a brother's schooling. They also read and wrote. The Lowell Offering, a magazine of their own writing, carried their essays and poems across the country. But the bargain tightened. Speedups, wage cuts, and board charges brought the women out in turn outs, as strikes were then called, in 1834 and 1836, and trade unions spread through the cities in the same years.

F. Wires: News at the Speed of the Signal

In 1844 news itself came loose from travel. Samuel F. B. Morse, a painter turned inventor, built an electromagnetic telegraph line between Washington and Baltimore with a federal appropriation, and on May 24, 1844 he sent its first message over the wire, the words What hath God wrought, chosen from the Bible. A message that had always moved at the speed of a horse, a boat, or a train now moved at the speed of the signal itself. Merchants learned prices in distant markets the day they changed. Newspapers printed news hours old from cities hundreds of miles away. Railroad dispatchers would soon run trains by wire. The telegraph had separated the speed of news from the speed of travel for the first time in human history.

G. The Gain and the Cost Together

Hold the gain and the cost together, because the people who lived the change held both. Goods grew cheaper and more various, distant places grew familiar, and a farm daughter could earn cash wages no earlier generation of women had commanded. Against that stand the bell that set the working day, the speedup that set its pace, a dependence on markets a household could not see or control, and mills whose looms were fed by cotton picked by enslaved people in the South. When the Panic of 1837 broke credit and closed mills, it broke most of the young trade unions with them. The factory and the wire had remade how Americans lived and worked. Who the remaking served remained an open argument.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Define the market revolution, with its dates. The market revolution is the transformation of the American economy between about 1815 and 1848, when canals, steamboats, railroads, and the telegraph cut the cost and time of moving goods and news. Before it, most households made most of what they used and traded their surplus close to home, because transport was slow and dear. After it, households produced for distant markets and bought from them. The reading's example carries the whole definition: an Ohio farmer selling wheat to a Rochester miller and buying Massachusetts cloth woven from southern cotton, without the household itself moving.

2. State the canal's effect, the railroad's advantages, and the mileage. The Erie Canal, finished in 1825 and running 363 miles from Albany to Buffalo, cut the cost of shipping a ton of freight between Buffalo and New York City from about one hundred dollars to under ten, and the time from about twenty days to about eight. Railroads ran in winter when canals froze, climbed country canals could not cross, and moved faster than any horse or boat. Track mileage rose from about twenty three miles in 1830 to some twenty eight hundred in 1840 and more than nine thousand in 1850. Credit also notes the DeWitt Clinton's first train between Albany and Schenectady in August 1831 and the Baltimore and Ohio's charter in 1827.

3. Explain the factory system by contrast, and define interchangeable parts. Under the factory system, workers gather in one building to work set hours at machines owned by their employer. It replaced artisans and households making goods at their own pace in their own shops and homes. The employer owned the machines, the waterpower and belts drove them, and a bell set the hours. Interchangeable parts are parts filed and machined to a standard pattern so that any part fits any unit. A broken part could then be replaced off the shelf, without a craftsman's hand fitting a new one to that single product, which is why the method, perfected in the federal armories, spread through New England shops.

4. Describe the Lowell bargain and what tightened it. The Boston Associates built factories, boardinghouses, and churches together at Lowell and recruited young women from New England farms. The women lived in company boardinghouses under keepers, kept a ten o'clock curfew, and were expected at church, under rules meant to guard their morals and their hours. Their cash wages ran higher than most work open to women, and women saved, sent money home, and paid for a brother's schooling, while the Lowell Offering carried their own writing across the country. The bargain tightened when the corporations raised the pace in speedups and cut wages and raised board charges, and the women answered with turn outs in 1834 and 1836, while trade unions spread through the cities.

5. Answer the lesson question using the telegraph and Section G. Strong answers hold both halves. The factory moved work out of the household into the employer's building, set its hours by a bell and its pace by a machine, paid cash wages that gave young women an independence no earlier generation had commanded, and fed its looms with cotton picked by enslaved people. The telegraph, opened between Washington and Baltimore on May 24, 1844 with Morse's message What hath God wrought, separated the speed of news from the speed of travel for the first time, so prices, news, and orders moved in hours instead of weeks. Together they bound households to distant markets they could not see or control, which is why the Panic of 1837, breaking credit and closing mills, broke most of the young trade unions as well. An answer that reports only cheaper goods, or only the bell and the speedup, has answered half the question.
