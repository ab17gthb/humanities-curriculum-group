## Edition discipline (binding on every document in this unit)
Period texts are public domain, but the edition still matters. Where the bank carries the text, quote the bank record and name the edition its sourceNote names. Where the bank does not carry it (Jackson's First Annual Message and Proclamation to South Carolina, the Indian Removal Act, the Treaty of New Echota, Webster's second reply to Hayne, Mann's Twelfth Report, Dix's Memorial, the Grimké sisters' writings, Douglass's Narrative), name the transcription or facsimile edition you actually used in the document's Source line and in the lesson's img/SOURCES.md: Richardson's Messages and Papers of the Presidents, the Avalon Project at Yale, the National Archives, the Library of Congress, Documenting the American South at the University of North Carolina, or a standard scholarly transcription. Spelling stays as the edition carries it; if the edition modernizes spelling, the Source line says so, exactly as the bank records do. Where no verbatim text from a named edition can be verified, write a teaching summary and label it a summary on its face, as the model units do. Fetch the text from the named source during the build; do not reproduce a famous quotation from memory and attach an edition name to it.

## Vocabulary (use verbatim everywhere: guided notes, decks, study materials)

### Lesson 1 (blueprint 1, 2)
- Andrew Jackson: the Tennessee general and seventh president, hero of New Orleans and victor in the elections of 1828 and 1832, whose presidency widened white men's democracy while it removed Native nations, broke the national bank, and strengthened the presidency itself
- Jacksonian democracy: the political movement of the 1820s and 1830s that celebrated the common white man, widened the vote, attacked privileged institutions such as the national bank, and held that the president, chosen by the majority, should govern energetically in the people's name
- corrupt bargain: the charge Jackson's supporters leveled at the election of 1824, that Henry Clay threw the House of Representatives vote to John Quincy Adams in return for appointment as secretary of state, a charge both men denied and Jacksonians rode to victory in 1828
- suffrage: the right to vote, which state after state extended in the 1820s until nearly every white man could vote regardless of property, while women, Native Americans, and most free Black men remained excluded
- spoils system: the practice of filling government offices with the winning side's supporters, named by Jackson's opponents for the claim that to the victor belong the spoils, and defended by Jacksonians as rotation that kept office from hardening into property
- rotation in office: Jackson's doctrine that public offices should pass regularly from citizen to citizen rather than become the possession of a permanent officeholding class, which he argued no man had any more right to hold than another
- Democratic Party: the party that formed around Andrew Jackson out of the election of 1828, joining southern planters, western farmers, and northern workingmen behind white manhood suffrage, strict limits on federal power, and suspicion of banks and chartered privilege

### Lesson 2 (blueprint 3, 4)
- Indian Removal Act: the law of May 1830 that authorized the president to offer Native nations land west of the Mississippi in exchange for their lands inside the states, the legal instrument under which the southeastern nations were driven out in the 1830s
- Cherokee Nation: the Native nation of the southern Appalachians that had adopted a written constitution, a syllabary and newspaper, farms, and missions by the 1820s, and whose government resisted Georgia's extension of state law over Cherokee country in the courts and in Congress
- Worcester v. Georgia: the Supreme Court decision of 1832 in which Chief Justice Marshall held that Georgia's laws had no force within Cherokee country, a judgment Georgia defied and President Jackson declined to enforce against the state
- Five Civilized Tribes: the name white Americans gave the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek, and Seminole nations of the Southeast for their written laws, schools, farms, and printing, and in some cases their holding of enslaved people, none of which protected their land from removal
- Treaty of New Echota: the treaty of December 1835 signed at New Echota by a minority of Cherokee acting without their government's authority, which the Senate ratified by a single vote and which supplied the legal cover under which the Cherokee were removed in 1838
- Trail of Tears: the forced removal of the Cherokee in 1838 and 1839, when some sixteen thousand people were rounded up, held in camps, and marched west in detachments through the winter, roughly a quarter of them dying on the road, and the name memory has extended to all the southeastern removals
- John Ross: the principal chief of the Cherokee Nation from 1828 until 1866, who led its resistance to removal through petitions, delegations, and litigation for a decade, and who was himself forced west with his people in 1838

### Lesson 3 (blueprint 5, 6)
- Tariff of Abominations: the Tariff of 1828, which raised duties on imported goods to protect northern manufacturers and which southerners denounced by that name because it raised the prices the planting states paid and invited foreign retaliation against their cotton
- nullification: the claim, drawn from the Kentucky Resolutions, that a state may declare a federal law void and of no force inside its borders, a doctrine later generations carried much further than its 1798 authors did in print
- John C. Calhoun: the South Carolina statesman who served as vice president under both Adams and Jackson, secretly wrote the Exposition and Protest, resigned the vice presidency in 1832 to defend nullification in the Senate, and became the leading theorist of the slaveholding South
- South Carolina Exposition and Protest: the pamphlet adopted by the South Carolina legislature in December 1828, written secretly by Vice President Calhoun, which argued that a state, as a party to the Constitution's compact, could nullify a federal law inside its borders until the Constitution itself was amended
- Ordinance of Nullification: the ordinance adopted by a South Carolina convention on November 24, 1832, declaring the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 void within the state, forbidding collection of their duties after February 1, 1833, and threatening secession if the federal government collected them by force
- Webster-Hayne debate: the Senate debate of January 1830 in which Robert Hayne of South Carolina defended nullification and Daniel Webster of Massachusetts answered that the Constitution was the people's government, not a compact of states, closing with a defense of liberty and union as one and inseparable
- Force Bill: the law of March 2, 1833, in which Congress authorized President Jackson to use the army and navy to collect the tariff duties in South Carolina, passed in the same week as the compromise tariff that let the state retreat without surrendering

### Lesson 4 (blueprint 7, 8)
- Bank War: the political struggle of 1832 to 1836 in which Jackson vetoed the Second Bank's recharter, removed the federal deposits to state banks, and destroyed the institution, which Clay and Biddle had forced to a vote four years early meaning to make it the election's deciding question
- Nicholas Biddle: the president of the Second Bank of the United States from 1823, a Philadelphia financier who ran the Bank as the regulator of the nation's currency and fought Jackson for its life with its loans, its contracts, and a deliberate contraction of credit
- pet banks: the state banks chosen by the Jackson administration to hold the federal deposits removed from the Second Bank in 1833, named in contempt by the Bank's defenders, whose easy lending on thin reserves fed the land speculation of the middle 1830s
- Specie Circular: the Treasury order of July 1836 requiring gold or silver, specie, in payment for public lands, issued to stop speculation bought with paper banknotes and blamed by critics for draining specie from eastern banks on the eve of panic
- Panic of 1837: the financial collapse of May 1837, when banks across the country suspended payment in specie, cotton prices and land values fell, and a depression followed that lasted into the 1840s and broke the presidency of Martin Van Buren
- Whig Party: the party that coalesced in 1834 from Jackson's opponents, National Republicans, Anti-Masons, and disaffected Democrats among them, named for the historic opponents of royal power and united at first chiefly by resistance to what they called executive usurpation
- independent treasury: President Van Buren's system, enacted in 1840, under which the federal government kept its money in its own vaults rather than in any bank, divorcing the Treasury from the banking system until the Civil War era rebuilt the connection

### Lesson 5 (blueprint 9, 10)
- market revolution: the transformation of the American economy between about 1815 and 1848, when canals, steamboats, railroads, and the telegraph cut the cost and time of moving goods and news, and households that had made most of what they used began producing for distant markets and buying from them
- factory system: the organization of production in which workers gather in one building to work set hours at machines owned by their employer, replacing the older pattern of artisans and households making goods at their own pace in their own shops and homes
- Lowell system: the labor system of the mills at Lowell, Massachusetts, where the Boston Associates built factories, boardinghouses, and churches together and recruited young women from New England farms to work the looms under rules meant to guard their morals and their hours
- interchangeable parts: the manufacturing method, perfected in the federal armories and spread through New England shops, of filing and machining every part of a product to a standard pattern so that any part fits any unit and a broken one can be replaced without a craftsman's hand fitting
- telegraph: the electromagnetic signaling system Samuel Morse demonstrated to Congress and opened between Washington and Baltimore in 1844, which separated the speed of news from the speed of travel for the first time in human history
- Samuel F. B. Morse: the painter turned inventor who developed the electromagnetic telegraph and the code of dots and dashes named for him, and whose first line, built with a federal appropriation, carried its first message on May 24, 1844
- trade union: an organization of workers in a trade formed to bargain over wages and hours and to strike when bargaining failed, which spread through the cities in the 1830s until the courts, the panic of 1837, and employer resistance broke most of them

### Lesson 6 (blueprint 11, 12)
- Second Great Awakening: the evangelical revival that swept the United States from the turn of the century into the 1840s, carried by camp meetings and itinerant preachers, which taught that every person could choose salvation and sent millions of converts toward the work of reforming society as well as themselves
- Charles Grandison Finney: the revival preacher whose Rochester meetings of 1830 and 1831 became the model of the new evangelism, who preached that conversion was an act of the sinner's own will and organized his converts into the temperance, antislavery, and moral reform societies of the age
- burned-over district: the region of western and central New York swept so often by revival and reform that preachers said it had no fuel left to burn, the seedbed in the 1830s and 1840s of abolitionism, women's rights, temperance crusades, and new religious movements
- temperance: the reform movement against the drinking of alcohol, organized nationally in the American Temperance Society of 1826, which moved from pledges of moderation in the 1820s to campaigns for total abstinence and legal prohibition in the 1840s
- Horace Mann: the Massachusetts education secretary whose annual reports from 1837 argued that tax supported common schools were the balance wheel of the social machinery, and who built the first American system of public schools with trained teachers, graded classes, and state supervision
- common schools: the tax supported public schools open to all children of a district, which reformers of Mann's generation spread across the northern states in the 1830s and 1840s as the republic's instrument for making citizens out of a diverse and mobile people
- Dorothea Dix: the Massachusetts teacher whose investigations of jails and almshouses in the 1840s exposed the insane confined in cages, closets, and cellars, and whose memorials to legislatures built the first generation of American public asylums

### Lesson 7 (blueprint 13, 14)
- abolition: the movement to end slavery in the United States, which in the 1830s turned from the gradual emancipation and colonization schemes of the founding generation to the demand that slavery, as a sin, end immediately and without compensation to the enslaver
- Walker's Appeal: the pamphlet David Walker, a free Black clothing dealer of Boston, published in 1829 and smuggled into the South, which called on enslaved people to resist their enslavement and warned white Americans that their professions of freedom stood condemned by their practice
- William Lloyd Garrison: the Boston editor who founded The Liberator in 1831, renounced gradualism and colonization in its first issue, and built the American Anti-Slavery Society into the organizational engine of immediatist abolition
- immediatism: the abolitionist doctrine of the 1830s that slavery, being a sin against God and man, must end at once, without gradual stages, compensation, or the removal of the freed people, the doctrine that split the antislavery movement from its colonizationist past
- Nat Turner: the enslaved preacher of Southampton County, Virginia, who in August 1831 led the deadliest slave rebellion in United States history, in which about sixty white people were killed, after which Virginia and the southern states tightened their slave codes and ended open debate on emancipation
- gag rule: the rule by which the House of Representatives from 1836 to 1844 tabled every antislavery petition without printing, reading, or debate, which former president John Quincy Adams, serving in the House, fought year after year as a violation of the right of petition until its repeal
- Frederick Douglass: the Maryland fugitive who escaped slavery in 1838, was discovered as an orator by the Massachusetts Anti-Slavery Society in 1841, and published his Narrative in 1845, a first person account whose literacy and argument answered the slaveholders' claim that the enslaved could not speak for themselves

### Lesson 8 (blueprint 15)
- Seneca Falls Convention: the convention held at Seneca Falls, New York, on July 19 and 20, 1848, organized by Lucretia Mott, Elizabeth Cady Stanton, and their associates, the first convention in the United States called to discuss the social, civil, and religious condition and rights of woman
- Declaration of Sentiments: the declaration adopted at Seneca Falls in 1848, modeled sentence by sentence on the Declaration of Independence, which held that all men and women are created equal and charged man with a history of repeated injuries and usurpations toward woman as its bill of grievances
- Elizabeth Cady Stanton: the Seneca Falls organizer who drafted the Declaration of Sentiments and insisted, against the advice of nearly every ally including her own husband, that its resolutions demand the elective franchise for women, the demand the convention adopted by a narrow margin
- Lucretia Mott: the Quaker minister and abolitionist of Philadelphia whose exclusion from the World's Anti-Slavery Convention of 1840, on account of her sex, first turned her and Stanton toward organizing for women's rights, and who presided over the generation that built the movement
- Grimké sisters: Sarah and Angelina Grimké of Charleston, who left the slaveholding South, became abolitionist lecturers to audiences of women and then of men and women together, and answered the clergy who ordered women silent with Angelina's Appeal of 1836 and Sarah's Letters on the Equality of the Sexes of 1837 and 1838
- coverture: the common law doctrine under which a married woman's legal existence was covered by her husband's, so that her property, her wages, and her standing in court passed under his control, the legal structure the Seneca Falls grievances were written against
- Married Women's Property Act: the New York law of April 1848, the first effective statute of its kind, which let a married woman hold real and personal property in her own name apart from her husband's debts, passed three months before Seneca Falls met in the same state
