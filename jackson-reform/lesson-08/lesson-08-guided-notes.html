Guided Notes: Women's Rights and Seneca Falls

Lesson question: Where did the women of 1848 get their argument, and what did they ask for first?

Unit question: What did democracy mean in Jackson's America, who was it for, and what did the era's reformers claim it still owed?

Key Terms

Seneca Falls Convention: the convention held at Seneca Falls, New York, on July 19 and 20, 1848, organized by Lucretia Mott, Elizabeth Cady Stanton, and their associates, the first convention in the United States called to discuss the social, civil, and religious condition and rights of woman

Declaration of Sentiments: the declaration adopted at Seneca Falls in 1848, modeled sentence by sentence on the Declaration of Independence, which held that all men and women are created equal and charged man with a history of repeated injuries and usurpations toward woman as its bill of grievances

Elizabeth Cady Stanton: the Seneca Falls organizer who drafted the Declaration of Sentiments and insisted, against the advice of nearly every ally including her own husband, that its resolutions demand the elective franchise for women, the demand the convention adopted by a narrow margin

Lucretia Mott: the Quaker minister and abolitionist of Philadelphia whose exclusion from the World's Anti-Slavery Convention of 1840, on account of her sex, first turned her and Stanton toward organizing for women's rights, and who presided over the generation that built the movement

Grimké sisters: Sarah and Angelina Grimké of Charleston, who left the slaveholding South, became abolitionist lecturers to audiences of women and then of men and women together, and answered the clergy who ordered women silent with Angelina's Appeal of 1836 and Sarah's Letters on the Equality of the Sexes of 1837 and 1838

coverture: the common law doctrine under which a married woman's legal existence was covered by her husband's, so that her property, her wages, and her standing in court passed under his control, the legal structure the Seneca Falls grievances were written against

Married Women's Property Act: the New York law of April 1848, the first effective statute of its kind, which let a married woman hold real and personal property in her own name apart from her husband's debts, passed three months before Seneca Falls met in the same state

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher

Teach this lesson last in the Jackson and Reform unit, after Lesson 7 on abolition, and before the review day and the unit test. It closes the unit at December 1848 and does not teach the Compromise of 1850, which opens the next unit. It pairs with the lesson activity, The Seneca Falls File, whose five stations print the Declaration of Sentiments, the convention report, Grimké's Letter I, the New York property act of 1848, and a summary of the London exclusion of 1840, and with the writing task, The Argument They Borrowed. Students need Lesson 6 on the Second Great Awakening and reform and Lesson 7 on immediatist abolition, including the Grimké sisters' lecturing and the clergy's answer to it. Coverture, the Declaration's grievance list, and the property act are new here. Students will learn how the women of 1848 built an argument out of scripture and the Declaration of Independence, what coverture took from a married woman, how a New York statute moved on property three months before the convention met, and what the convention asked for first, ending with the suffrage resolution that passed by a narrow margin. The lesson matches Common Core literacy standards for reading and writing about primary sources (RH.1, RH.2, RH.6, and RH.8 in the grades 9 to 10 and 11 to 12 bands) and the C3 Framework indicators for evaluating sources and communicating conclusions (D2.His.13 and D4.2). Timing is one class period of about fifty minutes for the reading and guided notes, with the activity in a second period. Materials are the printed reading, guided notes, and activity, and the slide deck with its printed teacher notes.

Summary table. Coverture, the condition: a married woman's legal existence was covered by her husband's, her property and wages under his control, and in the Declaration's phrase she was civilly dead in the eye of the law. It gave the women of 1848 the grievance their declaration was written against, stated in law rather than in custom. Antislavery, and London in 1840: women ran the movement's societies, petitions, and fairs through the 1830s, the Grimké sisters lectured to mixed audiences from 1837, and at London the women delegates were refused their seats and curtained in the gallery, where Mott and Stanton agreed to hold a convention on the rights of woman. It gave them the method, the speakers, and the occasion. Grimké in print, and the statute of April 1848: Sarah Grimké's Letters of 1837 and 1838 argued from scripture that the sexes were created equal, and on April 7, 1848 New York let a married woman hold property in her own name apart from her husband's debts. Together they gave the convention an argument already in print and a proof that the law could move. Seneca Falls, July 1848: five organizers called the convention, about three hundred people met on July 19 and 20, Stanton's Declaration rewrote the Declaration of Independence, the resolutions demanded immediate admission to all rights of citizens, the suffrage resolution passed by a narrow margin after Douglass spoke, and one hundred people signed. It gave the movement its text and its first demands.

Exit ticket 1. Coverture placed a married woman's property under her husband's control and made her wages his. The Declaration's phrase is that she was, in the eye of the law, civilly dead.

Exit ticket 2. London, in June 1840, at the World's Anti-Slavery Convention. The convention had just voted to refuse its women delegates their seats and had placed them behind a curtain in the gallery.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer naming both foundations and the narrow demand. The women of 1848 built on Grimké's scriptural argument that man and woman were created equal and on the Declaration of Independence, whose truths Stanton rewrote as the Declaration of Sentiments. The demand that passed by the narrowest margin was the elective franchise, the vote, carried only after Frederick Douglass spoke for it on the second day.
