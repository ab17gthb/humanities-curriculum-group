Writing Task: The Argument They Borrowed

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: where did the women of 1848 get their argument, and what did they ask for first? A strong answer names the foundations the argument stood on, weighs the evidence of April and July 1848 for what was asked first, and answers the strongest claim the other side can make from the same record. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your answer.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading, stations, and documents. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank wording, except where a bank item quotes a document.

Evidence Bank

1. Under coverture, in force in every state, a married woman's legal existence was covered by her husband's. Her property and her wages were his in law, and the Declaration of Sentiments called her, if married, civilly dead in the eye of the law. (Lesson reading, section A; Declaration of Sentiments, 1848, audited TPSP record.)

2. Sarah Grimké, Letters on the Equality of the Sexes, Letter I, July 11, 1837 (Boston: Isaac Knapp, 1838), argued from Genesis that Adam and Eve fell from innocence and happiness, but not from equality, and the Letters answered the clergy who had ordered women silent.

3. At the World's Anti-Slavery Convention in London in June 1840, the women delegates were refused their seats and curtained in the gallery. Lucretia Mott and Elizabeth Cady Stanton agreed there to hold a convention to discuss the rights of woman. (Lesson reading, section C; activity, Station 5, teaching summary.)

4. New York's Married Women's Property Act of April 7, 1848 (Laws of New York, chapter 200, section 1) let a married woman hold her property as her sole and separate property, as if she were a single female, apart from her husband's disposal and his debts, three months before Seneca Falls met in the same state.

5. The Declaration of Sentiments, adopted July 19 and 20, 1848, copied the Declaration of Independence sentence by sentence, held that all men and women are created equal, and insisted that women have immediate admission to all the rights and privileges which belong to them as citizens of these United States. (Report of the Woman's Rights Convention, 1848, audited TPSP record.)

6. The resolution demanding the elective franchise passed by a narrow margin, the only resolution not carried unanimously, after Frederick Douglass spoke for it on the second day. One hundred people, sixty eight women and thirty two men, signed the Declaration. (Lesson reading, section G.)

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • An answer argued from the record, not from the convention alone. The record has two foundations, a scriptural argument in print from 1837 and a founding text rewritten in 1848, built by a decade of antislavery organizing, and a full credit essay weighs both before answering. (Blueprint 15)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 carries the condition, coverture and civil death in law. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 2 carries the scriptural foundation, created equality argued from Genesis in 1837. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 3 carries the occasion, the London gallery of 1840 and the agreement made there. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 4 carries the sequence, a property statute in April 1848 preceding the convention by three months. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 5 carries the founding text and its central demand, immediate admission to all rights of citizens. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 6 carries the furthest ask, the franchise, and its narrow passage. (Blueprint 15)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the answer. The decisive link is borrowing with intent: students should argue that each foundation was chosen because it already held authority with the audience the women had to persuade, clergy and Bible readers in 1837, citizens of the republic in 1848.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest claim of the other side. An answer centered on 1776 must answer that Grimké reached created equality from scripture eleven years before Stanton reached it from the Declaration. An answer centered on the vote as the first ask must answer that the resolutions demanded immediate admission to all rights of citizens and that property had already been won in part in April, so the franchise was the furthest demand, not the only one.

Model opening for teacher reference. The women of 1848 got their argument from the two authorities their opponents could not dismiss. Grimké had shown from Genesis in 1837 that the sexes fell from innocence but not from equality, an argument built to answer clergy in their own book. Stanton copied the Declaration of Independence sentence by sentence in 1848, adding women to its truths, an argument built to answer citizens in their own founding text. What they asked for first followed the same order as their evidence: the grievances named the legal condition, and the statute of April 1848 shows the first of them, property, already moving before the convention met, while the resolutions demanded immediate admission to all rights of citizens and carried the franchise, the furthest ask, by the narrowest margin in the hall. A full model in this line follows the record from the curtained gallery of 1840 to the one hundred signatures of July 1848.

Common weak answers. The women invented the idea of equality at Seneca Falls (a result the record contradicts) ignores Grimké in print in 1837 and the founding text of 1776 the convention copied. The convention asked only for the vote (a single demand) skips the grievance list, the property statute that preceded it, and the resolutions' own language of immediate admission to all rights. An essay with no answer to the other side's strongest claim earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a weighing, not a verdict alone.
