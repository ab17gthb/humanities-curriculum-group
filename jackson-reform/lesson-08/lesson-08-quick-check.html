Quick Check: Civilly Dead

Directions: Read the two passages and the background, then answer the questions.

Document A. "He has made her, if married, in the eye of the law, civilly dead. He has taken from her all right in property, even to the wages she earns."

Source: Elizabeth Cady Stanton and the Seneca Falls Convention, Declaration of Sentiments, July 19, 1848 (National Park Service; punctuation normalized), as carried in the audited TPSP record.

Document B. "The real and personal property of any female who may hereafter marry, and which she shall own at the time of marriage, and the rents issues and profits thereof shall not be subject to the disposal of her husband, nor be liable for his debts, and shall continue her sole and separate property, as if she were a single female."

Source: Laws of the State of New York, chapter 200, section 1, passed April 7, 1848, in the transcription of the session laws consulted for this lesson.

Background. Under coverture, the common law doctrine in force in every state, a married woman's legal existence was covered by her husband's. Women had organized for a decade inside the antislavery movement, and in 1840 the World's Anti-Slavery Convention at London refused its women delegates their seats. Sarah Grimké's Letters of 1837 and 1838 argued from scripture that the sexes were created equal. At Seneca Falls on July 19 and 20, 1848, about three hundred people adopted the Declaration of Sentiments, and its suffrage resolution passed by a narrow margin after Frederick Douglass spoke.

1. In Document A, state what a married woman is in the eye of the law, and name the two things the passage says have been taken from her.

2. In Document B, state the comparison the statute uses to define the married woman's standing over her property, and name the two dangers her property is protected from.

3. Using the background, state the order of the two documents in time, naming each date, and explain what the earlier document's existence does to the grievance stated in the later one.

4. The convention asked for more than property. State the demand that passed by the narrowest margin at Seneca Falls, name who spoke for it on the second day, and connect both documents to the lesson question.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the standing, and the two things taken. A married woman is, in the eye of the law, civilly dead. The passage says he has taken from her all right in property, and even the wages she earns. Both items are required for full credit. (Blueprint 15)

2. State the comparison, and the two dangers. Her property continues her sole and separate property, as if she were a single female. It is protected from the disposal of her husband and from liability for his debts. (Blueprint 15)

3. State the order, and the effect. Document B is earlier, the New York act of April 7, 1848, and Document A is later, the Declaration adopted at Seneca Falls on July 19 and 20, 1848, in the same state. The statute had already restored a married woman's own property to her in law, so the Declaration's property grievance was partly answered before it was printed. The grievance still stood for wages, which Document A names and the act did not touch, and for every state but New York. (Blueprint 15)

4. State the narrow demand, its speaker, and the connection. The demand was the elective franchise, the vote. Frederick Douglass spoke for the resolution on the second day, and it passed by a narrow margin, the only resolution not carried unanimously. Together the documents answer the lesson question. The women of 1848 got their argument from the founding text Document A copies and from the legal record Document B belongs to, and what they asked for first ran from property, already moving in April, to immediate admission to all rights of citizens, with the franchise as the furthest reach of the ask. (Blueprint 15)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Questions 1 and 2 test whether students can read a grievance and a statute as answers to each other. Questions 3 and 4 test the second half of the blueprint line, the sequence of April and July 1848 and the suffrage resolution's narrow passage.
