Guided Notes: The Bank War and the Panic

Lesson question: Was the Bank a public good or a private monster, and who paid when it fell?

Unit question: What did democracy mean in Jackson's America, who was it for, and what did the era's reformers claim it still owed?

Key Terms

Bank War: the political struggle of 1832 to 1836 in which Jackson vetoed the Second Bank's recharter, removed the federal deposits to state banks, and destroyed the institution, which Clay and Biddle had forced to a vote four years early meaning to make it the election's deciding question

Nicholas Biddle: the president of the Second Bank of the United States from 1823, a Philadelphia financier who ran the Bank as the regulator of the nation's currency and fought Jackson for its life with its loans, its contracts, and a deliberate contraction of credit

pet banks: the state banks chosen by the Jackson administration to hold the federal deposits removed from the Second Bank in 1833, named in contempt by the Bank's defenders, whose easy lending on thin reserves fed the land speculation of the middle 1830s

Specie Circular: the Treasury order of July 1836 requiring gold or silver, specie, in payment for public lands, issued to stop speculation bought with paper banknotes and blamed by critics for draining specie from eastern banks on the eve of panic

Panic of 1837: the financial collapse of May 1837, when banks across the country suspended payment in specie, cotton prices and land values fell, and a depression followed that lasted into the 1840s and broke the presidency of Martin Van Buren

Whig Party: the party that coalesced in 1834 from Jackson's opponents, National Republicans, Anti-Masons, and disaffected Democrats among them, named for the historic opponents of royal power and united at first chiefly by resistance to what they called executive usurpation

independent treasury: President Van Buren's system, enacted in 1840, under which the federal government kept its money in its own vaults rather than in any bank, divorcing the Treasury from the banking system until the Civil War era rebuilt the connection

E. The contraction and its failure: through late 1833 and into 1834 the Bank contracted its loans sharply, producing a stringency meant to teach the public, and the president, that the currency could not live without its regulator. Petitions flooded Congress, but most voters blamed the Bank, so Biddle eased the contraction having demonstrated the private power the veto had charged. The boom on the other side: the pet banks, lending on thin reserves, fed a land boom bought with borrowed paper, while the Bank's federal charter expired in 1836.

F. The order and its date: the boom rested on state banknotes of doubtful backing. The Specie Circular of July 1836 ordered that after August 15 public lands be paid for in gold and silver only, with an exception for actual settlers expiring in December. The turn arriving with it: British lenders were calling in American credits and cotton prices were falling, so specie drained westward out of the eastern banks.

G. The panic and the payment: in May 1837 the New York banks suspended payment in specie and banks across the country followed. Cotton and land values collapsed, indebted merchants and farmers failed, workers lost work, and the depression lasted into the 1840s. Van Buren's answer: he refused to restore a national bank or return federal money to state banks, and his independent treasury, enacted in 1840, kept the government's money in its own vaults.

The Bank War at a Glance. The Bank and the trap, 1816 to 1832: the Second Bank chartered in 1816, Biddle regulating state banks by demanding specie for their notes, and Clay persuading Biddle to force the recharter vote in 1832. The veto and the removal, 1832 to 1834: the veto of July 10, 1832, the reelection, the deposits to the pet banks in 1833, the Senate censure, and the Whigs from 1834. The boom and the circular, 1834 to 1836: Biddle's contraction, pet bank lending feeding the land boom, the charter expiring in 1836, and the Specie Circular requiring gold and silver for land. The panic and the answer, 1837 to 1840: banks suspending payment in May 1837, values falling, depression into the 1840s, and the independent treasury of 1840.

Example: The Bank did public work, holding the government's money and keeping the currency sound, and it was also a private power, since Biddle contracted the nation's credit in 1833 to coerce a president.
