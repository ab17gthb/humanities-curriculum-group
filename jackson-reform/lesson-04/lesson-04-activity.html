The Bank File

Directions: The five stations below are the record of the Bank War, from the veto of July 1832 to the Treasury order of July 1836, with the farewell words of the president who fought it. Study each station and answer its questions. At every station, state what the Bank is shown doing, or being, in that record. In Part 2, judge whether the Bank was a public good or a private power, and who paid when it fell, and defend your judgment.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: The Bank Veto Message, July 10, 1832

Source: Andrew Jackson, Bank Veto Message, July 10, 1832, as carried in the audited TPSP bank record (Yale Avalon Project; from Richardson's Messages and Papers of the Presidents, 1897; punctuation modernized, as the record discloses). Cuts are marked with ellipses.

"[...] It is to be regretted that the rich and powerful too often bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes. Distinctions in society will always exist under every just government. Equality of talents, of education, or of wealth can not be produced by human institutions."

"[...] but when the laws undertake to add to these natural and just advantages artificial distinctions, to grant titles, gratuities, and exclusive privileges, to make the rich richer and the potent more powerful, the humble members of society [...] have a right to complain of the injustice of their Government."

"There are no necessary evils in government. Its evils exist only in its abuses. If it would confine itself to equal protection, and, as Heaven does its rains, shower its favors alike on the high and the low, the rich and the poor, it would be an unqualified blessing. [...]"

State the distinction Jackson draws between the inequality he accepts and the inequality he charges the Bank with creating, naming the three gifts the laws grant in his list. Then state his standard for a just government in the rains sentence. The veto is a president's argument in an election year, addressed to voters as much as to Congress. What in this passage is written to be judged by farmers, mechanics, and laborers rather than by lawyers, and how can you tell?

Station 2: King Andrew the First, 1833

Source: King Andrew the First, an anonymous print of 1833, as carried in the audited TPSP bank record (Library of Congress; image described in the record). The record's description is quoted below, and the print itself is printed with this station.

"A cartoon showing President Andrew Jackson dressed as a monarch in royal robes, holding a paper labeled "Veto" in one hand and a scepter in the other, standing on the torn Federal Constitution and the arms of Pennsylvania, with a book labeled "Judiciary of the United States" nearby. The image mocks his use of presidential power, especially the veto."

Read the print as an argument. Name four details the artist chose, the robes, the two objects in Jackson's hands, what lies under his feet, and the book, and state the single charge those details add up to. This print appeared in 1833, after the deposits were removed, not in 1832 after the veto alone. What had Jackson done between the veto and this print that let his opponents move the charge from one veto to kingship itself?

Station 3: Biddle's Answer, a Teaching Summary

Source: Teaching summary. This station is a summary, not a quotation. It is built from the record of the Bank's operations in 1833 and 1834 and from the congressional petitions the contraction produced, and it is labeled a summary on its face so that nothing in it is mistaken for a period text.

When the deposits were removed in the fall of 1833, Nicholas Biddle answered with the instrument his office gave him. The Bank cut back its loans, refused renewals it would ordinarily have granted, and pressed state banks to settle what they owed it, so that credit tightened across the country through the winter of 1833 and 1834. Merchants who could not renew their notes failed or cut their orders. Workingmen lost employment in the seaport cities. Petitions asking Congress for relief, some asking for the deposits to be restored and the Bank rechartered, arrived by the hundred. Biddle made no secret of the design in his correspondence with the Bank's friends: the distress, felt widely enough, would show the country what the Bank's management had been doing for it and force the government to retreat. The retreat never came. Through 1834 the petitions changed their target, and the public anger settled on the Bank that had chosen the stringency as a weapon. Biddle relaxed the contraction, the economy recovered, and the Bank's charter had two years left to run. What the episode left behind was a demonstration, conducted by the Bank's own president, that one officer of a private corporation could tighten or loosen the credit of the whole United States as policy required, which was the power the veto message had described in the abstract two years before.

List three things the Bank did to tighten credit in 1833 and 1834, and state the political result Biddle expected the distress to produce. Then state what the episode demonstrated about the Bank, in the summary's closing sentence, and why that demonstration damaged the Bank more than the distress damaged Jackson. This station is a summary and prints no quotation. Why does its label protect you as a reader, and what would change if its closing sentence were put in quotation marks?

Station 4: Jackson's Farewell Address, March 4, 1837

Source: Andrew Jackson, Farewell Address to the people of the United States, March 4, 1837, as carried in the Wikisource transcription of the address, fetched in full during this lesson's preparation. Spelling follows the transcription. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

"But experience has now proved the mischiefs and dangers of a paper currency, and it rests with you to determine whether the proper remedy shall be applied."

"In times of prosperity, when confidence is high, they are tempted by the prospect of gain or by the influence of those who hope to profit by it to extend their issues of paper beyond the bounds of discretion and the reasonable demands of business; and when these issues have been pushed on from day to day, until public confidence is at length shaken, then a reaction takes place, and they immediately withdraw the credits they have given, suddenly curtail their issues, and produce an unexpected and ruinous contraction of the circulating medium, which is felt by the whole community."

"The banks by this means save themselves, and the mischievous consequences of their imprudence or cupidity are visited upon the public."

State the cycle Jackson describes, step by step: what banks do when confidence is high, what happens when confidence is shaken, and who ends up holding the consequences, in his closing sentence's terms. Jackson delivered this address on his last day in office, two months before the banks suspended payment in May 1837. Does this passage read, after the fact, as a warning fulfilled or as a president excusing himself in advance? Answer from the passage's own wording, and name the detail that supports your reading.

Station 5: The Specie Circular, July 11, 1836

Source: Circular from Levi Woodbury, Secretary of the Treasury, to receivers of public money and the deposit banks, July 11, 1836, as transcribed by the American Presidency Project. Spelling follows the transcription. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

"In consequence of complaints which have been made of frauds, speculations, and monopolies in the purchase of the public lands, and the aid which is said to be given to effect these objects by excessive bank credits and dangerous, if not partial, facilities through bank drafts and bank deposits, [...] the President of the United States has given directions, and you are hereby instructed, after the 15th day of August next, to receive in payment of the public lands nothing except what is directed by the existing laws, viz, gold and silver, and in the proper cases Virginia land scrip: Provided, That till the 15th of December next the same indulgences heretofore extended as to the kind of money received may be continued for any quantity of land not exceeding 320 acres to each purchaser who is an actual settler or bona fide resident in the State where the sales are made."

State what complaints the circular opens with, what payment it requires for public lands after August 15, 1836, and the one exception it allows, with its acre limit, its class of purchasers, and its closing date. The circular speaks of frauds and monopolies as its reason. Using the reading, state the chain its critics drew from this order to the panic of the following May, and name which link of that chain this document itself can prove and which it cannot.

Part 2: The Closing Verdict

The five stations, with the reading, are the evidence for your judgment of what the Bank was, and of who paid for the war over it. Sort first, then judge.

The sorting. Enter each station number under what it shows, in one line each, with one quoted phrase or named fact from the station as the evidence: the Bank charged as privilege, the president charged as king, the Bank using its credit as a weapon, the banks' cycle described by their enemy, and the government's own order tightening the money. Station 3 is a labeled summary; cite its facts as facts of the summary's record, not as quotations.

Your verdict. Judge whether the Second Bank should be remembered chiefly as a public good or as a private power, and state who paid when it fell. Defend the judgment in a paragraph. Your verdict must use at least three stations' evidence, cited by number, and it must account for Station 3, where the Bank's own president used its credit as a political weapon, and for the panic of May 1837, which arrived after the Bank was already dead.

The strongest counterclaim. A classmate argues for the verdict you did not choose, and adds that killing the Bank caused the panic. State that claim at full strength from the documents, and then answer it: what does the record at the other stations show about where the boom's paper came from, and about what the Bank itself did when its life was threatened?

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: The Bank Veto Message

The two inequalities and the standard. Jackson accepts the inequality that comes from what he calls natural and just advantages, differences of talent, education, and wealth that no human institution can produce or erase, where every man is equally entitled to protection by law. He charges the Bank with the second kind: artificial distinctions added by law, titles, gratuities, and exclusive privileges, which make the rich richer and the potent more powerful while the humble members of society, the farmers, mechanics, and laborers named in the passage's cut portion, have a right to complain. His standard is equal protection, government showering its favors alike on the high and the low, the rich and the poor, as Heaven does its rains. The passage is written for the voters in its plainest choices: rich and powerful against humble members of society, privileges named as gifts, and the test of government stated as a weather image any reader can apply without a law book. Students should notice that the constitutional argument, which the message also makes, is not in these paragraphs. Jackson led the public version of the veto with equality before the law, and that ordering is itself evidence about the election he was fighting.

Station 2: King Andrew the First

The details and the single charge. The artist dresses Jackson in royal robes, puts a paper labeled Veto in one hand and a scepter in the other, stands him on the torn Federal Constitution and the arms of Pennsylvania, and lays a book labeled Judiciary of the United States at the scene. The details add up to one charge: the president governs as a king, above the Constitution, the courts, and the states, ruling by his own will. The print is dated 1833 rather than 1832 because of what happened between: Jackson ordered the federal deposits removed from the Bank and placed in state banks of his administration's choosing, while Congress was not in session, over the refusal of his own treasury secretary, whom he removed. A veto is a power the Constitution grants a president. Removing the government's money from the bank Congress had designated, on his own authority, is what let opponents move the charge from an unpopular veto to usurpation, and the Whig Party formed the next year took its name from the opponents of royal power for exactly this reason.

Station 3: Biddle's Answer, a Teaching Summary

The contraction, the expectation, and the demonstration. The Bank cut back its loans, refused renewals it would ordinarily have granted, and pressed state banks to settle what they owed it; any three such actions, as the summary states them, earn the point. Biddle expected the resulting distress, merchants failing, workingmen losing employment, petitions flooding Congress, to force the government to restore the deposits and recharter the Bank. The summary's closing demonstration is that one officer of a private corporation could tighten or loosen the credit of the whole United States as policy required. It damaged the Bank more than Jackson because it verified his charge from the Bank's own hand: the veto had described a private power over the currency in the abstract, and Biddle then used that power against the public's livelihoods in the open. The label protects the reader because it marks every sentence as a modern summary built from the record, so no student quotes Biddle or the Bank from a text that never existed. Put inside quotation marks, the closing sentence would falsely become a period document's own verdict, and the summary's honesty about its status is the only thing standing between a paraphrase and a fabricated quotation.

Station 4: Jackson's Farewell Address

The cycle and the two readings. The cycle runs: in prosperity, when confidence is high, banks extend their issues of paper beyond the bounds of discretion and the reasonable demands of business; confidence is at length shaken; the banks immediately withdraw the credits they have given and suddenly curtail their issues, producing an unexpected and ruinous contraction felt by the whole community; and the banks save themselves while the mischievous consequences of their imprudence or cupidity are visited upon the public. Both readings of the address can earn credit if they are argued from the wording. As a warning fulfilled, the cycle he describes is recognizably the cycle of 1836 and 1837, paper issued freely, confidence shaken, credit withdrawn, the public holding the consequences, and he had made the same argument since the veto. As an excuse prepared in advance, the passage assigns every step of the cycle to the banks' temptation, gain, and cupidity, and names no step taken by his own administration, though the Specie Circular at Station 5, his own Treasury's order, was already pulling specie westward as he wrote. The strongest answers notice that the address was published on March 4, 1837, and that the suspension of May 1837 made its readers choose between these two readings within ten weeks.

Station 5: The Specie Circular

The complaints, the requirement, and the chain. The circular opens with complaints of frauds, speculations, and monopolies in the purchase of the public lands, aided by excessive bank credits and dangerous facilities through bank drafts and deposits. After August 15, 1836, receivers are to take in payment for public lands nothing except gold and silver, and in the proper cases Virginia land scrip. The exception runs to December 15, 1836: the old indulgences as to the kind of money received may continue for up to 320 acres bought by an actual settler or bona fide resident of the state where the sales are made. The critics' chain runs: the order forced land buyers to produce specie, specie moved west out of the eastern banks that needed it as their reserve, those banks weakened just as British credit contracted and cotton fell, and in May 1837 they suspended. This document can prove the first link, its own requirement and its stated reasons. It cannot prove the movement of specie, the British contraction, or the suspension, and students who make the circular alone carry the whole panic have confused a government's order with the economy that received it.

Part 2: The Closing Verdict (Grading Guide)

Full credit should be awarded if the verdict:

  • Sorts every station with one quoted phrase or named fact for each, and treats Station 3 as the labeled summary it is.
  • Chooses one verdict, public good or private power, and defends it from at least three stations, cited by number, rather than from the Bank's reputation in general.
  • Accounts for Station 3: Biddle's contraction of 1833 and 1834 is the strongest single piece of evidence in the file, and it cuts in both directions at once, a regulator proving it could discipline the currency, and a private officer using that discipline against the public to save his institution.
  • Accounts for the payment: the Panic of 1837 arrived after the federal charter had expired, on a boom of pet bank paper, and its costs fell on indebted farmers and merchants and on workers who lost wages and work, none of them parties to the quarrel between Jackson and Biddle.
  • States the strongest rival claim at full strength before answering it. For a public good verdict: the Bank held the public money, supplied a uniform currency, and kept state banks honest by demanding specie for their notes, and no institution replaced that discipline before the panic came. For a private power verdict: its president was answerable to stockholders, not voters, used Bank loans and contracts to keep friends, and contracted the nation's credit as a weapon in 1833.
  • Answers the rival by naming what it cannot hold, most often the sequence: the boom that collapsed in 1837 was lent by the state banks the removal empowered, and the Bank's own conduct in 1833 supplied the proof of the power its defenders said it never abused.

Teacher note on the verdicts. The most common failure is a verdict argued from Station 1 alone, in which Jackson's charges are repeated as findings. Station 3 exists to test the charges against the Bank's behavior, and Station 5 exists to test Jackson's innocence against his own administration's order. The second failure is treating the panic as a verdict in itself. The panic proves the system after the Bank was fragile. It does not, by itself, prove the Bank was safe to keep, and a strong answer separates those two findings and says which stations support each.
