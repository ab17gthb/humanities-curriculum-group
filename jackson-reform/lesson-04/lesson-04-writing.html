Writing Task: Public Good or Private Power

The prompt. Write a developed essay of about 300 words answering the lesson question from this lesson's documents: was the Bank a public good or a private monster, and who paid when it fell? Judge whether the Second Bank should be remembered chiefly as a public good or as a private power, and defend the judgment against the strongest rival label in the record. Your essay must account for what the Bank's own president did in 1833 and 1834 and for the people who bore the Panic of 1837. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and The Bank File. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording. Keep the paired records whole wherever you use them: the veto's charges beside the contraction that tested them, and the Specie Circular beside the panic that followed it.

Evidence Bank

1. The Second Bank, chartered in 1816, held the federal deposits, moved the government's money, and issued notes that passed as a national currency. It regulated the state banks by presenting their notes for payment in gold and silver, forcing any bank that had lent too freely to pull in its loans, a discipline its defenders called the soundness of the currency.

2. Jackson's veto message of July 10, 1832 charged that laws granting titles, gratuities, and exclusive privileges made the rich richer and the potent more powerful, while farmers, mechanics, and laborers had a right to complain, and it set equal protection as the standard, government showering its favors alike on the high and the low, the rich and the poor.

3. The recharter came four years early because Henry Clay persuaded Nicholas Biddle to apply in the election year 1832, expecting Jackson either to sign against his convictions or to veto and lose the presidency. Jackson vetoed and was reelected by a wide margin, and the King Andrew the First print of 1833 answered by drawing him crowned and robed, standing on the torn Constitution.

4. In the fall of 1833 Jackson removed the federal deposits to selected state banks, the pet banks, over his treasury secretary's refusal. Biddle answered by contracting the Bank's loans through 1833 and 1834, tightening credit across the country to force the deposits back, and the public blamed the Bank for the distress rather than the president.

5. With the regulator dead in 1836, the pet banks lent on thin reserves and fed a land boom bought with borrowed paper. The Specie Circular of July 1836 required gold and silver for public lands after August 15, pulling specie west as British credit contracted and cotton prices fell.

6. In May 1837 banks across the country suspended payment in specie. Cotton and land values collapsed, indebted farmers and merchants failed, workers lost wages and work, and the depression lasted into the 1840s and broke Van Buren's presidency. His independent treasury, enacted in 1840, kept the government's money in its own vaults rather than in any bank.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment choosing one label, public good or private power, stated directly and held through the essay. Either label can earn full credit if the contraction of 1833 and 1834 and the payers of 1837 are carried and the rival label is answered. An essay that narrates the Bank War in order without judging what the Bank chiefly was has summarized the lesson rather than answered it. (Blueprint 7, Blueprint 8)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, drawn from at least two different parts of the record, the Bank's regulation and the veto, the early recharter and the cartoon, the removal and the contraction, or the boom, the circular, and the panic. An essay built on the veto alone cannot explain what the Bank did when its life was threatened. (Blueprint 8)
  • The Bank's regulator work stated wherever the verdict leans toward private power: it held the public money, supplied a uniform currency, and kept state banks honest by demanding specie for their notes, and no institution replaced that discipline before the panic. A private power essay that leaves the Bank no public function has read half the record. (Blueprint 7)
  • Biddle's contraction accounted for, not decorated with: through 1833 and 1834 the Bank cut its loans and pressed the state banks in order to produce distress that would force the deposits back, and the maneuver demonstrated the very power over national credit that the veto had charged. An essay that praises or condemns the Bank without its president's own use of its weapon has missed the file's strongest evidence. (Blueprint 7, Blueprint 8)
  • The strongest rival label stated at full strength and answered. Against a private power verdict: the discipline was real, the currency was uniform, and the panic followed the Bank's death rather than its life. Against a public good verdict: its president was answerable to stockholders, and he used the nation's credit against the nation's borrowers to save his charter. (Blueprint 7, Blueprint 8)
  • The payment assigned at the close: the Panic of May 1837, the collapse of cotton and land values, the failure of indebted farmers and merchants, the wages and work lost by people who held no Bank shares and signed no veto, and Van Buren's independent treasury of 1840 standing as the system's answer. (Blueprint 8)

Model opening for teacher reference. The Second Bank should be remembered chiefly as a private power that performed public work, and the two halves of that sentence are both in the documents. Its regulation was real: it held the government's money, issued a uniform currency, and forced loose state banks to pull in their loans by demanding specie for their notes. But when Jackson removed the deposits in 1833, Nicholas Biddle contracted the credit of the whole country to force a president to retreat, and a power that can be used that way, by an officer no voter chose, is the power the veto message had described. When the Bank fell, the bill was paid by the borrowers of the pet bank boom and the workers of the Panic of 1837, who had been parties to neither man's quarrel.

Common weak answers. A public good essay built on bank 1 and bank 6 alone cannot explain why the Bank's own president tightened credit on the whole country in 1833, or why a cartoonist could crown the president in the same season and find an audience for both charges at once.
