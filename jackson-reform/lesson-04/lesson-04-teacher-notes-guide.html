The Bank War and the Panic

Jackson and Reform · Lesson 4 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Jackson and Reform, Lesson 4 of 8 — after Lesson 3 (nullification). The two lessons are mirror arguments and should be taught in this order: in Lesson 3 a state claimed the power to annul a federal law; here the president claims a veto the Court's precedent undercut, then removes deposits on his own authority. Students are meant to notice the shape repeating — who may say no to whom — before the unit names it. The lesson question is double: was the Bank a public good or a private monster, and who paid when it fell?

What it pairs with

Pairs with: Lesson 3 (the nullification doctrine — Jackson's own constitutional claim in the veto message, judged by the standard he applied to Calhoun), Lesson 2 (enforcement refused for the Cherokee, force prepared here against a bank), and Lesson 8 (democracy's unfinished business — the independent treasury as the era's institutional residue). The activity, The Bank File, is the lesson's evidence base: the veto message, the King Andrew print, the contraction summary, the farewell address, and the Specie Circular.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: What a banknote is (a promise to pay specie on demand) and what a charter is; the First Bank's precedent at the level of Lesson 3's constitutional argument (McCulloch is referenced in the veto discussion, not retaught); and the nullification lesson's question of who judges the Constitution, which the veto message raises again from the president's side. No prior economics beyond the tariff arithmetic of Lesson 3 is assumed — the Bank's regulatory mechanism is taught on slide 5 as one plain device.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.9 (causation across the fight — the boom and panic traced to decisions, not moods), D2.His.13 (the veto, cartoon, and circular read as arguments), D2.Eco.8 and D2.Eco.10 (a regulator's role and the costs of its removal), D2.Civ.10 (executive power — veto versus removal); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.8, WHST.9-10.1 (the defended verdict).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain what the Second Bank did and how its specie discipline regulated state banks; to state the veto's charges and Clay and Biddle's 1832 calculation that provoked them; to trace the removal, the contraction, the pet bank boom, and the Specie Circular as one causal chain into the Panic of 1837; and to render the lesson's verdict — public good or private monster — while naming who paid when the Bank fell.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; the Bank and the trap (slides 4–9) 11 minutes; the veto (slides 10–11) 5 minutes; removal, contraction, and boom (slides 12–15) 10 minutes; the circular (slides 16–18) 6 minutes; the panic and the answer (slides 19–21) 6 minutes; glance, vocabulary, synthesis, and exit (slides 22–26) 7 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the guided notes, and The Bank File (the activity's five documents). The notebook page charts the struggle stage by stage; the quick check is "The Veto and the Circular"; the exit ticket has three parts — the Bank War and the pet banks defined, the circular in two sentences, and the lesson question in two or three.

Key vocabulary

Bank War · Nicholas Biddle · pet banks · Specie Circular · Panic of 1837 · Whig Party · independent treasury

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Title: The Bank War and the Panic

On the slide: title image, a five dollar note of the Bank of the United States dated 1829 (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line; the title; the standfirst — "A veto, a removal, a boom, and the bill, 1832 to 1840."

  • The title image is the paper currency at the center of this lesson, held by the Baker Library at Harvard Business School and reproduced from its public domain scan.
  • Open by holding the two halves of the lesson together: the first half is a political fight between named men — Andrew Jackson, Nicholas Biddle, and Henry Clay — over one chartered corporation.
  • The second half is what that fight did to people who appear in none of its portraits: the borrowers of the pet bank boom and the workers of the Panic of 1837.
  • The lesson question asks whether the Bank was a public good or a private monster, and who paid when it fell — students should hear both halves in the first minute.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type; the rule; the unit question in italic.

  • Ask: "Was the Bank a public good or a private monster, and who paid when it fell?"
  • The question is built in two parts, and the grading follows the build.
  • "Public good or private monster" asks students to judge the Second Bank from its conduct, not its reputation: the same institution that kept the currency sound also contracted the nation's credit as a political weapon, and both facts are in the documents.
  • "Who paid when it fell" turns the judgment toward consequences: the charter expired in 1836, the panic arrived in May 1837, and its costs landed on indebted farmers, failed merchants, and wage workers who had never held a Bank share.
  • The unit question stands behind both parts, because Jackson made the Bank War democracy's own argument — the people's president against a privileged corporation — and the lesson tests what that argument included and what it cost.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "A regulator destroyed, a boom unleashed"; the seven section rows, A–G.

  • Section A sets out what the Second Bank did: the federal deposits, a national currency, and regulation of the state banks by demanding specie for their notes.
  • Section B shows Clay and Biddle forcing the recharter question four years early in 1832; section C reads the veto of July 10, 1832.
  • Section D follows the removal of the deposits to the pet banks in 1833, the Senate censure, the King Andrew cartoon, and the Whig Party forming in 1834.
  • Section E covers Biddle's contraction of 1833 and 1834 and the land boom that followed the Bank's fall.
  • Section F reads the Specie Circular of July 1836; section G closes with the Panic of 1837, its payers, and Van Buren's independent treasury of 1840.

Slide 4 · Section A divider: The Bank and What It Did

On the slide: the Jackson portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "Chartered in 1816, directed from Philadelphia, the Bank stood over every borrower in the republic."

  • Picture: Andrew Jackson by Ralph Eleaser Whiteside Earl, painted in 1837, the year Jackson left office — the president as his own side wished him seen: plain, erect, and civilian.
  • Teach the Bank before the fight, because students who meet the institution only through Jackson's charges cannot judge the charges.
  • The Second Bank was chartered in 1816 for twenty years, held the government's deposits, issued notes that passed everywhere as a national currency, and kept the hundreds of state banks in check by one plain device: it presented their notes for payment in gold and silver, so a bank that lent too freely had to pull in its loans.
  • That is the whole machine — everything in this lesson is an argument about who should hold it, and whether anyone should.

Slide 5 · What the Bank did, 1816 to 1832

On the slide: five bullets — the federal deposits held; notes passing as a national currency; state bank notes presented for specie; loose banks forced to pull in loans; answerable to stockholders, not voters.

  • Read the five fragments as one institution described twice.
  • The first four are the public work: a single depository for the government's money, a currency a traveler could spend in any state, and a regulator whose discipline no legislature had to vote on because the specie demand did the work automatically.
  • Defenders called that the soundness of the currency, and they were describing something real.
  • The fifth fragment is the democratic objection, and it is equally factual: the officers who ran the machine were chosen by its stockholders, its president served from 1823 without facing an electorate, and its loans and printing contracts could reward friends.
  • Both descriptions were true in 1832 — the Bank War happened because the country had to choose which description would govern, and section B shows who chose the moment of decision.

Slide 6 · Nicholas Biddle, the regulator

On the slide: the Biddle portrait at right; four bullets — president from 1823; a Philadelphia financier of the first rank; manager of the national currency; fought with loans and contracts.

  • Picture: Nicholas Biddle by Charles Willson Peale, painted in the sitter's lifetime.
  • Note the contrast the two portraits set up: Jackson painted plain in 1837, Biddle painted as a gentleman of finance.
  • Biddle was no cartoon villain, and the lesson fails if he becomes one — a former diplomat and editor, chosen to head the Bank at thirty-seven, and by most accounts a genuinely able central banker before central banking had a name.
  • Under him the Bank's notes were as good as specie and its regulation was steady.
  • His flaw was constitutional rather than financial: he believed the Bank's usefulness placed it beyond the reach of electoral politics, and he was willing to use its economic power — its loans, its contracts, and in 1833 its credit itself — as political weapons.
  • Jackson's veto made exactly that charge; section E tests it.

Slide 7 · Stat: twenty years in the charter

On the slide: the big figure "20"; the label — "years in the charter Congress granted in 1816"; the italic note — the charter ran to 1836, and no decision was required in 1832.

  • State the arithmetic plainly, because the whole crisis turns on it.
  • Congress chartered the Second Bank in 1816 for twenty years, so the charter expired in 1836 and the institution could have been debated then, in an ordinary year, by an ordinary Congress — nothing required a decision in 1832.
  • The decision came in 1832 because two men wanted it then: Henry Clay, who needed an issue for his presidential campaign, and Nicholas Biddle, who believed an election year protected the Bank.
  • When students ask why the fight happened when it did, the answer is never that the charter ran out — the Bank's friends chose the ground, chose it four years early, and lost on it.
  • Keep that sequence in front of the class whenever the veto is described as Jackson picking a fight: on timing, the record runs the other way.

Slide 8 · Section B divider: The Question Forced Early

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "Clay needs an issue, Biddle needs a charter, and both men agree on 1832, four years before any answer is due."

  • Introduce the trap as a piece of political engineering, because its design explains everything that follows.
  • In 1831 and 1832 Henry Clay was assembling his run for the presidency against Jackson, and the Bank was his strongest ground: popular with merchants, admired by editors, and hated by the president.
  • Clay persuaded Biddle to apply for recharter at once rather than wait for 1836.
  • Their calculation had two horns, and either was supposed to gore Jackson: if he signed, he surrendered his convictions and split his own coalition; if he vetoed, Clay would carry the veto into the election as proof that one man's prejudice had destroyed the nation's finances.
  • Biddle accepted because election years had always protected the Bank — what neither man priced in was Jackson campaigning on the veto itself, which is section C.

Slide 9 · Clay's calculation, 1832

On the slide: the Clay portrait at right; five bullets — four years left on the charter; Biddle persuaded to apply at once; sign and surrender, or veto and be campaigned against; Congress passing the recharter in summer 1832.

  • Picture: an engraved portrait of Henry Clay, the Bank's champion in Congress and Jackson's opponent in the election of 1832.
  • Walk the class through the trap's logic slowly, because students tend to remember the veto as the opening move — it was the answer to one.
  • The bill passed Congress in the summer of 1832 and arrived on the president's desk with the campaign already running.
  • Biddle supported the timing with the Bank's resources, including loans that kept friendly newspapers friendly — a fact Jackson's message would not let the country forget.
  • Notice what the design assumed: that the Bank was too useful to attack and its president too respectable to caricature. Both assumptions were reasonable in the politics of 1828; the election of 1832 was a different politics — mass electorate politics — and the next section shows Jackson fighting on the new ground.

Slide 10 · Section C divider: The Veto

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "July 10, 1832. Jackson answers the trap with a message written for the voters, and the election becomes the referendum his enemies designed, with the sides reversed."

  • Frame the veto as a document before it is an act, because students will read it in The Bank File and its wording does the lesson's analytical work.
  • A veto is ordinarily a short constitutional objection sent back to Congress; Jackson's message of July 10, 1832 was a campaign address in veto form, published in every newspaper in the country, and its audience was the electorate Clay and Biddle had tried to frighten.
  • It made three arguments: that the Bank was unconstitutional despite the Supreme Court's ruling a generation earlier; that its stock and privileges made it a monopoly; and that government granting exclusive privileges made the rich richer at the expense of farmers, mechanics, and laborers.
  • The third argument is the one the country remembered, and it is the one this lesson's question is built from.

Slide 11 · What the veto charged

On the slide: five bullets — unconstitutional, the Court notwithstanding; foreign stockholders; exclusive privileges enriching the rich; farmers, mechanics, and laborers complaining; equal protection, favors like the rains on all alike.

  • Read the charges in the message's own order of strength.
  • The constitutional charge was the weakest legally, since the Supreme Court had upheld the first Bank's charter in 1819 — and Jackson's answer, that each branch must judge the Constitution for itself, was a doctrine students met in the nullification lesson wearing a different uniform.
  • The monopoly charge was the argument with a future: when laws grant titles, gratuities, and exclusive privileges, Jackson wrote, the humble members of society have a right to complain of the injustice of their government.
  • Hold the rains sentence for last and read it aloud: government confining itself to equal protection, showering its favors alike on the high and the low, the rich and the poor, as Heaven does its rains, would be an unqualified blessing.
  • That sentence, not the banking details, is why the veto survived its decade.

Slide 12 · Section D divider: The Deposits Removed

On the slide: the King Andrew print at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "The veto was a power the Constitution grants. The removal was Jackson's own, and his opponents drew him crowned for it."

  • Picture: King Andrew the First, the anonymous print of 1833 that students read as an argument in The Bank File, printed by the Library of Congress from its own impression.
  • Use it to mark the lesson's hinge: the veto of 1832 was fierce but constitutional, and it was ratified by an election.
  • The removal of the deposits in the fall of 1833 was something else — the president ordered the government's money taken out of the bank Congress had chosen to hold it, while Congress was away, over the refusal of his own secretary of the Treasury, whom he dismissed for refusing.
  • That sequence moved the opposition's charge from a bad veto to kingship — crown, robes, scepter, and the torn Constitution under the royal feet.
  • The Whig Party, formed the following year, took its very name from the enemies of kings.

Slide 13 · Removal, censure, and a new party, 1833 to 1834

On the slide: five bullets — the deposits to selected state banks, fall 1833; a secretary refusing and removed, Taney obeying; the "pet banks" name; the Senate censure; the Whigs coalescing in 1834.

  • Teach the removal as Jackson refusing to accept the veto's limits, because that refusal is the factual core under the cartoon.
  • The veto killed the recharter, but the Bank still held the public money and four years of charter, and Jackson read his reelection as a mandate not to wait.
  • The removal required a compliant Treasury: the secretary who refused was dismissed, and Roger Taney carried the order out.
  • The political answers came in pairs — in the Senate, Clay carried a censure resolution declaring the president had assumed power the Constitution had not granted him.
  • In the country, National Republicans, Anti-Masons, and disaffected Democrats began joining in 1834 as Whigs, named for the historic opponents of royal power, united at first chiefly by resistance to what they called executive usurpation.
  • A president who claimed to fight concentrated power had concentrated a new opposition into existence.

Slide 14 · Biddle's answer: the contraction, 1833 to 1834

On the slide: five bullets — loans cut and renewals refused; state banks pressed to settle; credit tightening, merchants failing, petitions flooding Congress; distress meant to force the deposits back; the public blaming the Bank, Biddle relenting.

  • Present the contraction from the labeled teaching summary in The Bank File, and tell students that is what it is: no single Biddle document carries the whole episode, so the file summarizes the record and says so.
  • Through the winter of 1833 and 1834 the Bank contracted its loans sharply; distress followed in the ports and the interior, and petitions for relief arrived in Congress by the hundred.
  • Biddle's design was to make the country feel the Bank's absence until it demanded the Bank's restoration.
  • The design failed in the one way he had not planned: the country blamed the hand it could see, and the hand was his.
  • In demonstrating that one officer could tighten the whole nation's credit as a political instrument, Biddle supplied the proof of Jackson's central charge, from his own counting room.

Slide 15 · The boom the removal fed

On the slide: the banknote portrait at right; four bullets — the federal charter expiring in 1836; pet banks lending on thin reserves; western land bought with borrowed paper; land sales climbing to 1836.

  • Picture: a portrait crop of the Bank's five dollar note of 1829.
  • The contrast to carry is between the note on screen, issued under the regulator's discipline, and the flood of state bank paper that replaced it.
  • Follow the incentives rather than the morals: the pet banks held the government's deposits, which swelled their reserves on paper, and every deposit bank competed to lend, because lending was profit and the federal balance cost nothing to hold.
  • Western land was the era's great speculation — its price rising, its buyers borrowing the purchase money from the same banks whose notes the land office accepted.
  • Settlers with real farms stood inside the same credit web as pure speculators, which is why section G's collapse reaches so far down.
  • By 1836 the boom's paper rested on reserves of gold and silver far thinner than the notes promised — and the administration that hated paper most had built the loosest paper system of the era.

Slide 16 · Section F divider: The Specie Circular and the Turn

On the slide: the section label and title; the standfirst — "July 1836: the Treasury orders gold and silver for the public lands, aiming at the speculators, and the order lands on an economy already turning."

  • Introduce the circular as the rare document in this unit whose text students can read entire in The Bank File — a working Treasury order rather than a state paper.
  • Its author on paper is Secretary Levi Woodbury, its direction is President Jackson's, and its occasion is stated in its own first sentence: complaints of frauds, speculations, and monopolies in the purchase of the public lands, aided by excessive bank credits.
  • The administration's diagnosis was sincere and partly right: the boom was being bought with paper of doubtful backing, and the Treasury itself was accumulating that paper for the nation's land.
  • The remedy was hard money orthodoxy — after August 15, 1836, the land offices would take gold and silver only.
  • What the remedy would do to the banks that held the nation's thin stock of specie is the section's second half.

Slide 17 · What the circular ordered, July 11, 1836

On the slide: five bullets — complaints named first; gold and silver after August 15; Virginia land scrip in the proper cases; settlers protected to December 15, up to 320 acres; specie moving west.

  • Read the order's mechanics with the class, because its exception tells students the target was speculation rather than settlement.
  • An actual settler or bona fide resident could still pay in the old kinds of money until December 15, for up to 320 acres; the speculator buying thousands of acres on bank credit could not.
  • As policy the design is coherent. As timing it was dangerous, and the danger is a matter of geography: specie was concentrated in the eastern banks, land was being sold in the West, and every land payment in coin now had to travel westward out of the reserves of banks whose notes still promised coin on demand at home.
  • Teach that movement as the order's unintended consequence, stated in the reading, and keep it separate from the order's stated purpose.
  • Documents prove their purposes; their consequences have to be traced — which is what section G does.

Slide 18 · Stat: 320 acres, the settler exception

On the slide: the big figure "320"; the label — "acres, the settler exception in the Specie Circular"; the italic note — actual settlers could pay in paper until December 15, 1836; speculators could not.

  • This small number is the circular read closely.
  • Three hundred twenty acres is a half section, a large family farm by the standards of the public land states, and the exception tells us the administration knew the difference between a settler and a speculator and tried to write it into the order.
  • The settler keeps the old indulgences as to the kind of money received, up to that acreage, until December 15, 1836; the large buyer must produce gold and silver after August 15.
  • When critics called the circular a blunt instrument, this clause is the answer and the admission at once: the order's authors aimed at the paper speculation, and they aimed carefully.
  • What they could not aim at was the state of British credit and the cotton market in the same winter, which turned a careful order into one strand of a general tightening.

Slide 19 · Section G divider: The Panic of 1837 and Who Paid

On the slide: the Van Buren portrait at right; the section label and title; the standfirst — "May 1837: the banks suspend. The president who inherits the collapse is not the president who fought the Bank."

  • Picture: Martin Van Buren, Jackson's chosen successor, photographed late in his life — the face students see is an old man's, and the presidency it belongs to was broken in its first season.
  • Set the section's chronology hard, because causation is the lesson's final skill.
  • Jackson retired to the Hermitage in March 1837, publishing a farewell address that warned the country against paper money and the money power.
  • In May the banks of New York suspended payment in specie and banks across the country followed, since a note no bank would redeem in coin was a promise suspended everywhere at once.
  • Cotton and land values collapsed, debtors failed, and workers lost wages and work; the depression that followed lasted, with brief relief, into the 1840s, and it consumed the presidency of the man in this portrait, who had cast no veto and signed no circular.

Slide 20 · May 1837: the suspension and the fall

On the slide: five bullets — New York banks suspending specie payment; banks following within weeks; cotton and land collapsing; farmers and merchants failing together; workers losing wages and work, depression to the 1840s.

  • Teach the suspension as a mechanism, not a mood.
  • A bank's note was a promise to pay gold or silver on demand; when the strongest banks in New York announced in May 1837 that they would not redeem their notes for a time, every holder of every bank's paper learned the promise was suspended.
  • Credit froze, and merchants who owed money they could no longer borrow failed in chains, each failure pulling down the next house's paper.
  • Cotton, the export that earned the country's specie, fell in price as British buyers contracted, and western land bought at boom prices would not sell at any price.
  • Notice who is absent from the fragments: neither Jackson nor Biddle appears, because by the spring of 1837 both men were private citizens — the panic had the Bank War's cast nowhere in it and the Bank War's consequences everywhere in it.

Slide 21 · Van Buren's answer: the independent treasury

On the slide: four bullets — no new national bank, no return to the pet banks; the government divorced from banking; federal money in federal vaults, 1840; the Bank dead, the currency question unsettled.

  • Close the narrative with the institutional answer, because the unit test asks for it by name.
  • Van Buren refused both restorations on offer: he would not charter a third national bank, which his party's hard money men would not forgive, and he would not put the government's money back into state banks, which the panic had just discredited.
  • His proposal was separation: under the independent treasury, enacted in 1840, the federal government kept its money in its own vaults, in the hands of its own officers, divorcing the Treasury from the banking system until the Civil War era rebuilt the connection.
  • Students should see the irony whole: the democracy that destroyed a bank to free the people's money from private power ended by locking the people's money in government strongboxes, outside the credit system entirely — while the country's actual currency went on being the notes of state banks.

Slide 22 · The Bank War at a glance

On the slide: the title "Privilege, power, and the bill"; the four-row table — the Bank and the trap; the veto and the removal; the boom and the circular; the panic and the answer.

  • This slide mirrors the closing table of the guided notes, and students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must.
  • The Bank and the trap: chartered in 1816, Biddle regulating by the specie demand, and Clay persuading him to force the vote in 1832.
  • The veto and the removal: the message of July 10, 1832, the reelection, the deposits to the pet banks in 1833, the censure, and the Whigs from 1834.
  • The boom and the circular: the contraction, pet bank lending, the charter expiring in 1836, and gold and silver ordered for land that July.
  • The panic and the answer: suspension in May 1837, depression into the 1840s, and the independent treasury of 1840.
  • Grade the table on dates, actors, and who bore each stage's cost.

Slide 23 · Vocabulary: the struggle and its men

On the slide: four terms with their short forms — Bank War, Nicholas Biddle, pet banks, Specie Circular. The definitions below are the guided notes wording, in full.

  • Say: "Bank War: the political struggle of 1832 to 1836 in which Jackson vetoed the Second Bank's recharter, removed the federal deposits to state banks, and destroyed the institution, which Clay and Biddle had forced to a vote four years early meaning to make it the election's deciding question."
  • Say: "Nicholas Biddle: the president of the Second Bank of the United States from 1823, a Philadelphia financier who ran the Bank as the regulator of the nation's currency and fought Jackson for its life with its loans, its contracts, and a deliberate contraction of credit."
  • Say: "pet banks: the state banks chosen by the Jackson administration to hold the federal deposits removed from the Second Bank in 1833, named in contempt by the Bank's defenders, whose easy lending on thin reserves fed the land speculation of the middle 1830s."
  • Say: "Specie Circular: the Treasury order of July 1836 requiring gold or silver, specie, in payment for public lands, issued to stop speculation bought with paper banknotes and blamed by critics for draining specie from eastern banks on the eve of panic."

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: the fall and the settlement

On the slide: three terms with their short forms — Panic of 1837, Whig Party, independent treasury. The definitions below are the guided notes wording, in full.

  • Say: "Panic of 1837: the financial collapse of May 1837, when banks across the country suspended payment in specie, cotton prices and land values fell, and a depression followed that lasted into the 1840s and broke the presidency of Martin Van Buren."
  • Say: "Whig Party: the party that coalesced in 1834 from Jackson's opponents, National Republicans, Anti-Masons, and disaffected Democrats among them, named for the historic opponents of royal power and united at first chiefly by resistance to what they called executive usurpation."
  • Say: "independent treasury: President Van Buren's system, enacted in 1840, under which the federal government kept its money in its own vaults rather than in any bank, divorcing the Treasury from the banking system until the Civil War era rebuilt the connection."
  • Notice the arc the three terms draw: the collapse, the party the collapse's politics built, and the institution the collapse's president built in answer.

Slide 25 · Closing synthesis: two findings, held together

On the slide: the title "Two findings, held together"; the two findings in large text — the Bank did the public's work and its president could tighten the nation's credit as a weapon, and did; when the Bank fell, the boom and panic were paid for by debtors and workers who were parties to neither man's quarrel.

  • Close the lesson by refusing to let either finding absorb the other, because the documents support both and the verdict work is graded on holding them together.
  • The Bank's public work is documented in The Bank File and the reading's first section: a national currency, the public money kept, and state banks disciplined into honesty by the specie demand.
  • The private power is documented by the Bank's own president: Biddle's contraction of 1833 and 1834, run from a Philadelphia counting room to coerce an elected government, is the veto's abstract charge demonstrated in fact.
  • And the payment is documented in section G's accounting: suspension in May 1837, cotton and land collapsed, debtors failed, workers idle, a depression into the 1840s, and a successor president broken by a crisis he did not make.
  • Students who hold only the first finding repeat the Bank's defense of itself; students who hold only the second cannot explain the panic.

Slide 26 · Now your turn: answer the question and exit ticket

On the slide: the closing title "Answer the question"; the task line — judge from the Bank's conduct, account for Biddle's contraction, name the payers of 1837; the rows — Activity (The Bank File), Notebook, Quick check (The Veto and the Circular), Writing (Public Good or Private Power); the exit ticket line.

  • Close the lesson by handing the verdict to the students, which is what this lesson exists to do.
  • The Bank File gives them the veto message from the audited bank record, the King Andrew the First print read as an argument, a labeled teaching summary of Biddle's contraction, Jackson's farewell address, and the Specie Circular — and asks them to judge whether the Bank was a public good or a private power and who paid when it fell.
  • The notebook page charts the struggle stage by stage with power and cost at each stage; the quick check sets the veto beside the circular.
  • The writing task asks for the verdict defended against its strongest rival, with the contraction and the payers of 1837 carried at full strength.
  • The exit ticket has three parts: the Bank War and the pet banks defined, the Specie Circular stated in two sentences, and the lesson question answered with both findings held together.
