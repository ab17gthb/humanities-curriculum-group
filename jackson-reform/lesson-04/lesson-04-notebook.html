Teacher Answer Key

Stage of the struggleWhat happenedWho held the power, and who bore the cost
The Bank and the trap, 1816 to 1832The Second Bank, chartered in 1816, held the federal deposits, issued a national currency, and regulated state banks by presenting their notes for payment in gold and silver. In 1832 Henry Clay persuaded Nicholas Biddle to apply for recharter four years early, meaning to make the veto cost Jackson the presidency.The Bank held the power: one corporation in Philadelphia, directed by Biddle from 1823, disciplined every borrower in the country. State banks and their borrowers bore the cost of its discipline whenever it called their notes in.
The veto and the election, 1832Jackson vetoed the recharter on July 10, 1832, charging that exclusive privileges made the rich richer and the powerful more powerful, and carried the veto to the voters. He was reelected by a wide margin that November.The president held the power, and used the veto, a constitutional instrument, in the open. Biddle and Clay bore the political cost: their trap made the Bank the election's question, and the electorate answered it against them.
The removal and the answer, 1833 to 1834Jackson removed the federal deposits to selected state banks, the pet banks, over one treasury secretary's refusal. The Senate censured him, cartoonists crowned him King Andrew the First, the Whig Party began forming in 1834, and Biddle contracted the Bank's loans through the winter, producing distress petitions across the country.Power was contested at every point: the president over the deposits, the Senate in censure, and Biddle over the nation's credit, which he tightened as a weapon. Merchants who could not renew notes and workers who lost employment bore the contraction's cost, and blamed the Bank for it.
The boom and the circular, 1834 to 1836The Bank's federal charter expired in 1836. The pet banks, holding federal money and lending on thin reserves, fed a land boom bought with borrowed paper. In July 1836 the Specie Circular ordered gold and silver only for public lands after August 15, pulling specie west as British credit contracted and cotton prices began to fall.The state banks held the lending power now, with no regulator over them. Speculators and settlers held paper gains. The Treasury held the risk, and the eastern banks held reserves that the land offices were draining westward.
The panic and the answer, 1837 to 1840In May 1837 banks across the country suspended payment in specie. Cotton and land values collapsed, debtors failed, workers lost wages and work, and depression lasted into the 1840s. Van Buren's independent treasury, enacted in 1840, kept the government's money in its own vaults.No one held the power to stop the collapse, which is the point of the stage. The cost fell on indebted farmers and merchants and on wage workers, and it broke Van Buren's presidency, though the war that set it up had been Jackson's and Biddle's.

The lesson in one paragraph. Strong answers judge the Bank from its conduct and name the payers from the last row. Example: The Bank did public work, holding the government's money and keeping the currency sound, and it was also a private power, because in 1833 its president contracted the nation's credit to coerce a president, which is the veto's charge proved by the Bank's own hand. When it fell, the pet banks lent the boom on thin reserves, the Specie Circular of July 1836 tightened the money on the eve of panic, and in May 1837 the bill arrived for indebted farmers, failed merchants, and workers who lost their wages. Van Buren answered in 1840 with the independent treasury, the government keeping its money in its own vaults rather than in any bank.
