Indian Removal

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Ground of the Question

In 1828 some 125,000 Native people still lived east of the Mississippi River. In the Southeast stood the five nations white Americans called civilized for their written laws, schools, farms, and printing: the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek, and Seminole. The Cherokee had gone furthest. Sequoyah completed a syllabary in 1821, written characters for the Cherokee language, and within a few years most Cherokee could read it. The nation adopted a written constitution in 1827 and in February 1828 began printing a bilingual newspaper, the Cherokee Phoenix, at its capital, New Echota. Their country was coveted for two reasons. Its soil grew cotton, the crop that made land into wealth, and in 1828 gold was found on Cherokee land in Georgia.

B. Georgia Presses, and the Cherokee Answer with Law

Georgia claimed a promise. In an 1802 compact the federal government had agreed to extinguish Native title to land inside Georgia as soon as it could be done peaceably and on reasonable terms. A generation later Georgia demanded payment at once. In 1829 and 1830 the state extended its laws over Cherokee country, declared the Cherokee government abolished, and prepared to survey the land and distribute it to white Georgians by lottery. The Cherokee answered without war. Under Principal Chief John Ross, in office from 1828, the nation hired lawyers, sent memorials and delegations to Congress, and carried its case to the Supreme Court of the United States, whose treaties had guaranteed Cherokee land.

C. The Removal Act and the President's Defense

Congress made removal national policy. The Indian Removal Act, signed on May 28, 1830, authorized the president to offer Native nations districts west of the Mississippi in exchange for their lands inside the states, and appropriated five hundred thousand dollars to carry the exchanges out. It passed the House of Representatives by five votes, 102 to 97. In his annual message of December 6, 1830, President Andrew Jackson defended the policy as benevolence. Removal, he told Congress, would separate Native people from white settlements, free them from the power of the states, and let them pursue happiness in their own way, perhaps to become a civilized and Christian community.

D. The Court Answers, and Is Not Obeyed

The Cherokee case reached the Court in two steps. In Cherokee Nation v. Georgia in 1831, Chief Justice John Marshall called the Cherokee a domestic dependent nation and dismissed the suit, holding it was not a foreign state entitled to sue Georgia directly. The next year Samuel Worcester, a missionary living in Cherokee country with the nation's permission, was prosecuted under Georgia's law for residing there without a license. In Worcester v. Georgia in 1832, Marshall held that the Cherokee formed a distinct community occupying its own territory, in which the laws of Georgia could have no force, and that the Georgia act under which Worcester was prosecuted was void. Georgia refused to obey and kept Worcester at hard labor. President Jackson declined to enforce the ruling. Worcester was freed in January 1833 only when Georgia's governor pardoned him. The Cherokee drew the lesson plainly: the republic's law ran where the republic chose to run it.

E. A Treaty Signed by a Minority

What the Court would not surrender, a treaty was found to sign away. Cherokee law, passed in 1829, made it a capital offense to sell the nation's land without its government's authority. In December 1835, while Ross and the nation's delegation were in Washington, a small group led by Major Ridge, John Ridge, and Elias Boudinot met federal commissioners at New Echota and on December 29 signed the Treaty of New Echota. It ceded all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi for five million dollars and land in the West, and gave the Cherokee two years to leave. Ross answered with a memorial protesting that the instrument was fraudulent, made by unauthorized individuals, and opposed by upwards of fifteen thousand Cherokee, nearly the whole nation. The Senate ratified the treaty in May 1836, by a single vote.

F. The March, and the Other Nations

The deadline fell in May 1838, under President Martin Van Buren. General Winfield Scott's troops rounded up some sixteen thousand Cherokee, held them in camps, and sent them west in detachments through the winter of 1838 and 1839. Roughly a quarter of the people, about four thousand, died on the road, among them Ross's wife, Quatie. Memory named that removal the Trail of Tears, and extended the name to all the southeastern removals. The Choctaw removed first, in 1831, in disorder. The Creek, after armed resistance in 1836, were marched in chains. The Chickasaw negotiated better terms. The Seminole fought the Second Seminole War, the costliest Indian war the United States ever fought, and a remnant was never removed from Florida. The Eastern Band of Cherokee remained in North Carolina, on land bought back by their agent William Holland Thomas.

G. Law at Every Step

Hold the lesson's two statements together, because both are true. Removal was carried out under law at every step: an act of Congress, a presidential message defending it, appropriations to pay for it, a treaty ratified by the Senate, and soldiers executing the orders the treaty was said to authorize. And the law's forms were used against the holding of the nation's own court, that Georgia's laws had no force in Cherokee country. The Cherokee had answered in the republic's own language, with a constitution, a newspaper, petitions, and lawsuits, and they won in its court. What they could not obtain was the one thing a judgment cannot supply for itself: a government willing to enforce it.

5. How did the United States take the Southeast, and what did the Cherokee do to stop it? Full credit answers hold both halves. The Cherokee stopped removal for a decade with the republic's own instruments: a constitution and newspaper, memorials and delegations to Congress, and two Supreme Court cases, the second won outright in 1832. The United States took the Southeast by law's forms used against the Court's own holding: the Removal Act of 1830, Georgia's extension laws, and the Treaty of New Echota, signed in December 1835 by the Ridge and Boudinot party without the Cherokee government's authority, protested by upwards of fifteen thousand Cherokee, and ratified by the Senate in May 1836 by a single vote.
