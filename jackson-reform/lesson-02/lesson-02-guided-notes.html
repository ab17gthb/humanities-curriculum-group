Guided Notes: Indian Removal

Lesson question: How did the United States take the Southeast, and what did the Cherokee do to stop it?

Unit question: What did democracy mean in Jackson's America, who was it for, and what did the era's reformers claim it still owed?

Key Terms

Indian Removal Act: the law of May 1830 that authorized the president to offer Native nations land west of the Mississippi in exchange for their lands inside the states, the legal instrument under which the southeastern nations were driven out in the 1830s

Cherokee Nation: the Native nation of the southern Appalachians that had adopted a written constitution, a syllabary and newspaper, farms, and missions by the 1820s, and whose government resisted Georgia's extension of state law over Cherokee country in the courts and in Congress

Worcester v. Georgia: the Supreme Court decision of 1832 in which Chief Justice Marshall held that Georgia's laws had no force within Cherokee country, a judgment Georgia defied and President Jackson declined to enforce against the state

Five Civilized Tribes: the name white Americans gave the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek, and Seminole nations of the Southeast for their written laws, schools, farms, and printing, and in some cases their holding of enslaved people, none of which protected their land from removal

Treaty of New Echota: the treaty of December 1835 signed at New Echota by a minority of Cherokee acting without their government's authority, which the Senate ratified by a single vote and which supplied the legal cover under which the Cherokee were removed in 1838

Trail of Tears: the forced removal of the Cherokee in 1838 and 1839, when some sixteen thousand people were rounded up, held in camps, and marched west in detachments through the winter, roughly a quarter of them dying on the road, and the name memory has extended to all the southeastern removals

John Ross: the principal chief of the Cherokee Nation from 1828 until 1866, who led its resistance to removal through petitions, delegations, and litigation for a decade, and who was himself forced west with his people in 1838

Summary table. The nation Georgia pressed: by 1828 the Cherokee had a syllabary (1821), a constitution (1827), and the bilingual Cherokee Phoenix (1828), and Georgia, claiming the 1802 compact and drawn by cotton land and the gold found in 1828, extended its laws over Cherokee country in 1829 and 1830 and prepared a land lottery; the pressure changed a sovereign nation's country into land a state claimed the right to distribute. The law and the defense: the Indian Removal Act of May 28, 1830 authorized exchanges of eastern land for districts west of the Mississippi, appropriated five hundred thousand dollars, and passed the House 102 to 97, and Jackson's message of December 6, 1830 defended it as benevolence; removal changed from a Georgia demand into national policy with a stated moral defense. The court and the treaty: Marshall dismissed the nation's own suit in 1831 as a domestic dependent nation, then held in Worcester v. Georgia (1832) that Georgia's laws had no force in Cherokee country, a judgment Georgia defied and Jackson declined to enforce, and the Treaty of New Echota, signed December 29, 1835 by the Ridge and Boudinot minority without authority and ratified by one vote in May 1836, supplied the legal instrument the court victory had denied the removal party. The march: in May 1838 troops rounded up some sixteen thousand Cherokee into camps and detachments and marched them west through the winter, roughly a quarter dying, about four thousand people, while the other nations' removals and the Eastern Band's remaining in North Carolina completed the map.

Exit ticket 2. In Worcester v. Georgia (1832) Chief Justice Marshall held that the Cherokee nation was a distinct community occupying its own territory, in which the laws of Georgia could have no force, and that the Georgia law under which Worcester was prosecuted was void. Georgia defied the judgment and kept Worcester imprisoned, and President Jackson declined to enforce the ruling against the state.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that names both the Cherokee resistance and the instruments of taking. The Cherokee resisted with law: memorials to Congress and two Supreme Court cases, winning in Worcester in 1832. The United States took the Southeast through the Removal Act of 1830 and the Treaty of New Echota, signed by an unauthorized minority in 1835 and ratified by a single vote, then enforced it with troops in 1838.
