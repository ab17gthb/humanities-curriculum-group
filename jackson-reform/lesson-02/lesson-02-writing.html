Writing Task: A Policy of Law or of Force

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: How did the United States take the Southeast, and what did the Cherokee do to stop it? A strong answer sets out the Cherokee resistance in the republic's own instruments, follows the legal forms removal used at each step, weighs the judgment of 1832 against the treaty of 1835, and ends by judging whether removal was a policy of law or of force. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. By 1828 the Cherokee had Sequoyah's syllabary (1821), a written constitution (1827), and the bilingual Cherokee Phoenix (1828), printed at New Echota. Their country was coveted for cotton land, and gold was found on it in Georgia in 1828.

2. Georgia, claiming the federal promise of the 1802 compact, extended its laws over Cherokee country in 1829 and 1830, declared the Cherokee government abolished, and prepared to distribute the land by lottery. The Cherokee under John Ross answered with lawyers, memorials to Congress, and lawsuits.

3. The Indian Removal Act of May 28, 1830 authorized the president to exchange eastern lands for districts west of the Mississippi and appropriated five hundred thousand dollars. It passed the House 102 to 97. Its seventh section promised that nothing in it authorized violating any existing treaty. Jackson's message of December 6, 1830 defended removal as a benevolent policy.

4. In Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831) Marshall called the Cherokee a domestic dependent nation and dismissed their suit. In Worcester v. Georgia (1832) he held that Georgia's laws could have no force in Cherokee country and that the Georgia act was void. Georgia defied the judgment, Jackson declined to enforce it, and Worcester was freed in 1833 by a governor's pardon.

5. The Treaty of New Echota, signed December 29, 1835 by Major Ridge, John Ridge, Elias Boudinot, and their party, ceded all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi for five million dollars. Cherokee law had made unauthorized sale of the nation's land a capital offense since 1829. Ross's memorial reported upwards of fifteen thousand Cherokee protesting it. The Senate ratified it in May 1836 by a single vote.

6. At the deadline in May 1838, troops rounded up some sixteen thousand Cherokee and marched them west through the winter of 1838 and 1839. Roughly a quarter, about four thousand people, died, among them Quatie Ross. The Creek were marched in chains in 1836, the Seminole fought the Second Seminole War, and the Eastern Band remained in North Carolina.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the prompt with a verdict. Either verdict earns full credit when weighed against the other side: law argued from the unbroken sequence of act, message, ratified treaty, and orders, or force argued from what each form concealed and from the army that closed the sequence. (Blueprint 3 and 4)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank items 1 and 2 carry the resistance: a nation with a syllabary, a constitution, and a press, answering extension laws with lawyers, memorials, and lawsuits. (Blueprint 3) Bank items 3 and 4 carry the legal contest: the Removal Act with its House margin of five votes, its appropriation, and its treaty proviso, and the two Marshall decisions with the fate of the second. (Blueprint 3 and 4) Bank items 5 and 6 carry the instrument and the cost: an unauthorized treaty ratified by one vote over the protest of upwards of fifteen thousand, and sixteen thousand people marched west with roughly a quarter dying. (Blueprint 4) A full credit essay draws on both the resistance and the taking.
  • Figures used as evidence, not decoration. 102 to 97, five hundred thousand dollars, five million dollars, fifteen thousand protesters, one vote, sixteen thousand marched, and four thousand dead each mark a step in the argument, and strong essays say what the step is.
  • A closing that lands on the lesson's holding. The Cherokee won the law's argument in the republic's own court, and the Southeast was taken anyway, under forms the court's holding had already answered. An essay that ends in 1832 without the treaty and the march has answered only half the question.

Model opening for teacher reference. The Cherokee did everything the republic said a people should do, and it was not enough. They wrote a constitution, printed a newspaper in their own syllabary, hired the republic's lawyers, and won in its Supreme Court, where the chief justice held that Georgia's laws had no force in their country at all. The United States took the Southeast all the same, and it took it in forms: an act passed by five votes, a treaty signed by men their own law condemned and ratified by a single vote over the protest of nearly the whole nation, and finally an army marching sixteen thousand people west in winter. A full model continues in this pattern through Jackson's benevolence defense and the act's own treaty proviso, and judges the policy by the distance between the forms and the facts they were laid over.

Common weak answers. The conquest answer that never mentions Worcester treats removal as simple seizure, and bank item 4 exists to prevent that: the taking had to route around a judgment. The legal answer that never mentions the memorial treats a signature as consent, and bank item 5 exists to prevent that. An essay built only on Jackson's message misses the lesson's structure, in which the president's defense is one station among five and the Cherokee records answer it.
