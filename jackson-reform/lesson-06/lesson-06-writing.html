Writing Task: Why Did Revival Produce Reformers?

The prompt. Between 1800 and 1848 the United States was swept by an evangelical revival, and the revival's converts founded the era's reform movements. Answer this question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Why did revival produce reformers, and what did they set out to fix first? Argue from the documents in the Reform File. A strong answer states the teaching about the human will that turned converts into workers, follows the first remedy the reformers chose and shows how its carrier widened, and ends by judging how far the reform logic reached by 1848, in the schoolhouse and at the asylum. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each one supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson documents. Write the history in your own sentences. Quotations from the records belong inside quotation marks, spelled as the records spell them.

Evidence Bank

1. The camp meeting, in summary: at Red River, Kentucky, in June 1800, and at Cane Ridge, Kentucky, in August 1801, outdoor meetings ran for days and drew thousands, by the usual estimates between ten and twenty thousand at Cane Ridge. Hearers wept, cried out, and fell to the ground under conviction of sin, and the Methodist church carried the form across the frontier by circuit riders.

2. Finney, Lectures on Revivals of Religion, Lecture I, 1835: "Religion is the work of man. It is something for man to do. It consists in obeying God. It is man's duty." And: "A revival is not a miracle. ... I said that a revival is the result of the right use of the appropriate means." His Rochester converts of 1830 and 1831 were organized into temperance, moral reform, and antislavery societies, staffed in large part by the women who formed the majority of converts.

3. The temperance pledge, in summary: the American Temperance Society, founded at Boston in 1826, spread a signed pledge, at first against ardent spirits only. Through the 1830s the pledge hardened into total abstinence, and by the 1840s the movement pressed state legislatures to prohibit the sale of alcohol outright.

4. Mann, Twelfth Annual Report, 1848: "But, if education be equably diffused, it will draw property after it by the strongest of all attractions; for such a thing never did happen, and never can happen, as that an intelligent and practical body of men should be permanently poor." And: "It does better than to disarm the poor of their hostility towards the rich: it prevents being poor."

5. Dix, Memorial to the Legislature of Massachusetts, January 1843: "I come as the advocate of helpless, forgotten, insane and idiotic men and women; of beings, sunk to a condition from which the most unconcerned would start with real horror; of beings wretched in our Prisons, and more wretched in our almshouses." And: "I proceed, Gentlemen, briefly to call your attention to the present state of Insane Persons confined within this Commonwealth, in cages, closets, cellars, stalls, pens! Chained, naked, beaten with rods, and lashed into obedience!"

6. The burned-over district, in summary: western and central New York, crossed by the Erie Canal and swept so often by revival that preachers said it had no fuel left to burn, became the seedbed of abolition societies, temperance crusades, women's rights organizing, and new religious movements in the 1830s and 1840s.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the prompt, resting the production of reformers on the revival's teaching about the will, that salvation could be chosen and religion was man's work to do, rather than on the era's general excitement. (Blueprint 11)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Item 2 establishes the doctrine in Finney's own sentences and its organizational result at Rochester in 1830 and 1831. (Blueprint 11) Item 3 establishes the first remedy and its widening carrier: the pledge of 1826, the signer, the society, and by the 1840s the legislature. (Blueprint 12) Item 1 supplies the instrument that gathered the converts, and item 6 the ground where the chain ran fastest. (Blueprint 11) Items 4 and 5 establish the reach by the 1840s: Mann's common schools argued as the prevention of poverty in 1848, and Dix's Memorial of 1843 carrying reform to people who could neither sign nor choose, with the legislature as the carrier. (Blueprint 12)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive distinction is between a mood and a mechanism: an essay that calls the era religious and reforming in the same breath, without the will, the means, and the society as the links, has used half the file.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest rival explanation. For the market revolution claim, that factories and canal towns explain the era's anxieties: the documents' remedies follow the revival's calendar and borrow its tools, the meeting, the pledge, the petition, and the society, and their authors state a theology of the will as their reason. For the claim that reform was only charity by the comfortable: Dix's record and Mann's tax argument both end at the legislature, asking law, not alms, to do the work.

Model opening for teacher reference. Revival produced reformers because it taught that the human will was free and then gave the freed will work to do. Finney stated the doctrine without decoration in 1835: religion is the work of man, and a revival is not a miracle but the result of the right use of the appropriate means. A convert made by that teaching had not been rescued into rest. He or she had been enlisted, and the societies the Rochester converts joined in 1830 and 1831, for temperance, for moral reform, and against slavery, were the enlistment written into organizations. A full model continues in this pattern, takes drink as the first evil fixed and follows the pledge from the signer of 1826 to the prohibition campaigns of the 1840s, answers the market revolution explanation directly, and closes on the reach of 1848, Mann arguing that schooling prevents being poor and Dix, five years earlier, asking a legislature to rebuild by law what defective legislation had built.

Common weak answers. Listing temperance, schools, and asylums as good causes the churches happened to favor, without Finney's teaching on the will, argues from a catalog and misses the lesson's question. Treating the pledge as a law reverses the record: the pledge bound a conscience first, and the movement reached the statute book only in the 1840s. Quoting Dix's cages passage as evidence about prisons in general misses her stated cause: she charged defective legislation, and her remedy was an act of the same legislature she addressed.
