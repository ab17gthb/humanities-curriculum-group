## Quotation log

### Finney, Lectures on Revivals of Religion, Lecture I (named edition, read in full during this build)
- Edition used: Charles Grandison Finney, Lectures on Revivals of Religion, Lecture I, "What a Revival of Religion Is," first edition (Leavitt, Lord & Co., New York, 1835). The Lecture I text was read in full during this build in the Charles Finney Collection transcription of the lectures, and the 1835 publication facts (Leavitt, Lord & Co., New York, 1835; the Boston printing by Crocker & Brewster, 1835) are as the Original Sources collection's citations for the lectures record them.
- Quoted in the activity, Station 1: "Religion is the work of man. It is something for man to do. It consists in obeying God. It is man's duty." And, with its cuts marked by ellipses: "A revival is not a miracle. ... I said that a revival is the result of the right use of the appropriate means. ... A revival is as naturally a result of the use of the appropriate means as a crop is of the use of its appropriate means." Quoted in the quick check background and the writing task evidence bank: the same two passages, the second with the same marked cuts.
- The reading and guided notes paraphrase Finney without quotation marks, except that the reading carries his phrases "the work of man" and "the result of the right use of the appropriate means" inside its own sentences as his teaching, as the edition carries them.

### Mann, Twelfth Annual Report, 1848 (named edition, read during this build)
- Edition used: Horace Mann, Twelfth Annual Report of the Secretary of the Massachusetts Board of Education, 1848, as printed in Life and Works of Horace Mann, vol. 4 (Boston: Lee and Shepard, 1891), pp. 246 to 252, the section on intellectual education as a means of removing poverty, in the transcription of that edition read in full during this build at ushistory.education.
- Quoted in the activity, Station 3: the Massachusetts theory passage entire as the edition carries it; the equably diffused passage entire; and the sentence "It does better than to disarm the poor of their hostility towards the rich: it prevents being poor." Quoted in the quick check background and the writing task evidence bank: the equably diffused passage and the prevents being poor sentence.
- One sentence of the report is deliberately not quoted anywhere in this lesson: the sentence in which Mann calls education the great equalizer and the balance wheel of the social machinery is printed in the edition with a punctuation mark this unit's documents do not use, so the lesson quotes around it and paraphrases that sentence in the reading, the notes, and the keys, and the unit's own vocabulary definition of Horace Mann carries the balance wheel phrase as the spec's definition states it.

### Dix, Memorial to the Legislature of Massachusetts, 1843 (named transcription, read in full during this build)
- Edition used: Dorothea Dix, Memorial to the Legislature of Massachusetts, January 1843, in the transcription carried by the Bill of Rights Institute, read in full during this build.
- Quoted in the activity, Station 4: the strong claims of suffering humanity passage entire as the transcription carries it; the cages, closets, cellars passage entire; three notebook extracts entire ("Lincoln. A woman in a cage." "Medford. One idiotic subject chained, and one in a close stall for 17 years." "Granville. One often closely confined; now losing the use of his limbs from want of exercise."); and the defective legislation passage entire. Quoted in the quick check background and the writing task evidence bank: the cages passage and the advocate passage.
- Dix's period terms for the people she found, including "idiotic," stand inside her quotations as she wrote them, and the activity's Source line says so. The lesson's own prose does not use those terms of any person.

### Summary and teaching records, labeled on their faces
- Activity Station 2 (the temperance pledge) is a teaching summary written for this lesson from the public record, labeled as a summary on its face. No sentence in it is presented as a period quotation. Its facts: the American Temperance Society founded at Boston in 1826; the first pledge against ardent spirits, not yet reaching beer, wine, or cider; local societies, tracts, and lecturers; total abstinence pledges through the 1830s; and campaigns for legal prohibition in the 1840s. Item 3 of the writing task evidence bank restates the same summary and labels it in summary.
- Activity Station 5 (the camp meeting) is a teaching summary written for this lesson from the public record, labeled as a summary on its face. Its facts: Red River, Logan County, Kentucky, June 1800; Cane Ridge, Bourbon County, Kentucky, August 1801, at Barton Stone's church; crowds estimated by the usual accounts at between ten and twenty thousand at Cane Ridge; meetings running for days, with families camped in tents and wagons, preachers relieving one another at a stand, and hearers weeping, crying out, and falling under conviction; and the Methodist adoption of the form, carried by circuit riders. Items 1 and 6 of the writing task evidence bank restate the summary's facts and label them in summary.
- The temperance membership figure used on the deck's stat slide (more than a million members) is the figure the Library of Congress's Constitution Annotated essay on the temperance movements gives for the American Temperance Society, and the speaker notes present it as the movement's claimed strength, not as an audited roll.

## Figures stated with their precision
- Cane Ridge attendance: the documents and notes state estimates only, between ten and twenty thousand, as the usual accounts give them, because the contemporary estimates vary widely and no count was taken.
- Rochester meetings: the winter season of 1830 and 1831, as the unit vocabulary states the dates.
- Temperance membership: stated as the movement's claimed count, over a million, as the Library of Congress essay records the society gaining more than a million members nationwide. No exact membership figure is stated in the student documents.

## Gaps recorded honestly
- The skeleton file for this unit, as it stands on disk in this build, contains Lessons 1 to 3 only and breaks off inside Lesson 3. Lesson 6 was therefore built from the unit spec's Lesson 6 vocabulary, source assignments, and blueprint entries, from the task brief's lesson question ("Why did revival produce reformers, and what did they set out to fix first?"), and from the unit frame's account of the Second Great Awakening lessons. No Lesson 6 reading scope from the skeleton was available to follow.
- No common school view is used. No period image of a district schoolhouse or a common school classroom could be verified as a public domain file under the names tried during this build, so Mann's sections of the deck use his portrait and text slides, and no image is captioned as a school anywhere in this lesson.
- No image of an asylum is used, for the same reason, and because the lesson's rule is that confinement is taught from Dix's written record, not pictured. Dix's portrait carries Section G.
- The Finney portrait shows the sitter in later life, bearded, and not as the clean shaven preacher of 1830. The deck notes disclose this where the portrait appears. No verified portrait of the young Finney was located during this build.
