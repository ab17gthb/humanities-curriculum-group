Guided Notes: Revival and Reform: The Second Great Awakening

Lesson question: Why did revival produce reformers, and what did they set out to fix first?

Unit question: What did democracy mean in Jackson's America, who was it for, and what did the era's reformers claim it still owed?

Key Terms

Second Great Awakening: the evangelical revival that swept the United States from the turn of the century into the 1840s, carried by camp meetings and itinerant preachers, which taught that every person could choose salvation and sent millions of converts toward the work of reforming society as well as themselves

Charles Grandison Finney: the revival preacher whose Rochester meetings of 1830 and 1831 became the model of the new evangelism, who preached that conversion was an act of the sinner's own will and organized his converts into the temperance, antislavery, and moral reform societies of the age

burned-over district: the region of western and central New York swept so often by revival and reform that preachers said it had no fuel left to burn, the seedbed in the 1830s and 1840s of abolitionism, women's rights, temperance crusades, and new religious movements

temperance: the reform movement against the drinking of alcohol, organized nationally in the American Temperance Society of 1826, which moved from pledges of moderation in the 1820s to campaigns for total abstinence and legal prohibition in the 1840s

Horace Mann: the Massachusetts education secretary whose annual reports from 1837 argued that tax supported common schools were the balance wheel of the social machinery, and who built the first American system of public schools with trained teachers, graded classes, and state supervision

common schools: the tax supported public schools open to all children of a district, which reformers of Mann's generation spread across the northern states in the 1830s and 1840s as the republic's instrument for making citizens out of a diverse and mobile people

Dorothea Dix: the Massachusetts teacher whose investigations of jails and almshouses in the 1840s exposed the insane confined in cages, closets, and cellars, and whose memorials to legislatures built the first generation of American public asylums

Summary table. The revival: the Second Great Awakening, from about 1800 into the 1840s, carried by camp meetings and circuit riders, taught that salvation was offered to every person and could be chosen or refused, breaking with the older teaching of a fate sealed before birth. Finney's method: conversion treated as an act of the sinner's own will, produced by the right use of means, protracted meetings, plain preaching aimed at a decision, and the anxious bench, stated in the Lectures on Revivals of Religion of 1835. The burned-over district: western and central New York, swept so often by revival that preachers said no fuel was left to burn, where abolition, temperance, women's rights, and new religious movements all drew members. Temperance: the American Temperance Society of 1826 fought drink first, with a pledge against ardent spirits that hardened into total abstinence in the 1830s and campaigns for legal prohibition in the 1840s. Schools and the asylum: Mann, secretary from 1837, argued for tax supported common schools and closed his work with the Twelfth Annual Report of 1848, and Dix's Memorial of January 1843 exposed the insane in cages, closets, and cellars and won an enlarged asylum at Worcester.

Exit ticket 1. Finney taught that a revival is not a miracle to wait for but the result of the right use of the appropriate means, as a crop follows sowing: protracted meetings, preaching in plain speech aimed at a decision, and the anxious bench, where sinners under conviction are prayed for by name.

Exit ticket 2. The first evil was drink. The American Temperance Society was founded at Boston in 1826 and fought it with a signed pledge, at first against ardent spirits only. The remedy then passed through two stages: total abstinence in the 1830s, and campaigns in the 1840s to have legislatures prohibit the sale of alcohol outright.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that runs the chain in order and uses two dates. The strongest answers state that revival taught that every person could choose salvation and that conversion was an act of the will, that the will that chose the good in the soul could choose against the sins of a society, and that converts organized, Finney's Rochester converts into temperance, moral reform, and antislavery societies after 1831, the temperance pledge of 1826 hardening toward prohibition, Mann's school reports from 1837 to 1848, and Dix's Memorial of 1843.
