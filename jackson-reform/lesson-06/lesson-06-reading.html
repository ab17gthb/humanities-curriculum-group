Revival and Reform: The Second Great Awakening

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. A Revival Sweeps the Country

The Second Great Awakening began around 1800 and ran for four decades. It opened on the frontier, where Methodist and Baptist preachers rode circuits through scattered settlements and Presbyterian ministers joined them at outdoor camp meetings that lasted for days. At Cane Ridge, Kentucky, in August 1801, thousands gathered in the woods to hear preacher after preacher, and people wept, shouted, and fell to the ground under conviction of sin. The revival then moved east and into the towns. Its teaching marked a break with the older Calvinism many of its preachers had inherited. Salvation was not a fate sealed before birth for a chosen few. It was offered to every person, and every person could choose it, or refuse it, now.

B. Finney and the New Measures

Charles Grandison Finney gave the eastern revival its method. A lawyer converted in 1821, he preached across western New York before his meetings at Rochester, on the Erie Canal, in 1830 and 1831 drew the town's merchants, lawyers, and workers into months of nightly services. Finney taught that conversion was not a wonder to wait for. In his Lectures on Revivals of Religion, published in 1835, he wrote that religion is the work of man, something for man to do, and that a revival is the result of the right use of the appropriate means, as a crop follows sowing. His means were new measures, protracted meetings night after night, preaching in plain speech aimed at a decision, and the anxious bench, where sinners under conviction sat before the congregation to be prayed for by name.

C. The Burned-Over District

Western and central New York felt the revival so often that preachers named it the burned-over district, a country swept by fire so many times that no fuel was left to burn. The Erie Canal ran through it, and the canal towns, Rochester, Utica, and Syracuse among them, filled with new people, new money, and new anxiety in a single generation. In that district revival did not end at conversion. It kept generating movements. Abolition societies, temperance crusades, and women's rights organizing all drew members there, and new religious movements rose there too, among them the Mormons, founded after Joseph Smith published his book at Palmyra in 1830. A region burned over by preaching became the seedbed of reform.

D. From Conversion to Reform

The step from revival to reform was built into the preaching. A convert had not only escaped punishment. He or she had chosen the good with the will, and the same will, preachers taught, could choose against the sins a whole society practiced. If people could be perfected by effort, then the institutions around them could be improved by effort too, and leaving them unimproved became its own kind of sin. Finney organized his Rochester converts into societies for temperance, for moral reform, and against slavery. Women, who formed the majority of converts in town after town, staffed those societies, circulated their petitions, raised their money, and learned in them the organizing skills every later movement in this unit will use.

E. Temperance, the First Crusade

Reformers fixed on drink first. Americans of the 1820s drank more alcohol than Americans have before or since, by the standard estimates, much of it whiskey sold cheap from frontier distilleries. Drunkenness filled the reformers' reports with ruined wages, beaten wives, neglected children, and workers unfit for the steady hours the new factories demanded. In 1826 ministers and laymen in Boston founded the American Temperance Society to fight drink with a signature. Members signed a pledge, at first against ardent spirits only, and promised one another a public standard to live by. Through the 1830s the pledge hardened into total abstinence, and by the 1840s temperance campaigners were asking legislatures to prohibit the sale of alcohol outright, carrying a revival method, the signed pledge, toward law.

F. Mann and the Common Schools

Horace Mann took the same logic into the schoolhouse. A Massachusetts lawyer and legislator, he became secretary of the state's new Board of Education in 1837 and held the post for eleven years, arguing in annual report after annual report that tax supported common schools, open to every child of a district, were the republic's best instrument for making citizens. Mann demanded more than schoolhouses. He wanted trained teachers, graded classes, longer terms, and state supervision, so that a child's schooling no longer depended on a district's wealth or indifference. In his Twelfth Annual Report, in 1848, he called education the great equalizer of the conditions of men and the balance wheel of the social machinery, arguing that schooling spread evenly would draw property after it and keep a republic of rich and poor from hardening into castes.

G. Dix and the Insane

Dorothea Dix carried reform to people who could not sign a pledge or sit in a schoolroom. A Massachusetts teacher, she taught a Sunday school class in the East Cambridge jail in 1841 and found insane men and women locked in unheated cells among the criminals. For nearly two years she inspected jails and almshouses across Massachusetts and wrote down what she saw, town by town. Her Memorial to the Legislature of Massachusetts, presented in January 1843, told the lawmakers that insane persons were confined in cages, closets, cellars, stalls, and pens, chained, naked, beaten with rods, and lashed into obedience. The legislature enlarged the state asylum at Worcester, and Dix carried the same method, inspect, record, and petition, to state after state, building the first generation of American public asylums.
