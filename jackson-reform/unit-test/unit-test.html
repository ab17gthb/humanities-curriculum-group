Part C: Sources (10 points)

Answer each question in complete sentences, citing the sources by letter.

Source A

President Andrew Jackson, the Bank Veto Message of July 10, 1832, vetoing the recharter of the Second Bank of the United States. From Andrew Jackson, 'Bank Veto Message,' July 10, 1832 (Yale Avalon Project; from Richardson's Messages and Papers of the Presidents, 1897; punctuation modernized). Cuts are marked with ellipses in brackets.

“[...] It is to be regretted that the rich and powerful too often bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes. [...] Distinctions in society will always exist under every just government. Equality of talents, of education, or of wealth can not be produced by human institutions. [...] There are no necessary evils in government. Its evils exist only in its abuses. [...] If it would confine itself to equal protection, and, as Heaven does its rains, shower its favors alike on the high and the low, the rich and the poor, it would be an unqualified blessing. [...]”

A1. (1 point) Jackson writes that equality in three things can not be produced by human institutions. Name one of the three.

A2. (2 points) State what government would be, in the passage's phrase, if it confined itself to equal protection, and state how it would shower its favors.

Source B

The South Carolina Convention, the Ordinance of Nullification, adopted November 24, 1832. From South Carolina Convention, 'Ordinance of Nullification,' 1832 (Paul Leicester Ford, ed., The Federalist, 1898; via the Yale Avalon Project; capitalization modernized). Cuts are marked with ellipses in brackets.

“[...] We, therefore, the people of the State of South Carolina, in Convention assembled, do declare and ordain [...] that the several acts and parts of acts of the Congress of the United States, purporting to be laws for the imposing of duties and imposts on the importation of foreign commodities, and now having actual operation and effect within the United States, and, more especially, an act entitled "An act in alteration of the several acts imposing duties on imports," approved on the nineteenth day of May, one thousand eight hundred and twenty-eight and also an act entitled "An act to alter and amend the several acts imposing duties on imports," approved on the fourteenth day of July, one thousand eight hundred and thirty-two, are unauthorized by the Constitution of the United States, and violate the true meaning and intent thereof and are null, void, and no law, nor binding upon this State, its officers or citizens. [...] And it shall be the duty of the Legislature to adopt such measures and pass such acts as may be necessary to give full effect to this Ordinance, and to prevent the enforcement and arrest the operation of the said acts and parts of acts of the Congress of the United States within the limits of this State. [...]”

B1. (2 points) State the two tariff acts the ordinance names by their approval dates, and state what the ordinance declares those acts to be.

B2. (2 points) State the duty the ordinance lays on the state legislature, in the passage's own words for its two purposes.

Source C

Elizabeth Cady Stanton and the Seneca Falls Convention, the Declaration of Sentiments, adopted July 19 and 20, 1848. From Elizabeth Cady Stanton and the Seneca Falls Convention, 'Declaration of Sentiments,' July 19, 1848 (National Park Service; punctuation normalized). Cuts are marked with ellipses in brackets.

“We hold these truths to be self-evident; that all men and women are created equal; that they are endowed by their Creator with certain inalienable rights; that among these are life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness; that to secure these rights governments are instituted, deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed. [...] The history of mankind is a history of repeated injuries and usurpations on the part of man toward woman, having in direct object the establishment of an absolute tyranny over her. To prove this, let facts be submitted to a candid world. [...] He has never permitted her to exercise her inalienable right to the elective franchise. [...] He has compelled her to submit to laws, in the formation of which she had no voice. [...] He has made her, if married, in the eye of the law, civilly dead. [...] He has taken from her all right in property, even to the wages she earns. [...] He has monopolized nearly all the profitable employments, and from those she is permitted to follow, she receives but a scanty remuneration. [...]”

C1. (1 point) The declaration says man has never permitted woman to exercise her inalienable right to what?

C2. (2 points) State two things the passage says man has done to a married woman, in the passage's own phrases about her standing in the eye of the law and her property.

Part D: Essay

The essay is scored on the six part holistic scale below, whichever prompt the student chose.

Score 5. A clear judgment is stated and held through the essay. At least three pieces of specific evidence from the unit are used accurately, the strongest point on the other side is stated fairly and answered, and the writing is organized in paragraphs with a conclusion that follows from the evidence.

Score 4. A clear judgment is stated and mostly held. Two or three pieces of specific evidence are used accurately, the other side is stated and answered, and the organization holds, with minor lapses in precision or development.

Score 3. A judgment is stated but drifts, or the evidence is specific in places and general in others. The other side is mentioned but answered thinly, or the answer to it is asserted rather than argued.

Score 2. The essay retells the removal story or the Seneca Falls story without a judgment that the evidence is made to serve, or the judgment is stated but supported by only one accurate piece of evidence. The other side does not appear.

Score 1. The response is a fragment, a single point restated, or evidence is used inaccurately in ways that change its meaning. Little of the prompt is answered.

Score 0. The response is blank, off the prompt, or copies the prompt without answering it.

For Prompt 1, strong evidence includes the Removal Act passed by five votes in the House and framed as an exchange of lands; Jackson's message defending removal as benevolence; the Cherokee constitution, newspaper, petitions, and delegations under John Ross; Marshall's holding in Worcester that Georgia law had no force in Cherokee country, defied by Georgia and unenforced by the president; the Treaty of New Echota signed by a minority without their government's authority, protested by nearly the whole nation, and ratified by one vote; and the roundup and winter march of 1838 and 1839, in which roughly a quarter of some sixteen thousand people died. Evidence on the other side includes the legal forms observed at every step, act, ratified treaty, and appropriation, and the negotiated removals of nations such as the Chickasaw, while the Seminole war shows where consent was refused outright. (Blueprint 4)

For Prompt 2, strong evidence includes the declaration modeled on the founding text, holding all men and women created equal and charging a history of repeated injuries and usurpations; the grievances against coverture, the married woman civilly dead, her property and wages taken, colleges and profitable employments closed; the franchise resolution carried by a narrow margin with Douglass arguing for it, the only resolution not adopted unanimously; the Grimké sisters' lectures and letters of the 1830s, which had made the argument in public a decade earlier; and the New York Married Women's Property Act of April 1848, proof that a legislature could already be moved. Evidence on the other side includes the narrow margin itself, the seventy two years the franchise demand would wait, and the fact that a declaration changes no statute by itself. Either prompt can earn full credit argued from either side of its question, provided the judgment is earned from the evidence and the other side is answered. (Blueprint 15)
