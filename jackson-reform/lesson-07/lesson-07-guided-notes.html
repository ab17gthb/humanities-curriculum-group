Guided Notes: Abolition Turns Immediate

Lesson question: What changed when abolitionists stopped asking gradually, and how did the country answer?

Unit question: What did democracy mean in Jackson's America, who was it for, and what did the era's reformers claim it still owed?

Key Terms

abolition: the movement to end slavery in the United States, which in the 1830s turned from the gradual emancipation and colonization schemes of the founding generation to the demand that slavery, as a sin, end immediately and without compensation to the enslaver

Walker's Appeal: the pamphlet David Walker, a free Black clothing dealer of Boston, published in 1829 and smuggled into the South, which called on enslaved people to resist their enslavement and warned white Americans that their professions of freedom stood condemned by their practice

William Lloyd Garrison: the Boston editor who founded The Liberator in 1831, renounced gradualism and colonization in its first issue, and built the American Anti-Slavery Society into the organizational engine of immediatist abolition

immediatism: the abolitionist doctrine of the 1830s that slavery, being a sin against God and man, must end at once, without gradual stages, compensation, or the removal of the freed people, the doctrine that split the antislavery movement from its colonizationist past

Nat Turner: the enslaved preacher of Southampton County, Virginia, who in August 1831 led the deadliest slave rebellion in United States history, in which about sixty white people were killed, after which Virginia and the southern states tightened their slave codes and ended open debate on emancipation

gag rule: the rule by which the House of Representatives from 1836 to 1844 tabled every antislavery petition without printing, reading, or debate, which former president John Quincy Adams, serving in the House, fought year after year as a violation of the right of petition until its repeal

Frederick Douglass: the Maryland fugitive who escaped slavery in 1838, was discovered as an orator by the Massachusetts Anti-Slavery Society in 1841, and published his Narrative in 1845, a first person account whose literacy and argument answered the slaveholders' claim that the enslaved could not speak for themselves

Summary table. The old answer: gradual emancipation paired with colonization asked enslavers to give up little now and Black Americans to give up their country; by 1830 the census counted just over two million enslaved people and slavery was growing in the cotton kingdom. The turn in print: Walker addressed Black readers directly in 1829 and called enslaved people to judge and resist their condition; Garrison recanted gradual abolition on January 1, 1831 and contended for immediate enfranchisement; the American Anti-Slavery Society, founded in December 1833, organized the doctrine. Southampton: Turner's rising of August 21 and 22, 1831 killed about sixty white people; Virginia debated ending slavery openly for the last time that winter, voted it down, and the southern states tightened their codes. The petitions: hundreds of thousands of signatures reached Congress; the House tabled them unread under the gag rule from 1836 to 1844, and John Quincy Adams fought the rule as a violation of the right of petition until its repeal.
