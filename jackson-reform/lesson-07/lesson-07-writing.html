Writing Task: What Changed When the Asking Stopped

The prompt. What changed when abolitionists stopped asking gradually, and how did the country answer? Answer in a developed essay of about 300 words, argued from the records in The Immediatism File and from the reading. A strong answer does more than list the new abolitionists. It states what the demand itself became, it follows one witness, Grimké or Douglass, through what that witness changed about who could argue, and it answers the strongest claim on the other side: that a movement this small, and this hated in both sections, changed nothing beyond its own newspapers.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The old answer: gradual emancipation paired after 1816 with colonization, the removal of free Black people to Liberia. By 1830 the census counted just over two million enslaved people, and slavery was growing in the cotton kingdom.

2. Walker, 1829: a free Black Boston clothing dealer addressed the colored citizens of the world directly, judged American slavery worse than the bondage of Egypt or Rome, and asked of enslavers' authority, 'Have we any other master but Jesus Christ alone?' The pamphlet traveled south sewn into clothing.

3. Garrison, January 1, 1831: in The Liberator's first issue he recanted gradual abolition as timidity, injustice, and absurdity, asked pardon of God, his country, and the enslaved, and contended for the immediate enfranchisement of the slave population. The American Anti-Slavery Society followed in December 1833.

4. Southampton, August 21 and 22, 1831: Nat Turner led the rising in which about sixty white people were killed. Virginia debated ending slavery openly for the last time that winter, voted it down, and the southern states tightened their codes against preaching, assembly, and print.

5. The machinery and the gag, 1835 to 1844: the Society flooded the mails and sent petitions by the hundreds of thousands. Southern postmasters seized the mail. The House tabled petitions unread under the gag rule from 1836 until John Quincy Adams won repeal in December 1844.

6. The witnesses: Grimké's Appeal of 1836 carried immediatism to southern women from inside the slaveholding household, asking them to read, pray, speak, and act. Douglass's Narrative of 1845, chapter VII, showed an enslaved boy trading bread for reading lessons and judged that slavery had turned his mistress's tender heart to stone.

7. The changed defense: mobs dragged Garrison through Boston in 1835 and killed Lovejoy at Alton in 1837. In 1837 Calhoun told the Senate slavery was a positive good, and its defenders stopped promising it would ever end.

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your claim, develop it with your evidence, answer the other side in full, and end by restating what the turn decided and what it left to the next generation.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that names both the change in the demand and the country's answer, not a claim that abolitionists were brave or that the era was divided in general. (Blueprint 13, 14)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, drawn from at least three different records, including Walker (bank 2) and one witness record (bank 6). Dates and figures must be right: 1829; January 1, 1831; December 1833; August 21 and 22, 1831; about sixty killed; the gag rule 1836 to 1844. (Blueprint 13, 14)
  • One witness followed through the change that witness worked: Grimké carrying the argument inside the slaveholding household to the women of the South, or Douglass answering the claim that the enslaved could not speak for themselves with a literate first person narrative. An essay that treats the Narrative as a general adventure story, or Grimké as a generic reformer, cannot earn full credit under this prompt. (Blueprint 13, 14)
  • The other side answered from the record. The strongest form of the answer grants what is true in the claim, that abolitionists were a hated minority and freed no one by pamphlet, and then shows what the records register: the South ended its open debate and rebuilt its public defense from necessary evil to positive good, Congress spent eight years gagging petitions rather than ignoring them, and the argument's terms after the 1830s were the terms the immediatists had set.

Model opening for teacher reference. When abolitionists stopped asking gradually, the question changed its tense. Walker in 1829 and Garrison in 1831 did not request a slower slavery or a kinder one; they demanded its immediate end as a sin, without stages, payment, or removal, and the witnesses who followed, a Charleston daughter writing to southern women and a fugitive writing his own literacy into evidence, made the demand in voices gradualism had no way to answer. The country answered with codes, a gag, and mobs, and in doing so conceded the turn's largest point: slavery's defenders stopped promising an ending. A full model continues in this pattern, carries one witness through the change that witness worked, answers the claim that a hated minority changed nothing, and closes on the new terms the argument would run on.

Common weak answers. A heroism claim (the abolitionists spoke truth to power) never names the recantation's terms or a single date, and cannot use the bank. A dismissal claim (nothing changed until the Civil War) ignores that Virginia's last open debate, the gag rule's eight years, and Calhoun's new defense are themselves changes, registered in law, and dated inside the lesson. An essay that begins the movement with Garrison and skips Walker has missed who spoke first, and the prompt's sequence is part of its argument.
