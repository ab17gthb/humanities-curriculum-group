Abolition Turns Immediate

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Old Answer: Gradual, and Gone

For two generations after independence, most white Americans who opposed slavery expected it to end slowly. The founding generation's plan, where it had one, was gradual emancipation, freedom granted by stages across decades, often paired after 1816 with colonization, the American Colonization Society's scheme to remove free Black people to Liberia, in Africa. By 1830 the census counted just over two million enslaved people, and slavery, far from fading in the cotton kingdom, was spreading and enriching the men who held slaves. The gradual answer had had fifty years. In the 1830s a new generation of abolitionists withdrew it, and said so in print.

B. Walker Speaks First, 1829

The first voice of the turn was a free Black man's. David Walker, a Boston dealer in used clothing, born free in North Carolina to an enslaved father and a free mother, published his Appeal in September 1829, addressed to the colored citizens of the world. Walker argued that American slavery was worse than the bondage of ancient Egypt or Rome, and he called on enslaved people to judge their condition and resist it. Sailors carried the pamphlet south sewn into clothing. Southern states answered with laws against its circulation, and Georgia's governor asked Boston to suppress it. Walker died in 1830, his Appeal already at work. A Black writer had spoken first, directly to Black readers, and had named patience as part of the machinery of bondage.

C. Garrison Recants, 1831

On January 1, 1831, William Lloyd Garrison published the first issue of The Liberator in Boston. Its opening editorial, 'To the Public,' was a public recantation. Garrison confessed that on July 4, 1829, he had assented to the popular doctrine of gradual abolition, and he asked pardon of God, his country, and the enslaved for a sentiment he now called full of timidity, injustice, and absurdity. In its place he contended for the immediate enfranchisement of the slave population, on the Declaration's ground that all men are created equal. The doctrine had a name, immediatism: slavery, being a sin, must end at once, without stages, payment, or removal. In December 1833 Garrison and his allies founded the American Anti-Slavery Society in Philadelphia to carry the doctrine into organization.

D. Southampton, August 1831

Eight months after The Liberator first appeared, Southampton County, Virginia, supplied the event by which many white Americans would judge the new abolition. Nat Turner, an enslaved preacher who had learned to read as a child and who believed signs in the heavens had appointed him, led a rising on the night of August 21 and 22, 1831. About sixty white people were killed before militia and armed residents crushed the rebellion in two days. Retaliation killed many more Black people, most of whom had taken no part. Turner was captured in October and hanged on November 11. Virginia then held the South's last open legislative debate on ending slavery, in the winter of 1831 and 1832, and voted it down. The southern states tightened their slave codes instead, against preaching, assembly, and print.

E. Organization, Print, and the Petitions

The immediatists built machinery to match their doctrine. Local antislavery societies multiplied across the North, more than a thousand by the late 1830s, their members working people, farmers, and, in great numbers, women, who ran the fairs, sewing circles, and petition drives. In 1835 the American Anti-Slavery Society flooded the mails with abolitionist print, and southern postmasters, with the Postmaster General's consent, let the sacks be seized and burned. Petitions by the hundreds of thousands went to Congress, many carrying women's signatures gathered door to door. In May 1836 the House answered with the gag rule, tabling every antislavery petition without printing, reading, or debate. Former president John Quincy Adams, now a Massachusetts congressman, fought the gag year after year as a violation of the right of petition, until the House repealed it in December 1844.

F. Women and Witnesses

The movement's witnesses widened beyond its first editors. Angelina Grimké, who had left her family's slaveholding household in Charleston, published her Appeal to the Christian Women of the South in 1836, asking southern women to read, pray, speak, and act, as the wives, mothers, sisters, and daughters of the men who made slavery's laws. With her sister Sarah she lectured to audiences of women, and then of men and women together. In 1845 Frederick Douglass, who had escaped slavery in Maryland in 1838 and had been discovered as an orator in 1841, published his Narrative. Its seventh chapter told how he learned to read after his mistress was forbidden to teach him, trading bread to poor white boys for lessons. Its literacy was itself an argument no proslavery claim could survive unchanged.

G. The Country Answers

The country answered immediatism with more than argument. Mobs broke up antislavery meetings across the North. In October 1835 a Boston crowd seized Garrison and dragged him through the streets with a rope before the mayor jailed him for his own safety. In November 1837 a mob at Alton, Illinois, killed the abolitionist editor Elijah Lovejoy defending his press. In the South, the defense of slavery changed its ground. Where an earlier generation had called slavery a necessary evil, John C. Calhoun told the Senate in 1837 that it was a positive good, and a literature grew to argue the point. Abolitionists remained a minority, hated in both sections. But they had moved the question. After the 1830s, slavery's defenders no longer promised it would end. They promised to keep it.

5. Answer the lesson question, with the change, the witnesses, the country's answer, and a judgment. Strong answers name the change first. Abolitionists stopped asking for slavery's slow end and demanded its immediate end as a sin, without payment or removal: Walker spoke that demand first, in 1829, directly to Black readers; Garrison recanted gradualism in print in 1831 and built the American Anti-Slavery Society on the new doctrine; and witnesses such as Angelina Grimké, who carried the demand to southern women in 1836, and Frederick Douglass, whose 1845 Narrative answered proslavery claims with an enslaved man's own literacy, widened who could make the argument. The country answered with the gag rule of 1836, which tabled antislavery petitions unread for eight years, with mobs that dragged Garrison through Boston in 1835 and killed Lovejoy at Alton in 1837, and with a southern defense that moved from necessary evil to Calhoun's positive good of 1837. Judged from the reading, the movement set the new terms: after the 1830s the argument was no longer about when slavery would end gradually, because its defenders had stopped promising an end at all.
