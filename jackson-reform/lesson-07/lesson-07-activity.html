The Immediatism File

Directions: Work through the five stations in order. Each station holds one record from abolition's turn to immediatism, or from the answer it met. For each record, state what it demanded, who it addressed, and what it risked. At the end, judge which record did most to end the era of gradual asking, and defend the judgment from the records.

Part 1: Five Stations

Station 1: Walker's Appeal, 1829

Source: David Walker, Walker's Appeal, 1829, as carried in the audited Primary Source Project bank record (Project Gutenberg; from the 1848 Henry Highland Garnet reprint). Quoted with cuts, marked by ellipses and disclosed here. Illustration: 'Am I not a man and a brother?', a woodcut emblem print published by the American Anti-Slavery Society, New York, 1837; Library of Congress, public domain. No likeness of David Walker exists from his lifetime, and none is shown in this lesson.

"... we, (colored people of these United States) are the most degraded, wretched, and abject set of beings that ever lived since the world began ... They tell us of the Israelites in Egypt, the Helots in Sparta, and of the Roman Slaves ... whose sufferings under those ancient and heathen nations were, in comparison with ours, under this enlightened and christian nation, no more than a cypher ..."

"I appeal to heaven for my motive in writing, who knows that my object is, if possible, to awaken in the breasts of my afflicted, degraded and slumbering brethren, a spirit of enquiry and investigation respecting our miseries and wretchedness in this Republican Land of Liberty!!!!!"

"Are we MEN!! I ask you, O my brethren! are we MEN? Did our creator make us to be slaves to dust and ashes like ourselves? ... Have we any other master but Jesus Christ alone?"

Walker was a free Black dealer in used clothing in Boston, born free in North Carolina. His Appeal was printed in Boston in 1829 and carried south by sailors, sewn into clothing bundles.

State what this record demanded. Walker addresses Black readers, not white ones. From the text, state the judgment he asks his brethren to make about their condition, and the comparison he draws between American slavery and the slavery of the ancient world.

State what this record risked. The Appeal was smuggled south in clothing and answered there with laws against its circulation. Using the reading, state why a pamphlet addressed to enslaved and free Black readers frightened southern authorities more than a petition addressed to Congress would have.

Station 2: Garrison, 'To the Public,' January 1, 1831

Source: William Lloyd Garrison, 'To the Public,' The Liberator, Boston, January 1, 1831, as carried in the audited Primary Source Project bank record (VIVA Pressbooks, American Literature I). Quoted with cuts, marked by ellipses and disclosed here.

"... I shall strenuously contend for the immediate enfranchisement of our slave population. In Park-street Church, on the Fourth of July, 1829, in an address on slavery, I unreflectingly assented to the popular but pernicious doctrine of gradual abolition. I seize this opportunity to make a full and unequivocal recantation, and thus publicly to ask pardon of my God, of my country and of my brethren the poor slaves, for having uttered a sentiment so full of timidity, injustice and absurdity. ..."

"I am aware, that many object to the severity of my language; but is there not cause for severity? I will be as harsh as truth, and as uncompromising as justice. ... I am in earnest, I will not equivocate, I will not excuse, I will not retreat a single inch, AND I WILL BE HEARD."

This editorial opened the first issue of Garrison's newspaper. He had arrived at immediatism after reading and printing Black abolitionists, Walker among them.

State what this record demanded. Name the doctrine Garrison recants, the three parties he asks pardon of, and the demand he puts in gradualism's place, in his own words.

State what this record risked. Garrison refuses moderation as a matter of principle. From the second passage, state the tone he promises his readers and his opponents, and why a public recantation, printed in his own paper's first issue, made retreat harder for him than a private change of mind would have.

Station 3: The Confessions of Nat Turner, 1831

Source: The Confessions of Nat Turner (Baltimore: published by Thomas R. Gray, 1831), as transcribed in the electronic edition at Documenting the American South, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. Quoted with cuts, marked by ellipses and disclosed here. Read the mediation on this record's face before you quote it: Turner spoke these words in a jail cell after his capture, and Thomas R. Gray, a Southampton lawyer, took them down, published them, held the copyright, and sold the pamphlet. Gray states in his own preface that he committed Turner's statements to writing 'with little or no variation, from his own words.' That claim is Gray's. No likeness of Nat Turner exists from his lifetime, and none is shown in this lesson.

Gray's preface: "I determined ... to commit his statements to writing, and publish them, with little or no variation, from his own words."

Turner, as Gray prints him: "SIR, ... You have asked me to give a history of the motives which induced me to undertake the late insurrection, as you call it ... I was thirty-one years of age the 2d of October last, and born the property of Benj. Turner, of this county."

"And my father and mother strengthened me in this my first impression, saying in my presence, I was intended for some great purpose ..."

"The manner in which I learned to read and write, not only had great influence on my own mind, as I acquired it with the most perfect ease, so much so, that I have no recollection whatever of learning the alphabet ..."

Turner led the rising of August 21 and 22, 1831, in Southampton County, in which about sixty white people were killed. He was hanged on November 11, 1831. Gray's pamphlet is the record through which his account survives, and it is the only kind of record of him there is.

State what this record shows. From Turner's account as printed, name two facts about his formation that the pamphlet itself records, one about what his parents and neighbors said of him as a child and one about how he learned to read.

State what this record risks as evidence. The words reach us through the man who prosecuted the case against Turner's world and sold the account. State what Gray claims about his transcription, and one reason a careful reader holds Turner's motives at one remove even while using this record.

Station 4: Douglass, Narrative, Chapter VII, 1845

Source: Frederick Douglass, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave (Boston: Anti-Slavery Office, 1845), chapter VII, as transcribed in Documenting the American South, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. Quoted with cuts, marked by ellipses and disclosed here.

"I lived in Master Hugh's family about seven years. During this time, I succeeded in learning to read and write. In accomplishing this, I was compelled to resort to various stratagems. I had no regular teacher."

"Slavery proved as injurious to her as it did to me. ... Slavery soon proved its ability to divest her of these heavenly qualities. Under its influence, the tender heart became stone, and the lamblike disposition gave way to one of tiger-like fierceness. The first step in her downward course was in her ceasing to instruct me."

"This bread I used to bestow upon the hungry little urchins, who, in return, would give me that more valuable bread of knowledge."

Douglass is describing Baltimore in the years after 1826. His mistress, Sophia Auld, had begun teaching him the alphabet before her husband forbade it, on the ground that learning would make an enslaved person unfit for slavery.

State what this record shows. Douglass's mistress began his instruction and then was made to stop it. From the text, state what slavery did to her, in his judgment, and name the first step of the change he records.

State what this record demanded of its readers. Douglass taught himself with bread traded for lessons, and published the account under his own name in 1845. Using the reading, state which proslavery claim a literate first person narrative by a fugitive answered simply by existing, and why immediatists put such narratives in print.

Station 5: Angelina Grimké, Appeal to the Christian Women of the South, 1836

Source: Angelina E. Grimké, An Appeal to the Christian Women of the South (New York: American Anti-Slavery Society, 1836), as transcribed in Project Gutenberg eBook #9915. Quoted with cuts, marked by ellipses and disclosed here.

"It is because I feel a deep and tender interest in your present and eternal welfare that I am willing thus publicly to address you. ... I ask you now, for the sake of former confidence, and former friendship, to read the following pages in the spirit of calm investigation and fervent prayer."

"I have attempted to vindicate the Abolitionists, to prove the entire safety of immediate Emancipation, and to plead the cause of the poor and oppressed."

Grimké wrote as a daughter of Charleston who had left her family's slaveholding household. Her readers were southern women, the wives, mothers, sisters, and daughters of the men who made slavery's laws, and she asked them to read, pray, speak, and act.

State what this record demanded. Grimké claims the right to be heard on the ground of former friendship and shared faith. From the text, state what she asks her readers to do with her pages first, before any action, and the three things she says the Appeal attempts.

State what this record risked. A white southern woman addressed southern women in print, by name, in defense of abolitionists. Using the reading, state why the movement valued a witness from inside the slaveholding household, and what such a witness stood to lose that a Boston editor did not.

Teacher Answer Key

Station 1: Walker's Appeal, 1829

Demanded. Walker asks his brethren to judge their own condition: colored people in the United States are, in his words, the most degraded, wretched, and abject set of beings that ever lived, and American slavery is worse than the bondage of Egypt, Sparta, or Rome, exceeding it so far that the ancient sufferings are no more than a cypher beside it. His question, 'Are we MEN,' and his claim that the enslaved have no master but Jesus Christ, withdraw obedience itself. He writes to awaken in his brethren a spirit of enquiry and investigation respecting their miseries in this Republican Land of Liberty.

Risked. A petition asks power for a hearing and accepts refusal. The Appeal bypassed white gatekeepers entirely, addressed the enslaved and free Black readers directly, and traveled in sailors' clothing into ports where its possession was evidence. Southern authorities answered with circulation laws, literacy laws, and Georgia's appeal to Boston to suppress it, because a text that taught enslaved people to judge their condition threatened slavery's daily government, not only its reputation.

Station 2: Garrison, 'To the Public,' 1831

Demanded. Garrison recants the popular but pernicious doctrine of gradual abolition, to which he had assented on July 4, 1829, and asks pardon of his God, of his country, and of his brethren the poor slaves for a sentiment full of timidity, injustice, and absurdity. In its place he contends strenuously for the immediate enfranchisement of the slave population. Students should note the recantation's form: a public confession, in print, on the first page of his own first issue.

Risked. He promises to be as harsh as truth and as uncompromising as justice, not to equivocate, excuse, or retreat a single inch, and to be heard. A recantation published cannot be quietly withdrawn; it made Garrison's own past the exhibit against gradualism and staked his paper's survival on the harsher doctrine. The risk ran the other way as well, and strong answers note it: the same refusal of moderation let opponents paint the whole movement by its editor's tone.

Station 3: The Confessions of Nat Turner, 1831

Shows. As printed, Turner records that his parents and neighbors said in his hearing that he was intended for some great purpose, strengthening the impression from his childhood, and that he learned to read and write with the most perfect ease, with no recollection of learning the alphabet. His account presents a literate, religious, deliberate man, formed in slavery's own county, not the ignorant instrument of outside agitators that proslavery accounts preferred.

Risks, as evidence. Gray claims he committed Turner's statements to writing with little or no variation from his own words. A careful reader holds the record at one remove because Gray chose the questions, the order, and the words on the page, held the copyright, and sold the pamphlet to a public he also addressed in a preface arguing for stricter enforcement of the slave laws. The Confessions is evidence of what Turner said only through evidence of what Gray printed. Teach the mediation as the station's main lesson, and keep the rising itself, about sixty white people killed on August 21 and 22, 1831, stated plainly and without decoration.

Station 4: Douglass, Narrative, Chapter VII, 1845

Shows. Douglass judges that slavery proved as injurious to Sophia Auld as to him: it divested her of her heavenly qualities, her tender heart became stone, and her lamblike disposition gave way to tiger-like fierceness. The first step in her downward course was her ceasing to instruct him. The chapter's argument runs through the enslaver's corruption as well as the enslaved boy's stratagems.

Demanded. The Narrative answered, simply by existing under its author's name, the slaveholders' claim that the enslaved could not speak for themselves. A fugitive's literate, argued, first person account, down to the bread traded to hungry little urchins for the more valuable bread of knowledge, made literacy itself evidence. Immediatists printed such narratives because testimony from inside slavery could not be answered by the gradualist's timetable.

Station 5: Grimké's Appeal, 1836

Demanded. Grimké first asks her readers to read: to take up her pages in the spirit of calm investigation and fervent prayer, on the ground of former confidence and friendship. She states the Appeal's three attempts herself: to vindicate the Abolitionists, to prove the entire safety of immediate Emancipation, and to plead the cause of the poor and oppressed. Her full program for southern women, read, pray, speak, and act, follows from the reading she demands here.

Risked. The movement valued a witness from inside the slaveholding household because she could not be dismissed as a northern stranger ignorant of slavery; she had grown up in the house. What she stood to lose, and lost, was her home standing: family, church, and city in Charleston, where the Appeal was treated as an enemy's work. A Boston editor risked subscribers and safety. Grimké risked the whole of her former life, and students should weigh testimony priced that way accordingly.

Part 2: The Verdict (Grading Guide)

Full credit verdicts define the ending they mean, use at least three stations, and answer the strongest case for a rival record. The strongest case for Walker is priority and audience: he spoke first, in 1829, to Black readers, and Garrison's own road to immediatism ran through Black abolitionist print. The strongest case for Garrison is organization: the recantation converted a doctrine into a paper, a society, and a petition machinery that ran for decades. The strongest case for Douglass is proof: the Narrative made gradualism's premise, that the enslaved were not yet ready to speak or be free, unreadable after 1845. The strongest case for Grimké is penetration: she alone of the five addressed slavery's own household in its own language of faith and kinship. Turner's Confessions is the record strong verdicts handle carefully: the rising, not the pamphlet, is what contemporaries answered with codes and the end of open debate, and any verdict resting on it must carry Gray's mediation with it. Verdicts that praise courage in general, or that quote a station's most famous sentence without its audience and risk, earn partial credit.
