## Subjects for which no likeness is used, recorded honestly
- David Walker: no likeness of Walker exists from his lifetime. None is shown in any document or in the deck, none was invented, and the activity and deck notes say so on their faces. Walker is present in this lesson in his printed words, as he was in 1829.
- Nat Turner: no likeness of Turner exists from his lifetime. Later prints that claim to show him are inventions or show other men, and none is used here. Turner is present through the Confessions record, with Thomas Gray's mediation stated wherever the record is used.
- Enslavement's violence: no image of violence is used as decoration anywhere in this lesson. The documents state death tolls in plain words, in the readings and in the Turner key, and the only visual of the institution is the Society's emblem print, an appeal image made by abolitionists themselves.

## Quoted texts and their editions
All period quotations in this lesson are drawn from the audited Primary Source Project bank file jackson-reform.json or from the named transcriptions below, and each document's Source line names the edition used:
- David Walker, Walker's Appeal, 1829, bank record walker-appeal-1829, whose sourceNote names Project Gutenberg, from the 1848 Henry Highland Garnet reprint. Quoted at Activity Station 1, with cuts marked by ellipses and disclosed in the Source line.
- William Lloyd Garrison, "To the Public," The Liberator, January 1, 1831, bank record garrison-to-the-public-1831, whose sourceNote names VIVA Pressbooks, American Literature I. Quoted at Activity Station 2 and in the quick check, with cuts marked by ellipses and disclosed in the Source lines.
- The Confessions of Nat Turner (Baltimore: published by Thomas R. Gray, 1831), as transcribed in the electronic edition at Documenting the American South, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill; the transcription was checked during this build against the full text of the 1831 Baltimore edition. Quoted at Activity Station 3, with cuts marked by ellipses and disclosed. The station states Gray's mediation on its face: Turner spoke in jail after capture, and Gray took the words down, published the pamphlet, held the copyright, and sold it, claiming in his preface to have written with little or no variation from Turner's own words.
- Frederick Douglass, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave (Boston: Anti-Slavery Office, 1845), chapter VII, as transcribed in Documenting the American South, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill; the wording quoted was verified during this build against two further transcriptions of the 1845 edition (Wikisource and the Frederick Douglass Papers Project), which agree word for word in the passages used. Quoted at Activity Station 4, with cuts marked by ellipses and disclosed.
- Angelina E. Grimké, An Appeal to the Christian Women of the South (New York: American Anti-Slavery Society, 1836), as transcribed in Project Gutenberg eBook #9915. Quoted at Activity Station 5, with cuts marked by ellipses and disclosed. This source is quoted from a named edition; it is not a teaching summary.
- No labeled teaching summary is used in this lesson's activity: all five stations quote named editions or audited bank records.
- No period text is quoted from memory anywhere in this lesson.

## Build note on the unit skeleton
The skeleton file on disk for this unit, jackson-reform-skeleton.md, ends partway through Lesson 3, so the Lesson 7 section was not present to read during this build.
