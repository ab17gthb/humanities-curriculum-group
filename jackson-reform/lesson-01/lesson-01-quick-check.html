Quick Check: Office Is Not Property

Directions: Read the passage and the background, then answer the questions.

Office is considered as a species of property, and government rather as a means of promoting individual interests than as an instrument created solely for the service of the people. ... The duties of all public officers are, or at least admit of being made, so plain and simple that men of intelligence may readily qualify themselves for their performance; and I can not but believe that more is lost by the long continuance of men in office than is generally to be gained by their experience.

Source: Andrew Jackson, First Annual Message to Congress, December 8, 1829, in James D. Richardson, editor, A Compilation of the Messages and Papers of the Presidents (Washington, 1896). Cuts are marked with ellipses.

Background. Jackson wrote the message in the first year of his presidency, after the election of 1828 had been decided by the widest white male electorate the country had yet seen. The states had spent two decades removing property limits on white men's voting. In Washington, most federal offices were held by men appointed years earlier. Jackson's administration removed some nine hundred of roughly nine thousand federal civil officers in his first years, a share his supporters defended as rotation in office and his opponents attacked as the spoils system, from a Senate phrase holding that to the victor belong the spoils.

1. In the passage, what has office come to be considered, and what does Jackson say government is, set against that habit?

2. State the claim Jackson makes about the duties of public officers, and the comparison he draws between long continuance in office and experience.

3. Using the background, give two facts: one about the electorate that sent Jackson to Washington, and one about the scale of the removals that followed. State what each fact shows about the doctrine.

4. An opponent argues that the passage is a defense of handing offices to party workers. State the strongest answer to that argument from the passage itself, and name the one point on which the passage gives the opponent ground.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What has office come to be considered, and what is government, set against it? Office is considered as a species of property. Government, in Jackson's phrase, is an instrument created solely for the service of the people, not a means of promoting individual interests. Credit requires both phrases. (Blueprint 1)

2. State the claim about the duties, and the comparison. The duties of all public officers are, or at least admit of being made, so plain and simple that men of intelligence may readily qualify themselves for their performance. The comparison: more is lost by the long continuance of men in office than is generally to be gained by their experience. (Blueprint 1)

3. Give two facts, one about the electorate and one about the removals, and what each shows. The electorate: Jackson was sent by the widest white male electorate yet seen, after two decades in which the states removed property limits on white men's voting; it shows the doctrine of open office was the governing form of the same widening that had made the voters. (Blueprint 1) The removals: some nine hundred of roughly nine thousand federal civil officers, defended as rotation and attacked as the spoils system; it shows the doctrine's practice was modest beside its legend, and that the argument of the era was over the principle, not the count. (Blueprint 2)

4. State the strongest answer from the passage, and the point it concedes. The answer: the passage argues a principle about office itself, that office held long becomes property and diverts government to the support of the few, and it asks Congress for a general law limiting appointments to four years, a rule for every officeholder and not a favor for any worker. The conceded point: the passage's own claim, that the duties are plain and simple enough for any man of intelligence, removes competence as a defense of the men being replaced, and an opponent can fairly ask who judges the intelligence of the replacements if not the winning party. (Blueprint 2)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Questions 1 and 2 test whether students can read the message for its doctrine, the skill Station 1 of the activity grades. Question 3 carries both of the lesson's blueprint entries, the widened electorate for Blueprint 1 and the spoils controversy for Blueprint 2. Question 4 predicts success on the writing task, because it requires separating a doctrine from the charge made against its practice.
