Guided Notes: The Election of 1828 and Jacksonian Democracy

Lesson question: What changed when the common white man got the vote, and who was still left outside it?

Unit question: What did democracy mean in Jackson's America, who was it for, and what did the era's reformers claim it still owed?

Key Terms

Andrew Jackson: the Tennessee general and seventh president, hero of New Orleans and victor in the elections of 1828 and 1832, whose presidency widened white men's democracy while it removed Native nations, broke the national bank, and strengthened the presidency itself

Jacksonian democracy: the political movement of the 1820s and 1830s that celebrated the common white man, widened the vote, attacked privileged institutions such as the national bank, and held that the president, chosen by the majority, should govern energetically in the people's name

corrupt bargain: the charge Jackson's supporters leveled at the election of 1824, that Henry Clay threw the House of Representatives vote to John Quincy Adams in return for appointment as secretary of state, a charge both men denied and Jacksonians rode to victory in 1828

suffrage: the right to vote, which state after state extended in the 1820s until nearly every white man could vote regardless of property, while women, Native Americans, and most free Black men remained excluded

spoils system: the practice of filling government offices with the winning side's supporters, named by Jackson's opponents for the claim that to the victor belong the spoils, and defended by Jacksonians as rotation that kept office from hardening into property

rotation in office: Jackson's doctrine that public offices should pass regularly from citizen to citizen rather than become the possession of a permanent officeholding class, which he argued no man had any more right to hold than another

Democratic Party: the party that formed around Andrew Jackson out of the election of 1828, joining southern planters, western farmers, and northern workingmen behind white manhood suffrage, strict limits on federal power, and suspicion of banks and chartered privilege

Teacher Answer Key

For the teacher

Teach this lesson first in the Jackson and Reform unit, before Indian Removal. It sets the electorate, the party, and the doctrine of rotation that Lessons 2 to 4 test against removal, nullification, and the Bank War. It pairs with the lesson reading and with the activity, The People's President File, whose five stations carry the First Annual Message, Margaret Bayard Smith's inauguration letter, and the suffrage and exclusion records. Prior knowledge: students need the Early Republic unit's ground, the elections and parties of the 1790s, the Electoral College and the House choice of the president, and the idea of suffrage as a state question. Standards: Common Core literacy in history and social studies (cite textual evidence, determine central ideas, and analyze a sequence of events) and the C3 Framework, Dimension 2, History (change and continuity, and the uses of evidence), stated state neutrally for teachers to map to their own frameworks. Students will learn how the white male electorate was made before Jackson ran, what the corrupt bargain charge was and why it worked unproven, and how the widening of the vote ran alongside the exclusion of women, free Black men, and Native nations. Timing: one period for the reading and these notes, a second period for the activity and writing task. Materials: the reading, these notes, the activity file, and the notebook page.

Summary table. The electorate remade: state after state removed property and taxpaying limits until by 1828 nearly every white man could vote, and parties answered with committees, newspapers, and turnout organization; the gainers were white men as a class. The doors that closed: women voted nowhere, New Jersey closed its once open vote to white men only in 1807, New York fenced free Black men with a property test in 1821, and Native nations stood outside the polity. 1824, the bargain, and 1828: Jackson led in 1824, the House chose Adams after Clay's support, Adams named Clay secretary of state, and the unproven corrupt bargain charge powered four years of organizing and the mass campaign of 1828, which cost Rachel Jackson her reputation in print and, Jackson believed, her life. Rotation and the party: Jackson's First Annual Message argued office was not property and should rotate among citizens, opponents named the practice the spoils system, and the Democratic Party took lasting shape around the movement.

Exit ticket 1. Between the 1810s and 1828 the states struck down the property and taxpaying limits on voting until nearly every white man could vote regardless of property.

Exit ticket 2. No evidence of a bargain between Adams and Clay has ever been produced, and both men denied it. The charge still worked because it fit the new electorate's experience: a first place finisher had lost in a House vote in Washington, and four years of committees and newspapers carried that story to voters who turned out in 1828 to reverse it.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that names the uncoupling of the white male vote from property, the doctrine of rotation in office stated in the First Annual Message of 1829 (with the spoils system as its opponents' name), and two groups left outside, drawn from women, free Black men losing the vote, Native nations, and enslaved people.
