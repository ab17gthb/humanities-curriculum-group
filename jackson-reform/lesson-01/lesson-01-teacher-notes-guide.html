Teacher Notes

Lesson 1 · The Election of 1828 and Jacksonian Democracy

A guide sheet for teaching the deck, in slide order. The slides carry the main ideas in a few sentences each; the teaching is here, in the form you can use while the slide is on the screen. Talking points are the content of the slide in bullets, with the votes and figures pulled out on their own lines so you can find and say them. Picture names what an image shows and what to point at. Ask is a question worth posing aloud, with the shape of a good answer. Note is enrichment: detail beyond the student reading, for the questions students ask you. Bracketed lines are delivery cues. Vocabulary definitions match the student guided notes word for word.

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image, Robert Cruikshank's 1829 print The President's Levee, or All Creation Going to the White House.

Picture: Robert Cruikshank's print of 1829, The President's Levee, or All Creation Going to the White House, held by the Library of Congress. It shows the crowd of inauguration day pressing toward the president's house, men and women of every dress and condition drawn as a satire by a hostile hand.

Ask: Hold the print in front of students and ask who these people thought the house belonged to. The lesson answers that question in order.

  • The states first remade the electorate by striking down property limits on white men's voting. The election of 1824 supplied a grievance, the campaign of 1828 a mass party, and the First Annual Message a doctrine of office.
  • The limits were drawn in the same years, and the lesson never lets them out of the frame.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: What changed when the common white man got the vote, and who was still left outside it? Unit question: What did democracy mean in Jackson's America, who was it for, and what did the era's reformers claim it still owed?

  • Read the question slowly, because its two halves are the whole lesson.
  • The first half asks what changed when the common white man got the vote: the vote left property behind, turnout became the test of a campaign, parties built committees and newspapers to reach men never courted before, and the president those voters chose declared that office itself should rotate among citizens.
  • The second half asks who was still left outside: women everywhere, free Black men losing ballots they had cast, Native nations outside the polity, and the enslaved people of the president's own plantation.
  • Students should hear both halves before any content, because every section that follows belongs to one half or holds the two together. Keep the question visible as the lesson moves, and return to it at the close.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: A new electorate and its boundaries, in seven stretches · A The electorate remade · B The doors that closed · C 1824 and the corrupt bargain · D The campaign of 1828 · E The inauguration as portrait · F Rotation and the spoils · G A wider democracy, and its limits

  • This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section, so students can use the letters on screen to find their place in their notes all hour.
  • Section A gives the electorate remade before the candidate, state by state. Section B gives the doors that closed as others opened, in New Jersey, New York, and Pennsylvania. Section C gives the election of 1824 and the corrupt bargain charge. Section D gives the campaign of 1828 and the attacks on Rachel Jackson.
  • Section E gives the inauguration in Margaret Bayard Smith's two scenes. Section F gives rotation in office and the spoils. Section G closes with the party formed and the limits stated whole. Tell students the letters are their filing system for everything that follows today.

Slide 4 · Section A: The Electorate Remade Before the Candidate (divider)

On the slide: The Electorate Remade Before the Candidate · The vote changed first. The candidate came second.

  • Begin before Jackson, because the lesson's first claim is that the electorate made the candidate possible, not the other way round.
  • In the early republic a vote was tied to a freehold or a tax receipt, on the stated theory that a man without a stake in the community could be bought or commanded by the man who employed him.
  • Between the 1810s and 1828 the property tests fell in one state after another, and by the time Jackson ran his second campaign, nearly every white man in the country could cast a ballot. The section that follows names the states and the years where the change can be dated.

Ask: What does that theory assume about the poor man's judgment? Let students say it plainly before you tell them the states abandoned it: that a man who works for wages has no independent judgment of his own, so his ballot would only repeat his employer's.

Slide 5 · The property tests fall, state by state

On the slide: New states enter with white manhood suffrage · Connecticut 1818, Massachusetts 1820 · New York removes its test for white men, 1821 · By 1828 the tests survive in only a few states

  • Work the items as a sequence, because sequence is the argument.
  • New states came into the union with white manhood suffrage in their constitutions, Indiana, Illinois, Alabama among them, and voters there never knew a property test.
  • Older states then rewrote their own rules rather than be outvoted in reputation: Connecticut in 1818, Massachusetts in 1820, and New York, the largest state, removing its property test for white men in 1821 and completing the change in 1826.
  • Teach the direction of travel: suffrage widened from the frontier inward. By 1828 the tests survived for white men in only a few states, Rhode Island, Virginia, and Louisiana among them, and the exception had become the oddity. Students should copy the state and year pairs into section A of their notes.

Slide 6 · Parties learn to organize the new voters

On the slide: County committees keep the lists · Party newspapers, passed hand to hand · Barbecues, parades, hickory poles · Turnout becomes the measure of a campaign

  • A wider electorate is only paper until somebody organizes it, and organizing is the craft this section names.
  • County committees kept lists of voters and saw them to the polls. Party newspapers, printed cheap and passed hand to hand, carried one version of events into taverns and workshops. Rallies, barbecues, and parades made politics a public season rather than a courthouse errand.
  • Teach students to connect the machinery to the man: Jackson's fame from New Orleans gave the organization a name every voter already knew, and the organization gave the fame a way to become ballots. Neither half wins 1828 alone.
  • The next slide puts a number on what the machinery delivered. The machinery was partisan from its first day, and both parties built it.

Slide 7 · 3x turnout

On the slide: 3x — the turnout of 1828 measured against the turnout of 1824 · More men eligible · More men reached · More men carried to the polls

  • Let the comparison carry the section. Turnout in the presidential election of 1828 ran near three times the turnout of 1824, the standard comparison historians use for the arrival of mass politics in the United States. The figure is a ratio, not a count, because the two elections are the cleanest before and after the suffrage changes and the organizing that followed them.
  • Then set the limit beside the number before leaving it: the tripled turnout was a turnout of white men, and section B begins exactly there.

Ask: What had to be true on the ground for three times as many ballots to be cast? A good answer names a piece of section A for each part: more men eligible, more men reached, more men carried to a poll.

Slide 8 · Section B: The Doors That Closed as Others Opened (divider)

On the slide: The Doors That Closed as Others Opened · The exclusions were enacted in the same decades as the widening.

  • Section B is the counterweight, and it should be taught at the same weight as section A, not as a footnote to it.
  • The claim is precise: the exclusions of this era were not leftovers waiting for a later generation to clear away. They were enacted, in statutes and constitutions, by the same generation that widened the white vote, sometimes in the same document that widened it. New York's convention of 1821 freed the white man's vote and fenced the Black man's in the same settlement.
  • Teach students to hold the two motions in one hand, widening and narrowing, because the unit's essential question cannot be answered from either motion alone. Nothing in this section is an afterthought, and it should not be taught as one.

Slide 9 · Three closings, in date order

On the slide: New Jersey 1807: the vote closed to white men only · New York 1821: a property test for Black men alone · Pennsylvania 1838: white written before freemen · Native nations outside the polity entirely

  • Work the three cases in date order.
  • New Jersey is the strangest and the most instructive: its constitution of 1776 gave the vote to all inhabitants worth fifty pounds, and for thirty years some women and some free Black men voted under it, until a disputed election gave the legislature its occasion and the law of 1807 limited the vote to free white male citizens.
  • New York in 1821 removed the property test for white men and set a property test for free Black men alone, two hundred fifty dollars in a freehold.
  • Pennsylvania closed the sequence in 1838, writing the word white before freemen in a new constitution. Native nations were never in the count at all: their peoples stood outside the polity as members of separate nations.

Slide 10 · 1807: the vote taken away

On the slide: 1807 — the year New Jersey took the vote from people who had cast it · Women and free Black men had voted under the constitution of 1776 for thirty years

  • Pause on the year, because 1807 teaches the direction of the era better than any summary. This was not the founding generation's oversight surviving into a more generous age.
  • In 1807 the age was already generous to white men, and New Jersey's legislature used a modern law to take ballots away from neighbors who had cast them within living memory, women who held property in their own names among them. The closing was deliberate, public, and contested at the time.
  • Students sometimes ask when Americans began arguing about who the people were. This lesson's answer is that the argument was never absent: it ran alongside every widening, and the wideners and the closers were frequently the same voters in the same decade.

Slide 11 · Section C: 1824 and the Cry of Corrupt Bargain (divider)

On the slide: 1824 and the Cry of Corrupt Bargain · Mathew Brady photograph of Henry Clay, taken about 1849, late in his life.

Picture: Mathew Brady's photograph of Henry Clay, taken about 1849, two decades after the events of this section. Say so to students: our clearest likeness of Clay comes from the end of his life.

  • Section C begins with the election of 1824, in which four candidates divided the country and its electoral vote.
  • Jackson's grievance was real in its facts and unproven in its charge, and students must learn to hold those two judgments separately.
  • The sequence on the next slide is the documented record, established from the returns and the House journal, and no part of it depends on what any man intended. What Clay intended is unknowable, and the lesson teaches only what he did.

Slide 12 · First in the votes, short of a majority

On the slide: Jackson 99 electoral votes, Adams 84 · No majority: the House chooses, by state · Clay, fourth and out, backs Adams · Adams elected February 9, 1825 · Clay named secretary of state in March

  • Teach the figures as the House saw them.
  • Jackson took 99 electoral votes, Adams 84, Crawford 41, and Clay 37, and no man held the majority of 131 the Constitution requires.
  • The contest moved to the House of Representatives, where each state delegation cast one vote and only the top three finishers stood, which put Clay, the Speaker of the House, out of the contest and in command of its outcome.
  • Clay declared for Adams, whose views on government he shared and whose rival he distrusted, and on February 9, 1825, the House elected Adams with thirteen state delegations. In March, Adams named Clay secretary of state.
  • Every step was constitutional. That is what made the charge that followed so durable: the people had watched the forms of the Constitution overturn the first place finisher.

Slide 13 · The record and the charge are different things

On the slide: Documented: returns, House vote, appointment · Charged: the office promised for the support · Denied by both men, never proven · Rode by Jacksonians to victory in 1828

  • Now separate the record from the charge, because the whole lesson turns on the separation.
  • The charge, cried in Jackson newspapers for four years, was that Clay had sold his support in advance for the secretaryship, the office then read as the stepping stone to the presidency. Both men denied any bargain. No letter, witness, or admission has ever established one, and historians teach the episode with the charge labeled a charge.
  • It fit what the new voters could see: a plurality winner set aside in Washington rooms. The corrupt bargain was truer to the new politics than to the facts, and it built the party that won 1828. That gap between charge and proof is the habit of mind the whole unit practices.

Ask: Why did an unproven charge outperform the documented record? A good answer goes to the fit: the charge matched what the new electorate already suspected about Washington, and it gave four years of grievance a single sentence.

Slide 14 · Section D: The Campaign of 1828 (divider)

On the slide: The Campaign of 1828 · Ralph Earl's portrait of Rachel Jackson, the campaign's hardest target.

Picture: Ralph Earl's Rachel Jackson, painted in the years of her husband's rise, showing the woman the campaign of 1828 would put at the center of its hardest attacks.

  • Section D treats the campaign as the new politics practiced without restraint: organization and spectacle on one side of the ledger, personal destruction on the other.
  • Rachel Donelson's first marriage had ended in a tangled divorce, final only after she and Jackson had married believing her free, and the couple remarried once the decree was certain. Adams newspapers revived the story in 1828 in its ugliest form.
  • Teach the episode soberly and exactly, because its consequence belongs on the next slide. Jackson never forgave the men he blamed, and he said so for the rest of his life.

Slide 15 · The new politics, in the open and in the mud

On the slide: Committees, barbecues, hickory poles · A party press praises the people's candidate · Coffin handbills and duels from the other side · Rachel attacked in print over her first marriage · Jackson wins wide, and Rachel dies in December

  • Work the campaign as two inventories.
  • The organization: committees in counties and towns, hickory poles planted in squares for Old Hickory, barbecues and parades that made voting a festival, and a party press that printed the general as the people's candidate and the administration as a corrupt circle.
  • The mud: coffin handbills listing men Jackson had executed or killed, attacks on his temper and his duels from the Adams side, and from both sides a willingness to print what a later age would call private.
  • The result was a wide Jackson victory in the popular vote and the Electoral College. Then give the cost its own sentence. Rachel Jackson died in December 1828, weeks after the voting, and her husband went to Washington a widower who believed the attacks had killed her.

Slide 16 · Section E: The Inauguration as Portrait (divider)

On the slide: The Inauguration as Portrait · Cruikshank's President's Levee print of 1829: all creation going to the White House.

Picture: The portrait crop of Cruikshank's President's Levee print, the crowd pressing on the White House.

  • Section E is built on one witness, Margaret Bayard Smith, a leader of Washington society who had known every administration since Jefferson's and wrote her account in a letter of March 1829, preserved in The First Forty Years of Washington Society, published in 1906 under Gaillard Hunt's editorship.
  • Smith gives the lesson two scenes from a single day, the crowd at the Capitol and the crowd in the president's house, and readers have quoted whichever scene suited them ever since.
  • Teach the two scenes as one account by one observer, because the tension inside the letter is its evidence. Her letter survives because her family papers were published three generations later.

Slide 17 · Smith's two crowds on one day

On the slide: At the Capitol: silent, orderly and tranquil · Without distinction of rank, in an immense mass · At the White House: glass and china broken · The president driven out a back way · Her close: the People would rule

  • Take the scenes in Smith's order.
  • At the Capitol she saw thousands and thousands of people, without distinction of rank, collected in an immense mass, silent, orderly and tranquil, waiting for the president in the portico, and she called the sight sublime.
  • At the White House reception the same people pressed after Jackson through the rooms, broke cut glass and china worth thousands of dollars in the scramble for refreshments, and drove the president out a back way to his lodgings at Gadsby's.
  • Smith's sentence for the second scene is the one students must weigh: the Majesty of the People had disappeared, and a rabble, a mob, had taken its place. Then read her closing sentence aloud: it was the People's day, and the People's President and the People would rule. Smith feared and recorded the same fact.

Slide 18 · Section F: Rotation in Office and the Spoils (divider)

On the slide: Rotation in Office and the Spoils · Thomas Sully's portrait of Andrew Jackson, the seventh president.

Picture: Thomas Sully's Andrew Jackson, the president the First Annual Message introduces in his own words.

  • Section F takes up the doctrine Jackson laid before Congress on December 8, 1829, and its text is in the activity as Station 1, quoted from Richardson's Messages and Papers of the Presidents.
  • The doctrine answers a question the election had posed: if the people rule, why should the government's offices stay filled by the men of the last administration and the last generation? Jackson's answer was rotation: offices are public trusts, not property, and they should pass among citizens the way the vote itself had passed beyond the propertied few.
  • The next slide gives the argument in its own order. The doctrine also suited the party's needs, and students may note both truths together.

Slide 19 · Office is not property

On the slide: Long office breeds indifference to the public · The duties are plain and simple to learn · More is lost by continuance than gained · His proposal: appointments limited to four years

[Quote the argument's three steps.]

  • First, long officeholding corrupts the holder's relation to the public: few men, Jackson wrote, can enjoy office and power for long without looking with indifference on the public interest, until office is considered as a species of property.
  • Second, the work does not require a permanent class: the duties of public officers are so plain and simple that men of intelligence may readily qualify themselves for their performance.
  • Third, the balance favors rotation: more is lost by the long continuance of men in office than is gained by their experience. His proposal followed the argument: extend the law limiting appointments to four years.
  • Students should notice the form: a democratic premise about office, argued as policy, addressed to Congress.

Slide 20 · 900 officers removed

On the slide: 900 — officers removed out of some 9,000 in the first years · Modest beside the legend · Enough to establish the principle of rotation

  • Let the count correct the legend in both directions. The removals of Jackson's first years came to some nine hundred of roughly nine thousand federal civil officers by the usual count, about one officeholder in ten, postmasters making up most of the number.
  • That is far less than the sweeping purge the phrase spoils system suggests, and students should say so. It is also nine hundred livelihoods and nine hundred appointments, enough to establish the principle that a change of administration changes the officers.
  • The name came from the other side: Senator William Marcy of New York, defending the practice in 1832, said the victors saw nothing wrong in the rule that to the victor belong the spoils, and opponents made the phrase the system's permanent title.

Slide 21 · Section G: A Wider Democracy, and Its Limits (divider)

On the slide: A Wider Democracy, and Its Limits · A party takes lasting shape, and its hero holds enslaved people.

  • Section G gathers the lesson into its final shape.
  • Out of the campaign of 1828 the Democratic Party took lasting form, joining southern planters, western farmers, and northern workingmen in one organization behind white manhood suffrage, strict limits on federal power, and suspicion of banks and chartered privilege. It was the first American party built to win a mass white electorate, and its machinery outlasted its founder.
  • Set the founder inside his own limits before the synthesis: Jackson was a Tennessee cotton planter whose Hermitage wealth rested on enslaved labor, a slaveholder carried to power by a movement preaching the equality of white men. The era's democracy and its exclusions had the same address. The synthesis on the next slide answers in the same two columns as the question.

Slide 22 · Changed, and left outside

On the slide: Changed: the vote uncoupled from property · Changed: a mass party and a doctrine of rotation · Outside: women, free Black men, Native nations · Outside: the enslaved people of the Hermitage

[Build the synthesis as the answer to the lesson question, in the question's own order.]

  • What changed: the vote was uncoupled from property for white men, turnout and organization became the currency of politics, a plurality grievance from 1824 became a governing party, and the president that party elected declared office itself a trust to rotate among citizens rather than a property to keep.
  • Who was left outside: women in every state, free Black men stripped of ballots in New Jersey and Pennsylvania and fenced in New York, Native nations outside the polity altogether, and the enslaved people whose labor supported the president's own house.
  • Hold both columns up together and do not let either one fall. Neither column is the lesson alone, and students should be marked on holding both.

Slide 23 · Vocabulary: the general, the movement, the charge

On the slide: Andrew Jackson · Jacksonian democracy · corrupt bargain

[Have students copy the definitions exactly as they appear in the guided notes, because the unit test draws on them.]

  • Andrew Jackson: the Tennessee general and seventh president, hero of New Orleans and victor in the elections of 1828 and 1832, whose presidency widened white men's democracy while it removed Native nations, broke the national bank, and strengthened the presidency itself
  • Jacksonian democracy: the political movement of the 1820s and 1830s that celebrated the common white man, widened the vote, attacked privileged institutions such as the national bank, and held that the president, chosen by the majority, should govern energetically in the people's name
  • corrupt bargain: the charge Jackson's supporters leveled at the election of 1824, that Henry Clay threw the House of Representatives vote to John Quincy Adams in return for appointment as secretary of state, a charge both men denied and Jacksonians rode to victory in 1828

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: the vote, the system, the doctrine, the party

On the slide: suffrage · spoils system · rotation in office · Democratic Party

[Have students copy the definitions exactly as they appear in the guided notes.]

  • suffrage: the right to vote, which state after state extended in the 1820s until nearly every white man could vote regardless of property, while women, Native Americans, and most free Black men remained excluded
  • spoils system: the practice of filling government offices with the winning side's supporters, named by Jackson's opponents for the claim that to the victor belong the spoils, and defended by Jacksonians as rotation that kept office from hardening into property
  • rotation in office: Jackson's doctrine that public offices should pass regularly from citizen to citizen rather than become the possession of a permanent officeholding class, which he argued no man had any more right to hold than another
  • Democratic Party: the party that formed around Andrew Jackson out of the election of 1828, joining southern planters, western farmers, and northern workingmen behind white manhood suffrage, strict limits on federal power, and suspicion of banks and chartered privilege

Slide 25 · Now your turn: the people's president file

On the slide: The people's president file · Activity, notebook, quick check, and writing for the lesson · Exit ticket

  • The activity, The People's President File, runs five stations through the evidence: the First Annual Message, Smith's inauguration letter, the widening suffrage summary, the corrupt bargain record, and the exclusion record of 1807 and 1838, ending in a ledger of standing gained and lost and a verdict on whether 1828 deserves its name as the birth of democratic politics in America.
  • The notebook page asks students to rebuild six pieces of the story with evidence. The quick check tests the First Annual Message passage against its background. The writing task weighs the widening against the boundary and closes on Smith's sentence.
  • Exit ticket, three parts: the suffrage change stated in one sentence; the bargain charge explained as unproven and decisive; and the lesson question answered with the change, the doctrine, and two groups left outside.
