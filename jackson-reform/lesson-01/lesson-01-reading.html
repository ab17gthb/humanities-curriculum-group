The Election of 1828 and Jacksonian Democracy

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Electorate Remade Before the Candidate

The vote came first, and the candidate came second. In the early republic most states limited voting to white men who owned property or paid taxes, on the theory that only a man with a stake in the community could judge independently. Between the 1810s and the late 1820s, state after state struck those limits down, new states entering with white manhood suffrage in their constitutions and older states rewriting theirs to match, until by 1828 nearly every white man could vote whether he owned an acre or rented a room.

Politics changed shape to fit the new electorate. Turnout became the measure of a campaign. Parties built county committees, printed newspapers by the thousand, and carried their case to voters never asked before.

B. The Doors That Closed as Others Opened

The widening had a boundary, and the same generation drew it. Women voted nowhere. New Jersey had allowed property holding women and free Black men to vote under its first constitution, and in 1807 its legislature closed the vote to white men only. Free Black men lost the right in state after state or saw it fenced with property tests set for them alone, as New York did in 1821. Native nations stood outside the polity entirely, treated as separate peoples whose members were not citizens.

For white men, standing no longer depended on land. For everyone else, the line hardened at exactly the moment it softened for white men. The expansion and the exclusion were parts of one settlement, not two separate stories.

C. 1824 and the Cry of Corrupt Bargain

Four candidates split the election of 1824. Andrew Jackson, the Tennessee general who had won the Battle of New Orleans in 1815, finished first in the popular and electoral votes but held no majority in the Electoral College, so the House chose among the top three. Henry Clay, out of the running himself, threw his support to John Quincy Adams. The House made Adams president, and Adams named Clay secretary of state.

Jackson's supporters called the result a corrupt bargain, a deal in which Clay had sold the presidency for a cabinet seat. Both men denied it, and no evidence of a bargain has ever been produced. The charge did its work anyway. For four years Jackson's organizers carried it through the new electorate as proof that a small circle in Washington could overturn the people's choice.

D. The Campaign of 1828

The campaign of 1828 was the new politics in the open. Jackson's committees staged barbecues and parades, planted hickory poles in town squares, and fed a press that praised the general as the people's candidate. Adams's supporters answered in kind, and each side dug into the other's private life. Newspapers revived the tangled record of Rachel Jackson's first marriage and printed her as unfit for the White House.

Jackson won by a wide margin. Rachel Jackson died in December 1828, between the election and the inauguration, and her husband believed the attacks had killed her. He arrived in Washington in March 1829 a widower, leading a movement convinced that the government had been stolen from the people in 1824 and returned four years later.

E. The Inauguration as Portrait

Thousands came to Washington for March 4, 1829, and stood in silence at the Capitol while Jackson took the oath, a crowd Margaret Bayard Smith described as collected without distinction of rank. At the White House reception that followed, the crowd pressed through the rooms after him, broke glass and china in the scramble for refreshments, and drove the new president out a back way to his lodgings. Smith set the two scenes side by side, and readers have used her letter as evidence for both Jacksons ever since.

Both readings saw the same fact. People who had never entered the president's house believed it was theirs to enter, because the election had told them the government belonged to the majority.

F. Rotation in Office and the Spoils

Jackson stated his doctrine in his First Annual Message of December 8, 1829. Office, he argued, had come to be treated as a species of property. The duties of public officers were plain and simple enough for men of intelligence to learn, more was lost by long continuance in office than gained by experience, and no man had more right to hold an office than another. Rotation in office was his answer.

The removals were modest beside the legend, some nine hundred of nine thousand federal civil officers by the usual count. The principle outlasted the numbers. Opponents named the practice from a Senate phrase, that to the victor belong the spoils, and the spoils system entered the language as the charge that office had become payment for party work.

G. A Wider Democracy, and Its Limits

Out of the campaign a party took lasting shape, the Democratic Party, joining southern planters, western farmers, and northern workingmen behind white manhood suffrage and suspicion of banks and chartered privilege. Its hero was a Tennessee cotton planter who held enslaved people at his Hermitage plantation. The democracy that bore his name widened the standing of white men as a class while the people he enslaved, the Native nations east of the Mississippi, the free Black men losing the vote, and all women stood outside the widening.

That double fact is the ground of this unit. The lessons that follow test the people's president against removal, the bank fight, and nullification, asking throughout what the era's democracy was, whose it was, and what it did with the power the majority gave it.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State what changed in the state voting laws, and name two things parties now had to build. State after state struck down the property and taxpaying limits that had fenced the vote, new states entering with white manhood suffrage in their constitutions and older states rewriting theirs, until by 1828 nearly every white man could vote whether he owned an acre or rented a room. To win the new voters, parties had to build county and town committees and a party press of newspapers printed by the thousand, and to organize turnout as the measure of a campaign. Credit requires the legal change and any two organizational answers.

2. Give the New Jersey and New York examples, and state where women and Native nations stood. New Jersey had allowed property holding women and free Black men to vote under its first constitution, and in 1807 its legislature closed the vote to white men only. New York in 1821 removed the property test for white men while fencing free Black men with a property test set for them alone. Women voted nowhere. Native nations stood outside the polity entirely, treated as separate peoples whose members were not citizens of the surrounding states.

3. Lay out the election of 1824 in order, and state what evidence exists for the corrupt bargain charge. Jackson finished first in the popular vote and the electoral vote but held no majority in the Electoral College, so the House of Representatives chose among the top three finishers. Henry Clay, out of the running himself, threw his support to John Quincy Adams. The House made Adams president, and Adams named Clay secretary of state, the office then understood as the stepping stone to the presidency. For the charge itself: both men denied any bargain, and no evidence of one has ever been produced. The sequence is documented; the bargain is a charge, and full credit answers keep the two apart.

4. Describe the campaign of 1828, the attacks on Rachel Jackson, and the outcome. Jackson's committees staged barbecues and parades, planted hickory poles in town squares, and fed a friendly press that praised the general as the people's candidate, while Adams's supporters answered in kind and each side dug into the other's private life. Against Rachel Jackson, newspapers revived the tangled record of her first marriage and printed her as unfit for the White House. Jackson won by a wide margin. Rachel Jackson died in December 1828, between the election and the inauguration, and Jackson believed the attacks had killed her; he came to Washington a widower.

5. What changed when the common white man got the vote, and who was still left outside it? Full credit answers hold both halves in one paragraph. What changed: the vote itself was uncoupled from property for white men, turnout and party organization became the currency of politics, and the winner of 1828 governed on a doctrine, stated in the First Annual Message of December 8, 1829, that office was not property, that its duties were plain enough for ordinary men of intelligence, and that posts should rotate among citizens, a doctrine opponents named the spoils system. Who was left outside: women everywhere, free Black men losing the vote in states that had allowed it, Native nations outside the polity, and the enslaved people held by the president himself at the Hermitage. A paragraph that celebrates the widening without the boundary, or lists the exclusions without the change in the electorate and the doctrine, earns partial credit.
