Writing Task: Why Did the War Fail on Its Own Terms?

The prompt. Answer the question in a developed essay of about 300 words: How did containment lead the United States into Vietnam, and why did the war fail on its own terms? Argue the sequence before you judge it. A strong answer sets out the road in, the domino theory, the refused elections, and the advisers, then the authority and the buildup, the planned resolution of 1964 and the force grown past half a million, and then the turn and the ending, Tet, Vietnamization, the Paris Accords, and the fall of Saigon, and weighs the whole sequence against the war's stated aim. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, drawn from both halves of the bank, and explain how each piece supports your judgment.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the Escalation Ledger. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. Vietnam was divided at the seventeenth parallel by the Geneva settlement of 1954. Elections to reunify the country, set for 1956, were never held, because Diem, expecting Ho Chi Minh to win, refused to hold them and the United States backed the refusal, reasoning from the domino theory.

2. McGeorge Bundy's draft memorandum of May 25, 1964 set out ten steps toward wider war, with a congressional resolution eighth and an initial strike against North Vietnam tenth. It stated the aim as a limit: Communism shall not take over Southeast Asia, and the United States did not intend the destruction of the Hanoi regime. (Audited TPSP record bundy-memorandum-vietnam-1964.)

3. The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution of August 1964, passed after the August 2 attack on the Maddox and a reported second attack that almost certainly never happened, gave the president authority to take all necessary measures to repel attacks on American forces and prevent further aggression. It expired only when the president determined the area was secure. (Audited TPSP record gulf-of-tonkin-resolution-1964.)

4. President Johnson, in a private telephone conversation with Bundy on May 27, 1964, said of Vietnam: "It just worries the hell out of me. I don't see what we can ever hope to get out of there with once we're committed." He closed the thought with a warning: "It's damned easy to get in a war, but it's going to be awfully hard to ever extricate yourself if you get in." (Audited TPSP record lbj-bundy-phone-conversation-1964.)

5. The Tet Offensive of January 1968 struck more than one hundred cities and towns and breached the American embassy grounds in Saigon. The attackers lost heavily and the Viet Cong never fully recovered, and the offensive still broke American belief in official claims of progress. Johnson limited the bombing and left the 1968 race on March 31.

6. President Nixon's Vietnamization address of November 3, 1969 held that the defense of freedom was particularly the responsibility of the people whose freedom was threatened, and ordered a substantial increase in the training and equipment of South Vietnamese forces as American troops withdrew. The Paris Peace Accords of January 1973 removed the last American troops and left North Vietnamese forces in the south, and Saigon fell on April 30, 1975.

7. Senator Robert F. Kennedy, writing to Professor John F. Bayliss on September 15, 1967, argued that candor was a duty in wartime: "The people of our country have a right to expect not easy answers but candid evaluation of our conduct and our position in the world." The letter is quoted at the length the audited record carries it. (Audited TPSP record rfk-vietnam-letter-bayliss-1967.)

Plan Before You Write

My judgment (one sentence that answers both halves of the question directly):

My evidence picks (bank numbers, at least one from items 1 to 4 and at least one from items 5 to 7) and what each one proves:

The strongest evidence against my judgment, and how I will answer it:

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your judgment, present the road in and the buildup, present the turn and the ending, answer the strongest evidence against your judgment in one or two sentences, and end by restating what the record shows.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment that answers both halves of the prompt directly, how containment produced the war and why it failed on its own terms, rather than narrating one half. (Blueprint 11 and Blueprint 12)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, with at least one from each half. Bank items 1 through 4 carry the road in: the division and the refused elections under the domino theory, the Bundy sequence and its stated limit, the Tonkin authority and the status of the second attack, and Johnson's private warning to himself. (Blueprint 11) Bank items 5 through 7 carry the turn and the ending: Tet's double result, Vietnamization and the Paris settlement that left the central question open, the fall of Saigon, and the contemporary demand for candid evaluation. (Blueprint 12)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the judgment. The strongest essays name the war's own terms as the standard, a standing non-Communist South Vietnam and neighbors kept from falling in sequence, and measure the ending against that standard.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest evidence on the other side, answered from the record rather than dismissed. An essay arguing failure must answer the attackers' losses at Tet and the fact that the neighboring states did not fall in sequence. An essay defending the war's conduct must answer the planned resolution, the second attack's status, and April 30, 1975.

Model opening for teacher reference. Containment led the United States into Vietnam by turning a divided country into a test of a worldwide theory, and the war failed on the terms that theory set. The Geneva division and the refused elections of 1956 made South Vietnam a standing commitment, Bundy's ten steps made wider war a plan before it was a response, and the Tonkin Resolution handed the plan a blank authority. A full model continues in this pattern, carries the sequence through Tet and Vietnamization to the Paris settlement and the fall of Saigon, answers the strongest opposing evidence, and closes on what the record shows.

Common weak answers. Buildup only (the theory, the resolution, the figures) describes how the war came without judging why it failed. Ending only (Tet and Saigon) makes the same error in reverse. A judgment with no stated standard for the war's own terms earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a failure measured against the war's stated aim, not against the writer's general view of the war.
