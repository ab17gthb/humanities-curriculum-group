Guided Notes: Vietnam: The War Containment Built

Lesson question: How did containment lead the United States into Vietnam, and why did the war fail on its own terms?

Unit question: How did the struggle between the United States and the Soviet Union reshape the world and American life, and why did it end the way it did?

Key Terms

domino theory: the belief, stated publicly by President Eisenhower in 1954, that if one country fell to communism its neighbors would fall one after another like a row of dominoes

Viet Cong: the Communist guerrilla forces and political organization in South Vietnam, supplied and directed from North Vietnam, that fought the South Vietnamese government and its American allies

Gulf of Tonkin Resolution: the resolution Congress passed in August 1964 giving the president authority to take all necessary measures to repel attacks on American forces and prevent further aggression in Southeast Asia

escalation: the steady enlargement of a military commitment step by step, as the American ground force in Vietnam grew from advisers to more than half a million troops

Tet Offensive: the coordinated attacks by North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces across South Vietnam in January 1968, a military defeat for the attackers that shook American confidence in official claims of progress

Vietnamization: President Nixon's policy of withdrawing American troops while building up South Vietnam's forces to carry the fighting themselves

draft lottery: the system begun in 1969 that chose the order in which young men were called for the draft by a random drawing of birth dates

Summary table. Division and refusal: the Geneva settlement divided Vietnam at the seventeenth parallel and Diem, backed by the United States, refused the 1956 reunification elections; an insurgency opened in the south and the commitment became permanent. The resolution: the Bundy memorandum planned a congressional resolution as step eight of ten in May 1964, and Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution that August on the August 2 attack and a reported second attack that almost certainly never happened; the president held authority to take all necessary measures, with no declaration of war. Escalation: Rolling Thunder and ground troops from March 1965 carried the force past 184,000 by the end of 1965 and past half a million by 1968; progress was measured by body counts and the draft fell hardest on the poor and the young. Tet and the turn: the January 1968 offensive lost heavily on the ground and broke American belief in official progress claims; Johnson limited the bombing and left the 1968 race. Vietnamization and after: American troops withdrew while the war widened into Cambodia and Laos, four students were killed at Kent State, and the draft became a lottery; the Paris Accords of 1973 removed the last American troops without settling who governed South Vietnam, and Saigon fell on April 30, 1975.

Exit ticket 1. The memorandum shows the resolution was planned in advance. Bundy's draft of May 25, 1964 listed a congressional resolution as the eighth of ten steps toward wider war, months before the Tonkin reports gave Congress its reason to pass one.

Exit ticket 2. About sixteen thousand advisers at the end of 1963, more than 184,000 troops by the end of 1965, and more than half a million by 1968.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that pairs a buildup fact with an ending fact. The strongest answers run from the domino theory through the 1964 resolution and the growth past half a million, and close on the fall of Saigon in 1975 and the neighbors that did not fall, concluding that a strategy built to hold a line in Asia ended by consuming the commitment it was meant to limit.
