Vietnam: The War Containment Built

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Road In: Division and the Dominoes

At Dien Bien Phu in May 1954 French forces surrendered to Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh, ending the French fight to hold Indochina. The Geneva settlement that summer divided Vietnam at the seventeenth parallel, with Ho's Communists in the north and Ngo Dinh Diem, backed by the United States, in the south. Elections to reunify the country were set for 1956.

The elections never came. Diem, expecting Ho to win, refused to hold them, and Washington backed the refusal, reasoning from the domino theory, the belief, stated publicly by President Eisenhower in 1954, that if one country fell to communism its neighbors would fall one after another like a row of dominoes. The Viet Cong, the Communist guerrilla forces and political organization in South Vietnam, supplied and directed from North Vietnam, that fought the South Vietnamese government and its American allies, opened an insurgency against Diem.

B. The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution

Advisers sent under Eisenhower grew under President Kennedy to about sixteen thousand by the end of 1963. On May 25, 1964, before any crisis, National Security Adviser McGeorge Bundy sent President Johnson a draft memorandum listing ten steps toward wider war. A congressional resolution stood eighth, and an initial strike against North Vietnam tenth.

On August 2 North Vietnamese boats attacked the destroyer Maddox. A second attack, reported on August 4 against the Maddox and the Turner Joy, almost certainly never happened. Johnson ordered air strikes and went to Congress. On August 7 Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, the resolution Congress passed in August 1964 giving the president authority to take all necessary measures to repel attacks on American forces and prevent further aggression in Southeast Asia. The House voted unanimously, and two senators dissented.

C. Escalation

The resolution became the legal basis for a full scale war. From March 1965 Rolling Thunder bombing struck North Vietnam, and the first American ground combat troops came ashore at Da Nang. The force passed 184,000 by the end of 1965 and half a million by 1968. This was escalation, the steady enlargement of a military commitment step by step, as the American ground force in Vietnam grew from advisers to more than half a million troops.

Units swept villages in search and destroy operations, and commanders measured progress by body counts, the reported enemy dead. The draft supplied much of the manpower, and its calls fell hardest on the poor and the young. Johnson had told Bundy in May 1964 that getting out would prove far harder than getting in. He widened the war anyway.

D. The Tet Offensive

On January 30 and 31, 1968, during the Tet new year holiday, North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces attacked across South Vietnam. They struck more than one hundred cities and towns, held most of Hue for weeks, and breached the grounds of the American embassy in Saigon. The Tet Offensive was the coordinated attacks by North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces across South Vietnam in January 1968, a military defeat for the attackers that shook American confidence in official claims of progress. Attacker losses were heavy, and the Viet Cong never fully recovered.

Americans had been told the enemy was weakening. News showed an enemy able to attack everywhere at once. Johnson's standing collapsed. On March 31 he limited the bombing and announced that he would not seek reelection.

E. Vietnamization and the Wider War

President Nixon chose a slower exit. His policy was Vietnamization, President Nixon's policy of withdrawing American troops while building up South Vietnam's forces to carry the fighting themselves. His address of November 3, 1969 put the manpower burden on the threatened people themselves. Troop levels fell yearly.

The war widened even as the American force shrank. In 1970 Nixon sent troops into Cambodia and expanded bombing there and in Laos. Protests followed across the country, and on May 4, 1970, Ohio National Guardsmen killed four students at Kent State. The draft lottery, the system begun in 1969 that chose the order in which young men were called for the draft by a random drawing of birth dates, replaced local board choices with a televised drawing.

F. The Accords and the Fall of Saigon

Talks in Paris dragged on beside the fighting. The Paris Peace Accords of January 1973 set a ceasefire, freed American prisoners, and required the last American combat troops to leave in March. North Vietnamese troops stayed in place in the south. Who would govern South Vietnam was left to Vietnamese arms.

In the spring of 1975 North Vietnam attacked in force, and South Vietnamese units fell back, then collapsed. On April 30, 1975, North Vietnamese tanks entered Saigon as American helicopters lifted out the last embassy staff and thousands of Vietnamese. The Saigon government ceased to exist, and Vietnam was reunified under Hanoi.

G. The Costs and the Reckoning

More than 58,000 Americans died in the war. Vietnamese dead numbered in the millions, and bombing left deep damage across Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia. At home the war fed inflation, broke Johnson's presidency, and left a distrust of official statements that outlasted the fighting. Congress repealed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution in 1971 and passed the War Powers Resolution in 1973, reclaiming war power handed over in 1964.

The war had been built as containment applied to Asia. Each step, defended as necessary to keep the next domino standing, made the next step easier to take and harder to refuse. The war failed on its own terms. South Vietnam fell, and its neighbors did not fall in the predicted sequence.
