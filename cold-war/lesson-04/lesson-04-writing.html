Writing Task: Peace at the Edge

The prompt. Did massive retaliation and brinkmanship keep the peace, or did they gamble with survival? Answer in a developed essay of about 300 words, argued from the station records in The Brink File. A strong answer does more than praise or condemn the doctrine. It states what the doctrine promised, it weighs the megaton scale of the weapons behind the promise, and it answers the strongest claim on the other side. Your essay must use Eisenhower's farewell warning directly, whichever side you take.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The arsenal: Ivy Mike released an estimated 10.4 megatons in November 1952, the Soviet Union tested a thermonuclear weapon in August 1953, and Castle Bravo released an estimated 15 megatons in March 1954, far more than predicted.

2. The doctrine: Dulles, January 12, 1954. Local defenses must be reinforced by the further deterrent of massive retaliatory power, and the United States would depend primarily on a great capacity to retaliate, instantly, by means and at places of its own choosing, a maximum deterrent at a bearable cost.

3. The home front: the 1951 Duck and Cover film taught schoolchildren to duck under desks and cover the back of the neck and the face when they saw the flash, a routine built for atomic attack in a hydrogen bomb age.

4. The secret war: covert action overthrew Mossadegh in Iran in August 1953 and Arbenz in Guatemala in June 1954. The Shah fell to revolution in 1979, and Guatemala entered decades of civil war.

5. The shock: Sputnik, launched October 4, 1957, carried no weapon, but its rocket proved the Soviet Union could reach across an ocean, and the American answer built NASA and pressed the missile program forward.

6. The warning: Eisenhower, January 17, 1961. The United States had been compelled to create a permanent armaments industry of vast proportions, and the nation must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that takes one side and states the ground for it, not a claim that the doctrine was important or dangerous in general. (Blueprint 7)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, including the megaton scale of the weapons from bank item 1, stated as estimates. Bank items 2 and 3 support the doctrine and the home front. (Blueprint 7) Bank items 4 and 5 support the widening contest. (Blueprint 8)
  • A direct use of Eisenhower's farewell warning from bank item 6. An essay for the doctrine must explain why the president who built it warned against the military-industrial complex in 1961. An essay against the doctrine must reckon with the fact that Eisenhower also held the buildup necessary and kept the threats credible for eight years without a superpower war.
  • One claim from the other side answered from the record rather than dismissed. The record's dates decide most rival claims in this set, so accurate dates in the answer are a strong sign of a full credit essay.

Model opening for teacher reference. Massive retaliation kept a kind of peace, and it did so by gambling. The doctrine promised that an attack on an ally would be answered instantly, at places and with means of American choosing, and behind that promise stood weapons measured in megatons, at an estimated 10.4 megatons for Ivy Mike and an estimated 15 for Castle Bravo. A full model continues in this pattern, granting the peace its real weight, eight years without a direct superpower war, weighing the home front and covert records against it, and closing on Eisenhower's own warning as the judgment of the man who ran the system.

Common weak answers. A biggest weapon claim (the bombs were huge, so the doctrine worked) measures power without showing how a threat held at the edge stays under control. A no war claim (nothing happened, so there was no gamble) ignores Castle Bravo's unpredicted yield and the error and pride the reading names. An essay that never uses Eisenhower's warning cannot earn full credit under this prompt, because the prompt requires it.
