Brinkmanship and the Nuclear Shadow

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. An Arsenal That Could End Societies

The hydrogen bomb, a thermonuclear weapon far more powerful than the atomic bombs of 1945, first tested by the United States in 1952 and by the Soviet Union in 1953, was tested first at Enewetak Atoll in November 1952. Ivy Mike released an estimated 10.4 megatons of power and erased the island of Elugelab. The bombs of 1945 had been measured in kilotons. The new weapons were measured in megatons.

The Soviet Union answered in August 1953 with a thermonuclear test, and in March 1954 the American Castle Bravo test released an estimated 15 megatons, far more than predicted. This was the arms race, the competition between the United States and the Soviet Union to build more and more powerful weapons, each side answering the other's advances, until each side could destroy the other's society.

B. Massive Retaliation and Brinkmanship

Secretary of State John Foster Dulles announced the doctrine in January 1954. Local defenses, Dulles said, must be reinforced by the further deterrent of massive retaliation, and the country would depend primarily on a great capacity to retaliate, instantly, by means and at places of its own choosing. This was massive retaliation, the Eisenhower administration's declared policy of answering Communist aggression with the threat of a full nuclear strike, rather than matching force at the point of attack.

Dulles called the method behind the doctrine brinkmanship, the practice of pushing a dangerous confrontation to the edge of war in order to force the other side to back down, described by Secretary of State John Foster Dulles. A threat works only if the other side believes it might be carried out, even when doing so would mean catastrophe for both. Its danger was built in, because a confrontation at the edge could go over it by error or pride.

C. The Shadow at Home

The new weapons changed life at home. A 1951 Federal Civil Defense Administration film built around Bert the Turtle taught schoolchildren a routine for atomic attack. When you see the flash, the film instructed, duck and cover, under a desk or against a wall, and cover the back of your neck and your face. Schools ran drills, and some families built fallout shelters.

A desk offered real protection against flying glass at a distance, but against a hydrogen bomb near a city the routine could not deliver what its cheerfulness promised. The drills show a government making an unmanageable danger feel manageable, and children who practiced for the end of the world between arithmetic and lunch.

D. Covert Action: Iran and Guatemala

The administration also reached for a quieter weapon. Covert action, a secret government operation meant to influence events abroad while hiding the sponsor's hand, carried out in the Cold War chiefly by the Central Intelligence Agency, helped overthrow Mohammad Mossadegh, Iran's elected prime minister, in August 1953 after he nationalized the British controlled oil industry. The Shah returned to power, and the United States gained an ally on the Soviet border.

In June 1954 the pattern repeated in Guatemala, where a CIA backed force overthrew Jacobo Arbenz, the elected president whose land reform had taken land from the United Fruit Company. Both operations were fast and cheap beside a war, and both were counted as victories. Their longer record ran the other way. The Shah fell to revolution in 1979, and Guatemala entered decades of civil war.

E. Two Alliances and the Wider Contest

In May 1955 the Soviet Union formed the Warsaw Pact, the military alliance formed in 1955 joining the Soviet Union and the Communist states of Eastern Europe, created in answer to NATO and West Germany's place in it. Europe now held two armed alliances facing each other, each pledged to defend its members.

The contest also widened beyond Europe. Newly independent nations emerged across Asia and Africa as empires collapsed, and both superpowers courted and armed them. Aid and weapons flowed to governments whose aims were often local, and whose people paid the price when the rivalry turned their countries into battlegrounds.

F. Sputnik and the Space Race

Sputnik, the first artificial satellite, launched by the Soviet Union in October 1957, whose success shocked the United States and opened the space race, went into orbit on October 4, 1957. The sphere, about 58 centimeters across and weighing about 83.6 kilograms, carried no weapon, and its beeps could be heard around the world. What it proved was the power of its rocket, of the kind that could carry a warhead across an ocean.

Americans heard the beeping as evidence that their country had fallen behind. Congress answered with the National Aeronautics and Space Administration in 1958 and a missile program pressed forward at speed. The space race ran on the same rockets and the same rivalry as the arms race itself.

G. The Warning from Inside

On January 17, 1961, three days before leaving office, Eisenhower used his farewell address to warn about the machine his buildup had created. The United States, he said, had built a permanent armaments industry of vast proportions, joined to an immense military establishment, and the nation must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex.

The warning carried weight because of who gave it. Eisenhower had commanded the Allied armies in Europe and presided over this buildup himself, and he still judged that it could endanger the liberties it existed to defend. The rivalry had been pushed into threats, secrets, and contests at the edge, leaving open whether the rules that held the peace were wisdom or luck.
