Guided Notes: Brinkmanship and the Nuclear Shadow

Lesson question: How did nuclear weapons change the rules of the rivalry, and how close did brinkmanship bring the world to war?

Unit question: How did the struggle between the United States and the Soviet Union reshape the world and American life, and why did it end the way it did?

Key Terms

arms race: the competition between the United States and the Soviet Union to build more and more powerful weapons, each side answering the other's advances

hydrogen bomb: a thermonuclear weapon far more powerful than the atomic bombs of 1945, first tested by the United States in 1952 and by the Soviet Union in 1953

massive retaliation: the Eisenhower administration's declared policy of answering Communist aggression with the threat of a full nuclear strike, rather than matching force at the point of attack

brinkmanship: the practice of pushing a dangerous confrontation to the edge of war in order to force the other side to back down, described by Secretary of State John Foster Dulles

Warsaw Pact: the military alliance formed in 1955 joining the Soviet Union and the Communist states of Eastern Europe, created in answer to NATO and West Germany's place in it

covert action: a secret government operation meant to influence events abroad while hiding the sponsor's hand, carried out in the Cold War chiefly by the Central Intelligence Agency

Sputnik: the first artificial satellite, launched by the Soviet Union in October 1957, whose success shocked the United States and opened the space race

F. Sputnik and the Space Race

Sputnik went into orbit on October 4, (13) ____________, a sphere about (14) ____________ centimeters across weighing about 83.6 kilograms. Congress answered with the National Aeronautics and Space Administration in (15) ____________ and a missile program pressed forward at speed.

G. The Warning from Inside

On January 17, 1961, Eisenhower warned that the nation must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex. He had commanded the Allied armies in Europe and presided over the buildup himself, and he still judged that it could endanger the liberties it existed to defend.

Summary table. The arsenal and the doctrine: hydrogen weapons raised the scale from kilotons to megatons, Ivy Mike at an estimated 10.4 megatons in 1952 and Castle Bravo at an estimated 15 in 1954, and Dulles answered with massive retaliation and brinkmanship, which risked a confrontation going over the edge by error or pride. The home front: the 1951 Duck and Cover film and school drills taught children a routine that protected against glass and debris at a distance but could not deliver safety against a hydrogen bomb near a city. The secret war: covert action overthrew Mossadegh in Iran in 1953 and Arbenz in Guatemala in 1954, fast and cheap at the time, followed by revolution in Iran in 1979 and decades of civil war in Guatemala. The wider contest and the warning: the Warsaw Pact of 1955 faced NATO in Europe, both superpowers armed newly independent nations, Sputnik shocked the United States in 1957, and Eisenhower warned in 1961 against unwarranted influence by the military-industrial complex.

Exit ticket 1. Massive retaliation is the Eisenhower administration's declared policy of answering Communist aggression with the threat of a full nuclear strike, rather than matching force at the point of attack.

Exit ticket 2. Iran: covert action helped overthrow Prime Minister Mohammad Mossadegh in August 1953, the Shah returned to power, and the Shah's government fell to revolution in 1979. Guatemala: a CIA backed force overthrew President Jacobo Arbenz in June 1954, and the country entered decades of civil war and military rule.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that uses at least two facts and addresses the new rules, for example the megaton scale, the threat held at the edge, covert action, or the fear carried home in drills and Sputnik. The strongest answers end on whether the peace was held by wisdom or by luck.
