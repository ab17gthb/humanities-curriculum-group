The Wall Falls: The Cold War Ends

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Reformer Takes Power

In March 1985 Mikhail Gorbachev became the leader of the Soviet Union. The country he inherited had stagnated for more than a decade. Its factories produced goods few people wanted, its shops stood half empty, and its harvests fell short while its army fought a costly war in Afghanistan and its missile forces matched the American arsenal weapon for weapon. The system still commanded obedience, but it had stopped delivering progress, and its leaders had grown old repeating formulas they no longer believed. Gorbachev concluded that the Soviet Union had to change in order to survive. He did not set out to end the Cold War or to dissolve his own country. He set out to save the system by reforming it.

B. Glasnost and Perestroika

Gorbachev worked with two instruments. The first was glasnost, a policy of openness that loosened censorship, freed political prisoners, and let newspapers and citizens argue about failures that officials had hidden for decades, including crimes of the Stalin years. The second was perestroika, a program of restructuring meant to make the economy and the political system work by loosening central control. Each policy fed the other. Open discussion exposed how badly the economy was failing, and economic failure weakened the Communist Party's claim to rule. Reform had been designed to strengthen the party's control. Instead it taught Soviet citizens to criticize the party in public, and it taught the peoples of the empire that obedience was now a choice.

C. A Second Chance at Diplomacy

Reform at home changed Soviet policy abroad. Gorbachev sought relief from the arms race so the country could spend its strength at home, and he negotiated with President Ronald Reagan, the American leader who had called the Soviet Union an evil empire in 1983. At Reykjavik in Iceland in October 1986 the two men came within reach of abolishing nuclear weapons before the talks broke down over Reagan's missile defense program, the Strategic Defense Initiative. In June 1987 Reagan stood at the Brandenburg Gate in Berlin and challenged Gorbachev to prove his reforms were real by tearing down the wall. That December the two leaders signed the INF Treaty in Washington, the first agreement to destroy an entire class of nuclear weapons.

D. The Year It Broke: 1989

In 1989 the peoples of Eastern Europe tested what Gorbachev's restraint meant. In Poland, voters swept the Solidarity movement into government in elections the Communists had agreed to hold, and Gorbachev accepted the result instead of sending tanks as earlier Soviet leaders had done in Hungary and Czechoslovakia. Hungary opened its border with Austria, and East Germans began leaving through it by the thousands. In East Germany itself, Monday demonstrations in Leipzig grew from hundreds to hundreds of thousands through the autumn, demanding free travel and free elections. At each step Gorbachev refused to use the Soviet army stationed in Eastern Europe to hold Communist governments in power. The empire was not overthrown in 1989. Its guarantee was withdrawn.

E. November 9: The Night the Wall Opened

On November 9, 1989, East Germany's government tried to ease the pressure by loosening its travel rules. At a press conference that evening, a spokesman who had not been fully briefed announced the new rules and, pressed on when they took effect, answered that they applied immediately. Television carried his words across both German states. Thousands of East Berliners walked to the crossing points and demanded to pass. The border guards had no orders covering crowds of that size, and late that night they stood aside and let the people through. Berliners from both sides climbed onto the wall at the Brandenburg Gate and attacked it with hammers. No shot was fired. The central symbol of divided Europe had been breached by a garbled announcement and a crowd.

F. The Settlement of 1990

What followed was negotiation at speed. Free elections in East Germany in March 1990 returned a government committed to unification, and Chancellor Helmut Kohl of West Germany pressed for a fast settlement while Gorbachev's consent could still be won. The two German states and the four wartime occupying powers settled the external terms, and on October 3, 1990, East and West Germany joined in a single state, a member of NATO. The Warsaw Pact, the Soviet alliance built to answer NATO, dissolved in 1991, and the Baltic states of Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania recovered the independence Moscow had taken from them in 1940. Germany was whole for the first time since 1945, and it had been settled by signatures rather than by war.

G. The Center Gives Way: 1991

The last act took place in Moscow. In August 1991 hard line officials who blamed Gorbachev's reforms for the empire's collapse seized him at his vacation home and announced a state of emergency. The coup failed within three days. Boris Yeltsin, the elected president of the Russian republic, rallied resistance from atop a tank outside the Russian parliament, crowds defended the building, and army units refused orders to attack. In December the republics moved without Gorbachev. Ukraine voted for independence, and the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus declared the Soviet Union dissolved. On December 25, 1991, Gorbachev resigned, and the Soviet flag came down over the Kremlin. The Cold War had ended without the direct war both sides had armed against for forty five years.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What condition did Gorbachev inherit in 1985, and what did he conclude? A Soviet Union that had stagnated for more than a decade: factories producing goods few people wanted, shops half empty, harvests falling short, a costly war in Afghanistan, and an arsenal matching America's weapon for weapon, under leaders repeating formulas they no longer believed. Gorbachev concluded the country had to change in order to survive. He did not set out to end the Cold War or to dissolve his own country; he set out to save the system by reforming it. Require the last point; the lesson turns on it.

2. Define glasnost and perestroika, and explain how each weakened the party's control. Glasnost was openness: loosened censorship, freed political prisoners, and public argument about failures officials had hidden, including crimes of the Stalin years. Perestroika was restructuring: loosening central control so the economy and the political system would work. Open discussion exposed how badly the economy was failing, which weakened the party's claim to rule, and restructuring loosened the party's grip on the institutions it had commanded. Together they taught citizens to criticize the party in public and taught the peoples of the empire that obedience was now a choice.

3. Put the three events in order, with dates, and state what broke down at Reykjavik. Reykjavik, October 1986. Reagan at the Brandenburg Gate, June 1987. The INF Treaty signed in Washington, December 1987. At Reykjavik, Reagan and Gorbachev came within reach of abolishing nuclear weapons, and the talks broke down over Reagan's missile defense program, the Strategic Defense Initiative, which Gorbachev wanted limited and Reagan would not give up. The sequence itself is the teaching point: the near miss came first, the public challenge second, and the first agreement to destroy a class of weapons third.

4. Reconstruct the night of November 9, 1989. The government intended a limited easing of travel rules to relieve pressure. Its spokesman, not fully briefed, announced the rules at a press conference and, pressed on timing, said they applied immediately. Television carried the announcement into both German states. Thousands of East Berliners went to the crossings and demanded to pass. The guards, holding no orders for crowds of that size, stood aside late that night, and crowds climbed the wall at the Brandenburg Gate with hammers. No shot was fired. The reading calls it an accident because no one in the chain planned the opening that night: a concession was planned, its timing was misstated, and the crowd and the guards decided what the words would mean.

5. Which cause carried the most weight? Accept any of the three causes, or a stated combination, when it is carried by two facts from the reading. For Gorbachev's choices: his refusal in 1989 to use the Soviet army to hold Communist governments in power, and his acceptance of Poland's election result and of a unified Germany inside NATO. For the crowds: the Leipzig demonstrations growing from hundreds to hundreds of thousands, the East Berliners who forced the crossings on November 9, and the Moscow crowds who defeated the August 1991 coup. For the system's own failure: a decade of stagnation, empty shops, and failed harvests before Gorbachev took power, which made reform unavoidable. The strongest answers answer the obvious counterclaim: that Gorbachev could simply have ordered the army to fire, or that the crowds could not have moved against an empire still willing to shoot.
