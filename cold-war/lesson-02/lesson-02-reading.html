The Cold War in Asia: China and Korea

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Shocks of 1949

In the last months of 1949, two blows landed in Washington. On August 29, the Soviet Union tested its first atomic bomb, ending the American monopoly years earlier than planners had expected. On October 1, Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China, the Communist state Mao Zedong proclaimed in 1949 after his forces won the Chinese civil war, recognized by the Soviet Union and not by the United States. Chiang Kai-shek and his defeated Nationalists fled to Taiwan.

Together they put a Communist government over a quarter of the world's people and gave Moscow the bomb. Congress and the press asked who had lost China.

B. NSC-68: The Answer on Paper

The answer was written in secret. NSC-68, the secret American planning paper of 1950 that described the Cold War as a global struggle and called for a large buildup of American military strength, was finished in April 1950. It described the Soviet Union as driven by a fanatic faith and seeking absolute authority over the rest of the world, with the United States as the main obstacle to that design.

NSC-68 called for defense spending to rise to roughly three times its current level, for larger forces, and for aid to allies on every continent. Truman set it aside as too costly, since no Congress would vote the taxes in peacetime. The paper waited for an event that would make its price look necessary.

C. Invasion and Intervention

Korea had been divided since 1945 along the 38th parallel, the line of latitude chosen in 1945 to divide Soviet and American occupation zones in Korea, which became the border between North and South Korea. North of the line the Soviet Union backed Kim Il Sung. South of it the United States backed Syngman Rhee.

On June 25, 1950, North Korean troops crossed the parallel and took Seoul, the southern capital, in three days. The United Nations Security Council, meeting during a Soviet boycott of its sessions, condemned the invasion and called on member states to repel it. Truman ordered American forces into the fight under the United Nations flag, without asking Congress for a declaration of war. The Korean War, the war of 1950 to 1953 in which the United States and United Nations forces fought North Korea and China to preserve a non-Communist South Korea, had begun.

D. Inchon and the Drive North

The first summer went badly. North Korean troops drove the defenders into the Pusan Perimeter, a small corner around the port of Pusan. General Douglas MacArthur answered with the boldest move of the war. On September 15, 1950, Marines landed at Inchon, the port for Seoul far behind the front, climbing the harbor seawalls.

The landing cut the North Korean army off from its supplies. Seoul was retaken within two weeks, the army in the south collapsed, and United Nations forces crossed the 38th parallel in pursuit. The aim of the war changed as they advanced. Preserving South Korea was no longer the object. The forces drove toward the Yalu River, the border with China, to unify Korea by force.

E. China Enters and the War Settles

China had warned that it would not stand aside while American forces approached its border. The warnings were discounted in Washington and Tokyo. Chinese troops struck in late October. On November 25, they attacked in force. Hundreds of thousands of Chinese soldiers had crossed the Yalu in secret.

At the Chosin Reservoir, Marines fought their way out of encirclement in bitter cold. Seoul fell again in January 1951 and was retaken in March. By the summer of 1951, the front had settled near the 38th parallel, and the war hardened into stalemate, a condition in fighting in which neither side can gain ground or force a decision, however long the fighting continues. For two more years, soldiers fought over named hills while negotiators argued at Panmunjom.

F. MacArthur and Truman: Who Commands a Limited War

MacArthur had never accepted the limits Washington set on the war. He wanted to blockade the Chinese coast, bomb Chinese bases in Manchuria, and bring Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist troops into the fight. Truman and his advisers refused each step. Their war was a limited war, a war fought for limited aims with some weapons and targets deliberately left unused, rather than for the enemy's total defeat, because a wider war risked Soviet entry and a third world war.

MacArthur pressed his case in public, undercutting a peace move Truman was preparing. On April 11, 1951, Truman relieved him of his commands, defending the act as the price of civilian control of the military. MacArthur addressed Congress on April 19. He closed by quoting an old barracks ballad: "old soldiers never die; they just fade away." The policy he had challenged did not change.

G. Armistice Without Victory

Armistice talks opened in July 1951 and ran for two years. The hardest question was the prisoners, thousands of whom refused to return home, and the United States refused to force them. On July 27, 1953, the commanders signed an armistice, an agreement to stop fighting; the Korean armistice of 1953 ended the shooting without a peace treaty or a political settlement.

The line of contact ran close to the 38th parallel, roughly where the war had begun, and a demilitarized zone drawn along it. More than 36,000 Americans had died, along with Korean dead in the millions and Chinese dead in the hundreds of thousands. Korea set the template for the American wars after it: undeclared, fought for limited aims, and ended by negotiation rather than by victory.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Name the two shocks of 1949, with dates, and why they read as losses. On August 29, 1949, the Soviet Union tested its first atomic bomb, ending the American atomic monopoly years earlier than planners had expected. On October 1, 1949, Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China after his forces won the Chinese civil war, and the Nationalist government fled to Taiwan. Together they put a Communist government over a quarter of the world's people and gave Moscow an atomic weapon, so Congress and the press treated them as American losses and asked who had lost China.

2. What did NSC-68 argue, what did it call for, and why was it set aside? NSC-68, finished in April 1950, described the Soviet Union as driven by a fanatic faith and seeking absolute authority over the rest of the world, with the United States as the main obstacle to that design. It called for defense spending to rise to roughly three times its current level, with larger ground, sea, and air forces and aid to allies on every continent. Truman set it aside as too costly, because no Congress would vote the taxes to pay for it in peacetime. The Korean War, weeks later, supplied the event that made its price look necessary, and the buildup it demanded became policy.

3. How did the United States enter the war in June 1950? North Korea invaded South Korea across the 38th parallel on June 25, 1950, and took Seoul in three days. The United Nations Security Council, meeting while the Soviet delegate was boycotting its sessions, condemned the invasion and called on member states to repel it. Truman ordered American air, sea, and then ground forces into the fight under the United Nations flag. The step Congress never took was a declaration of war: the war was fought on presidential orders and a Security Council resolution, which is the first entry in the template the reading closes with.

4. Trace the turn of the war in the autumn of 1950. After the defense of the Pusan Perimeter, MacArthur landed Marines at Inchon on September 15, 1950, far behind the front. The landing cut the North Korean army off from its supplies, Seoul was retaken within two weeks, and the army in the south collapsed. United Nations forces then crossed the 38th parallel and changed the aim of the war from preserving South Korea to unifying Korea by force, driving toward the Yalu River. China, whose warnings had been discounted, struck in late October and attacked in force on November 25 with hundreds of thousands of troops who had crossed the Yalu in secret. The advance became a retreat, and by the summer of 1951 the front had settled near the 38th parallel.

5. How did the Cold War turn into a shooting war in Asia, and why did Korea end without victory? Full credit answers hold the two halves together. The turn to shooting: the shocks of 1949 made Communist expansion read as a single global design, NSC-68 priced the answer as a worldwide military buildup, and North Korea's invasion of June 25, 1950 gave the paper's logic a battlefield, which the United States entered under the United Nations flag without a declaration of war. The ending without victory: the drive to the Yalu brought China into the war, the front settled into stalemate near the 38th parallel, Washington refused MacArthur's wider war in order to keep the war limited and dismissed him for pressing it in public, and two years of talks produced an armistice on July 27, 1953 on a line roughly where the war had begun, with more than 36,000 American dead and no peace treaty. Answers that end the story at Inchon, or that treat the armistice as a defeat pure and simple, earn partial credit: South Korea was preserved, which was the original aim, and unification, the added aim, was given up.
