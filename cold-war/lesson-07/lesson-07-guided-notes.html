Guided Notes: Détente, Human Rights, and the Evil Empire

Lesson question: Why did the rivals try to ease the Cold War in the 1970s, and why did the confrontation return in the 1980s?

Unit question: How did the struggle between the United States and the Soviet Union reshape the world and American life, and why did it end the way it did?

Key Terms

détente: the easing of tension between the United States and the Soviet Union pursued in the 1970s, built on arms agreements, trade, and regular summit meetings

arms control: negotiated limits on the weapons the superpowers could build or deploy, agreed in treaties rather than won in competition

SALT: the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks between the United States and the Soviet Union, and the agreements they produced in 1972 and 1979 limiting nuclear delivery vehicles

Helsinki Accords: the 1975 agreement in which Western nations recognized Europe's postwar borders and the Soviet Union and its allies pledged respect for human rights, a pledge dissidents used against their own governments

Reagan Doctrine: the policy of the 1980s under which the United States aided anti-Communist insurgencies fighting Soviet-backed governments, in Afghanistan, Nicaragua, and elsewhere

Strategic Defense Initiative: President Reagan's 1983 proposal to build a defense in space against nuclear missiles, called Star Wars by its critics, which promised protection deterrence could not

Evil Empire: President Reagan's name for the Soviet Union in a speech of March 1983, declaring it the focus of evil in the modern world and rejecting the idea that the two systems were morally equal

Summary table. Détente built, 1972 to 1975: Nixon opened relations with China in February 1972, signed the first SALT agreements in Moscow that May freezing missile launchers, and trade in grain and technology followed, all of it held up at home by critics who charged that the Soviet Union kept building while the United States held back. Helsinki and human rights: thirty five nations signed the Accords in August 1975, the West recognized Europe's postwar borders, and the Soviet bloc pledged respect for human rights, a pledge dissidents in Moscow and Prague quoted back to their own governments through watch groups that sent the record abroad. Collapse, 1979: the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December killed the bargain, and Carter answered with a grain embargo, an Olympic boycott, and the withdrawal of SALT II from Senate consideration. Confrontation returns, 1981 to 1983: Reagan raised defense spending, aided anti-Communist insurgencies under the Reagan Doctrine, named the Soviet Union an evil empire in March 1983, and announced the Strategic Defense Initiative the same month, in a year that also brought the Korean airliner shootdown and the Able Archer exercise.

Exit ticket 1. The interim agreement froze each side's number of land based and submarine launched missile launchers. It left each side free to modernize and replace the missiles and launchers it already had, which is why the arsenals kept changing under the freeze and why critics charged that the agreement limited less than it appeared to.

Exit ticket 2. Western nations recognized Europe's postwar borders, giving the Soviet Union the settlement in Europe it had wanted since 1945. The Soviet Union and its allies pledged respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms. Dissidents inside the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe turned the pledge against their own governments, forming watch groups that recorded violations and sent the record abroad.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that covers all three parts with facts from the notes. The strongest answers state that the rivals stepped back because the Soviet Union had reached near nuclear parity and faced China while the United States was carrying the Vietnam War; that the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 ended the bargain and brought an embargo, a boycott, and the death of SALT II; and that the confrontation returned through the defense buildup, the Reagan Doctrine, the evil empire speech, or the Strategic Defense Initiative, with the danger of 1983 stated at the level the evidence supports.
