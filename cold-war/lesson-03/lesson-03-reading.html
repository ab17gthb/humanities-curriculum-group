The Red Scare at Home

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Fuel for the Fear

The Second Red Scare was not invented from nothing. In August 1949 the Soviet Union tested its first atomic bomb, years earlier than American officials had expected. Weeks later Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China after his Communist forces won the Chinese civil war. In June 1950 North Korea invaded South Korea, and American soldiers were soon fighting a Communist enemy in Asia. Soviet espionage was also real. Soviet agents had passed atomic secrets to Moscow, and American codebreakers held evidence of spy networks they could not fully show in public. Fear of communism abroad became fear of Communists at home, inside the government itself.

B. The Loyalty Program

In March 1947 President Harry Truman signed Executive Order 9835 and created a federal loyalty program. The order required a loyalty investigation of every person entering federal civilian employment, and agencies checked the workers already on the payroll as well. Investigators consulted FBI files and the files of the House Un-American Activities Committee, among other sources. Loyalty boards judged workers under a standard that asked whether reasonable grounds existed for believing a person disloyal. Membership in, or sympathetic association with, an organization the Attorney General had listed as subversive could count against a worker. Federal employees and many other workers also signed loyalty oaths, sworn statements of loyalty and denials of Communist membership. Millions of workers were checked. A few hundred lost federal jobs on loyalty grounds, and many more resigned while under investigation.

C. HUAC Comes to Hollywood

The House Un-American Activities Committee, known as HUAC, had investigated alleged Communist influence in American life since before the war. In October 1947 it turned to the film industry. Friendly witnesses, including studio heads and actors, answered freely and named colleagues they said held Communist views. The committee then called witnesses it considered unfriendly and asked each one whether he was now, or had ever been, a member of the Communist Party. Ten screenwriters and directors, soon known as the Hollywood Ten, refused to answer. They argued that the First Amendment protected their beliefs and associations. The House of Representatives cited all ten for contempt of Congress. Courts upheld the citations, and the ten went to prison in 1950 for terms of six months to a year.

D. The Blacklist and the Wider Reach

Prison was only part of the price. In November 1947 the major studio heads announced that the ten would not work again until they had cleared themselves, and a blacklist spread through film and broadcasting. Studios, networks, and sponsors circulated lists of people barred from employment because of suspected Communist ties or refusal to cooperate with investigators. Some people named had belonged to the Communist Party. Others had signed petitions, joined organizations later placed on a government list, or simply known the wrong people. Hundreds of actors, writers, and directors lost work without a trial and without a public charge they could answer. The machinery reached far beyond Hollywood. Teachers, union members, and state and city workers faced oaths, hearings, and dismissals across the country.

E. The Spy Cases: Hiss and the Rosenbergs

Two spy cases kept the fear hot. Alger Hiss, a former State Department official, denied under oath that he had passed government documents to a Soviet agent. He was convicted of perjury in January 1950 and served time in prison, and his case convinced many Americans that Communists had reached high office. Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were tried in 1951 for conspiracy to pass atomic secrets to the Soviet Union. The case against them rested heavily on testimony from Ethel's brother, who said he had passed atomic bomb sketches to Julius. Both were convicted and sentenced to death. They were executed in June 1953, the first American civilians put to death for espionage in peacetime. Americans argued over the evidence and the sentence then, and historians argue over both still.

F. McCarthy and the List That Changed

Senator Joseph McCarthy of Wisconsin built a national campaign on this fear. On February 9, 1950, speaking at a Lincoln Day dinner in Wheeling, West Virginia, he claimed to hold a list of Communists working inside the State Department. Reporters present heard him claim one number. When he read the speech into the Congressional Record eleven days later, the printed text spoke of 57 cases. The number changed as he repeated the charge, and he never produced evidence the Senate committee assigned to check his claims could confirm. That committee found no Communists in the department. None of it slowed him. His method, public accusation repeated as if repetition were evidence, gave the era its name, McCarthyism. For four years his charges set the terms of national politics, and few officeholders dared to challenge him.

G. Television, Censure, and the Reckoning

The turn came on television. In 1954 the Army accused McCarthy's office of seeking special favors for a former aide, and McCarthy accused the Army of sheltering Communists. The Senate hearings on the charges ran for 36 days with cameras in the room, and millions watched McCarthy interrupt and bully witnesses. On June 9 the Army lawyer, Joseph Welch, answered an attack on a young lawyer in his firm by asking whether the senator had no sense of decency, and the hearing room applauded. That December the Senate voted 67 to 22 to condemn McCarthy's conduct, a vote treated as a censure, and his power collapsed. The scare faded slowly after him. Thousands had lost jobs, careers had ended on suspicion and association, and the Supreme Court narrowed the loyalty program's legal tools only after the scare itself had burned down.
