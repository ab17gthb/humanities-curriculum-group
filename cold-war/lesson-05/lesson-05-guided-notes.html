Guided Notes: Cuba: Revolution and the Missile Crisis

Lesson question: Why did the Soviet Union put missiles in Cuba, and how did thirteen days in October 1962 change the Cold War?

Unit question: How did the struggle between the United States and the Soviet Union reshape the world and American life, and why did it end the way it did?

Key Terms

Cuban Revolution: the revolution that brought Fidel Castro to power in Cuba in 1959, overthrowing the government of Fulgencio Batista and turning toward alliance with the Soviet Union

Bay of Pigs invasion: the failed invasion of Cuba in April 1961 by Cuban exiles trained and supplied by the United States, defeated on the beach within three days

deterrence: the policy of preventing an enemy attack by holding a threat of retaliation so destructive that the attack would cost more than it could gain

Cuban Missile Crisis: the confrontation of October 1962 in which the United States discovered Soviet nuclear missiles being installed in Cuba and forced their withdrawal short of war

naval quarantine: President Kennedy's name for the naval blockade of Cuba in 1962, chosen because a blockade is legally an act of war and quarantine was not

hotline: the direct communications link between Washington and Moscow established in 1963 so the two leaders could reach each other at once in a crisis

Limited Test Ban Treaty: the treaty of 1963 in which the United States, the Soviet Union, and Britain banned nuclear weapons tests in the atmosphere, in space, and under water

Summary table. Before the crisis: Castro's revolution of January 1, 1959 overthrew Batista, land reform and seizures struck American property, the February 1960 Soviet trade agreement tied Cuba to Moscow, and the failed Bay of Pigs invasion of April 1961 taught Castro to seek a Soviet guarantee and taught Khrushchev to read Kennedy as pushable. Discovery and choice: a U-2 photograph of October 14, 1962 showed medium range missile sites near San Cristobal; after six days of secret Executive Committee deliberation, Kennedy announced the discovery on October 22 and imposed the naval quarantine, which took effect October 24. The closest day: October 27 brought Khrushchev's second letter adding the Turkey condition, the shootdown of Major Anderson's U-2 over Cuba, and a straying American U-2 over Siberia, followed that night by the secret Robert Kennedy and Dobrynin meeting. Settlement and safeguards: publicly, Soviet missiles left Cuba and the United States pledged not to invade it; secretly, the Jupiter missiles were removed from Turkey months later; in 1963 the hotline and the Limited Test Ban Treaty followed.

Exit ticket 1. Castro accepted the missiles as a shield against another American invasion attempt, which the Bay of Pigs failure had taught him to expect, this time with greater force.

Exit ticket 2. Public part, announced in 1962: Soviet missiles withdrawn from Cuba under international observation in exchange for an American pledge not to invade Cuba. Secret part, denied at the time: the American Jupiter missiles were quietly removed from Turkey months later.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that pairs the Soviet reasons with the crisis's results and uses two dates. The strongest answers name deterrence of an invasion of Cuba, the Jupiter missiles in Turkey, and the cheap closing of the missile gap as the reasons, give October 14, 22, 24, 27, or 28 for the crisis itself, and close on the hotline and the Limited Test Ban Treaty of 1963 as what thirteen days built.
