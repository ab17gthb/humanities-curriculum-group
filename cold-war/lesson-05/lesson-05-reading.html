Cuba: Revolution and the Missile Crisis

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. An Island Ninety Miles Away

Cuba sits ninety miles from Florida, close enough that its politics had never been distant in Washington. Its sugar economy was tied to the United States, American companies held large Cuban properties, and its government under Fulgencio Batista rested on American support. Batista jailed opponents, and resentment of his regime and of American influence ran deep.

On January 1, 1959, that order ended. The Cuban Revolution, the revolution that brought Fidel Castro to power in Cuba in 1959, overthrowing the government of Fulgencio Batista and turning toward alliance with the Soviet Union, put Castro in Havana after Batista fled. The United States recognized the new government within days.

B. The Turn Toward Moscow

Castro's government set out to remake Cuba, and its first measures struck American interests directly. A land reform broke up large estates, including American owned sugar land. The government seized American property without payment Washington would accept, and narrowed the space for political opposition. Each step drew an American protest, and each protest fed Castro's charge of subordination.

In February 1960, Cuba signed a trade agreement with the Soviet Union, exchanging Cuban sugar for Soviet oil, credits, and aid. Washington answered by cutting off Cuban sugar sales to the United States. In January 1961 the two countries broke diplomatic relations.

C. The Bay of Pigs and What It Taught

President Kennedy inherited a secret plan to overthrow Castro, drawn up under President Eisenhower, and in April 1961 he let it go forward. On April 17, about 1,400 Cuban exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs. The Bay of Pigs invasion, the failed invasion of Cuba in April 1961 by Cuban exiles trained and supplied by the United States, defeated on the beach within three days, collapsed when the expected Cuban uprising never came and Kennedy refused American air power.

The failure taught both sides a lesson. Castro concluded that the United States would try again with greater force, and he sought a stronger guarantee from Moscow. Khrushchev concluded that Kennedy was young and could be pushed. The missile decision of 1962 rested on both readings.

D. Why Missiles, and the Photograph

Khrushchev's reasons were mixed. Missiles in Cuba would protect the island by deterrence, the policy of preventing an enemy attack by holding a threat of retaliation so destructive that the attack would cost more than it could gain. They would answer the American Jupiter missiles in Turkey, within range of Soviet cities. They would also narrow the missile gap cheaply, since Soviet missiles could reach the United States from Cuba but could not yet reach it in numbers from Soviet soil.

The deployment was secret, and a camera found it. On October 14, 1962, an American U-2 photographed the countryside near San Cristobal, and analysts identified Soviet medium range missile sites under construction. Those missiles could strike much of the eastern United States with little warning. Kennedy was told on October 16.

E. Thirteen Days: The Quarantine

For six days Kennedy deliberated in secret with advisers called the Executive Committee. The choices were an air strike, an invasion, or pressure at sea. Kennedy chose the sea. On October 22 he addressed the nation, announced the discovery, demanded the missiles out, and imposed a naval quarantine, President Kennedy's name for the naval blockade of Cuba in 1962, chosen because a blockade is legally an act of war and quarantine was not. Any missile launched from Cuba, he warned, would be treated as a Soviet attack requiring a full retaliatory response.

The quarantine took effect on October 24. Some Soviet ships bound for Cuba turned back at the line. Work on the sites went on at full speed, and American forces prepared for invasion if pressure failed.

F. October 27: The Closest Day

On Friday, October 26, a long letter from Khrushchev offered a way out: the missiles would be withdrawn if the United States pledged not to invade Cuba. The next morning a second, harder letter, made public, added a condition, the removal of the Jupiter missiles from Turkey. Kennedy's advisers answered the first letter and set the second aside.

That Saturday the crisis nearly escaped them. A Soviet surface to air missile shot down an American U-2 over Cuba, killing its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson. Another American U-2 strayed over Soviet territory in Siberia before turning back. Neither leader had ordered the day's shooting. That night Robert Kennedy met secretly with Ambassador Dobrynin.

G. The Settlement and What It Built

On Sunday, October 28, Khrushchev announced that the missiles would be withdrawn. The public settlement had two parts: Soviet missiles out of Cuba under international observation, and an American pledge not to invade Cuba. The Cuban Missile Crisis, the confrontation of October 1962 in which the United States discovered Soviet nuclear missiles being installed in Cuba and forced their withdrawal short of war, had ended in thirteen days. A second part stayed secret. The Jupiter missiles were quietly removed from Turkey months later, a trade both governments denied at the time and later evidence established.

Thirteen days built two safeguards. The first was the hotline, the direct communications link between Washington and Moscow established in 1963 so the two leaders could reach each other at once in a crisis. The second was the Limited Test Ban Treaty, the treaty of 1963 in which the United States, the Soviet Union, and Britain banned nuclear weapons tests in the atmosphere, in space, and under water. Under both sat a rule the crisis had taught: neither side could afford to lose control of a direct confrontation.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Name two steps that struck American interests, and the agreement that tied Cuba to Moscow. Any two of the land reform that broke up large estates including American owned sugar land, the seizure of American property without payment Washington would accept, and the narrowing of space for political opposition, though the first two are the direct hits on American interests. The tying agreement was the February 1960 trade agreement with the Soviet Union, exchanging Cuban sugar for Soviet oil, credits, and aid. The sequence matters for grading: each American protest fed Castro's charge of subordination, and the sugar cutoff answered the trade deal. (Blueprint 9)

2. Explain the two lessons of the Bay of Pigs. Castro concluded that the United States would try again with greater force, so he sought a stronger Soviet guarantee, which became his reason for accepting Soviet missiles on Cuban soil. Khrushchev concluded that Kennedy was young and could be pushed, which lowered the risk as Moscow calculated it of a secret deployment ninety miles from Florida. Both lessons were readings of the same failure, and both were necessary to produce October 1962. (Blueprint 9)

3. State Khrushchev's three reasons, with the supporting fact for each. Protection of Cuba by deterrence, supported by Castro's expectation of another American invasion attempt after the Bay of Pigs. An answer to the American Jupiter missiles in Turkey, supported by their position within range of Soviet cities. A cheap narrowing of the missile gap, supported by the fact that Soviet missiles able to reach the United States from Cuba could not yet reach it in numbers from Soviet soil. The reading notes that historians weigh the reasons differently, and students should not present any single one as settled. (Blueprint 10)

4. Why quarantine, and what did the word buy? A blockade is legally an act of war, and Kennedy chose the word quarantine so the action would not carry that legal status. The word bought time and room: the United States could stop offensive weapons at sea, hold a step in reserve short of war, and leave Khrushchev a choice that was not surrender under a declared act of war. Full credit connects the naming to the deliberation that produced it, in which air strike and invasion were the rejected alternatives. (Blueprint 10)

5. State both parts of the settlement, and why the second stayed secret. Publicly, the Soviet Union withdrew its missiles from Cuba under international observation, and the United States pledged not to invade Cuba. Secretly, the American Jupiter missiles were quietly removed from Turkey months later, a trade both governments denied at the time. Announcing the Turkey trade in 1962 would have made the settlement read as a swap under pressure, weakened the American position inside NATO, whose council had approved the Turkish deployment, and handed critics in both countries a charge of dealing over an ally's head. The Dobrynin cable in the activity records the secrecy condition in the Soviet ambassador's own report. A paragraph that gives both parts without the alliance reason earns partial credit. (Blueprint 10)
