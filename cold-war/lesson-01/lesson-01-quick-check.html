Quick Check: The Doctrine and the Line

Directions: Read the passage and the background, then answer the questions.

The passage. I believe that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures. I believe that our help should be primarily through economic and financial aid which is essential to economic stability and orderly political processes.

Source: Harry S. Truman, Truman Doctrine Address, March 12, 1947 (Harry S. Truman Library, Public Papers), as carried in the audited TPSP record.

Background. In February 1946 George Kennan's Long Telegram argued from Moscow that Soviet power expanded from ideology and insecurity and withdrew before strong resistance. In March 1946 Churchill named the iron curtain at Fulton, Missouri. In February 1947 Britain told Washington it could give Greece and Turkey no further aid after March 31. Greece faced Communist guerrillas, and Turkey faced Soviet pressure over the straits.

1. In the passage, what policy does Truman declare, and by what kind of help does he say it should chiefly work? Answer in your own words, using one phrase from the passage as evidence.

2. Kennan's Long Telegram reached Washington a year before this address. State his claim about what Soviet power does when it meets strong resistance, and explain how that claim supports the policy Truman declares.

3. Truman asked Congress for four hundred million dollars for two named countries, yet the passage names no country at all. Explain what the passage commits the United States to beyond Greece and Turkey.

4. A critic in 1947 argues that the passage promises more than the United States can deliver. State the critic's strongest point, using the wording of the passage itself.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What policy does Truman declare, and by what kind of help? He declares that the United States will support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures, the definition of the Truman Doctrine in the lesson vocabulary. The help should be primarily through economic and financial aid, which he ties to economic stability and orderly political processes. Credit requires the promise and the kind of help, with a quoted phrase such as attempted subjugation or economic and financial aid. (Blueprint 2)

2. State Kennan's claim about resistance, and how it supports the policy. Kennan claimed that Soviet power, though impervious to the logic of reason, is highly sensitive to the logic of force and usually withdraws when strong resistance is encountered at any point. That claim supports Truman's policy because a power that backs down before firm resistance can be contained by support to the peoples it presses, without a direct war between the superpowers. (Blueprint 1)

3. What does the passage commit the United States to beyond Greece and Turkey? It commits the United States to a general rule: support for free peoples resisting subjugation anywhere the case arises, not only in the two countries named in the appropriation request. The passage names no enemy and no place, and students should name that openness as the commitment's size and its risk. (Blueprint 2)

4. State the critic's strongest point from the passage's wording. The passage promises support to free peoples, with no limit of place, case, or cost stated in it, against subjugation by armed minorities inside a country as well as by outside pressures. A commitment written that wide can be claimed in cases the government of 1947 never considered, and the country can be held to the words long after the occasion has passed. Answers must work from the passage's own breadth rather than from later events. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Question 2 is the hinge diagnostic: students who can connect Kennan's claim about resistance to Truman's promise have the analysis to policy line the unit test will ask for. Question 4 predicts success on the writing task, because it requires the doctrine's own wording read against itself.
