Writing Task: A Doctrine Without Borders?

The prompt. Answer the closing question of the lesson in a developed essay of about 300 words: Did the Truman Doctrine commit the United States to more than containment required, or was its worldwide wording the point of the policy? A strong answer does more than state a side. It works through the build in order, from Kennan's analysis to the doctrine's wording to the instruments that followed it, and it weighs what the wording promised against what the strategy needed. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading, stations, and documents. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording, except where a bank item quotes a document.

Evidence Bank

1. Kennan, Long Telegram, February 22, 1946: Soviet power is highly sensitive to logic of force and can easily withdraw, and usually does, when strong resistance is encountered at any point. Containment on this account needed firm resistance at the points tested, not a promise covering every country on earth. (Foreign Relations of the United States, via Wikisource, as carried in the audited TPSP record. Cuts are marked with ellipses in the station text.)

2. Churchill at Fulton, March 5, 1946, named a line in one continent: From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic an iron curtain has descended across the Continent. The division he described was European, and the capitals behind the line were the capitals of Central and Eastern Europe. (Delivered text as printed by the Churchill Archives Centre.)

3. Truman, March 12, 1947: I believe that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures. The sentence names no country, no enemy, and no limit. (Harry S. Truman Library, Public Papers, as carried in the audited TPSP record.)

4. The occasion for the doctrine was specific. Britain could aid Greece and Turkey no longer after March 31, 1947, Greece faced Communist guerrillas, Turkey faced Soviet pressure over the straits, and the request before Congress was four hundred million dollars for those two countries.

5. The Marshall Plan, 1948 to 1952, gave about thirteen billion dollars to rebuild Western Europe, directed not against any country or doctrine but against hunger, poverty, desperation, and chaos. Its theater was Europe, and its tool was economic recovery. (George C. Marshall, Harvard Address, June 5, 1947, as carried in the audited TPSP record.)

6. At Berlin in 1948 and 1949 the doctrine's promise met a test in Europe and held: the Western powers supplied West Berlin by air for eleven months rather than abandon it, and the Soviet Union lifted the blockade. NATO followed in 1949, an alliance for the North Atlantic, not for the world.

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your claim, develop it with your three pieces of evidence in the order of the build, answer the other side in one or two sentences, and end by restating what the doctrine's wording did that a narrower promise would not have done.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the question directly and holds the doctrine's wording up against the strategy's need. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 supports the narrow reading: Kennan's strategy needed resistance at points tested. (Blueprint 1) Bank item 2 supports the geographic reading: the division named in 1946 was European. (Blueprint 1) Bank items 3 and 4 support the contrast at the heart of the task: a universal sentence spoken for a two country request. (Blueprint 2) Bank items 5 and 6 support either side, since the instruments that followed were European in scale while the promise that announced them was not. (Blueprint 2) A full credit essay draws on at least two of the three groups.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive link is the gap between occasion and wording: a request for four hundred million dollars for Greece and Turkey was defended in a sentence that names no country, no enemy, and no limit, and students should argue about what that gap was for.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest argument on the other side, such as the point that a universal rule deterred without new debates in each case, or the point that the instruments actually built, the Marshall Plan and NATO, stayed regional while the words did not, answered from the record rather than dismissed.

Model opening for teacher reference. The doctrine committed the United States to more than containment required, and the excess was in the sentence, not the strategy. Kennan's analysis asked for firm resistance where Soviet power tested the line, and the instruments built in the doctrine's name, aid to Greece and Turkey, the Marshall Plan, the airlift, NATO, all worked in Europe, along the line Churchill had named. The sentence Truman spoke worked everywhere. It named no country and set no limit, so any later government could claim its authority for a case in a place the men of 1947 had never discussed, and opponents of any later commitment could quote the same sentence back. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the Fulton and Berlin evidence in build order, answering the deterrence argument from the record, and closing on what the worldwide wording did that a European promise would not.

Common weak answers. Opinion only (Truman was right to stand up to Moscow, or the doctrine started every later war) asserts a side without working the build. Wording only (the sentence was broad) skips the question of what the strategy needed, and need only (Europe was the theater) skips the sentence the prompt asks students to judge. A claim with no other side stated earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a weighing, not a verdict alone.
