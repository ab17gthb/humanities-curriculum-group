Allies to Adversaries: Containment Takes Shape

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. Partners of Necessity

The United States and the Soviet Union became allies because both faced Hitler, not because they trusted each other. Their systems had stood opposed since the Russian Revolution, and each side entered the partnership expecting the other to return to rivalry once the common enemy fell. The war hid the division without ending it.

Victory in 1945 left two powers standing far above the rest, each a superpower, a nation with power and influence far beyond that of other states, able to project military and political strength across the world. Soviet armies held Eastern Europe. American and British armies held the West. Where the armies stopped, the new map hardened.

B. The Alliance Unravels

At Yalta in February 1945 the allies had pledged free elections in the liberated states of Eastern Europe. The pledge broke in Poland first, where Moscow installed a government of its choosing and arrested Polish leaders who objected. A pattern followed across the region, as coalition governments gave way to Communist rule backed by Soviet force.

Pressure reached beyond Eastern Europe. Soviet troops stayed in northern Iran past the date agreed for withdrawal, and Moscow demanded joint control of the Turkish straits. Each move was small enough to excuse on its own. Together they described a power testing how far it could push.

C. Two Documents Name the New Reality

On February 22, 1946, George Kennan sent Washington a cable of about eight thousand words that became known as the Long Telegram. Kennan argued that Soviet expansion grew from ideology joined to an old Russian sense of insecurity, that Soviet power took no unnecessary risks, and that it withdrew when it met strong resistance at any point.

Eleven days later Winston Churchill spoke at Fulton, Missouri, with President Truman seated on the platform. Churchill named the division Kennan had described: From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic an iron curtain has descended across the Continent. The Iron Curtain was the boundary Winston Churchill named in 1946 between the Soviet-dominated states of Eastern Europe and the free states of the West, and the division of Europe it described.

D. A Strategy Takes Shape

Kennan's answer became the strategy called containment, the American strategy of blocking the further expansion of Soviet power and communism, first argued by George Kennan and adopted as the guiding policy of the early Cold War. Containment did not promise to roll Soviet power back where it already stood. It promised to hold the line where Soviet power had not yet reached, and to hold it for as long as holding took.

Kennan read the contest as political and economic before it was military. Soviet power, in his account, fed on weakness and disorder. A strategy that strengthened those states, repaired their economies, and restored their confidence would deny Moscow its openings without a direct war between the two superpowers.

E. The Doctrine and the Plan

In March 1947 Britain told Washington it could no longer aid Greece, where Communist guerrillas fought the government, or Turkey, under Soviet pressure over the straits. On March 12 President Truman asked Congress for four hundred million dollars for both countries and framed the request as a worldwide duty. The Truman Doctrine was the policy President Truman declared in 1947, that the United States would support free peoples resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or outside pressures.

That June, Secretary of State George Marshall proposed aid to rebuild all of Europe. The Marshall Plan was the American program of 1948 to 1952 that gave about thirteen billion dollars in aid to rebuild the economies of Western Europe after the Second World War. Moscow barred the states under its control from taking part, and the plan bound the recovering West to the United States.

F. The Test at Berlin

Berlin sat deep inside the Soviet occupation zone, divided among the four victorious powers, and in June 1948 the Soviet Union closed the roads, railways, and canals linking the western sectors to the West. The move tested whether the Western powers would abandon two million people or fight to keep them.

They answered by air. The Berlin Airlift was the American and British air supply of West Berlin in 1948 and 1949, flown around the Soviet blockade of the city's land routes. Planes landed around the clock for eleven months, carrying food and coal into the city. In May 1949 the Soviet Union lifted the blockade. The city had held, and containment had passed its first direct test without a shot fired.

G. An Alliance, Two Germanys, and a Second Bomb

In April 1949 the United States signed the treaty creating NATO, the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, the alliance formed in 1949 in which the United States, Canada, and Western European nations pledged to treat an attack on one member as an attack on all. It was the first peacetime alliance in American history, and it committed American power to the defense of Europe in advance of any attack.

Germany divided to match. The Western zones became the Federal Republic of Germany in 1949, and the Soviet zone became the German Democratic Republic that October. In August 1949 the Soviet Union tested its first atomic bomb, ending the American monopoly. Containment had been built as a political and economic strategy. Events were about to make it military and global.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Why was the partnership an alliance of necessity, and what did victory leave standing? The two states were divided by ideology long before 1945 and united only by a common enemy, Hitler; each expected rivalry to resume once he fell. Victory left two superpowers and a divided map. Soviet armies held Eastern Europe, American and British armies held the West, and the line where the armies stopped became the line the Cold War was fought along.

2. Name two ways the alliance unraveled. Inside Eastern Europe, the Yalta pledge of free elections broke, first in Poland, where Moscow installed a government of its choosing and arrested objecting leaders, then across the region as coalition governments gave way to Communist rule. Beyond Eastern Europe, Soviet troops stayed in northern Iran past the agreed withdrawal date, and Moscow demanded joint control of the Turkish straits. Credit either outside example.

3. What did Kennan argue about expansion and resistance? Kennan argued that Soviet expansion was driven by ideology joined to a traditional Russian sense of insecurity, not by any single Western act, and that Soviet power was cautious rather than reckless: it took no unnecessary risks, ignored arguments, respected force, and withdrew when it met strong resistance at any point. That second claim is the ground of containment, because a power that backs down before firm resistance can be blocked without war.

4. How did the Marshall Plan serve containment as economic aid? Kennan's account held that Soviet power fed on weakness and disorder. By giving about thirteen billion dollars from 1948 to 1952 to rebuild Western Europe, the plan attacked the hunger and chaos in which Communist parties grew, restored the confidence of the states on the Soviet border, and bound the recovering West to the United States. Moscow's refusal to let its satellites take part confirmed the division the plan worked along.

5. What supports the closing judgment that containment was about to turn military and global? Full credit answers build from the lesson's own sequence. The Berlin Blockade was already a military instrument answered by a military airlift. NATO committed American power to Europe's defense in advance, the first peacetime alliance in the country's history. Germany divided into two armed camps' states in 1949, and in August of that year the Soviet atomic bomb ended the American monopoly, so the rivalry now included the weapon neither side could use without risking everything. A paragraph that asserts the judgment without at least two of these facts earns partial credit.
