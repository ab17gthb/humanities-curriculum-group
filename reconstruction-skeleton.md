# The Reconstruction Unit: Assignment Skeleton

_Sixth unit of the Knoweth US History course (courseId u6), built fifteenth and last in the build order, after the Progressive Era pilot, the Gilded Age, Imperialism and World War I, the Twenties, the Depression and New Deal, the World War I, the World War II, the Cold War, the Civil Rights, the Modern America, the Colonial America, the Founding, the Early Republic and Expansion, the Jackson and Reform, and the Sectional Crisis and Civil War units. Every lesson has the same working plate so teachers can pick pieces without losing the line of the unit._

## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- What did Reconstruction settle about freedom, citizenship, and the federal Union, and what did it leave open?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's core plan is 18 instructional days. The unit opens in April 1865's aftermath, with the war won and nothing about the peace decided: a president from Tennessee offering the South restoration on easy terms, a federal bureau feeding the hungry and supervising labor contracts, four million people stating their own terms for freedom in petitions, letters, conventions, and schools, and the restored southern governments answering with Black Codes that rebuilt coercion in statute form. It follows the constitutional settlement Congress built against the president's resistance: the Thirteenth Amendment ratified, the Civil Rights Act of 1866 passed over a veto, the Fourteenth Amendment written and then imposed as the price of readmission by the Reconstruction Acts, a president impeached and acquitted by one vote. It follows the new political order those measures created, Black men voting, organizing in Union Leagues, and sitting in Congress and the statehouses, and the Fifteenth Amendment closing the amendment trilogy. It follows freedom built on the ground, in schools freedpeople taxed themselves to sustain, in independent churches, and in a land settlement that never came, whose absence bent free labor into sharecropping and the crop lien. It follows the counterrevolution: the Ku Klux Klan's night riding, the federal Enforcement Acts that broke the first Klan, and then the slower retreat, amnesty for the old leadership, a Supreme Court narrowing the amendments in their first reading, a depression turning northern eyes homeward, paramilitary leagues operating by daylight, and a last civil rights act passed and left unenforced. It closes with the disputed election of 1876 and the Compromise of 1877, the last troops withdrawn, the amendments standing in the Constitution and their enforcement standing down. Three boundaries hold. The Civil War itself is prior knowledge, taught in the Sectional Crisis and Civil War unit; its campaigns, emancipation as a war measure, and Appomattox are named in prose and nowhere retaught. The Thirteenth Amendment's passage by Congress on January 31, 1865, is likewise prior knowledge, a wartime event the Sectional unit named; its ratification in December 1865 and the settlement built on it are this unit's ground, and this unit's Thirteenth Amendment definition is carried character for character from the Sectional unit's Lesson 8 definition, in the guided notes, the deck notes, and the study guide, exactly as the Sectional build directed. The Gilded Age is not this unit's ground: no industrialization, big business, or labor movement content is taught, and the story stops at 1877; the long shadow Reconstruction cast across the century after it is named in framing and closing prose only, and no twentieth century movement content is taught. The era is taught with its peoples in the frame throughout: freedpeople defining freedom in wages, land, family, and schools and building the institutions to hold it; Black voters and officeholders exercising a citizenship the law had just written; white southerners divided among Unionists, Republicans, and a counterrevolution that organized terror as politics; and a North whose commitment, tested by depression and weariness, set the settlement's limits. Period texts are public domain, but the edition still counts: every quotation names its transcription or facsimile edition, and the audited TPSP bank file for this era (reconstruction.json, 22 records) is the first source wherever it carries the text. One image discipline governs the unit: the violence of the counterrevolution is taught in testimony and text, stated plainly and never lingered on, and no image of violence, of the Klan's work, or of any victim is used as decoration in any student document or deck; the era's cartoons, which the bank presents described, are taught from the bank's descriptions where no verified public domain file is reproduced.

**Topics (the six state-neutral era topics the planner's coverage model uses):**

1. The postwar settlement: presidential Reconstruction, the Bureau, and the Black Codes
2. Freedom in law: the Thirteenth Amendment and the Civil Rights Act
3. Citizenship remade: the Fourteenth Amendment and congressional Reconstruction
4. The vote and the new politics: the Fifteenth Amendment and Black officeholding
5. Freedom on the ground: land, labor, schools, and churches
6. The counterrevolution and the end: violence, retreat, and 1877

## Lesson 1: After Appomattox: Presidential Reconstruction

The war ends and the questions it left stand in a row. Lincoln's assassination and Johnson's succession, stated plainly as the frame. Johnson's restoration program at working level: the Amnesty Proclamation of May 29, 1865, pardons, provisional governors, the state conventions' three required acts, and readmission without congressional terms. The Freedmen's Bureau at working level: created March 3, 1865, as a wartime agency, its rations, hospitals, labor contracts, courts, and schools, and its agents' impossible position between freedpeople and planters. Freedom arriving unevenly: General Orders No. 3 at Galveston, June 19, 1865. The South's answer: the restored governments elect ex-Confederate leaders, and the Black Codes rebuild labor control in law, Mississippi's Vagrant Law read at working level. Freedpeople's first answers: the Edisto Island petition against losing the land they had settled, and Jourdon Anderson's letter pricing thirty two years of stolen labor. The reading closes on the fall of 1865's open question, whether the settlement Johnson had built could stand when Congress returned in December. Lesson question: "What did Johnson's restoration give back to the South, and what did it leave freedpeople to fight for?"

**Activity concept:** The settlement on paper. Students work five records, Johnson's restoration terms (a labeled teaching summary built from the Amnesty Proclamation of May 29, 1865, and the North Carolina proclamation, the public record), the Mississippi Vagrant Law (audited bank record), the Edisto Island petition (audited bank record), Jourdon Anderson's letter (audited bank record), and General Orders No. 3 (audited bank record), and for each one state whose freedom the record enlarged or narrowed and by what instrument, then judge which record a freed family in December 1865 would have felt most in its daily life, and defend the judgment from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** Reconstruction, Andrew Johnson, presidential Reconstruction, Freedmen's Bureau, Black Codes, Amnesty Proclamation, Jourdon Anderson

## Lesson 2: The Thirteenth Amendment and the Meaning of Freedom

Ratification as a fight, not a formality: the amendment's text read whole, the border states and the restored South ratifying under Johnson's pressure, Georgia's vote completing the count, and the December 1865 proclamation of ratification. Section 2's enforcement power, the clause that made Congress freedom's author and not only its witness. The exception clause read carefully and stated plainly, the seed of convict leasing named in one sober sentence. What freedom meant, stated by freedpeople themselves: the freedmen's conventions of 1865 and 1866 demanding land, wages, family protection, schools, and the vote; families searching for sold kin; marriages legalized; names chosen. Congress answers the Black Codes: Trumbull's Civil Rights Act of 1866, defining citizenship and its rights over the amendment's new power, Johnson's veto and the first override of a major bill in the country's history, and the Freedmen's Bureau bill fight beside it. The reading closes on the question December 1866 left open, whether a statute could hold against a president and a hostile South, or whether freedom needed the Constitution itself rewritten again. Lesson question: "What did the Thirteenth Amendment settle, and why did Congress decide a statute was not enough?"

**Activity concept:** Freedom's terms, drafted twice. Students work five records, the Thirteenth Amendment in full (audited bank record), Iowa's ratification resolution (audited bank record), the Civil Rights Act of 1866, Section 1 (audited bank record), a labeled teaching summary of the freedmen's conventions' demands built from the public record of the 1865 and 1866 conventions, and Johnson's veto message of the Civil Rights bill (quoted from the American Presidency Project transcription if the builder verifies it, otherwise a labeled teaching summary, the choice stated in SOURCES.md), and for each one state which of freedpeople's stated terms the record answered and which it left standing, then judge whether, by the end of 1866, freedom existed more in law or in life, and defend the judgment from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** Thirteenth Amendment, Civil Rights Act of 1866, Radical Republicans, Thaddeus Stevens, Lyman Trumbull, freedmen's conventions, freedpeople

## Lesson 3: Congress Takes Charge: The Fourteenth Amendment and the Reconstruction Acts

The Joint Committee on Reconstruction hears the South testify about itself. The Fourteenth Amendment drafted as the Civil Rights Act's constitutional armor and more: Section 1 read at working level, citizenship defined by birth, privileges or immunities, due process, equal protection, and Sections 2 through 4's machinery of representation, disability, and debt. Tennessee ratifies and returns; the other ten refuse, and the amendment hangs. Violence instructs the North: Memphis in May 1866 and New Orleans in July 1866, stated plainly and briefly, and the elections of 1866 return a Congress determined to act over the president. The Reconstruction Acts of March 1867 at working level: the ten states set aside, five military districts, registration of an electorate that includes Black men, conventions, new constitutions with Black suffrage, ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment as the price of readmission. The collision with Johnson: the Tenure of Office Act, Stanton's removal, impeachment in February 1868, and acquittal by one vote in May, the presidency weakened and the congressional settlement intact. The reading closes on the summer of 1868, the amendment proclaimed ratified and seven states readmitted, with the settlement now resting on an electorate the acts had created. Lesson question: "How did Congress rewrite the Constitution and the South's governments between 1866 and 1868, and at what cost?"

**Activity concept:** The amendment and the machinery. Students work five records, the Fourteenth Amendment, Section 1 (audited bank record), a labeled teaching summary of the Reconstruction Acts' terms built from the Statutes at Large, a labeled teaching summary of the Memphis and New Orleans violence of 1866 built from the congressional investigations' public record, the Articles of Impeachment against Johnson (quoted from the Avalon Project or House record transcription if the builder verifies it, otherwise a labeled teaching summary, the choice stated in SOURCES.md), and Waud's "The First Vote" (audited bank record, the image presented as the bank presents it), and for each one state which failure of the 1865 settlement the record answered, then judge whether Congress in 1867 acted as a conqueror, a constitution maker, or both, and defend the judgment from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** Fourteenth Amendment, Reconstruction Acts, military districts, Joint Committee on Reconstruction, Tenure of Office Act, Edwin M. Stanton, impeachment

## Lesson 4: The New Political Order: The Vote and Black Officeholding

An electorate made in a year: registration under the acts, the Union Leagues organizing in churches and schoolhouses, and some seven hundred thousand Black voters enrolled where none had voted before. The conventions of 1867 and 1868 and the constitutions they wrote, public schools, civil rights, and fairer taxes, the most democratic charters the South had known. The governments that followed and the people who staffed them: carpetbaggers and scalawags named as the hostile labels they were and the coalitions they hid, Black legislators by the hundreds, and at the top Hiram Rhodes Revels taking Jefferson Davis's Senate seat in 1870 and P. B. S. Pinchback governing Louisiana in the winter of 1872 and 1873, with Blanche K. Bruce's full Senate term closing the lesson's roster. The Fifteenth Amendment: proposed February 1869, ratified February 1870, its text read carefully for what it forbids and what it leaves open, the loopholes of literacy and poll taxes named as the text's silences, and the woman suffrage split stated plainly, the alliance of abolition and women's rights breaking over whether this was the Negro's hour. The reading closes on 1870's doubled fact, a democracy broader than the country had ever run, resting on federal protection it could not itself supply. Lesson question: "Who governed the reconstructed South, and what did the Fifteenth Amendment settle and leave open?"

**Activity concept:** The new electorate speaks and is pictured. Students work five records, the Joint Resolution proposing the Fifteenth Amendment (audited bank record), Revels's speech on desegregating the District of Columbia schools (audited bank record), Waud's "The First Vote" (audited bank record, presented as the bank presents it), Nast's "Emancipation" (audited bank record, presented as the bank presents it), and a labeled teaching summary of the 1868 South Carolina convention and legislature built from the public record, and for each one state what claim about Black citizenship the record makes and who is making it, then judge which record a skeptical northern voter of 1870 would have found hardest to answer, and defend the judgment from the documents.

## Lesson 5: Freedom on the Ground: Land, Labor, Schools, and Churches

The land question at the center: Special Field Orders No. 15 and the forty thousand settled on the coastal reserve, Johnson's restoration of the land to its pardoned owners, the Edisto petitioners' answer carried forward from Lesson 1, Stevens's confiscation plan argued and defeated, and the Southern Homestead Act's poor acres. What freedpeople built without land: the labor contract's discipline and its evasions, the refusal of gang labor, women withdrawing field labor to their households, and the compromise nobody chose on purpose, sharecropping, read through the standard terms of a share contract stated as a labeled summary, with the crop lien's arithmetic worked plainly, the furnishing merchant's prices, the year's settlement, and the debt that renewed itself. The Freedmen's Savings Bank, savings as freedom's discipline and the 1874 failure's cost. The schools: freedpeople taxing themselves, building schoolhouses before churches, a quarter million students by 1870, the teachers Black and northern, and the schools' enemies named. The churches: the AME and the independent Baptist congregations, the first property Black communities owned, doubling as schoolhouses, league halls, and the new politics' organizing ground. The reading closes on the lesson's doubled verdict, a people building freedom's institutions at speed out of nearly nothing, on an economic base the land settlement had denied them. Lesson question: "What did freedpeople build, and what did the land settlement they never got cost what they built?"

**Activity concept:** Freedom's ledger. Students work five records, Special Field Orders No. 15 (quoted from the National Archives or Freedmen and Southern Society transcription if the builder verifies it, otherwise a labeled teaching summary, the choice stated in SOURCES.md), a labeled teaching summary of a sharecropping contract's standard terms, built from the bank's Louisiana contract record and disclosed on its face as drawn from an 1879 contract because the system's standard terms are what the lesson teaches, a labeled teaching summary of the freedmen's school movement built from the Bureau reports' public record, a labeled teaching summary of the Freedmen's Savings Bank's charter and failure built from the public record, and the Edisto Island petition revisited (audited bank record) as the land question's own statement, and for each one state what asset, skill, or protection the record put in freedpeople's hands and what it left in others', then judge whether the sharecropping settlement was a compromise, a defeat, or both at once for different families, and defend the judgment from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** Special Field Orders No. 15, forty acres and a mule, sharecropping, crop lien, freedmen's schools, African Methodist Episcopal Church, Freedmen's Savings Bank

## Lesson 6: The Counterrevolution: The Klan and the Enforcement Acts

Terror organized as politics: the Klan founded at Pulaski in 1866 as a social club and rebuilt within a year as the Invisible Empire, its dens, disguises, and night riding aimed at the Republican electorate the acts had created, its targets teachers, officeholders, and voters named in the testimony. The testimony read at length and with sobriety: Abram Colby of Georgia, former legislator, dragged from his house in 1869 and whipped for his politics, telling a congressional committee in 1872 what was done to him and what he refused to sell, his daughter's name spoken in the record and never used as decoration here. Forrest's dissolution order of 1869 and his congressional denials, stated as the record gives them. The federal answer: the Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871, the Ku Klux Klan Act's sections read at working level, federal supervisors and marshals at the polls, Grant's suspension of habeas corpus in nine South Carolina counties in October 1871, the hundreds of indictments, and the first Klan broken by 1872, the proof that federal power, used, worked. The reading closes on the answer's price and premise, that protection depended on a northern will to sustain it, and on the violence changing its clothes rather than dying, the rifle clubs and leagues of the middle seventies waiting in the next lesson. Lesson question: "What did the Klan's terror aim at, and what did it take to break the first Klan?"

**Activity concept:** Testimony and answer. Students work five records, Abram Colby's testimony (audited bank record), the Enforcement Act of 1871 (audited bank record), a labeled teaching summary of a second Klan testimony built from the 1872 Joint Select Committee report's public record, a labeled teaching summary of Grant's October 1871 proclamation and the South Carolina prosecutions built from the public record, and a labeled teaching summary of Forrest's 1871 congressional testimony built from the public record, and for each one state what the record shows terror doing or law answering, then judge whether the Enforcement Acts are better described as civil rights law, counterinsurgency, or both, and defend the judgment from the documents. The testimony is taught with documentary sobriety: stated plainly, never lingered on, and no image of the Klan or its work is used anywhere in the lesson.

**Vocabulary:** Ku Klux Klan, Nathan Bedford Forrest, Abram Colby, night riding, Enforcement Acts, Ku Klux Klan Act, federal marshals

## Lesson 7: The Retreat: Courts, Panic, and the Leagues

The settlement dismantled by inches. Amnesty in 1872 returns the old leadership to eligibility, and the Liberal Republican revolt makes retreat respectable in the party that built Reconstruction. The courts read the amendments narrowly at their first opportunity: the Slaughter-House Cases of April 1873, Miller's syllabus distinguishing national from state citizenship and leaving the great body of civil rights where the war found them, the ruling's logic stated fairly and its consequence stated plainly. The Panic of 1873 and the long depression turn the North to its own hard times; elections in 1874 give the House to the Democrats for the first time since the war. The counterrevolution changes its uniform: Louisiana's White League states white supremacy as an open platform in 1874, fights at Coushatta and at Liberty Place in New Orleans, and is answered by federal troops and then by northern weariness at the answering. The last assertion: Charles Sumner's Civil Rights Act, passed in 1875 after his death, guaranteeing public accommodations and jury service, a Black congressman's floor defense of it in Hays's speech, and the act's life as law mostly unenforced, its later fate named in one sentence of framing. Nast's two 1874 cartoons read as the North's mirror, "Worse Than Slavery" indicting the league and the Klan together and "Colored Rule" carrying the era's caricature, taught as evidence of the image war and of its maker's own limits. The reading closes on 1876 approaching with three southern states still Republican and the country's will to hold them visibly failing. Lesson question: "By what steps, legal, economic, and violent, did the country retreat from the settlement between 1872 and 1876?"

**Activity concept:** The retreat in five records. Students work five records, the Slaughter-House Cases syllabus (audited bank record), the Louisiana White League Platform (audited bank record), Charles Hays's speech reaffirming the rights of African Americans (audited bank record), the Civil Rights Act of 1875, Sections 1 and 2 (audited bank record), and Nast's "Worse Than Slavery" (audited bank record, presented as the bank presents it), and for each one state which pillar of the settlement the record raised or struck, then rank the retreat's causes, law, money, violence, and weariness, and defend the ranking from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** Slaughter-House Cases, Amnesty Act of 1872, Liberal Republicans, Panic of 1873, White League, Civil Rights Act of 1875, Thomas Nast

## Lesson 8: 1876 and the End: What Was Settled, What Was Left Open

The unit's closing lesson and the course's early survey's close. The election of 1876 at working level: Tilden's quarter million popular vote majority, the four states' disputed returns, Florida, Louisiana, South Carolina, and Oregon, and Frost's cartoon of October 1876 picturing the Democratic campaign's southern arithmetic before the votes were counted. The Electoral Commission's fifteen members and its 8 to 7 awards. The Compromise of 1877: the Wormley House bargain's terms stated plainly, a cabinet seat, internal improvements, and the withdrawal of the last troops, and April 1877's orders removing the soldiers from the statehouses at Columbia and New Orleans. Then the audit the whole unit has prepared, conducted on the unit's own documents: the three amendments read once more as the settled law they remain, citizenship defined, slavery abolished with its enforcement clause, the vote protected against racial denial in the text; and beside them the open accounts, land never redistributed, protection withdrawn, the amendments' enforcement left to a future the lesson names only as framing, the long work the settlement's survivors and their children would inherit. Hayes's inaugural pledge of honest local self government read as the bargain's public face. The reading closes the course's first half where the Sectional unit closed its argument, on the distance between what the law had settled and what the country's will would hold. Lesson question: "What did Reconstruction settle, and what did its ending leave open?"

**Activity concept:** The settlement audited. Students return to five records with the whole unit behind them, the Thirteenth Amendment in full (audited bank record), the Fourteenth Amendment, Section 1 (audited bank record), the Joint Resolution proposing the Fifteenth Amendment (audited bank record), Frost's "Of Course He Wants to Vote the Democratic Ticket" (audited bank record, presented as the bank presents it), and Hayes's First Inaugural Address (quoted from the American Presidency Project transcription if the builder verifies it, otherwise a labeled teaching summary, the choice stated in SOURCES.md), and for each one enter it in a two column audit, settled or left open, with the evidence for the entry, then judge whether 1877 ended Reconstruction or only ended its federal enforcement, and defend the judgment from the documents. The reprinting of the three amendments here is deliberate and disclosed: the closing lesson audits the settlement in its own texts.

**Vocabulary:** Compromise of 1877, Rutherford B. Hayes, Samuel J. Tilden, Electoral Commission, Redeemers, home rule, Redemption
