The Confederation and the Road to Philadelphia

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. A League of Friendship, by Design

The Articles of Confederation were the first constitution of the United States, drafted by Congress in 1777 and ratified by all thirteen states in 1781. Maryland held up ratification for four years, until the states with western land claims surrendered them to the union. The design had reasons. A generation that had fought a distant legislature's taxes built the government it thought safe. The Articles joined the states in a firm league of friendship. Each state kept its sovereignty and held one vote in Congress. Congress could declare war, make treaties, coin money, and run a post office. It could not levy a tax, regulate commerce, or compel a state to obey. Major measures needed nine states, and every amendment needed all thirteen.

B. An Empty Treasury

The limits showed first in money. Congress paid its bills by requisition, asking each state to supply a share of the common treasury, and each state decided for itself what it would pay. By the middle of the 1780s they were sending a small fraction of what Congress asked, and no law could make them send more. The Continental Army went unpaid, its officers' promised pensions unfunded. In March 1783, at Newburgh, New York, discontent among the officers came close to open defiance. Washington met them, spoke against any threat of force, and talked them down. The union kept its army's loyalty that day because one man persuaded it, not because its government could pay what it owed.

C. A Union That Could Not Act

Abroad, the same weakness read as unreliability. The peace treaty of 1783 required the states to let British creditors collect prewar debts and to treat Loyalist property fairly. State laws blocked both, and Congress could not make the states comply. Britain answered by holding its frontier posts at Niagara, Detroit, and Michilimackinac, on land the treaty had granted the United States. Spain closed the Mississippi River at New Orleans to American trade in 1784, cutting western farmers off from their outlet to the sea, and Congress was too weak to answer. At home, British goods flooded American ports while American merchants were barred from the British West Indies. New York laid duties on firewood from New Jersey and produce from Connecticut, and its neighbors answered in kind.

D. The Land Ordinances: The Great Achievement Begins

The record was not all failure, and its greatest work began with land. Maryland's stand forced the landed states to cede their western claims, creating a national domain held in common north and west of the Ohio River. In the Land Ordinance of 1785, Congress set the method for turning that domain into farms and revenue. Surveyors would divide the land into townships of six miles square, each township into thirty six sections of one square mile. One section in every township was reserved for public schools. That grid still shows from the air across the Midwest, in straight roads and square fields. A Congress that could not collect a tax had drawn the survey lines along which a continent would settle.

E. The Northwest Ordinance and Virginia's Statute

Congress also built the machine that turned territory into states. The Northwest Ordinance of July 13, 1787 governed the territory north of the Ohio River. It promised new states admission on equal footing with the original thirteen, guaranteed trial by jury and freedom of worship, encouraged schools, and barred slavery from the whole territory. It also presumed to settle land whose Native owners had never sold it, freedom's territory surveyed across other peoples' homes. Virginia's legislature passed the Statute for Religious Freedom, written by Thomas Jefferson in 1777 and carried through in 1786 under James Madison's management. It disestablished Virginia's church and declared that no person could be compelled to support any worship or suffer in body or goods for religious opinion. The decade could legislate like a founding age.

F. Debt, Taxes, and Shays's Rebellion

The crisis came from the war's debts. Governments owed soldiers and lenders, and many soldiers' certificates had passed to speculators who bought them cheap. States taxed in hard money to pay those debts. Western farmers, short of coin, faced foreclosure and debtors' prison. Some legislatures answered with paper money and laws delaying collection. Massachusetts chose heavy taxes in specie. From August 1786, crowds of indebted farmers, many of them veterans, closed the courts there to stop foreclosure judgments. Their leaders included Daniel Shays, a captain who had fought at Bunker Hill and Saratoga. A militia financed by private subscription among Boston merchants scattered the rebels at the Springfield arsenal in January 1787, where four men died. By February the movement was broken. Massachusetts then eased its taxes, and its voters turned the governor out.

G. The Road to Philadelphia

Reform had begun before the shooting. In 1785, Virginia and Maryland commissioners met at Mount Vernon, settled a Potomac River dispute, and proposed a wider meeting on trade. The Annapolis Convention of September 1786 drew only five states, and in Hamilton's draft report it called instead for a convention at Philadelphia in May 1787 to render the federal government adequate to the exigencies of the union. Shays's Rebellion gave that call its force among men of property who feared the republic was coming apart. In February 1787 Congress endorsed a convention for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation. That summer fifty five delegates from twelve states, Rhode Island sending none, traveled to Philadelphia. Many were officers, lawyers, merchants, and planters of the Revolution, and several states would have been surprised at what their instructions produced by September.

Teacher Answer Key

1. The design of the Articles. The Articles joined the thirteen states in a firm league of friendship in which each state retained its sovereignty and held one vote in Congress, whatever its size. Congress could declare war, make treaties, coin money, and run a post office (any three earn credit). It could not levy a tax, could not regulate commerce, and could not compel a state to obey. Major measures needed nine states and amendments needed all thirteen. The design was deliberate: a generation that had fought a distant legislature's taxes built a center it could starve. (Blueprint 11)

2. Requisitions and Newburgh. Congress paid its bills by requisition, asking each state to supply a share of the common treasury in proportion set by Congress, with the taxes laid by the state legislatures themselves. Each state decided what it would actually pay, and by the middle of the 1780s the states were sending a small fraction of what Congress asked, with no legal way for Congress to compel more. The army went unpaid and the officers' promised pensions unfunded. At Newburgh in March 1783, discontent among the officers came close to open defiance of Congress, and Washington talked his officers down in person. The point students should state: the crisis was settled by persuasion, not by any power the government held. (Blueprint 11)

3. The posts and the river. The Treaty of Paris required the states to let British creditors collect prewar debts and to treat Loyalist property fairly. State laws and courts blocked both, and Congress, which had made the treaty, held no power to make a state comply with it. Britain cited those violations to justify keeping its garrisons at Niagara, Detroit, and Michilimackinac, on land the treaty had granted the United States. Spain closed the Mississippi at New Orleans in 1784, cutting western farmers off from their outlet to the sea; answering Spain required either trade concessions Congress could not deliver or force it could not pay for. In both cases the failure ran the same way: Congress spoke for the union abroad but could not bind the states at home. (Blueprint 11)

4. The ordinances, and the contradiction. The Land Ordinance of 1785 set the survey of the national domain: townships of six miles square divided into thirty six sections of one square mile, land sold to raise revenue, and one section in every township reserved for public schools. The Northwest Ordinance of July 13, 1787 governed the territory north of the Ohio: it promised states formed there admission on an equal footing with the original thirteen, guaranteed trial by jury and freedom of worship, encouraged schools, and barred slavery from the whole territory. The contradiction: the territory whose liberty the Ordinance guaranteed was land whose Native owners had never sold it, so the Confederation's monument rests on land taken as if it were empty. The Virginia Statute belongs in a full answer as the decade's second founding text, disestablishing Virginia's church and protecting religious opinion from civil penalty. (Blueprint 12)

5. The lesson question, answered. The government failed to hold the union together because its design denied it the powers a union needs: no tax, no commerce power, no way to compel a state, and amendment only by unanimity. The road to Philadelphia ran through the states' own attempts at repair. Virginia and Maryland commissioners met at Mount Vernon in 1785, settled their Potomac dispute, and proposed a wider meeting. The Annapolis Convention of September 1786 drew five states and, under Hamilton's draft, called for a convention at Philadelphia in May 1787 to render the federal government adequate to the exigencies of the union. Shays's Rebellion, suppressed by a privately financed militia while Congress watched, gave the call its urgency. Congress endorsed the convention in February 1787 for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles, and fifty five delegates from twelve states attended. (Blueprint 11 and 12)
