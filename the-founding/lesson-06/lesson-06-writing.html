Writing Task: Failure, Draft, or a Job Without Tools?

The prompt. The Articles of Confederation governed the United States from 1781 to the Philadelphia convention of 1787. Answer this question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Were the Articles a failure, an unfinished draft, or a government overtaken by a job it was never given the tools for? Argue from the documents in the Confederation File. A strong answer weighs the achievements at full value before it judges the failures, separates what the design withheld from what the men of the decade did with what they had, and ends by explaining why the design could or could not have been repaired from inside. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each one supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson documents. Write the history in your own sentences. Quotations from the records belong inside quotation marks, spelled as the records spell them.

Evidence Bank

1. Article II of the Articles provides that each state "retains its sovereignty, freedom and independence, and every Power, Jurisdiction and right, which is not by this confederation expressly delegated to the United States, in Congress assembled."

2. Article VIII supplies the common treasury by requisition: the taxes for each state's proportion "shall be laid and levied by the authority and direction of the legislatures of the several states." By the middle of the 1780s the states were sending a small fraction of what Congress asked, and the army's officers, their pensions unfunded, had to be talked down by Washington at Newburgh in 1783.

3. Article XIII bars every alteration unless it is "agreed to in a congress of the united states, and be afterwards confirmed by the legislatures of every state." One state could block any repair, including a tax power.

4. The Northwest Ordinance of July 13, 1787 promised states formed north of the Ohio admission "on an equal footing with the original States in all respects whatever," guaranteed freedom of worship, encouraged schools, and declared that "there shall be neither slavery nor involuntary servitude in the said territory," while its proviso allowed a fugitive from an original state to be reclaimed, and its good faith clause promised Native peoples their lands would never be taken without consent.

5. Virginia's Statute for Religious Freedom, passed January 16, 1786, enacted that "no man shall be compelled to frequent or support any religious worship, place or ministry whatsoever," and that religious opinion "shall in no wise diminish, enlarge or affect" a man's civil capacities. It was a state's law, on a subject Congress had no power to touch.

6. In 1786 and 1787, indebted Massachusetts farmers under leaders including Daniel Shays closed the courts to stop foreclosures. Congress could neither supply troops nor pay for a force. A militia financed by private subscription among Boston merchants scattered the rebels at the Springfield arsenal in January 1787, where four men died, and the state then eased its taxes and voted its governor out.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that chooses one of the three verdicts, or a stated combination, and rests it on the design's own text rather than on the decade's reputation. (Blueprint 11)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Items 1 and 2 establish the failure case in the Articles' own words: sovereignty reserved to the states, revenue left to state legislatures, an unpaid army and a Newburgh crisis settled by persuasion. (Blueprint 11) Item 3 establishes the repair question: unanimity for amendments. Items 4 and 5 establish the achievement case: the Northwest Ordinance's statehood machine and slavery bar of 1787, and Virginia's Statute of 1786, with item 4's proviso and good faith clause marking where the achievement itself fell short. (Blueprint 12) Item 6 supplies the crisis that turned argument into a convention. (Blueprint 11)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive distinction is between powers withheld and powers exercised: an essay that calls the Confederation simply weak, without noting that it legislated for a continent where no state blocked it, has used half the file.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest rival verdict. For failure: that a government which produced the Northwest Ordinance and held a union together through a war and a peace was not nothing, answered by asking what it could compel. For unfinished draft: that drafts get finished, and Article XIII's unanimity rule meant this one could only be replaced, answered by noting that replacement was the finish the Annapolis call of 1786 already pointed toward.

Model opening for teacher reference. The Articles were a government overtaken by a job it was never given the tools for. The design was honest about its limits: Article II kept sovereignty in the states, Article VIII left the treasury to state legislatures, and the men who wrote those clauses had just fought a king's tax collectors and meant the center to stay weak. Then the decade handed that league a war debt it could not collect, a treaty it could not enforce, a continent to survey and govern, and an armed rising in Massachusetts it could only watch. A full model continues in this pattern, weighs the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 and the Virginia Statute of 1786 as the counterweight, answers the failure verdict directly, and closes on Article XIII: a design that required all thirteen states to approve any repair had written its own replacement as the only working exit.

Common weak answers. Listing the Confederation's failures without the ordinances argues from the textbook verdict and misses the lesson's instruction to weigh the achievements first. Treating Shays's Rebellion as the cause of the Constitution, rather than as the crisis that gave a repair movement already under way its urgency, reverses the chronology the reading establishes. Quoting Washington's first wish on slavery, from Station 4, as if it were a policy confuses a private letter with a law; the bank's item 4 proviso is the corrective evidence.
