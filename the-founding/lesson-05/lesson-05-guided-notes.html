Guided Notes: Winning and Losing: The War's Home Fronts and Its Peoples

Lesson question: Who else fought, paid, and waited through the war, and what did its promise mean to them?

Unit question: Why did the colonies break with Britain, and what did Americans build in the place of the empire they rejected?

Key Terms

Dunmore's Proclamation: the order of November 1775 by Virginia's royal governor, Lord Dunmore, offering freedom to enslaved people and indentured servants held by rebels if they left their masters and bore arms for the king

inflation: a general rise in prices that shrinks what money can buy, which struck the United States during the war as Congress printed paper money faster than the states could support it

camp followers: the women and children who traveled with the Continental and British armies, cooking, washing, nursing, and carrying supplies, a workforce the armies depended on and paid little or nothing

Deborah Sampson: the Massachusetts woman who enlisted in the Continental Army in 1782 disguised as a man named Robert Shurtliff, served more than a year, and was honorably discharged when her sex was discovered

Joseph Brant: the Mohawk leader, known in his own language as Thayendanegea, who led Haudenosaunee forces allied with Britain during the war and afterward pressed his people's claims to their land

Sullivan expedition: the American campaign of 1779, ordered by Washington, that burned some forty Seneca and Cayuga towns and their cornfields in answer to frontier raids, driving thousands from their homes

gradual emancipation: the method by which northern states ended slavery after the Revolution, freeing children born to enslaved mothers only after years of service, so slavery died slowly rather than at once

Summary table. Households and camp followers: the war asked them to run farms and shops with men gone, to make boycotts and homespun hold, and to cook, wash, nurse, and carry for the armies; it gave them the work of the war at home and in camp, at little or no pay, and in Philadelphia in 1780 it gave their organizing a public result, shirts bought for the army. Black Americans: the war asked them to choose between a British offer of freedom for bearing arms and an American cause that first barred them; it gave some thousands a road out of slavery through flight and service, cost most of Dunmore's recruits their lives to smallpox and fever, and left some five thousand who served the United States to claim freedom they had earned. Native nations: the war asked them to choose a side to shield their land; the Haudenosaunee Confederacy split, and it cost the Seneca and Cayuga some forty towns and their corn in 1779 and the Cherokee towns and land cessions in the South. Loyalists: the war asked them to stand with the king's order; it cost them oaths, property, and banishment, and at the peace some sixty thousand or more left, among them some three thousand Black Loyalists recorded in the Book of Negroes. The reckoning: the era's promises were quoted back in freedom petitions, and the answers were Vermont's constitution of 1777, Pennsylvania's gradual act of 1780, and the Massachusetts court decisions of 1783, slow and partial answers that left slavery standing across the South.

Exit ticket 1. In November 1775 Dunmore offered freedom to enslaved people and indentured servants held by rebels, on the condition that they leave their masters and bear arms for the king. Some hundreds reached his lines and served in an Ethiopian Regiment, and most of them died of smallpox and fever in the camps.

Exit ticket 2. The Oneida and Tuscarora sided with the Americans; the Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, and most Onondaga sided with the British. In 1779 the Sullivan expedition burned about forty Seneca and Cayuga towns and their stored corn and drove thousands of people toward Niagara and a starving winter.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that pairs a fact from Black Americans' war with a fact from the reckoning. The strongest answers run from Dunmore's offer, the flight to British lines, or the five thousand who served the United States on one side to the freedom petitions, Vermont in 1777, Pennsylvania's gradual act of 1780, or the Massachusetts courts in 1783 on the other.
