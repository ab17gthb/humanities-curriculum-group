Writing Task: What Freedom Meant

The prompt. Answer the question in a developed essay of about 300 words: For whom did the Revolution most change what freedom meant, and for whom did it change it least? Argue both halves. A strong answer chooses one people or person for each half, sets out what the war offered them and what it cost them, and weighs what actually stood after 1783. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, drawn from both halves of the bank, and explain how each piece supports your judgment.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and the Whose War activity. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. In November 1775, Lord Dunmore, Virginia's royal governor, proclaimed freedom to enslaved people and indentured servants held by rebels who would bear arms for the king. Some hundreds reached his lines and served in an Ethiopian Regiment whose uniform reportedly bore the words Liberty to Slaves, and most of them died of smallpox and fever in the camps. (Audited TPSP record dunmore-proclamation-1775.)

2. Across the war, thousands of enslaved people ran to British lines wherever the armies passed. The American side first barred Black men from the Continental Army on slaveholders' protests, then enlisted them as manpower ran short. Some five thousand Black men served in American forces, the standard estimate, among them the First Rhode Island Regiment of 1778, raised substantially from enslaved men who earned freedom by enlisting.

3. On March 31, 1776, Abigail Adams asked her husband John to remember the ladies in the new code of laws and not to put unlimited power into the hands of husbands, warning that women would foment a rebellion if they had no voice in the laws. John answered with a laugh and a refusal, and the new state codes left husbands' legal power standing. (Audited TPSP record abigail-adams-remember-the-ladies.)

4. Deborah Sampson enlisted in 1782 as Robert Shurtliff, served more than a year in a light infantry company of the 4th Massachusetts Regiment, was wounded, and was honorably discharged in 1783 when a fever's treatment discovered her sex. Massachusetts later granted her a pension.

5. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy split over the war: the Oneida and Tuscarora sided with the Americans, and the Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, and most Onondaga with the British, Joseph Brant among their leaders. In 1779 the Sullivan expedition burned about forty Seneca and Cayuga towns and their stored corn, and in the peace of 1783 Britain ceded Haudenosaunee land to the United States without Haudenosaunee consent.

6. Some sixty thousand or more Loyalists left the United States at the peace, an estimate. Among them were some three thousand Black Loyalists whose names, ages, and former owners the British recorded in the Book of Negroes at New York in 1783, so their freedom would hold. Many of those families moved again in 1792, to Sierra Leone.

7. Enslaved petitioners in Massachusetts argued in January 1777 that they held a natural and unalienable right to freedom and asked for an act freeing them and their children at twenty one. No act passed. Vermont's constitution of 1777 barred adult slavery, Pennsylvania's act of 1780 freed only the children of enslaved mothers and only after years of service, and the Massachusetts courts ended slavery in 1783 in the cases of Elizabeth Freeman and Quock Walker.

Plan Before You Write

My judgment (one sentence that answers both halves of the question directly):

My evidence picks (bank numbers, at least one from items 1 to 4 and at least one from items 5 to 7) and what each one proves:

The strongest evidence against my judgment, and how I will answer it:

Write the Essay

Write about 300 words. Begin with your judgment, present the case for the half you call most changed, present the case for the half you call least changed, answer the strongest evidence against your judgment in one or two sentences, and end by restating what the record shows.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment that answers both halves of the prompt directly, naming for whom the Revolution most changed what freedom meant and for whom it changed it least, rather than narrating one half. (Blueprint 9 and Blueprint 10)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, with at least one from each half. Bank items 1 through 4 carry the offers and the individual claims: Dunmore's offer and its conditions, the flight to British lines and the five thousand who served the United States, Abigail Adams's request and its refusal, and Sampson's service and discharge. (Blueprint 9) Bank items 5 through 7 carry the settlements: the Haudenosaunee split, the burned towns, and the cession of 1783, the Loyalist evacuation and the Book of Negroes, and the petition of 1777 beside the northern emancipations that followed. (Blueprint 10)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the judgment. The strongest essays weigh what stood after 1783, not only what was offered during the war: an offer of freedom that ended in a fever camp, a written record of freedom that survived an ocean crossing, a pension granted, and a cession signed without consent are different kinds of change, and a full answer says why.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest evidence on the other side, answered from the record rather than dismissed. An essay stressing how much changed for Black Loyalists must answer the camps at Dunmore's lines. An essay stressing how little changed must answer the Massachusetts courts of 1783 and the three thousand names in the Book of Negroes.

Model opening for teacher reference. The Revolution changed what freedom meant most for the people who could take hold of its language or its offers and make them hold in writing, and it changed least for the people whose claims were answered by other men's signatures. A full model continues in this pattern, carries Dunmore's offer beside the Ethiopian Regiment's graves and Abigail Adams's letter beside its laughing answer, carries the burned towns and the cession of 1783 beside the Book of Negroes and the Massachusetts decisions, answers the strongest opposing evidence, and closes on what the record shows.

Common weak answers. One half only (a celebration of the offers, or a catalog of the refusals) does half the task. A judgment that treats Dunmore's proclamation as a general emancipation has misread its parenthesis, and a judgment that treats the northern emancipations as immediate freedom has misread gradual emancipation, whose definition the guided notes carry. A verdict for Sampson as most changed must reckon with the fact that her road was open to almost no one else.
