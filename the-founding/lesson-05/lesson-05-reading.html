Winning and Losing: The War's Home Fronts and Its Peoples

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. A War Run by Civilians

Congress fought the war without the power to tax. It printed paper money to pay soldiers and buy supplies, and as the bills multiplied they sank until a dollar in coin could take hundreds in paper, and the phrase not worth a Continental entered the language. States requisitioned food, wagons, and horses from stripped farms, and fixed prices by law that sellers evaded. At home, wives ran farms and shops with husbands and sons gone, households boycotted British cloth and spun homespun as a political act, and in 1780 the women of Philadelphia raised money door to door to buy shirts for soldiers.

B. The Armies' Own Households

The camp followers, women and children in the thousands, traveled with both armies, cooking, washing, nursing, sewing, and carrying water and cartridges under fire, for rations and little or no pay. The armies could not have moved or fed themselves without them. Some women fought outright. Margaret Corbin took her husband's place at a cannon at Fort Washington in 1776, was wounded, and was pensioned by Congress. Deborah Sampson enlisted in 1782 as Robert Shurtliff, served more than a year in the light infantry, was wounded, and was honorably discharged in 1783 when a fever's treatment discovered her sex. At Monmouth in 1778, Mary Ludwig Hays carried water to the guns. Her story hardened over the following century into the legend of Molly Pitcher, a name the record does not give her: the water carrier is documented, the pitcher is tradition.

C. Women Thinking Politically

In October 1774, fifty one women at Edenton in North Carolina signed a resolve to boycott British tea and cloth, under the lead of Penelope Barker. A London cartoon mocked them, and the mockery is evidence itself, since no one caricatures an act without weight. The resolve stands among the first organized political acts by American women. On March 31, 1776, Abigail Adams wrote to her husband John in Congress asking him to remember the ladies in the new code of laws, to refuse husbands unlimited power, and warning that women would foment a rebellion if they had no voice in the laws. John answered with a laugh and a refusal.

D. Black Americans' War

Lord Dunmore, Virginia's governor, driven from Williamsburg to a warship, proclaimed in November 1775, offering freedom to enslaved people and indentured servants held by rebels who would bear arms for the king. Some hundreds reached his lines and served in an Ethiopian Regiment whose uniform reportedly bore the words Liberty to Slaves, and most died of smallpox and fever in the camps. Wherever the armies passed, thousands of enslaved people ran to British lines, and flight to the enemy became the largest act of self liberation. The American record ran the other way, then bent. Slaveholders' protests kept Black men out of the Continental Army at first. As manpower ran short the ban gave way, and some five thousand Black men served, the standard estimate, among them the First Rhode Island Regiment of 1778, raised substantially from enslaved men who earned freedom by enlisting. Black sailors crewed both navies.

E. Native Nations' War

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy, whose Great Law had kept its peace since before Columbus, broke apart over the war. The Oneida and Tuscarora sided with the Americans. The Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, and most Onondaga sided with the British, under leaders including the Mohawk Joseph Brant, Thayendanegea, educated in Connecticut and presented at court in London, using the British alliance as a shield for his people's land. The frontier war was a war of raids and burnings. Washington answered in 1779 with the Sullivan expedition, some four thousand men ordered to lay waste the Seneca and Cayuga country. It burned about forty towns and their stored corn, driving thousands toward Niagara and a starving winter. The southern states burned Cherokee towns in 1776 after Cherokee attacks on frontier settlements, and Cherokee land cessions began before the war's end. Native nations had chosen for reasons of their own and paid the war's highest price in land.

F. The Loyalists' War

Perhaps a fifth of the white population, the conventional estimate, remained Loyalist, from officeholders to small farmers who wanted the king's order left standing. Through the war they faced test oaths, tarring and feathering, confiscation of their estates, and banishment. Many served in Loyalist regiments, and in the South the Revolution was in part a civil war. At the peace, some sixty thousand or more Loyalists left the new United States, an estimate, for Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Quebec, Britain, Florida, and the Caribbean. Among them were some three thousand Black Loyalists, whose names, ages, and former owners the British recorded at New York in 1783 in the Book of Negroes, so their freedom would hold. Many moved again in 1792, to Sierra Leone in West Africa.

G. The Reckoning the Promises Demanded

The promises were quoted back. Enslaved people in Massachusetts and New Hampshire petitioned for freedom in the era's own language, arguing a natural and unalienable right to the freedom the new states claimed. Vermont's constitution of 1777 barred adult slavery. Pennsylvania's act of 1780 began gradual emancipation, freeing children born to enslaved mothers only after years of service. In Massachusetts the courts ended slavery in 1783, in the cases of Elizabeth Freeman and Quock Walker. George Washington, writing to John Francis Mercer in 1786, called it his first wish to see a plan adopted for abolishing slavery. The man who wrote those words continued to hold people in slavery at Mount Vernon until his death.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Explain how Congress paid for the war and what that payment did to prices and households. Congress had no power to tax, so it printed paper money to pay soldiers and buy supplies. As the bills multiplied they sank in value until a dollar in coin took hundreds in paper, which is the origin of the phrase not worth a Continental. The depreciation was inflation, prices rising as money bought less, felt in every market town. States added requisitions of food, wagons, and horses from farms already stripped by both armies, and price fixing laws that sellers evaded. The household burden fell heavily on women, who ran farms and shops with men gone, made boycotts and homespun into political acts, and in Philadelphia in 1780 raised money door to door to buy shirts for the army. Full credit requires the printing, the depreciation, and one household fact.

2. Set the camp followers beside Corbin, Sampson, and Hays. The camp followers, women and children in the thousands with both armies, cooked, washed, nursed, sewed, and carried water and cartridges under fire for rations and little or no pay, and the reading states the armies could not have moved or fed themselves without them. Margaret Corbin took her husband's place at a cannon at Fort Washington in 1776, was wounded, and was pensioned by Congress. Deborah Sampson enlisted in 1782 as Robert Shurtliff, served over a year in the light infantry, was wounded, and was honorably discharged in 1783 when a fever's treatment discovered her sex. Mary Ludwig Hays carried water at Monmouth in 1778. The line the reading draws is this: Hays the water carrier is documented, while Molly Pitcher, the name later tradition gave her, is legend built over the following century, and students should not merge the two.

3. State Dunmore's offer and the two facts set against it. Dunmore's Proclamation of November 1775 offered freedom to enslaved people and indentured servants held by rebels, on the condition that they leave their masters and bear arms for the king. Some hundreds reached his lines and served in an Ethiopian Regiment whose uniform reportedly bore the words Liberty to Slaves. Against the offer the reading sets the camps: most of those men died of smallpox and fever. It also sets the longer record: enslaved people ran to British lines in the thousands all through the war, while the American side first barred Black men from the Continental Army on slaveholders' protests, then enlisted them as manpower ran short, some five thousand serving in all by the standard estimate, including the First Rhode Island Regiment of 1778, raised substantially from enslaved men who earned freedom by enlisting.

4. Contrast the Haudenosaunee and Loyalist wars. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy split: the Oneida and Tuscarora sided with the Americans, and the Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, and most Onondaga with the British, Joseph Brant among their leaders, using the alliance as a shield for their land. In 1779 the Sullivan expedition, some four thousand men, burned about forty Seneca and Cayuga towns and their stored corn and drove thousands toward Niagara and a starving winter. The Loyalists, perhaps a fifth of the white population by the conventional estimate, faced oaths, tarring and feathering, confiscation, and banishment at home, and at the peace some sixty thousand or more left the United States, an estimate. Among them were some three thousand Black Loyalists, whose names, ages, and former owners the British recorded in the Book of Negroes at New York in 1783 so their freedom would hold against American claims.

5. Answer the lesson question using the petitions and Washington's letter together. Strong answers hold the claim and the record in one paragraph. The war's promise was quoted back at its makers: enslaved petitioners in Massachusetts and New Hampshire argued from a natural and unalienable right to freedom, the era's own words. The answers were real and partial. Vermont's constitution of 1777 barred adult slavery, Pennsylvania's act of 1780 freed only the children of enslaved mothers and only after years of service, and the Massachusetts courts ended slavery in 1783 in the cases of Elizabeth Freeman and Quock Walker. Washington's 1786 letter to John Francis Mercer, calling abolition his first wish, stands beside his own record of holding people in slavery at Mount Vernon until his death. A paragraph that gives the petitions without the slow answers, or the answers without the letter's contradiction, has answered half the question.
