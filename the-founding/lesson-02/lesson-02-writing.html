Writing Task: How a Protest over Tea Became a War

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: How did a protest over tea become a war? A strong answer traces the steps in order, the Tea Act and the Tea Party, the Intolerable Acts, the colonies answering together, and the march to Concord, and it judges the steps, naming the one that did most to make reconciliation impossible and answering the strongest rival step. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording.

Evidence Bank

1. The Tea Act of May 1773 let the East India Company sell tea directly through its own agents, cutting out colonial merchants, at a lower price, with the Townshend duty still on the tea. On December 16, 1773, Boston men disguised as Mohawks emptied 342 chests into the harbor in about three hours, harming nothing else. Hewes, remembering the night in 1834, insisted no disorder took place.

2. The Boston Port Act of 1774 closed the harbor from June 1 until the town paid for the destroyed tea. The Massachusetts Government Act rewrote the colony's charter, and colonists counted the Quebec Act as a fifth blow. A London print of 1774 showed American resistance to a British audience as violent mob rule.

3. In the fall of 1774, fifty six delegates from twelve colonies met at Philadelphia. The Declaration and Resolves claimed the colonists had never ceded to any power a right to dispose of their life, liberty, and property without consent, and the Association enforced a boycott through elected committees.

4. On April 19, 1775, Barker's diary recorded that the colonists fired one or two shots and that his men rushed in and fired without orders. The militiamen's sworn testimony of April 25 recorded that the regulars fired on a dispersing company, no man in it firing first. Eight Americans died at Lexington; the standard counts give some 273 British casualties against about 95 American.

5. The Second Continental Congress created the Continental Army under George Washington of Virginia. On June 17, 1775, militia on Breed's Hill twice threw back British assaults before their ammunition failed, and the British victory cost more than a thousand casualties.

6. In July 1775 Congress sent the Olive Branch Petition, professing loyalty and begging the king to interpose. George III answered with a Proclamation of Rebellion, and the Prohibitory Act put the colonies outside the king's protection. At the turn of 1776 the colonies had an army and governments of their own in fact, with independence not yet declared.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the lesson question and names a decisive step. Any of the four steps earns full credit when judged against a rival: the Tea Party, the Intolerable Acts, the colonies uniting and arming, or the shooting at Lexington. (Blueprint 3 and Blueprint 4)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank items 1 through 3 carry the protest and the punishment: the Tea Act's trap and the disciplined destruction of 342 chests, the Port Act's price and the rewritten charter, and Congress speaking for twelve colonies through the Declaration and Resolves and the Association. (Blueprint 3) Bank items 4 through 6 carry the war: the two irreconcilable Lexington records and the day's standard counts, Washington's army and the cost of Bunker Hill, and the Olive Branch answered by the Proclamation of Rebellion. (Blueprint 4) A full credit essay draws on both groups.
  • Dates and figures used as evidence, not decoration. The years 1773, 1774, and 1775, the 342 chests, the fifty six delegates and twelve colonies, and the 273 against 95 counts each carry a step in the argument, and strong essays say what the step is. Casualty figures must be carried as the standard counts, as the bank carries them.
  • A closing that lands on the turn of 1776. The colonies had an army before Boston, committees and congresses governing in fact, and a king who had declared them in rebellion, while independence remained undeclared and many who fought still called themselves Britons. An essay that ends in 1775 without this position has answered only half the question.

Model opening for teacher reference. A protest over tea became a war because each side answered the other's last act with a larger one, and the largest answer was Parliament's. The Tea Act made a cheap cup a surrender of the taxing question, and Boston replied by destroying 342 chests in disciplined silence. Parliament then punished a town rather than trying offenders: it closed Boston's port until the tea was paid for, rewrote the Massachusetts charter, and sent Gage with regiments to enforce the package. That punishment, not the tea, is what the other colonies could not let stand, because every charter in America was written by the same authority that had just rewritten one. A full model continues in this pattern through Congress and the Association, the winter of powder, and Lexington, and closes at the turn of 1776, with a war being fought by men who had not yet declared independence.

Common weak answers. The first shot alone answer treats Lexington as an accident that explains itself, with no account of why seven hundred regulars were marching to Concord at dawn, which bank items 1 through 3 supply. The stubborn colonists answer and the tyrannical king answer each assign the whole escalation to one side's character, and neither can use the Olive Branch Petition, in bank item 6, which shows Congress professing loyalty after the shooting had begun. A chronology with no judgment lists the steps in order without ever weighing which step closed the door, which is the weighing the prompt demands.
