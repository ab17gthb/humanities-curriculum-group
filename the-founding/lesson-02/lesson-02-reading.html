From Protest to Revolution: 1773 to 1776

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Tea Act and the Trap of Cheap Tea

The Tea Act of May 1773 was built as a rescue and read in America as a trap. The East India Company sat on unsold tea and stood near bankruptcy. Parliament let it sell tea directly in the colonies through its own agents, cutting out the colonial merchants who had handled the trade. The price would fall below what colonists paid before, even with the Townshend duty still in place. That was the trap. Every cheap cup drank would concede Parliament's right to tax America.

The colonies read the bargain whole. At Charleston the tea was landed and left to rot in a warehouse. New York and Philadelphia turned their ships back. At Boston, Governor Hutchinson refused to let the Dartmouth leave without unloading, against a legal deadline.

B. The Night of December 16, 1773

On December 16, 1773, a mass meeting at Old South Meeting House voted that the tea should not be landed. That night some one hundred men, thinly disguised as Mohawks, boarded the three tea ships at Griffin's Wharf. In about three hours they emptied 342 chests of tea into the harbor. They harmed nothing else, stole nothing, and by the participants' later accounts swept the decks clean afterward.

The discipline was itself an argument. The men presented the destruction as an act against a tax and a monopoly, not as theft. One participant, the shoemaker George Hewes, remembered the night sixty years later, in a recollection in the activity. News crossed the Atlantic within weeks, and Parliament answered in the spring.

C. Parliament Answers: The Intolerable Acts

Parliament's answer came in the spring of 1774, as laws the colonists named the Intolerable Acts. The Boston Port Act closed the harbor from June 1 until the town paid for the tea. The Massachusetts Government Act rewrote the colony's charter, putting its council and town meetings under royal control. The Administration of Justice Act allowed accused royal officials to be tried outside the colony. A new Quartering Act widened the army's power to house soldiers. Colonists counted the Quebec Act, tolerating Catholic worship in Quebec and extending that province to the Ohio River, as a fifth blow.

General Thomas Gage arrived as governor, with regiments behind him, to enforce the acts. Parliament expected Boston to stand alone and submit. Instead the punishment of one town pushed the colonies toward acting as one people.

D. The Colonies Answer Together

The colonies fed the blockaded town, with supplies arriving from as far away as South Carolina. In September and October 1774, fifty six delegates from twelve colonies, Georgia absent, met at Philadelphia as the First Continental Congress. Its Declaration and Resolves restated the colonial case, that colonists held the rights of Englishmen and had never given any power the right to dispose of life, liberty, and property without consent.

Congress then adopted the Continental Association, a boycott of British imports, and later of exports, enforced by committees elected in every county and town. Enforcing the boycott, those committees quietly became a second government beside the royal one. From Massachusetts came the Suffolk Resolves, urging the people to arm and drill.

E. The Winter of Powder

Through the fall and winter both sides seized magazines and cannon where they could. Massachusetts minutemen, militiamen pledged to turn out at a minute's notice, drilled on town greens while committees gathered powder at Concord, eighteen miles from Boston.

On the night of April 18, 1775, Gage sent some seven hundred regulars from Boston to seize the stores at Concord. Paul Revere and William Dawes rode ahead to warn the countryside. At Lexington, Captain John Parker's company stood waiting near the green as the regulars came up the road at dawn.

F. April 19, 1775: Lexington and Concord

Lexington green survives in two records that disagree. Lieutenant John Barker, a British officer on the march, wrote in his diary that the colonists fired one or two shots, and that his men then rushed in and fired without orders. A week later, thirty four militiamen swore testimony that the regulars fired on them while the company was dispersing, backs turned, and that no man in their company fired first. Eight Americans died on the green.

The column marched on to Concord, where militia fired on the regulars at the North Bridge, then fought the long march back from behind walls and trees. The standard counts give some 273 British casualties against about 95 American for the day. By nightfall, thousands of militia surrounded Boston, and the town was under siege.

G. The First Year of War

The Second Continental Congress met in May 1775 and turned a siege into a war. It created the Continental Army from the militia before Boston and chose George Washington of Virginia to command it, so the South would own New England's war. On June 17, 1775, militia on Breed's Hill twice threw back British assaults before their ammunition failed. The British took the hill at a cost of more than a thousand casualties, and both sides learned the war's price in one afternoon.

In July 1775 Congress sent the Olive Branch Petition, professing loyalty and begging the king to interpose against his ministers. Before it arrived, George III issued his Proclamation of Rebellion in August 1775, and the Prohibitory Act put the colonies outside the king's protection and seized their shipping as an enemy's. By the turn of 1776 royal governors had fled or been arrested in colony after colony, committees governed in fact, and an army stood before Boston. Independence had not been declared, and many who fought still called themselves Britons.

Teacher Answer Key

1. Why was cheap tea a trap? The Tea Act of May 1773 let the East India Company, near bankruptcy and holding mountains of unsold tea, sell directly in the colonies through its own agents. Direct sale cut out the colonial merchants who had handled the trade, and it lowered the price below what colonists had paid before. The Townshend duty on tea stayed in place, so every cheap cup purchased conceded Parliament's claimed right to tax America. Charleston left its tea to rot in a warehouse, New York and Philadelphia turned their ships back, and Boston's governor Hutchinson refused the Dartmouth permission to leave without unloading.

2. Name three of the Intolerable Acts, and the Quebec Act. The Boston Port Act closed the harbor from June 1, 1774 until the town paid for the tea. The Massachusetts Government Act rewrote the charter and put its council and town meetings under royal control. The Administration of Justice Act allowed accused royal officials to be tried outside the colony. A new Quartering Act widened the army's power to house soldiers. Colonists counted the Quebec Act as a fifth blow because it tolerated Catholic worship in Quebec and extended that province to the Ohio River, land the seaboard colonies claimed.

3. Which colony was absent, and how did the Association build a second government? Georgia was absent; the other twelve colonies sent fifty six delegates to Philadelphia in September and October 1774. The Declaration and Resolves restated the colonial case in terms of the rights of Englishmen and consent. The Continental Association then organized a boycott of British imports and later exports, and it was enforced by committees elected in every county and town. Committees elected in every county and town to enforce the boycott were exercising government power, so a second government grew up beside the royal one while claiming only to defend the old rights.

4. State each record's claim about the first shot, and the day's counts. Lieutenant John Barker, a British officer writing in his diary the same day, recorded that the colonists fired one or two shots and that his men rushed in and fired without any orders. The thirty four Massachusetts militiamen, in sworn testimony taken on April 25, 1775, declared that the company was dispersing with its backs turned when the regulars fired on them, and that no man in the company fired on the regulars before the regulars fired on them. Eight Americans died at Lexington. The standard counts for April 19 as a whole give some 273 British casualties against about 95 American.

5. How did a protest over tea become a war? Full credit answers trace the escalation in steps and land on the closing paradox. The Tea Act made cheap tea a test of the taxing right, and Boston destroyed 342 chests rather than concede it. Parliament answered with the Intolerable Acts, punishing a whole town and rewriting a charter, and the colonies answered together, feeding Boston and meeting in Congress, whose Association committees became a second government. Through the winter of powder both sides seized stores and drilled, until Gage's expedition met the militia at Lexington on April 19, 1775. Congress then raised the Continental Army under Washington, the militia bloodied the regulars at Bunker Hill, and the king answered the Olive Branch Petition with a Proclamation of Rebellion. Full credit answers close where the reading closes: by the turn of 1776 the colonies had an army, a siege, and governments of their own in fact, while independence remained undeclared.
