Quick Check: Two Records of One Morning

Directions: Read the background and the two records, then answer the questions.

Background. On the night of April 18, 1775, General Gage sent some seven hundred British regulars from Boston to seize colonial military stores at Concord. Warned by riders, Captain John Parker's militia company waited near Lexington green and met the column at dawn on April 19. Shots were fired, eight Americans were killed, and the column fought its way back to Boston by evening. Two records of the morning survive side by side. Lieutenant John Barker, a British officer on the march, wrote in his diary that the colonists fired one or two shots and that his men then rushed in and fired without any orders. Six days later, thirty four Lexington militiamen swore testimony that the regulars fired on their company while it was dispersing, backs turned, and that no man in the company fired first. The standard counts for the day give some 273 British casualties against about 95 American.

1. State the mission of Gage's column on April 19, 1775, and name the town where it first met the militia.

2. State each record's claim about the first shot at Lexington, naming the record and its maker for each claim.

3. Barker admits two things about his own men. Name both, and explain why an admission inside a private diary carries weight with historians.

4. The militiamen swore their testimony on April 25 and Massachusetts published it in a pamphlet at once. Explain why the timing and the sworn form were chosen, naming the audiences the pamphlet had to move.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the mission, and name the town. The column of some seven hundred regulars marched from Boston to seize the colonial military stores gathered at Concord. It first met the militia at Lexington, where Captain Parker's company stood near the town green at dawn on April 19, 1775. (Blueprint 3)

2. State each record's claim about the first shot. Barker's diary, written by a British officer the same day, claims the colonists fired one or two shots first, upon which his men rushed in and fired. The militiamen's sworn testimony of April 25, 1775 claims the company was dispersing with its backs turned when the regulars fired on it, and that not a gun was fired by any person in the company on the regulars, to their knowledge, before the regulars fired on them. (Blueprint 4)

3. Name Barker's two admissions, and why they carry weight. Barker admits that his men fired without any orders, and that after the volley they were so wild they could hear no orders while their officers tried to form them on the common. A private diary was written for the writer himself, not for publication or persuasion, so an admission against his own side's discipline has no propaganda purpose to serve. Historians therefore read the admission as likely candid, even while they weigh his claim about the first shot against his interest in it. (Blueprint 4)

4. Why the timing, and why the sworn form? April 19 would be judged in print within weeks, in the other colonies and in Britain, and whoever fired first would carry the blame for starting a war. Massachusetts took sworn testimony on April 25, six days after the fighting and before British official reports could fix the story, and published it in a pamphlet, A Narrative of the Excursion and Ravages of the King's Troops. The sworn form, thirty four named men before justices of the peace, was meant to outrank rumor for two audiences: the other colonies, whose men and money Massachusetts needed for the siege of Boston, and British readers, who had to decide whether the morning was rebellion or massacre. (Blueprint 4)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Question 2 is the entry diagnostic: students who merge the two records into one account have missed the lesson's central skill, holding two irreconcilable records apart and weighing each by its maker, its moment, and its audience. Question 4 predicts success on the activity verdict, because it requires students to treat publication itself as a step in the escalation.
