Guided Notes: From Protest to Revolution: 1773 to 1776

Lesson question: How did a protest over tea become a war?

Unit question: Why did the colonies break with Britain, and what did Americans build in the place of the empire they rejected?

Key Terms

Tea Act: the British law of 1773 that let the East India Company sell tea directly in the colonies at a lower price while keeping the Townshend tax on tea in place

Boston Tea Party: the protest of December 16, 1773, in which Boston men disguised as Mohawks boarded three tea ships and threw 342 chests of tea into the harbor rather than let it be landed and taxed

Intolerable Acts: the laws Parliament passed in 1774 to punish Massachusetts for the Tea Party, closing Boston's port and rewriting the colony's charter, which the colonists called Intolerable and Britain called the Coercive Acts

First Continental Congress: the meeting of delegates from twelve colonies at Philadelphia in the fall of 1774 that answered the Intolerable Acts with a declaration of rights and a boycott of British goods

minutemen: the Massachusetts militiamen who promised to be ready at a minute's notice, who turned out along the road from Lexington and Concord on April 19, 1775

Loyalist: a colonist who remained loyal to Britain during the Revolution, whether from conviction, interest, or fear of the rebellion's disorder, and who opposed independence

Patriot: a colonist who supported resistance to Britain and, from 1776, American independence, and who took the rebellion's side in the war

Summary table. The tea destroyed: on December 16, 1773, men disguised as Mohawks emptied 342 chests into Boston Harbor and harmed nothing else; it changed the quarrel from argument to destroyed property that Parliament would demand be paid for. The punishment: the Intolerable Acts of 1774 closed the port, rewrote the Massachusetts charter, and put trials and town meetings under royal control; the punishment of one town made the quarrel every colony's. The united answer: twelve colonies met in the First Continental Congress, restated their rights in the Declaration and Resolves, and enforced a boycott through the Continental Association, whose committees became a second government. The first shots: Gage's expedition to seize the Concord stores met the militia at Lexington on April 19, 1775, and by nightfall Boston was besieged; protest had become war, even though independence remained undeclared into 1776.

Exit ticket 1. The Intolerable Acts were the laws Parliament passed in 1774 to punish Massachusetts for the Tea Party, closing Boston's port and rewriting the colony's charter. The act that closed the harbor was the Boston Port Act, in force from June 1, 1774 until the town paid for the destroyed tea.

Exit ticket 2. Barker, a British officer writing in his diary, claimed the colonists fired one or two shots first and his men then fired without orders. The militiamen, in sworn testimony, claimed the regulars fired on their company while it was dispersing with its backs turned, and that no man in the company fired first.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that moves through at least two steps of the escalation, such as the Tea Party answered by the Intolerable Acts, the colonies uniting in Congress and the Association, and the march to Concord ending in fighting at Lexington, and that closes with the turn of 1776 position: an army before Boston and committees governing in fact, with independence still undeclared and many who fought still calling themselves Britons.
