The Imperial Crisis: Taxation and Resistance

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Bill for Victory

Britain came out of the French and Indian War holding Canada and an empire in North America, and holding the bill for both. The national debt had nearly doubled during the war. Ministers in London resolved that the colonies, defended by the victory, should help pay for their own defense in the years ahead.

The charge they meant the colonies to carry was a standing army of some ten thousand soldiers in North America, kept there to guard the frontier and the new territories. The ministry of George Grenville designed a revenue program in 1764 and 1765 to raise the money in America. The program broke with the older practice under which colonial assemblies voted the taxes their own people paid.

B. The Stamp Act

The Stamp Act, passed in March 1765, was the break in that practice. Earlier British taxes touching the colonies had fallen on trade, on goods moving in and out of colonial ports. The Stamp Act fell directly on the colonies themselves. It taxed printed paper used inside the colonies, including newspapers, pamphlets, legal documents, licenses, and playing cards.

The tax was collected through stamped paper sold by royal distributors appointed in each colony. Every newspaper, deed, and deck of cards had to carry the stamp and its price. The tax fell hardest on printers, lawyers, and merchants, the people who ran the colonies newspapers, courts, and assemblies, and who could answer in print.

C. Argument and Combination

The colonial answer came first as argument. In May 1765 the Virginia House of Burgesses adopted resolves put forward by Patrick Henry claiming that only the colonists own assemblies could tax them, because the colonists were represented in those assemblies and nowhere else. In October 1765 delegates from nine colonies met at New York in the Stamp Act Congress and declared their rights and grievances, holding that no taxes could be imposed on the colonies but by their respective legislatures.

Argument was joined by combination. Merchants in the port cities organized boycotts of British goods. Households made the boycotts real. Women spun and wove homespun cloth so families could stop buying British textiles, and homespun became a public sign of resistance worn in the street.

D. Crowds, Resignations, and Repeal

The third register was the crowd. Groups calling themselves the Sons of Liberty organized protests across the colonies. They hung stamp men in effigy, threatened the distributors appointed to sell the stamps, and in Boston a crowd wrecked the house of Thomas Hutchinson, the lieutenant governor. Under that pressure every stamp distributor in America resigned. When the act took effect on November 1, 1765, no stamps were sold, and the law stood unenforced.

The boycott carried the argument to London. British merchants, losing their American trade, petitioned Parliament for relief. In February 1766 Benjamin Franklin testified before Parliament that the colonies would never submit to the stamp tax. In March 1766 Parliament repealed the act. On the same day it passed the Declaratory Act, claiming full power and authority to make laws binding the colonies in all cases whatsoever. The colonies celebrated the repeal and largely looked past the claim that came with it.

E. The Townshend Acts and the Farmer

The second round opened in 1767 with the Townshend Acts, duties on imported glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea. The duties were framed as external taxes on trade, of a kind the colonists had seemed to accept. The revenue was assigned to pay the salaries of royal governors and judges in America. That assignment struck at the power of the purse, the practice by which assemblies controlled royal officials by voting, or withholding, their salaries each year.

John Dickinson answered in Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania, published in newspapers through the winter of 1767 and 1768. Dickinson argued that a duty laid for the single purpose of levying money was a tax whatever its form, and that the Townshend duties were taxes of that kind. In February 1768 the Massachusetts assembly sent a Circular Letter to the other assemblies carrying the same argument across the colonies.

F. Troops and Blood in Boston

To back the customs men, the ministry sent two regiments to Boston in 1768. Soldiers and townspeople lived on top of each other for two years, and friction built in the streets. On the night of March 5, 1770, a crowd surrounded a small guard outside the Custom House, taunting the soldiers and throwing ice and debris. The soldiers fired. Five men died, among them Crispus Attucks, a sailor of African and Native descent.

An engraving by Paul Revere, printed within weeks and copied from a drawing by Henry Pelham, fixed the event in colonial memory as a massacre, showing soldiers firing in a line into an unarmed crowd. The soldiers own accounts told a different story. Captain Thomas Preston wrote that he stood between his men and the crowd and gave no order to fire. Samuel Drowne, a Boston resident, swore in testimony that he heard Preston give the order. At the trial that followed, John Adams defended the soldiers. Two were convicted of manslaughter and branded on the thumb. Six were acquitted.

G. The Quiet Years

In 1770 Parliament repealed the Townshend duties on every item but tea. The tea duty stayed in place to hold the principle the Declaratory Act had claimed. A period of quiet followed. In 1772 and 1773 the colonies built committees of correspondence to share news and coordinate answers to British measures. The quiet years wired the colonies together for the crisis that came next.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the charge Britain meant the colonies to carry, and the program that carried it. The charge was a standing army of some ten thousand soldiers in North America, kept to guard the frontier and the new territories won in the French and Indian War. The national debt of Britain had nearly doubled during the war, and ministers resolved that the colonies should help pay for their own defense. The revenue program was designed by the ministry of George Grenville in 1764 and 1765 to raise the money in America itself.

2. Explain what was new about the Stamp Act. Earlier British taxes touching the colonies had fallen on trade, on goods moving in and out of colonial ports. The Stamp Act of March 1765 fell directly on the colonies themselves. It taxed printed paper used inside the colonies, including newspapers, pamphlets, legal documents, licenses, and playing cards, and it was collected through stamped paper sold by royal distributors appointed in each colony. Credit requires the contrast with trade taxes, two taxed items, and the stamped paper collection method.

3. Name the three registers of the colonial answer, with evidence for each. Argument: the Virginia Resolves of May 1765, in which Patrick Henry claimed that only the colonists own assemblies could tax them, and the Stamp Act Congress of October 1765, where delegates from nine colonies at New York declared that no taxes could be imposed on the colonies but by their respective legislatures. Combination: merchant boycotts of British goods, made real in households where women spun and wove homespun cloth so families could stop buying British textiles. Crowd action: the Sons of Liberty hung stamp men in effigy, threatened the distributors, and in Boston a crowd wrecked the house of Lieutenant Governor Thomas Hutchinson, and every stamp distributor in America resigned before November 1, 1765, so no stamps were sold.

4. State what repeal and the Declaratory Act each did, and why the quarrel stayed unfinished. Repeal, in March 1766, canceled the Stamp Act after British merchants hurt by the boycott petitioned for relief and Benjamin Franklin testified in February 1766 that the colonies would never submit to the tax. The Declaratory Act, passed the same day, claimed that Parliament had full power and authority to make laws binding the colonies in all cases whatsoever. The pairing left the quarrel unfinished because the colonists had won the tax and lost the principle. Parliament gave up one revenue law while asserting the right to pass the next one, and the Townshend Acts of 1767 tested that claim within a year.

5. Why did Britain tax the colonies after 1763, and why did the colonists resist? Full credit answers hold both halves and close on later evidence. Britain taxed because the French and Indian War had nearly doubled the national debt and left a standing army of some ten thousand soldiers in North America whose cost ministers assigned to the colonies through the Grenville revenue program of 1764 and 1765. The colonists resisted because the taxes were new in kind and in authority. The Stamp Act taxed them directly, inside their own colonies, by a Parliament in which they had no representatives, against the standing practice that their own assemblies voted the taxes they paid. The Townshend Acts then showed that the form of a tax could change while its purpose, raising revenue and paying royal governors and judges outside assembly control, stayed the same, and the posting of regiments to Boston and the killings of March 5, 1770 showed what enforcing the claim could cost. A paragraph that gives only the debt, or only the slogan of representation, earns partial credit.
