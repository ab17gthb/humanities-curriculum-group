Quick Check: Two Accounts of One Night

Directions: Read the two passages and the background, then answer the questions.

Preston. At this Time I was between the Soldiers and the Mob, parleying with, and endeavouring all in my Power to persuade them to retire peaceably; but to no Purpose. ... On my asking the Soldiers why they fired without Orders, they said they heard the Word "Fire," and supposed it came from me. ... I assured the Men that I gave no such Order, that my Words were, "Don't fire, stop your Firing:"

Source: Capt. Thomas Preston, Captain Preston's Account of the Boston Massacre, 1770 (Public Advertiser, London, April 28, 1770; via the Gilder Lehrman Institute), as carried in the audited TPSP record. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

Drowne. ... the deponent heard Capt. Preston say to the soldiers, "Damn your bloods! why don't you fire?" The soldiers not regarding those words of their captain, he immediately said, "Fire." Upon which they fired irregularly, pointing their guns variously in a part of a circle as they stood ...

Source: Samuel Drowne, testimony of March 16, 1770 (from A Short Narrative of the Horrid Massacre in Boston, 1770; original at the Massachusetts Historical Society; spelling modernized), as carried in the audited TPSP record. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

Background. The troops were in Boston because of the tax quarrel that began with the Stamp Act of 1765, Parliament's first direct tax on the colonies, laid to help pay the debt of the French and Indian War and continued, after the Stamp Act was repealed, in the Townshend duties of 1767. British regiments had been posted to Boston since 1768 to back the customs men collecting the Townshend duties. On the night of March 5, 1770, a crowd surrounded a small guard outside the Custom House, taunting the soldiers and throwing ice and debris. The soldiers fired and killed five men, among them Crispus Attucks. Captain Preston commanded the guard. He was arrested and tried for murder, defended by John Adams. Two soldiers were convicted of manslaughter and branded on the thumb, and six were acquitted. Preston wrote his account from jail and sent it to London. Drowne gave his testimony under oath to a Boston town committee gathering statements about the night.

1. In his passage, what does Preston say his own words to his men were, and what does he say the men told him afterward?

2. Drowne swears he heard Preston say two things to the soldiers. Quote both, and state what happened, in Drowne's account, immediately after the second one.

3. Using the background, give two facts: one about why British regiments had been posted to Boston, and one about the trial that followed the shooting. State what each fact shows.

4. A reader argues that Drowne's testimony settles the question of the order, because he swore to it under oath. State the strongest answer to that argument from the two passages and the background.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What does Preston say his words were, and what did the men tell him? He says his words were, "Don't fire, stop your Firing." He says that when he asked the soldiers why they fired without orders, they said they heard the word "Fire" and supposed it came from him. Credit requires both halves and the quoted words. (Blueprint 2)

2. Quote the two things Drowne swears Preston said, and what followed. "Damn your bloods! why don't you fire?" and then, "Fire." Drowne says the soldiers, not regarding the first words, fired irregularly upon the second, pointing their guns variously in a part of a circle as they stood. Credit requires both quotations and the irregular firing that followed. (Blueprint 2)

3. Give two facts, one about the posting and one about the trial, and what each shows. For the posting: the regiments had been in Boston since 1768 to back the customs men collecting the Townshend duties, the second round of the revenue program that began with the Stamp Act of 1765, laid to help pay the debt of the French and Indian War. It shows the shooting grew out of the tax quarrel rather than a local feud: the soldiers stood in that street because Parliament had sent them to enforce a revenue law the colonies were resisting. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2) For the trial, any one: Preston was arrested and tried for murder; John Adams, a leader of the resistance, defended the soldiers; two soldiers were convicted of manslaughter and branded on the thumb; six were acquitted. It shows a resistance movement that still claimed British justice: it prosecuted the soldiers in a British court, accepted a defense by one of its own leaders, and lived with a split verdict rather than a lynching or a blanket condemnation. (Blueprint 2)

4. State the strongest answer to the argument that the oath settles the question. An oath settles what a witness swears, not what happened. Drowne's testimony was gathered by a Boston town committee collecting statements to shape how London judged the event, one of ninety six depositions published for that purpose, so his account carries a purpose as well as an oath. Preston, writing from jail under a murder charge for a London audience, swears to the opposite. The two sworn and published accounts contradict each other on the single point of the order, and the trial itself, which heard both kinds of testimony, convicted only two men of manslaughter and acquitted six. The honest conclusion is that the order remains disputed in the record, and students should hold it as disputed. (Blueprint 2)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Questions 1 and 2 test whether students can keep two opposed accounts separate in their own notes, the skill the activity Station 4 grades. Question 4 predicts success on the writing task, because it requires a judgment about evidence rather than a choice of sides. Question 3 carries both of the lesson's blueprint entries, the posting for Blueprint 1 and the trial for Blueprint 2.
