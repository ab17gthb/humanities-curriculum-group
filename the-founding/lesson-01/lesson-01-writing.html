Writing Task: Why Repeal Did Not End the Quarrel

The prompt. Answer the closing question of the lesson in a developed essay of about 300 words: why did repeal in 1766 not end the quarrel between Britain and the colonies? A strong answer does more than list what happened next. It begins from the claim each side had staked by 1766, works through the measures that tested those claims, and weighs the document that best explains the failure of repeal against the strongest rival document. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading, stations, and documents. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank wording, except where a bank item quotes a document.

Evidence Bank

1. The Stamp Act Congress of October 1765, nine colonies meeting at New York, declared that no taxes ever have been, or can be constitutionally imposed on the colonies, but by their respective legislatures. (Declaration of Rights and Grievances, 1765, Avalon Project, Yale Law School.)

2. Parliament repealed the Stamp Act in March 1766 after British merchants hurt by the boycott petitioned for relief and Benjamin Franklin testified that the colonies would never submit to the tax. On the same day Parliament passed the Declaratory Act, claiming full power and authority to make laws binding the colonies in all cases whatsoever, and declaring colonial resolutions that denied that power utterly null and void. (Lesson reading, section D, and the Declaratory Act, 1766, Avalon Project, Yale Law School.)

3. The Townshend Acts of 1767 taxed imports including glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea, and assigned the revenue to pay royal governors and judges, outside the assemblies' yearly power to vote or withhold those salaries. (Lesson reading, section E.)

4. John Dickinson, in Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania, Letter II, 1767, argued that duties imposed for the single purpose of levying money upon us were taxes whatever their form, and called the claim an innovation, and a most dangerous innovation. (Wikisource transcription of the collected edition of 1768.)

5. Two regiments were posted to Boston in 1768 to back the customs men. On March 5, 1770, soldiers fired into a crowd and killed five men. Captain Preston and Samuel Drowne gave opposed sworn and published accounts of whether an order to fire was given, and at trial two soldiers were convicted of manslaughter and six were acquitted. (Lesson reading, section F, and the audited TPSP records of Preston's account and Drowne's testimony.)

6. In 1770 Parliament repealed the Townshend duties on every item but tea. The tea duty stayed in place to hold the principle the Declaratory Act had claimed, and in 1772 and 1773 the colonies built committees of correspondence to coordinate their answers. (Lesson reading, section G.)

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that names the surviving claim, not only the surviving tax. Repeal removed the Stamp Act and left standing the Declaratory Act assertion of power in all cases whatsoever, against the Stamp Act Congress standard that only the colonial legislatures could tax. (Blueprint 1 and Blueprint 2)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 fixes the colonial standard of 1765. (Blueprint 1) Bank item 2 carries the hinge: the same day pairing of repeal and assertion. (Blueprint 2) Bank item 3 shows the assertion at work within a year, in a new form and with the assemblies' power of the purse as its target. (Blueprint 2) Bank item 4 shows the colonial test that exposed the new form, Dickinson's purpose test. (Blueprint 2) Bank item 5 shows the cost of enforcement when the claim was backed by regiments in Boston. (Blueprint 2) Bank item 6 closes the arc: repeal repeated in 1770 with the principle deliberately kept in the tea duty. (Blueprint 2)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. The decisive link is claim against claim: students should argue that the quarrel was never only about one tax bill, because each side had stated a rule about who may tax, and neither rule was withdrawn in 1766.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest rival, such as the argument that the boycott, not any document, forced repeal and could have ended the matter, answered from the record: the boycott won the tax and could not repeal the Declaratory Act, which no colonial body had power to touch.

Model opening for teacher reference. Repeal did not end the quarrel because it settled a bill and left two rules standing against each other. The Stamp Act Congress had declared in 1765 that taxes could be imposed on the colonies only by their own legislatures, and the Declaratory Act, passed on the day of repeal itself, answered that Parliament could bind the colonies in all cases whatsoever and that colonial denials of that power were null and void. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the Townshend Acts and Dickinson answer in lesson order, weighing the Boston killings as the price of enforcing the claim, and closing on the tea duty kept in 1770 as proof that London meant to hold the principle even while surrendering the revenue.

Common weak answers. The colonists were angry (a mood, not a claim) skips both stated rules. The Townshend Acts alone (a new tax started a new fight) misses that Dickinson and the assemblies read the duties through the 1765 and 1766 documents from the first week. A claim with no rival stated earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a weighing, not a verdict alone.
