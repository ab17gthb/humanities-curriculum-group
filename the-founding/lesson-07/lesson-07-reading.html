The Constitution and the Great Compromises

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The Room in Philadelphia

The Convention met in the Pennsylvania State House and organized on May 25, 1787, once seven states were present. Fifty five delegates from twelve states attended. Rhode Island sent none. The delegates elected George Washington to preside, and he sat through the debates in silence, speaking only once, on the final day. The Convention worked behind closed doors, under a rule of secrecy that barred members from publishing anything spoken in the house, so a delegate could reverse himself without answering for it at home.

James Madison arrived eleven days early, carrying a plan already drafted. Each night he wrote the debates out in full. The Convention kept no official record of its arguments, so Madison's notes remain the fullest account of how the Constitution was made.

B. The Virginia Plan

Congress had called the Convention to revise the Articles of Confederation, for that sole and express purpose. Within the first week the Virginia delegation, speaking through Governor Edmund Randolph, proposed a new national government. The Virginia Plan, drafted chiefly by Madison, built a legislature of two houses, both apportioned by population or wealth, with power to legislate wherever the states were incompetent and to strike down state laws that conflicted with national ones. That legislature would choose the executive and the judiciary. The plan ran on numbers. The large states, holding most of the union's people, would hold most of its power, and the small states would give up their equal vote under the Articles.

C. The New Jersey Plan and the Deadlock

On June 15 William Paterson of New Jersey answered for the small states. The New Jersey Plan kept a legislature of one house, in which every state cast a single vote as under the Articles, and patched the Confederation with the powers it had lacked, the power to tax, the power to regulate trade, and an executive and judiciary to carry the union's laws out. Representation consumed the rest of June, and on July 2 the vote on equal representation in the second house tied, state against state. A committee of one member from each state went to find terms both sides could accept.

D. The Great Compromise

The committee's terms, adopted on July 16 by five states to four, became the Great Compromise. The House would be apportioned among the states by population, its members chosen by the people for two year terms, and every bill for raising revenue would begin in that house. The Senate would hold two members from each state, chosen by the state legislatures for six year terms, so Delaware counted exactly as Virginia did. The Virginia Plan supplied the people's house and the new government's strength. The New Jersey Plan supplied the states' house and their equality within it.

E. Counting the Enslaved

Apportionment required one answer first. How would the count treat the enslaved? Southern delegates counted the enslaved as people when seats were divided, as property when taxes were counted. The settlement, borrowed from a Confederation tax formula of 1783, counted each enslaved person as three fifths of a person, for representation and direct taxes alike. A state holding one hundred thousand enslaved people gained sixty thousand in its count, though not one of those counted could cast a ballot. The word slavery appears nowhere in the Constitution. The institution stands in the document's arithmetic, and through the House count in the electoral college. Congress could not prohibit the Atlantic slave trade before 1808, and a person held to service escaping into another state was to be delivered up on claim. Madison wrote later that the Convention had not confronted the thing by its right name.

F. The Design Assembled

The Convention assembled its design. Under federalism, power is divided between the national government and the states, and the states kept the ordinary business of life, property, crime, and family. The national powers are enumerated, and a clause empowering Congress to make laws necessary and proper to carry them out stretches the list toward unforeseen cases. The government is divided by the separation of powers into legislative, executive, and judicial branches, then laced together by checks and balances, a presidential veto Congress can override, Senate consent to treaties and appointments, impeachment, and judges holding office during good behavior.

The president is chosen by electors, not by Congress or direct national vote. News crossed the continent slowly, voters could not know distant candidates, and the framers feared cabal in one national contest. The three fifths count also raised the slave states' weight in choosing a president. Amendments require two thirds of Congress to propose and three fourths of the states to ratify.

G. What Was Left Out, and the Close

On September 12 George Mason moved that a committee prepare a bill of rights. The Convention refused, with no state voting in favor, holding the states' own declarations of rights protection enough. Mason, Elbridge Gerry, and Edmund Randolph declined to sign. What the document opens with was written last. The Committee of Style began not with the Articles' list of state names but with We the People of the United States. On September 17 Benjamin Franklin, too weak to read his own words aloud, had James Wilson read it. Franklin confessed that parts of the Constitution did not have his approval, asked every member to doubt a little of his own infallibility and sign, and pointed to the sun carved on Washington's chair, which he now knew to be rising. Thirty nine delegates signed, and the Constitution went to the states, needing nine of thirteen to take effect.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the Convention's orders, the Virginia proposal, its drafter and presenter, and its apportionment rule. Congress had called the Convention to revise the Articles of Confederation, for that sole and express purpose. In the first week the Virginia delegation proposed a new national government instead. The Virginia Plan was drafted chiefly by James Madison and presented by Governor Edmund Randolph. It built a legislature of two houses, both apportioned by population or wealth, with power to legislate wherever the states were incompetent and to strike down conflicting state laws, and that legislature would choose the executive and the judiciary. (Blueprint 13)

2. Explain the deadlock and its settlement, with both dates, the margin, and the two houses' rules. The New Jersey Plan's one house with one vote per state and the Virginia Plan's apportionment by population left the Convention with two designs and no road between them. On July 2, 1787 the vote on equal representation in the second house tied, state against state. A committee of one member from each state found terms, and on July 16 the Convention adopted the Great Compromise by five states to four. The House of Representatives is apportioned by population, its members chosen by the people for two year terms, and revenue bills begin there. The Senate holds two members from each state, chosen by the state legislatures for six year terms. (Blueprint 13)

3. Work the arithmetic, name what the count ran toward, the two further protections, and the missing word. At three fifths, one hundred thousand enslaved people added sixty thousand to a state's count (100,000 x 3/5 = 60,000). The count set the state's share of House seats and of direct taxes, and because a state's presidential electors equal its members of Congress, the same count raised the slave states' weight in the electoral college, though not one of the people counted could vote. The two further protections were the clause barring Congress from prohibiting the Atlantic slave trade before 1808 and the clause requiring a person held to service who escaped into another state to be delivered up on claim. The word slavery appears nowhere in the document. Madison wrote later that the Convention had not confronted the thing by its right name. (Blueprint 14)

4. Describe the assembled design, with federalism defined, the branches, two checks, and the electoral college's reasons. Under federalism, power is divided between the national government and the state governments, each acting directly on the people within its own sphere, and the states kept the ordinary business of life, property, crime, and family. The separation of powers divides the federal government into legislative, executive, and judicial branches. Checks include the presidential veto, which Congress can override; the Senate's consent to treaties and appointments; impeachment; and judges holding office during good behavior. Any two earn the point. The president is chosen by electors because, in the debates' stated reasons, news crossed the continent slowly and voters could not know distant candidates, and a single national contest invited cabal. Alongside those stated reasons, the three fifths count raised the slave states' weight in choosing a president. Students should state that advantage as part of the design's effect, as the reading does, without claiming it was the only reason offered in the room. (Blueprint 14)

5. Answer the lesson question using the close, with the September 12 motion, the Preamble's opening, Franklin's request, and the signing count. Strong answers hold strength and limit in one paragraph. On September 12 George Mason moved that a committee prepare a bill of rights, and the Convention refused with no state voting in favor. The Committee of Style opened the document with We the People of the United States, resting the government on the people themselves rather than on the states as parties. On September 17 Franklin, whose closing speech James Wilson read aloud, confessed that parts of the Constitution did not have his approval and asked every member to doubt a little of his own infallibility and sign. Thirty nine delegates signed. A government strong enough to act came out of the Virginia Plan's frame and the enumerated powers. A government limited enough to trust was the delegates' claim for the divided powers, the checks, and an amendment process requiring two thirds and three fourths, and the missing bill of rights is the limit the Convention declined to write, which the ratification fight then took up. (Blueprint 13, 14)
