Teacher Notes

The Founding · Lesson 7 · The Constitution and the Great Compromises

These are the speaker notes from the slide deck, in slide order, set for printing. The deck itself carries the main ideas in a few fragments per slide; the teaching is here. Print this and keep it beside you while you teach. Each entry is headed by its slide number and shows what is on that slide, so you can find your place without flipping back to the screen. Vocabulary definitions match the student guided notes word for word.

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: The Constitution and the Great Compromises · Philadelphia, May 25 to September 17, 1787

The image is the first page of the engrossed Constitution, the parchment signed on September 17, 1787 and held by the National Archives. Open by placing this lesson after Lesson 6. The Confederation government could not tax, could not make the states obey the peace treaty, and had watched Massachusetts farmers close the courts. Congress answered by calling a convention to revise the Articles. What happened in Philadelphia instead is this lesson: fifty five delegates from twelve states, Rhode Island absent, spent one summer building a new government. Tell students the lesson runs on documents they will handle themselves, the Preamble, Madison’s notes, the text of Article I, and Franklin’s last speech, and that every compromise in the lesson gets priced as well as praised.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: How did fifty five men in one room design a government strong enough to act and limited enough to trust? · Unit question: Why did the colonies break with Britain, and what did Americans build in the place of the empire t

Read the question in its two halves and keep both alive all hour. Strong enough to act answers the Confederation’s failure, a government that could declare war and sign treaties but could not raise a dollar or compel a state. Limited enough to trust answers the Revolution’s own fear, because these men had fought a king and a Parliament they called tyrannical, and their constituents would not ratify a new tyranny with an American address. Every section of the lesson presses one half or the other. The Virginia Plan is strength. The deadlock is the small states pricing that strength. The compromises are the two halves being forced into one document, and the closing argument, in the writing task, asks students to judge whether the limits were built in or merely claimed afterward.

Section A sets the room, its secrecy rule, its presiding officer, and its record keeper. Section B is the Virginia Plan, and section C the small states’ answer and the deadlock it produced. Section D is the Great Compromise of July 16. Section E states the count of the enslaved plainly, in the document’s own arithmetic, with its two companion protections. The summary table at the end of the guided notes asks students to rebuild this map stage by stage.

Slide 4 · Section A: The room in Philadelphia (divider)

On the slide: The Room in Philadelphia · George Washington, by Gilbert Stuart, begun 1795. / The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Open Access.

The portrait is Gilbert Stuart’s Washington, begun in 1795 and held by the Metropolitan Museum of Art, which releases the image under Open Access. Washington’s role this summer was silence. Elected to preside on the first day, he sat in a chair at the front of the room, put questions, kept order, and offered no argument on any clause until the final day, when he spoke once in favor of widening the ratio of representation. His silence was the office working as intended, and his known presence did other work besides: the country trusted the chair because of the man in it, and the delegates knew the presidency they were designing would first be held by him. Section A also names the room’s second fixture, Madison in his seat near the front, taking the notes that make this the best documented founding of the age.

Slide 5 · A government argued in secret

On the slide: A government argued in secret · ▪ The Pennsylvania State House, May 25, 1787 / ▪ Fifty five delegates, twelve states / ▪ Rhode Island sends no one / ▪ Washington presides, in silence / ▪ Madison writes the debates e

The engraving shows the Pennsylvania State House from Birch’s Views of Philadelphia, drawn in 1798 and 1799, a dozen years after the Convention but the same building, in the same form. Teach the secrecy rule as a working tool rather than a scandal. Members were barred from publishing anything spoken in the house, the windows stayed shut against the summer heat, and sentries kept the curious away. The rule let a delegate test an argument in June and abandon it in August without a newspaper at home holding him to it, and the great compromises of July are hard to imagine without that shelter. Against the shelter, set the cost students will meet at Station 2 of the activity: the public got a finished document and almost no record, and the fullest account of the debates is one man’s private notebook, published when every delegate was dead.

Slide 6 · 55 delegates from twelve states

On the slide: 55 · delegates from twelve states attended the Convention · Rhode Island sent none · The Convention organized on May 25, 1787, once seven states were present

Let the number correct two habits at once. Fifty five is small enough that students should picture a room, not a congress: a single chamber in the State House, men who had soldiered, practiced law, planted, and traded together for a decade, arguing face to face for four months. It is also a chosen company, not a sample of the country. No women sat in it, no enslaved or free Black American sat in it, no Native nation was represented in it, and the poorest farmers, the men who had closed the courts in Massachusetts the winter before, were present only as the problem the room had been called to solve. Keep the double fact in view all lesson, because the document’s authority claim, We the People, was written by these fifty five men, and the ratification fight in the next lesson is the country answering them.

Slide 7 · Section B: The Virginia Plan (divider)

On the slide: The Virginia Plan · A new national government in place of a revised league.

Section B is the Convention’s founding act of disobedience, and students should feel its audacity in the record’s own terms. Congress had authorized a convention for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation. Virginia’s delegation arrived with a replacement already drafted, chiefly by Madison during the days he waited in Philadelphia for a quorum, and Edmund Randolph, Virginia’s governor, presented it in the first week. The plan did not patch the league. It proposed a national government of the kind the Revolution had been fought, in part, to prevent: a legislature empowered against the states themselves, choosing the executive and the judiciary. Everything after this section is the Convention deciding how much of the Virginia Plan it dared to keep, and at what price in small state votes.

Slide 8 · A new government, not a revision

On the slide: A new government, not a revision · ▪ Presented by Edmund Randolph / ▪ Drafted chiefly by James Madison / ▪ Two houses, both apportioned by population / ▪ Power to strike down state laws / ▪ The legislature chooses e

The portrait is John Vanderlyn’s Madison of 1816, in the White House collection, the standard likeness of the man who did more than any other delegate to shape this document. Walk the plan’s logic slowly. Apportionment by population or wealth makes representation follow numbers, which is a democratic principle and a regional fact in the same breath: Virginia, Pennsylvania, and Massachusetts together held a large share of the union’s people, and a population legislature hands them the government. The negative on state laws, Madison’s cherished clause, would have let Congress veto any state law it judged contrary to the union, a power beyond anything the finished Constitution contains. Note what the plan assumes about sovereignty. Under the Articles the states are the parties and Congress their agent. Under this plan the people are the party and the states are districts with governments. That reversal, more than any single clause, is what the small states heard in the first week.

Slide 9 · Section C: The New Jersey Plan and the deadlock (divider)

On the slide: The New Jersey Plan and the Deadlock · One house, one vote per state, and a tie that nearly ended the Convention.

Section C gives the small states their full argument before the deadlock, because students who meet the New Jersey Plan only as an obstacle miss what it defended. Delaware’s delegates had instructions from home forbidding them to surrender the equal vote, and the small states’ fear was concrete rather than proud: in a population legislature, three large states could outvote the other ten on any question, tax them, and govern them as provinces. William Paterson’s plan of June 15 kept the Articles’ frame, one house and equal votes, and repaired its known failures by adding the powers to tax and to regulate trade. The Convention then spent the rest of June discovering that neither side could carry the room, which is what a deadlock between principles, each half right, looks like from inside.

Slide 10 · One house, one vote per state

On the slide: One house, one vote per state · ▪ Paterson answers for the small states, June 15 / ▪ The Articles’ frame, kept and patched / ▪ New powers to tax and to regulate trade / ▪ Representation consumes the rest of June / ▪

Teach the tie of July 2 as the Convention’s nearest approach to failure. The question was equal representation in the second house, the Senate that did not yet exist, and the states divided evenly, with Georgia’s delegation split and Massachusetts divided in its vote as the records report the day. Delegates said in the room what the reading repeats: the Convention might break up, and the union with it. The rescue was procedural, and students should learn to respect procedure as a historical force. A committee of one member from each state, the grand committee, took the question out of the full room over the July 4 recess and returned with terms neither plan contained: one house by population, one house equal. Section D takes up those terms. Ask the class, before moving on, what each side would have to surrender to accept them, because the answer is the definition of a compromise they will use all unit.

Slide 11 · Section D: The Great Compromise (divider)

On the slide: The Great Compromise · The engrossed Constitution, page one, 1787. / National Archives.

The parchment on screen is the engrossed Constitution’s first page, the text as Jacob Shallus wrote it out for the signing, held by the National Archives. Section D reaches the settlement the whole document stands on. Connecticut’s delegates, Roger Sherman and Oliver Ellsworth, had carried the two house formula into the grand committee, and on July 16 the Convention adopted it by five states to four, with Massachusetts divided, the narrowest consequential margin of the summer. Teach the date and the margin as facts students must produce exactly, because the unit test reaches for them: a government for three million people turned on one state’s vote in a committee report. Then slow down on the settlement’s shape, because its two halves answer to two different theories of what the union is, and both theories were left standing in the finished frame.

Slide 12 · Two houses, two opposite rules

On the slide: Two houses, two opposite rules · ▪ The House: apportioned by population / ▪ Chosen by the people, two year terms / ▪ Every revenue bill begins in the House / ▪ The Senate: two from every state / ▪ Chosen by state le

Set the two rules side by side and make students state what each one says about the union. The House is the Virginia Plan’s child: representation by population, members chosen directly by the people, short terms to keep them answerable, and the power of the purse begun in the people’s house, a deliberate inheritance from the colonial assemblies’ long fight over money bills. The Senate is the New Jersey Plan’s child: the states as equals, two voices each, chosen by the state legislatures rather than by voters, with long terms meant to cool the House’s heat. Delaware counts exactly as Virginia did, and the reading’s sentence is the one to hold: the union carries a double nature in its frame, a people in one house and states in the other. Every later argument about the Senate’s fairness begins in this room and this vote, and students should be able to run the argument in both directions before the hour ends.

Slide 13 · 5 to 4 on July 16

On the slide: 5 to 4 · states adopted the Great Compromise on July 16, 1787 · The settlement that made the rest of the document possible, carried by a single state’s margin

Stand on the margin for a full minute, because students raised on the document’s permanence underrate how close it came to not existing. Five states for the committee’s terms, four against, Massachusetts divided and so counted on neither side. Had one delegation changed its vote, the report fails, the Convention returns to the July 2 tie with its tempers spent, and the most likely sequel is adjournment and a union dissolving into regional confederacies, the outcome the delegates themselves named in debate. The lesson question asks how fifty five men designed a government. Part of the honest answer is that the design survived by one vote in the middle of July, and that the delegates who lost that vote, the large state men who had written the Virginia Plan, accepted the loss and stayed in the room. Institutions, students should hear, are built by losers who stay.

Slide 14 · Section E: Counting the enslaved (divider)

On the slide: Counting the Enslaved · Three fifths, in the document’s own arithmetic, and slavery protected without its name.

Section E is the lesson’s center of gravity, and it should be taught at the reading’s pace, in plain words. The Great Compromise apportioned the House by population, which forced the question the Articles had dodged since 1783: how does the count treat people who are held as property, who cannot vote, hold office, or leave? The southern position in the room was stated without embarrassment in the debates Madison recorded: enslaved people should count as people when seats were divided and as property when wealth and taxes were counted. The settlement the Convention reached was not invented in 1787. It was borrowed from a Confederation tax formula of 1783, which had set the same fraction for requisitions the states never paid. Name the section for what it is before the first slide of content: this is where the Constitution priced enslaved people into the republic’s arithmetic.

Slide 15 · Three fifths of a person

On the slide: Three fifths of a person · ▪ Counted as people for seats, as property for taxes / ▪ The fraction borrowed from a tax formula of 1783 / ▪ Five enslaved people add three to a state’s count / ▪ 100,000 held adds 60,000

Work the arithmetic on the board exactly as the quick check and the unit test will ask for it, because the fraction is easy to recite and easy to misunderstand. The clause adds to the whole number of free persons three fifths of all other persons. One hundred thousand multiplied by three fifths is sixty thousand: a state’s delegation grows by the count of sixty thousand people who do not exist as voters anywhere in the system. Then state whose interest the number served, in the record’s terms. The tie between representation and direct taxes was the northern answer, the same count that raised a state’s seats would raise its share of direct taxes, and students should give that answer its weight. Then set it down again: direct taxes were rare and hard to levy, while House seats voted on every law, every year, and the advantage compounded for as long as slavery lasted, which is the honest completion of the sentence.

Slide 16 · 3/5: the fraction in the arithmetic

On the slide: 3/5 · of a person: the fraction the Constitution counted for each enslaved person · Applied to House seats and to direct taxes alike, and carried through the House count into the electoral college

This is the slide to teach slowly and without decoration. The word slavery appears nowhere in the Constitution, and students should be asked to find the institution anyway, the way the lesson has taught them to find it: in the arithmetic. Because a state’s presidential electors equal its members of Congress, the three fifths count raised the slave states’ weight in choosing every president, an advantage that ran through the republic’s first seven decades and shaped its politics on slavery itself. Madison recorded the discomfort in the room, in the delegates’ own words, at Station exercises later in the hour, and he wrote in his later years that the Convention had not confronted the thing by its right name. Hold both facts together for the class: men in that room knew what the clause did, said so in debate, and adopted it anyway, because the union they were building was priced in this currency. That is the settlement the writing task will ask students to judge.

Slide 17 · Guarded in careful language

On the slide: Guarded in careful language · ▪ The Atlantic slave trade protected until 1808 / ▪ Congress barred from prohibiting it before that year / ▪ Escape into a free state: delivered up on claim / ▪ The word slavery appears

Take the two companion clauses in turn, in the document’s own wording, because the wording is the evidence. The trade clause does not say slave trade. It bars Congress from prohibiting the migration or importation of such persons as any of the states shall think proper to admit, before the year 1808, a date twenty years out that let delegates tell themselves and their constituents that the trade had been given an end. The return clause does not say enslaved or fugitive. It speaks of a person held to service or labour, escaping into another state, to be delivered up on claim of the party to whom the service is due, which made every free state’s soil an errand route for slave catchers and wrote the obligation into the Constitution itself. Ask students why men who could write We the People with such economy chose fog here. The record answers plainly: a named institution would have cost votes in the North and confessions in the South, and the union was being held together, in this section, by what the text refused to say.

Slide 18 · Section F: The design assembled (divider)

On the slide: The Design Assembled · Power divided between nation and states, then divided again inside the nation.

Section F assembles what the compromises left standing, and its organizing thought is division twice over. The first division is federalism, the settlement between the national government the Virginia Plan demanded and the states the New Jersey Plan defended: each level acts directly on the people within its own sphere, and the states keep the ordinary business of life. The second division is the separation of powers inside the federal government, legislature, executive, and judiciary, each armed against the others by the checks the delegates laced between them. Present the section as engineering with a theory of human nature inside it. The framers had read their history and their own summer: power concentrated invites abuse, so the design assumes ambition in every officeholder and sets the ambitions against one another. Students met the theory’s plain version in the vocabulary, and the slides now walk its machinery part by part.

Slide 19 · Divided, then laced together

On the slide: Divided, then laced together · ▪ Federalism: nation and states, each in its sphere / ▪ Enumerated powers, listed one by one / ▪ Necessary and proper, toward unforeseen cases / ▪ A veto, and an override to answer it /

The image is the first printed edition of the Constitution, run off by Dunlap and Claypoole in September 1787 so the delegates could read the finished text entire, the ancestor of Washington’s annotated draft at Station 3. Walk the machinery in the order a law travels. Congress, holding the enumerated powers of Article I, passes a bill. The president signs or vetoes it; Congress may override by two thirds of both houses. The Senate consents to the treaties the president negotiates and the appointments he makes. The courts, their judges holding office during good behavior rather than at any politician’s pleasure, judge cases under the laws, and judicial review, the power to void a law against the Constitution, stands nowhere written in the text and everywhere implied by a written constitution that calls itself supreme law. End on the enumeration’s double edge: a list limits, and the necessary and proper clause stretches the list, and the argument between those two facts is the next two centuries of American politics in embryo.

Slide 20 · Chosen by electors, for tangled reasons

On the slide: Chosen by electors, for tangled reasons · ▪ Not by Congress, not by direct national vote / ▪ Slow news: voters could not know distant men / ▪ A single national contest invited cabal / ▪ The three fifths count raised

Teach the electoral college with the care the reading uses, because this is where a single cause is most often sold as the whole story. The debates state several reasons in the delegates’ own voices. Election by Congress, the Virginia Plan’s method, would make the president the legislature’s creature, bargaining for his office and his reelection. Direct election faced a continent of slow news, where voters in Georgia could not know a candidate from New Hampshire, and a single national pool for votes invited cabal and foreign intrigue in the framers’ telling. Electors, meeting separately in their own states, answered both objections. Alongside the stated reasons, state the effect without inflating it into the sole cause: because electors equal a state’s members of Congress, the three fifths count raised the slave states’ weight in choosing presidents, an advantage the design carried whether or not every delegate named it aloud. Scholars argue over how heavily that effect weighed in the room. The documents in this lesson let students see both the stated reasons and the arithmetic, and the honest sentence holds the two together.

Slide 21 · Section G: What was left out, and the close (divider)

On the slide: What Was Left Out, and the Close · The State House from Birch’s Views, 1800: / the room emptied, the document gone out to the states.

Section G closes the summer on two facts that pull against each other, and the lesson ends by holding them together rather than choosing. The first is an omission: on September 12, with the text nearly finished, George Mason moved for a committee to prepare a bill of rights, and the Convention refused without a single state voting in favor, on the stated ground that the states’ own declarations of rights were protection enough. The second is an opening: the Committee of Style began the document not with the states’ names, as the Articles had, but with We the People of the United States. Mason, Gerry, and Randolph carried the omission into the open and refused to sign. The Preamble’s claim went out to the states in the same envelope. Students should leave the section able to state both facts in one breath, because the ratification contest of the next lesson is nothing but those two facts argued at full length.

Slide 22 · No bill of rights; We the People

On the slide: No bill of rights; We the People · ▪ September 12: Mason moves for a bill of rights / ▪ Refused, with no state voting in favor / ▪ The states’ declarations held protection enough / ▪ The Preamble written last, by the

Slow down on the refusal’s reasoning before judging it, because the delegates were not careless men having a tired afternoon, though they were tired and said so. Their argument ran that the federal government held only enumerated powers, that a bill of rights against a government of listed powers was unnecessary, and that listing some rights might endanger others by implying the list was complete. The states’ declarations, meanwhile, bound state governments, which held the general police power over daily life, and the new federal government was not, in this telling, the danger the declarations guarded against. The argument was coherent and it failed politically within the year, as the next lesson shows: state after state demanded amendments as the price of ratification, and Mason’s refusal became the Anti-Federalists’ strongest card. Then turn to the opening. We the People was Gouverneur Morris’s penmanship in the Committee of Style, and its claim, that the people and not the states ordained the government, was the boldest sentence in the document and the one its opponents attacked first.

Slide 23 · 39 signed on September 17

On the slide: 39 · delegates signed the Constitution on September 17, 1787 · Three refused: George Mason, Elbridge Gerry, and Edmund Randolph · Nine of thirteen states would make it law among those that ratified

Close the count honestly. Thirty nine signatures stand on the engrossed parchment, and the attestation above them claims the unanimous consent of the states present, a form of words Gouverneur Morris devised so that signing attested the Convention’s act without pledging every signer’s approval of every clause, the formula Franklin’s speech had pleaded for. Three men who had sat all summer refused: Randolph, who had presented the Virginia Plan on the Convention’s first working days; Mason, the bill of rights’ author of five days before; and Gerry of Massachusetts. Their refusals were not defections from the founding. They were the founding’s first opposition, and each man carried his reasons home to the ratification fight, where the document’s fate would be decided by conventions of the people in the states, nine of thirteen required, exactly as Article VII provided. Franklin’s rising sun belongs to this slide’s story: watching the last signatures, he told the men near him that the sun carved on Washington’s chair, which had puzzled him all summer, was rising.

Slide 24 · The Convention at a glance

On the slide: Five stages, one summer · The room · May 25 to September 17, 1787 · Secrecy, Washington silent, Madison recording · The deadlock · Virginia Plan against New Jersey Plan · July 2: the vote on equality ties · The settlemen

This slide mirrors the final table in the guided notes, The Convention at a Glance, and students should complete that table now, from memory where they can and from the lesson where they must. The rows compress the arc. The room pairs the Convention’s working conditions, secrecy and a silent chair, with the record those conditions produced. The deadlock pairs the two plans with the July 2 tie that nearly dissolved the Convention. The settlement holds July 16 and its five to four margin. The count row refuses euphemism, in the table as in the reading: seats and electors built on people held as property. The close pairs the refused bill of rights with the thirty nine signatures and the Preamble’s claim. Grade the table generously on wording and strictly on pairing: every stage must show what was settled and what it cost or left open, because that pairing is the lesson’s whole habit of mind.

Slide 25 · Synthesis: what the room built, and what it charged

On the slide: What the room built, and what it charged · ▪ The strength: a national government that could act / ▪ The limit: divided powers, checked at every joint / ▪ The settlement: people in one house, states in the other / ▪ T

Draw the lesson together around the question’s two halves, because the exit ticket and the writing task both return to them. The strength is real and students should be able to inventory it: enumerated national powers with a taxing power the Confederation never held, a House resting on the people, an executive with energy, courts independent of annual politics. The limits are real in the same inventory: two houses that must agree, a veto with its override, consent and impeachment, judges beyond reach, and an amendment rule that denies any bare majority the power to remake the frame. Then state the price in the plain words the lesson has used all hour. The union’s arithmetic rested on enslaved people counted at three fifths toward other men’s power, the trade in them protected for twenty years, their escape made a constitutional errand, and the whole arrangement carried in language that never names it. The delegates knew, and signed, and Franklin called the sun rising. Students now judge that sentence for themselves in the verdict.

Slide 26 · Vocabulary: words for the meeting and the plans

On the slide: Words for the meeting and the plans · Constitutional Convention · the Philadelphia meeting of 1787 that wrote the Constitution · Virginia Plan · Madison’s plan: a legislature apportioned by population · New Jersey Plan ·

These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them. The first term is Constitutional Convention: the meeting at Philadelphia from May to September 1787, called to revise the Articles of Confederation, which instead wrote the Constitution of the United States. The second is Virginia Plan: the plan proposed at the Convention by Virginia's delegation, drafted chiefly by James Madison, for a strong national government with a legislature apportioned by population. The third is New Jersey Plan: the plan proposed at the Convention by the small states, which kept a one house legislature with equal votes for every state under a strengthened Articles of Confederation. The fourth is Great Compromise: the Convention's settlement, also called the Connecticut Compromise, which apportioned the House of Representatives by population and gave every state two senators in the Senate.

Slide 27 · Vocabulary: words for the count and the design

On the slide: Words for the count and the design · Three-Fifths Compromise · each enslaved person counted as three fifths of a person · federalism · power divided between nation and states · separation of powers · three branches, each

Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly, and the set of seven is complete with these. The first is Three-Fifths Compromise: the Convention's settlement that counted each enslaved person as three fifths of a person when apportioning representatives and direct taxes among the states. Students should anchor the term to both of its applications, seats and taxes, because the test reaches for the pairing. The second is federalism: the division of power between a national government and state governments, each supreme in its own sphere, which the Constitution established in place of the Confederation. The third is separation of powers: the division of the federal government into legislative, executive, and judicial branches, each with its own powers and the means to check the others, so no single branch could rule alone. Test the pairing students most often blur: federalism divides power between levels of government, while separation of powers divides it among branches at the same level.

Slide 28 · Now your turn: answer the question and exit ticket

On the slide: Answer the question · How did fifty five men in one room design a government strong enough to act and limited enough to trust? Argue it from the records, with dates, votes, and the count. · Activity · Philadelphia in Fiv

Close by returning to the lesson question and handing the lesson to the work. The activity, Philadelphia in Five Records, runs five stations through the summer: the Preamble entire, Madison’s Notes for September 17 with Franklin’s speech inside them, Washington’s annotated draft, Article I, Section 2 in the National Archives transcription, and Franklin’s closing speech in the Avalon Project text of Madison’s Notes, ending in a verdict on which compromise was most necessary and which cost the republic most. The notebook page charts the Convention’s four great questions by proposal, settlement, and price. The quick check tests the Preamble’s claim and its six purposes, the three fifths arithmetic, and the summer’s sequence of dates against blueprint entries 13 and 14. The writing task requires one compromise followed through both its settlement and its price. The exit ticket has three parts: the Great Compromise defined in one sentence, the count worked for a state holding 240,000 free people and 100,000 enslaved people, and the lesson question in two or three sentences.
