Declaring Independence

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A. The King Becomes the Target

Through 1775 the resistance described itself as a defense of British liberties. Colonists petitioned, boycotted, and fought while insisting that Parliament and the king's ministers, not the king, had broken the old constitution. Independence was a word many of the same newspapers treated as ruin and treason. The king closed that middle ground himself. In August 1775 he proclaimed the colonies in open rebellion and ordered his officers and loyal subjects to suppress it. His Prohibitory Act that December put American ships and goods outside his protection, an enemy's property. He also hired German soldiers to fight in America, some thirty thousand across the war by the standard count. Colonists read the three acts together: a king who made war on his subjects had dissolved the bond from his own side.

B. Common Sense Changes the Argument

Into that opening stepped Thomas Paine, an English immigrant who had reached Philadelphia in 1774. His pamphlet Common Sense, published in January 1776 in fewer than fifty pages, refused the old framing. The problem, Paine argued, was not one tax or one set of ministers. Monarchy itself was corrupt, condemned in Scripture, and absurd in nature, and hereditary succession passed power to children and fools as readily as to the able. A continent, he argued, should not be governed by an island. He wrote in the plain language of the Bible and the tavern, for readers who had never opened a work of political theory. It sold well over one hundred thousand copies within months, by the estimates historians work from, in colonies of about two and a half million people.

C. The Colonies Turn Toward Independence

Through the spring of 1776 the colonies moved behind the argument. Royal governments collapsed or fled, conventions drafted new state constitutions, and one assembly after another instructed its delegates in Congress to vote for independence. The instructions carried weight because many delegates lacked authority from home to take the final step. On June 7 Richard Henry Lee of Virginia rose in Congress and moved that the United Colonies are, and of right ought to be, free and independent States. Congress postponed the vote three weeks so the remaining colonies could send instructions, and used the interval to prepare the public statement the decision required. A vote to break with Britain needed an argument the world could judge.

D. Drafting and Cutting the Declaration

Congress appointed a committee of five to draft it: John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Roger Sherman, Robert Livingston, and Thomas Jefferson. Jefferson did the writing, at a portable desk in a rented Philadelphia room, drawing on the language of natural rights and a decade of colonial grievance lists. Congress then edited hard, cutting about a quarter of the text and striking whole passages. The largest cut removed Jefferson's charge that the king had waged cruel war against human nature itself in the slave trade, carried distant peoples into slavery in another hemisphere, and blocked colonial laws meant to end the trade. The passage fell, the record shows, because South Carolina and Georgia would not accept it and northern delegates tied to ports that profited from the trade would not press it.

E. The Argument: Truths and Consent

The text opens with its theory, truths it calls self-evident, before it names a single injury. All men are created equal, and their Creator endows them with rights no government gives and none may rightly take, among them life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. Governments exist to secure those rights, and they draw their just powers from the consent of the governed. That theory is a social contract: the people obey, the government protects their rights, and a government destructive of those ends may be altered or abolished. The preamble also limits its own claim. Long established governments, it warns, should not be changed for light and transient causes, so the break must be earned by the evidence that follows.

F. The Argument: Charges Against the King

The evidence is a list of charges, and every charge names the king alone, because the bond being dissolved ran to the Crown, not to Parliament. The king had refused assent to necessary laws, dissolved assemblies, obstructed justice, kept standing armies in peace, cut off trade, imposed taxes without consent, denied jury trials, and sent colonists overseas for trial. The closing charges move from misgovernment to war. He had plundered coasts, burned towns, and hired foreign mercenaries. He had excited domestic insurrections, meaning Dunmore's offer of freedom to enslaved people who left rebel masters to fight for the king. He had brought on the inhabitants of the frontiers the merciless Indian Savages, the document's own words for Native nations allied with Britain. Both charges stand in the same document that declares all men created equal.

G. Adoption, Risk, and the Standard Left Open

Congress voted for independence on July 2 and adopted the Declaration's text on July 4. John Dunlap printed it that night, and his broadside carried it to the states. Washington had it read to his army on July 9, as New Yorkers pulled down the king's statue to melt into bullets. Most signers signed on August 2, pledging their lives, fortunes, and sacred honor to words British law treated as treason. The Declaration freed no one by itself and settled nothing about who counted as the people. Abigail Adams had written to her husband that March to remember the ladies, warning that women would not hold themselves bound by laws in which they had no voice. He answered with a laugh. The standard stood anyway, in words the excluded could quote back.

5. Answer the lesson question. Full credit answers hold the theory and the need together. The Declaration argued that governments rest on the consent of the governed and exist to secure natural rights, that George III had broken that contract by a long train of abuses charged against him alone, and that the colonies were therefore absolved from allegiance and free and independent. Americans needed the argument because independence was treason in British law and ruin in many colonists' minds: the resistance had spent a decade claiming British liberties, foreign help required a stated cause, and the delegates' own authority ran through the instructions their colonies sent that spring. Strong answers add the record's limits: Congress struck Jefferson's charge on the slave trade before adoption, and the closing grievances against the king sit in the same document as the claim that all men are created equal. Any three accurate facts, one on the grievances and one on the deleted passage, earn full credit.
