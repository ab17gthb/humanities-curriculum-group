Guided Notes: Declaring Independence

Lesson question: What did the Declaration argue, and why did Americans need an argument at all?

Unit question: Why did the colonies break with Britain, and what did Americans build in the place of the empire they rejected?

Key Terms

Common Sense: Thomas Paine's pamphlet of January 1776 that argued in plain language that monarchy itself was corrupt and that America should declare independence and found a republic

natural rights: the rights held to belong to all people by nature rather than by any government's gift, which the Declaration of Independence names as life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness

unalienable rights: rights that cannot be taken away or surrendered, which the Declaration of Independence holds are given to all men by their Creator and stand beyond any government's reach

consent of the governed: the principle that a government draws its just powers from the agreement of the people it governs, the ground on which the Declaration of Independence rests its case

social contract: the theory that government rests on an agreement between rulers and ruled, in which the people obey the laws and the government protects their rights, and either side's breach can dissolve the bond

republic: a state governed by representatives chosen by its citizens under law, without a king or hereditary ruler, the form the American states adopted after independence

Declaration of Independence: the document adopted by the Continental Congress on July 4, 1776, written chiefly by Thomas Jefferson, that declared the thirteen colonies free and independent states and argued the case from natural rights and the king's abuses

The Argument at a Glance. The target: through 1775 the resistance blamed Parliament and the king's ministers; the king's own acts of 1775, the Proclamation of Rebellion, the Prohibitory Act, and the hiring of some thirty thousand German soldiers by the standard count, made the king himself the target, because the bond to be dissolved ran to the Crown. The pamphlet: Paine's Common Sense of January 1776 recast the quarrel as monarchy against republic and sold well over one hundred thousand copies within months, by the estimates historians work from. The theory: all men are created equal and hold unalienable rights; governments draw just powers from the consent of the governed and may be altered or abolished when destructive of those ends. The charges: a long train of abuses against the king alone, closing with the war charges, Dunmore's freedom offer charged as excited domestic insurrections, and Native nations allied with Britain named in the document's own words as merciless Indian Savages. The decision: independence voted July 2, the text adopted July 4, and Congress's largest cut, the slave trade charge, removed before adoption.

Exit ticket 1. Natural rights are the rights held to belong to all people by nature rather than by any government's gift. The Declaration names life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness among them.

Exit ticket 2. The largest cut removed Jefferson's charge that the king had waged cruel war against human nature itself in the slave trade, carried distant peoples into slavery in another hemisphere, and blocked colonial laws meant to end the trade. The record shows it fell because South Carolina and Georgia would not accept it and northern delegates tied to ports that profited from the trade would not press it.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer pairing a grievance fact with a need fact. The strongest answers run from the charges against the king alone, the only bond the document could dissolve, to the need: a decade of resistance had claimed British liberties, independence was treason in British law, and the delegates acted on instructions their colonies sent that spring, so the break required a public argument the world could judge.
