Writing Task: Why an Argument

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: What did the Declaration argue, and why did Americans need an argument at all? A strong answer states the argument first, natural rights held by all, government by consent, and a king charged with a long train of abuses. It then explains why a public argument had to exist: a decade of resistance had claimed British liberties rather than independence, the delegates acted on instructions sent that spring, foreign powers would not aid an unexplained rebellion, and the signers were publishing words British law treated as treason. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and activity. Write in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank wording.

Evidence Bank

1. Through 1775 the resistance blamed Parliament and the king's ministers. The king closed the middle ground: the Proclamation of Rebellion of 1775, the Prohibitory Act, and German soldiers hired, some thirty thousand across the war by the standard count.

2. Paine's Common Sense, January 1776, fewer than fifty pages, argued that monarchy itself was corrupt, condemned in Scripture and absurd in nature, and that hereditary succession passed power to children and fools. It sold well over one hundred thousand copies within months, by the estimates historians work from, in colonies of about two and a half million people.

3. In the spring of 1776 the colonies instructed delegates to vote for independence, and on June 7 Lee moved that the United Colonies are, and of right ought to be, free and independent States. Congress postponed the vote three weeks for the remaining instructions.

4. A committee of five prepared the statement and Jefferson did the writing. Congress cut about a quarter of his draft, striking his charge that the king had waged cruel war against human nature itself in the slave trade. South Carolina and Georgia would not accept it, and northern delegates tied to the trade would not press it.

5. The adopted preamble holds that all men are created equal and endowed with unalienable rights, that governments derive just powers from the consent of the governed, and that a destructive government may be altered or abolished, while warning against change for light and transient causes.

6. The grievances charge the king alone: assent refused, assemblies dissolved, taxes without consent, coasts plundered, mercenaries hired, insurrections excited, and Native nations named as merciless Indian Savages in the document's words. Congress voted independence July 2 and adopted the text July 4.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the prompt, the argument stated as theory plus charges, and the need stated as a public justification before the colonies, the world, and the signers' own consciences. (Blueprint 5 and Blueprint 6)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 supports the turn from ministers to the king. (Blueprint 5) Bank item 2 supports Paine's reframing and its reach, stated as an estimate. (Blueprint 5) Bank item 3 supports the instructions and the postponed vote. (Blueprint 6) Bank item 4 supports the drafting and the deleted passage. (Blueprint 6) Bank item 5 supports the preamble's theory and its caution. (Blueprint 6) Bank item 6 supports the grievances against the king alone and the adoption sequence. (Blueprint 6) A full credit essay draws on at least two groups, the road to the document and the document itself.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. An essay that lists Paine, the grievances, and the signing without saying which half of the question each fact answers has assembled the lesson's events without its argument, and should earn partial credit.
  • An ending that keeps the record's limit in view, as the lesson does: the Declaration stated a standard of consent and equal rights in words wide enough for the excluded to quote back, and it freed no one by itself.

Model opening for teacher reference. The Declaration argued a contract and a breach. Its preamble holds that all men are created equal, that their rights come from their Creator rather than from any government, and that just powers rest on the consent of the governed. Its grievances then charge one defendant, the king, with the long train of abuses that the preamble's own caution required before a long established government could be thrown off. America needed that argument in print because muskets alone could not make independence lawful in anyone's eyes, including many Americans'. The resistance had spent ten years claiming the rights of Britons; in 1776 it had to explain, to wavering neighbors, to foreign courts, and to soldiers asked to fight, why it was now dissolving the bond entirely, and why the king's own acts of 1775 had dissolved it first. A full model continues in this pattern, adding Paine's January reframing and the June instructions, then closing on the standard the document left standing and the people it left outside it.

Common weak answers. A birthday answer (the Declaration announced independence on July 4) drops the argument entirely: the vote came on July 2, and the text exists because the vote needed defending. A list answer (the Declaration lists grievances) drops the theory that makes the grievances a legal case rather than a complaint, and drops the caution that disciplines both. A clean hands answer (the Declaration proclaimed universal freedom) ignores the passage Congress struck on the slave trade and the closing grievances' own words about enslaved people and Native nations. Credit requires both records held at once: the standard the document set, and the cuts and charges inside it.
