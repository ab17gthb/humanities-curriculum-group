Writing Task: Who Won the Argument of 1787 and 1788

The prompt. Answer the closing question of the lesson in a developed essay of about 300 words: who won the argument of 1787 and 1788, the Federalists who got their Constitution or the Anti-Federalists who got its first ten amendments? A strong answer states what each side won in the settlement the documents record, weighs the wins against each other using the convention votes, and answers the strongest claim the other side can make from the same record. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your verdict.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading, stations, and documents. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank wording, except where a bank item quotes a document.

Evidence Bank

1. Madison, in Federalist No. 10, defined a faction as citizens united by a passion or interest adverse to the rights of others, and argued that extending the sphere multiplies parties and interests, so a majority can rarely hold a harmful motive or act in unison. (Federalist No. 10, 1787, Congress.gov, audited TPSP record.)

2. Brutus, in Brutus No. 1, argued that the necessary and proper and supremacy clauses gave the new government absolute and uncontroulable power, and that a free republic cannot long subsist over a country of the great extent of these states. (Brutus No. 1, 1787, Constitution.org, audited TPSP record.)

3. Madison, in Federalist No. 51, argued that ambition must counteract ambition, and that power divided between two governments and subdivided among departments gives rights a double security. (Federalist No. 51, 1788, Congress.gov, audited TPSP record.)

4. Delaware ratified unanimously on December 7, 1787. Massachusetts ratified in February 1788, 187 to 168, while recommending amendments for the first Congress. New Hampshire became the ninth state in June 1788. Virginia ratified 89 to 79 and New York 30 to 27, both recommending amendments. (Lesson reading, section E.)

5. The new government opened at New York in April 1789, Washington inaugurated on April 30 after every elector voted for him. Congress built the executive departments and the federal courts. Rhode Island stayed outside until May 1790, when it ratified by two votes. (Lesson reading, sections E and F.)

6. Madison introduced amendments on June 8, 1789. Seventeen passed the House, twelve the Senate, and ten were ratified by December 15, 1791, closing with a Ninth Amendment guarding unlisted rights and a Tenth reserving undelegated powers. (Lesson reading, section G.)

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A verdict argued from the settlement, not from the slogans of either side. The settlement has two halves, a Constitution in force and a Bill of Rights inside it, and a full credit essay weighs both before naming a winner. (Blueprint 15)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. Bank item 1 carries the Federalist argument at its strongest, the control of faction by extension. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 2 carries the Anti-Federalist argument at its strongest, consolidation and size. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 3 carries the design defense, the double security of the compound republic. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 4 carries the votes and the Massachusetts form, ratification with recommended amendments, at 187 to 168, 89 to 79, and 30 to 27. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 5 carries the launch, a government opened on schedule with Rhode Island still outside. (Blueprint 15) Bank item 6 carries the redemption, twelve amendments proposed in 1789 and ten ratified by December 15, 1791. (Blueprint 15)
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the verdict. The decisive link is the price of the close votes: students should argue that the amendments were not a gift after victory but a condition of it, set at Massachusetts and copied at Richmond and Poughkeepsie, whichever winner they name.
  • One or two sentences answering the strongest claim of the other side. A Federalist verdict must answer that its Constitution passed three big states only with the amendments promised. An Anti-Federalist verdict must answer that its side lost every vote on the frame itself and that the amendments it won were proposed and carried by Madison, its leading opponent in the argument.

Model opening for teacher reference. The Federalists won the argument and the Anti-Federalists set its terms, and the second half of that sentence is the one the votes prove. The Constitution Brutus warned against was ratified by nine states in nine months and opened for business in April 1789 with the compound republic intact. Yet it passed Massachusetts by nineteen votes, Virginia by ten, and New York by three, and in each of those conventions the majority bought its margin with the same promise, amendments recommended to the first Congress. A full model in this line follows the promise to its redemption, Madison amendments of June 8, 1789 and the ten ratifications completed on December 15, 1791, and closes on the Tenth Amendment, where the boundary Brutus demanded stands written inside the document he opposed.

Common weak answers. The Federalists won because the Constitution passed (a result, not a weighing) ignores that the passing itself was conditioned on the amendments. The Anti-Federalists won because we now prize the Bill of Rights (a present judgment, not a 1788 one) skips the record that their remedy, confederation kept and the frame rejected, was voted down in state after state. An essay with no answer to the other side's strongest claim earns partial credit, because the prompt asks for a weighing, not a verdict alone.
