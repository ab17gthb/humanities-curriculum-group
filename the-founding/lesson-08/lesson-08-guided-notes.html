Guided Notes: Ratification and the Bill of Rights

Lesson question: How was the Constitution sold to a skeptical people, and what did the skeptics win?

Unit question: Why did the colonies break with Britain, and what did Americans build in the place of the empire they rejected?

Key Terms

ratification: the formal approval of a proposed constitution or treaty by the body empowered to accept it, which for the Constitution meant conventions in nine of the thirteen states

Federalists: the supporters of the Constitution in the ratification contests of 1787 and 1788, who argued that the union needed a stronger national government to survive

Anti-Federalists: the opponents of the Constitution in the ratification contests of 1787 and 1788, who argued that it gave the national government too much power and threatened liberty, above all by omitting a bill of rights

Federalist Papers: the eighty-five essays written by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay under the name Publius to persuade New York to ratify the Constitution, published in newspapers in 1787 and 1788

faction: in Madison's argument in Federalist No. 10, a group of citizens united by a passion or interest harmful to the rights of others or to the whole community, whose effects a large republic could control

Bill of Rights: the first ten amendments to the Constitution, proposed by the First Congress in 1789 and ratified by the states by December 1791, which list rights the federal government may not violate

amendment: a formal change or addition to a constitution, made for the Constitution of the United States by proposal of two thirds of Congress and ratification by three fourths of the states

Summary table. The Federalist answer: Hamilton, Madison, and Jay, writing as Publius, argued that an extended republic controls faction and that a government divided between nation and states and among departments controls itself, with justice as its end. It won ratification in nine states by June 1788 and in eleven by the end of July. The Anti-Federalist warning: Brutus and the writers beside him argued that a free republic could not span a continent, that the necessary and proper and supremacy clauses would consolidate power, and that a frame with no bill of rights left liberty unfenced. It lost the votes and set the price of the wins, since state after state ratified while recommending amendments. The state contests: Delaware ratified unanimously on December 7, 1787, Massachusetts settled 187 to 168 in February 1788 on ratification with recommended amendments, New Hampshire became the ninth state in June, Virginia ratified 89 to 79, New York 30 to 27 in July, and Rhode Island held out until May 1790. The Bill of Rights: Madison introduced amendments on June 8, 1789, seventeen passed the House and twelve the Senate, and ten were ratified by December 15, 1791, closing with the Ninth and Tenth Amendments, which guard rights the list does not name and reserve powers the Constitution does not delegate.

Exit ticket 1. Conventions elected in each state for that single purpose had to approve the Constitution. Approval by nine of the thirteen states put it in force among the states that ratified. The plan bypassed the state legislatures.

Exit ticket 2. A faction is a group of citizens, a majority or a minority, united by a passion or an interest harmful to the rights of others or to the whole community. In a large republic, parties and interests multiply across the territory, so an unjust majority can rarely form or find itself, while in a small republic a majority faction can form and rule at once.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that names both wins and the joining settlement. The Federalists won the Constitution itself, ratified by nine states by June 1788. The Anti-Federalists won the first ten amendments, promised in the Massachusetts settlement of ratification with recommended amendments and ratified by December 15, 1791. Strong answers name a close vote, such as Massachusetts 187 to 168, Virginia 89 to 79, or New York 30 to 27, as the ground on which the promise had to be made.
