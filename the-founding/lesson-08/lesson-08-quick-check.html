Quick Check: Two Arguments About Size

Directions: Read the two passages and the background, then answer the questions.

Document A. "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens; or if such a common motive exists, it will be more difficult for all who feel it to discover their own strength, and to act in unison with each other."

Source: James Madison, Federalist No. 10, 1787 (Congress.gov; spelling modernized), as carried in the audited TPSP record.

Document B. "In a republic, the manners, sentiments, and interests of the people should be similar. If this be not the case, there will be a constant clashing of opinions; and the representatives of one part will be continually striving against those of the other. [...] The United States includes a variety of climates. The productions of the different parts of the union are very variant, and their interests, of consequence, diverse."

Source: Brutus (attributed to Robert Yates), Brutus No. 1, 1787 (Constitution.org), as carried in the audited TPSP record. Cuts are marked with ellipses.

Background. The Constitution took effect among the states that ratified it once nine of thirteen state conventions approved it, beginning with Delaware on December 7, 1787. Massachusetts ratified in February 1788, 187 to 168, while recommending amendments, and New Hampshire became the ninth state in June. Virginia ratified 89 to 79 and New York 30 to 27. Madison introduced amendments in the First Congress on June 8, 1789, and ten were ratified by December 15, 1791.

1. In Document A, state the two effects Madison says extending the sphere has on a majority that shares a harmful motive.

2. In Document B, state the condition Brutus says a republic requires of its people, and name the consequence he predicts when the condition fails.

3. Using the background, state the Massachusetts settlement of February 1788, giving its vote, and explain what the settlement promised the losing side.

4. The two documents argue opposite conclusions from the same fact, the variety of interests in a large country. State the fact as each writer uses it, and judge which writer the ratification record of 1788 supports on the narrow question of whether a large republic could be formed at all.

Teacher Answer Key

1. State the two effects of extending the sphere. It makes it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens, and, if such a common motive exists, it makes it more difficult for all who feel it to discover their own strength and to act in unison with each other. Both effects are required for full credit. (Blueprint 15)

2. State the required condition and the predicted consequence. In a republic, the manners, sentiments, and interests of the people should be similar. When they are not, there will be a constant clashing of opinions, and the representatives of one part will be continually striving against those of the other, retarding the operations of government. (Blueprint 15)

3. State the Massachusetts settlement and its promise. The Massachusetts convention ratified the Constitution at once, by 187 to 168 in February 1788, and at the same time recommended amendments for the first Congress to take up. The promise to the losing side was that the objection they had pressed hardest, the missing bill of rights, would be repaired inside the new government rather than by rejecting it. Madison redeemed the promise by introducing amendments on June 8, 1789. (Blueprint 15)

4. State the shared fact as each writer uses it, and judge the narrow question. The shared fact is the variety of parties, interests, climates, and manners across a large territory. Madison uses the variety as a safeguard, since multiplied interests keep an unjust majority from forming or coordinating. Brutus uses the same variety as a defect, since a republic needs similar manners, sentiments, and interests, and diversity sets its representatives striving against each other. On the narrow question of formation, the record of 1788 supports Madison: conventions in nine states ratified by June 1788 and the government opened in April 1789, so a large republic could be formed. Credit answers that keep the judgment narrow, since whether the formed republic would work as Madison predicted is a separate question the documents leave open. (Blueprint 15)

Teacher note. This check is formative. Questions 1 and 2 test whether students can keep two opposite uses of one fact separate in their own notes, the skill the activity grading guide rewards. Questions 3 and 4 test the settlement half of the blueprint line, the Massachusetts compromise and the Bill of Rights it produced.
