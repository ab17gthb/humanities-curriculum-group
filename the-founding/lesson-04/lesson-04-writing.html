Writing Task: What Won the War

The prompt. Write a developed essay of about 300 words answering the lesson question from this lesson's documents: how did the United States survive a war against the world's strongest empire, and win it? Name what won the war, in your judgment, and defend the judgment against the strongest rival cause in the record. Your essay must give the survival its full weight, the defeats of 1776 and the Valley Forge winter included, and must carry the figures the lesson states, with their labels, wherever you use them. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the evidence bank, and explain how each piece supports your claim.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and The War in Five Records. Write the history in your own sentences, and use the facts rather than copying the bank's wording. Keep the paired records whole wherever you use them: Saratoga beside the alliance it purchased, and the Yorktown surrender beside the treaty that converted it into a country.

Evidence Bank

1. Britain entered the war with the world's strongest navy, a funded treasury, a professional army, and hired German auxiliaries. Congress could not tax, the army was enlisted by the year and often unpaid, and the currency was losing value. Washington's strategy was to keep an army in being, since Britain had to destroy that army or hold a continent.

2. In 1776 the British took Long Island, New York City, and Fort Washington, and the army retreated across New Jersey with enlistments expiring. On Christmas night 1776 Washington recrossed the Delaware with some 2,400 men and took Trenton at dawn, then won again at Princeton on January 3, 1777. Recruits, supplies, and credit flowed back to a cause weeks from dissolving.

3. Burgoyne surrendered at Saratoga on October 17, 1777, some 5,800 men by the standard count, after Howe sailed to take Philadelphia instead of meeting him. On February 6, 1778 France signed the Treaty of Alliance, recognized the United States, and entered the war with money, ships, and soldiers. Its Article 8 bound both allies not to lay down their arms until American independence was assured.

4. At Valley Forge in the winter of 1777 and 1778 some two thousand men died of disease, hunger, and exposure, the figure stated as an estimate. Waldo's diary records provisions scarce, a camp crying No Meat, and soldiers still showing what he called a spirit of alacrity and contentment. Steuben drilled the survivors, and in June 1778 the army fought the British to a standstill at Monmouth. In May 1778 Congress addressed a tired country directly, admitting enemies in the country's bosom and framing the choice as war or the bitter cup of slavery.

5. Charleston fell in May 1780 with an entire American army, and a second army was destroyed at Camden that August. Militia destroyed a Loyalist force at King's Mountain in October 1780, Morgan wrecked Tarleton's legion at Cowpens in January 1781, reporting his own loss as 12 killed and 60 wounded against more than 500 enemy prisoners, and Greene wore Cornwallis down until he abandoned the Carolinas for Virginia.

6. In the summer of 1781 Washington and Rochambeau marched from New York to Virginia while de Grasse's French fleet sealed the Chesapeake. Some seventeen thousand American and French troops closed around Cornwallis's eight thousand, the figures stated as the standard counts, and Cornwallis surrendered on October 19, 1781. The Treaty of Paris of September 3, 1783 recognized the United States as free, sovereign, and independent, with a boundary at the Mississippi and the Great Lakes.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A judgment naming what won the war, stated directly and held through the essay. Endurance, the alliance, the southern turn, and the Yorktown combination can each earn full credit if the survival is carried and the rival cause is answered. An essay that narrates the war in order without ranking its causes has summarized the lesson rather than answered it. (Blueprint 7, Blueprint 8)
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately, drawn from at least two different parts of the record, the odds and 1776, Saratoga and the alliance, Valley Forge and the address, the southern turn, or Yorktown and the peace. An essay built on Yorktown alone cannot explain why there was an army and a fleet to close the trap. (Blueprint 8)
  • The survival stated at full strength wherever the verdict leans toward allies or battles: the defeats of 1776, an army weeks from dissolving that December, and a winter in which some two thousand men died, the figure stated as an estimate, without the army dissolving. A winning essay that shrinks the survival to enlarge the winning has failed the record. (Blueprint 7)
  • Figures carried with their labels wherever they are used: some 5,800 surrendered at Saratoga by the standard count, some two thousand dead at Valley Forge as an estimate, and some seventeen thousand allied troops around Cornwallis's eight thousand at Yorktown as the standard counts. A figure stated as exact that the lesson labels an estimate or a standard count is an accuracy error, not a style choice. (Blueprint 7, Blueprint 8)
  • The strongest rival cause stated at full strength and answered. Against an endurance verdict: without the French fleet there is no trap at Yorktown, and endurance explains survival, not victory. Against an alliance verdict: France signed in February 1778 with an army that had first survived 1776 and won at Saratoga, and an alliance with a dissolved army is a dead letter. Against a southern turn verdict: Morgan and Greene put Cornwallis in Virginia, and French ships and French regiments closed the trap he had walked into. (Blueprint 8)
  • The treaty given its work wherever the verdict ends at Yorktown: the surrender of October 19, 1781 was a fact on a field, and the Treaty of Paris of September 3, 1783 converted it into a recognized country with a Mississippi boundary. An essay that ends in 1781 has answered how the war was won on the field and left the winning's conversion unexamined. (Blueprint 8)

Model opening for teacher reference. The war was won by an army that refused to stop existing, armed at the last by the allies its survival had earned. Washington's strategy required only that an American army stay in being, and it nearly failed its test: beaten off Long Island, stripped of New York and Fort Washington, weeks from dissolving in December 1776, and thinned by some two thousand deaths, an estimate, at Valley Forge. The army that endured won the proof at Saratoga, where Burgoyne surrendered some 5,800 men by the standard count, and the proof purchased the French alliance. A full model continues through the southern turn and the Yorktown trap, answers the alliance alone claim at full strength, and closes on the judgment restated.

Common weak answers. An alliance essay built on bank 3 and bank 6 alone cannot explain why France waited for Saratoga, or what Congress was doing addressing a tired country in May 1778. An essay that reports figures without their labels repeats the numbers while dropping the lesson's discipline about them.
