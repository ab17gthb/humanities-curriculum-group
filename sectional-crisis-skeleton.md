## Unit frame

**Essential question**

- How did compromise over slavery finally fail, and what did the war it caused settle about slavery and the Union, and what did it leave open?

**Shape:** 8 content lessons, 1 review day, 1 test day. The planner's core plan is 18 instructional days. The unit opens with the Compromise of 1850, the settlement the Early Republic unit's closing question demanded and the Jackson and Reform unit deliberately did not teach: California knocking at the door as a free state, the Senate balanced at fifteen and fifteen, and a dying generation of giants making one last bargain whose enforcement clause, the Fugitive Slave Act, put slavery's officers on northern streets. It follows the crisis sequence the settlement failed to end: the antislavery argument at full voice in Stowe, Douglass, and Truth; the Kansas-Nebraska Act's repeal of the Missouri line and the guerrilla war in Kansas; a new sectional party and a Supreme Court decision that tried to settle the question out of politics; the Lincoln-Douglas debates, John Brown's raid, the election of 1860, and secession defended in the seceding states' own words. Then the war itself: Sumter and the picnic battle's lesson, the rival strategies, the cost read at Antietam, emancipation as policy and as self-executed fact, Black soldiers under arms, Gettysburg and Vicksburg, Grant's arithmetic and Sherman's march, Appomattox, the Second Inaugural's verdict on the war's cause, and Lincoln's assassination as the closing event of April 1865. Three boundaries hold. The Missouri Compromise and the gag rule era belong to the Early Republic and Jackson and Reform units; they are prior knowledge here, named in prose and nowhere retaught as core. The Thirteenth Amendment's passage by the House on January 31, 1865, is named in Lesson 8 as a wartime event, no more: the Reconstruction amendments' passage debates, the Freedmen's Bureau, and presidential Reconstruction belong to the Reconstruction unit (u6), which opens with the postwar settlement, and none of them is taught here. The unit closes at the war's end in 1865, with Appomattox and the assassination, and with the freed people's land, safety, and vote standing exactly where the war left them, unanswered on purpose, because answering them is the next unit's work. The era is taught with its peoples in the frame throughout: fugitives and the committees that defended them, an enslaved woman writing the president to ask whether she was free, Black soldiers fighting for equal pay as well as for the Union, the women who petitioned emancipation into a constitutional amendment, and the enslaved people who freed themselves by walking to the Union lines in numbers no proclamation ordered. Period texts are public domain, but the edition still counts: every quotation names its transcription or facsimile edition, and modernized texts are used only where the bank's audited record carries them, with the modernization the bank record itself discloses. One image discipline is stated here because the war made it necessary: Alexander Gardner's Antietam photographs of the Confederate dead are taught through the audited bank record's description and the documented public effect of their exhibition, and are not reproduced in any student document or deck in this unit, the same practice the Civil Rights unit applied to photographs of the dead; where a famous image's provenance cannot be certified, the gap is named on the document's face, as the prior units name theirs.

## Lesson 1: The Compromise of 1850 and the Fugitive Slave Act

**Lesson question:** What did the Compromise of 1850 settle, and what did its Fugitive Slave Act ask the North to do?

**Reading scope:** The question the war with Mexico left on the table: the Wilmot Proviso's fight (prior knowledge, named only) had adjourned without an answer, and in 1849 California, peopled overnight by the gold rush, applied for statehood as a free state, which would break the Senate's balance of fifteen free and fifteen slave and leave the South a permanent minority in one house while its share of the other shrank. The president's road and the Senate's: Zachary Taylor, a Louisiana slaveholder elected as a war hero, proposing to admit California and New Mexico at once and let the territories skip the question; Henry Clay, seventy two and returned to the Senate, laying out his omnibus resolutions in January 1850, a package built to give each section a victory to carry home. The old men speaking for the last time: Calhoun, dying, his speech of March 4, 1850, read aloud by another senator because he could no longer project his voice, demanding the South's equality in the common territories and warning that the cords of Union were snapping one by one; Webster three days later, his Seventh of March speech, pleading for the Union against both abolitionist pressure and southern disunion, and abolition's answer, Whittier's "Ichabod" mourning the fallen glory of the man Massachusetts had sent. The package's failure and its passage in pieces: the omnibus collapsing in July, Clay exhausted and gone to Kentucky, and Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois, the Little Giant, taking the measures up one at a time and passing each with a different majority, Taylor's death in July putting Millard Fillmore, who would sign, in the presidency. The terms themselves, stated flatly: California free; Utah and New Mexico organized as territories with their statehood constitutions to decide slavery when they came; the Texas boundary settled and its debt assumed; the slave trade, not slavery, abolished in the District of Columbia; and the Fugitive Slave Act, the settlement's price and its poison. The Act at working level, because the rest of the unit runs on it: alleged fugitives seized on an owner's affidavit, tried before federal commissioners rather than judges, the accused barred from testifying and denied a jury, the commissioner paid ten dollars when he remanded and five when he freed, every bystander liable to be summoned into a posse, and heavy fines and prison for anyone who fed, hid, or rescued. The lesson closes on the settlement's double reception: both sections' newspapers calling it a final settlement of the slavery question, and northern statehouses beginning at once to pass the personal liberty laws that would make its enforcement a war of jurisdictions, which is where Lesson 2 takes it up.

**Activity concept:** The compromise file. Students work five records, Clay's compromise measures in excerpt (the resolutions and the settlement's terms, in a named transcription of the Congressional Globe or the Statutes at Large, edition named), Webster's Seventh of March speech in excerpt (named edition), Calhoun's speech of March 4, 1850, in excerpt (the Works of John C. ## Lesson 2: The Antislavery Argument at Full Voice

**Lesson question:** What did the antislavery writers of 1851 and 1852 ask their readers to feel, to judge, and to do?

**Reading scope:** The enforcement that made the argument necessary, told first as narrative: the Fugitive Slave Act at work in northern streets, Thomas Sims carried back from Boston under armed guard in April 1851, the rescue of William "Jerry" Henry at Syracuse that October, the Christiana resistance in Pennsylvania that September, where the Maryland slaveholder Edward Gorsuch came with a federal warrant and died at William Parker's door, and Anthony Burns in 1854, arrested in Boston, tried before a commissioner, and marched to the revenue cutter between lines of federal troops while church bells tolled and a courthouse stood draped in black, the lesson stating what the crowds proved, that rendition had become a public spectacle the republic staged against itself. The novel that answered: Harriet Beecher Stowe, who had lived eighteen years in Cincinnati across the river from Kentucky, writing Uncle Tom's Cabin from the slave narratives and from Josiah Henson's account in particular, serialized in 1851 and published as a book in March 1852, selling in its first year at numbers no American book had reached (the usual estimates stated as estimates), its argument built from the slave narrative's evidence and aimed at the Christian mother's conscience, slavery shown corrupting the enslaver's household as surely as it destroyed the enslaved one, families parted at the auction block as the book's recurring wound. The South's answer and the book's reach: banned across the South, answered by a shelf of anti-Tom novels, translated everywhere, and the anecdote of Lincoln greeting Stowe as the little woman who made the great war stated as anecdote, printed decades later and unverifiable, the lesson modeling the difference between a story everyone repeats and a fact anyone can check. The oration: Frederick Douglass at Rochester on July 5, 1852, invited to celebrate the Fourth and answering that the nation's birthday was not his, his speech read here in the bank's audited text, the founding's promises audited against the slave's present, the speech's scorching of the churches and its closing hope both carried. The witness at Akron: Sojourner Truth, born enslaved in New York, freed by that state's gradual emancipation, speaking at the women's rights convention at Akron in May 1851, and the lesson's deliberate source problem, two surviving texts of one speech, Marius Robinson's report printed in the Anti-Slavery Bugle that June and Frances Dana Gage's version published twelve years later, which gave the speech its famous refrain and its dialect, the two read side by side so students learn to ask of any famous quotation who wrote it down and when. The enslaved woman's own book, arriving with the war: Harriet Jacobs, seven years hidden in a crawl space above her grandmother's storeroom within earshot of her children, escaped to the North and writing Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl under a changed name in 1861, her account of what slavery did to women stated as the argument the movement's men could not make for her. The reading closes on the argument's instruments assembled by 1852: the novel, the oration, the narrative, and the rescue, each asking something different of its reader, feeling, judgment, witness, or action, and the political parties still promising that the question could be managed without them.

**Activity concept:** The antislavery file. Students work five records, Uncle Tom's Cabin in excerpt (the 1852 Jewett first edition text, by way of the Project Gutenberg transcription, edition named), Douglass's "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?" in excerpt (audited bank record), Sojourner Truth's Akron address in Robinson's 1851 transcription (audited bank record), the same address in Gage's 1863 version (audited bank record, carried in the Jackson and reform era file), and Harriet Jacobs's Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl in excerpt (audited bank record), and for each one state what it asks of its reader, then judge two things: which of the argument's forms, the novel, the oration, or the first person narrative, the documents show reaching furthest by 1861, and which of the two Truth texts a historian may quote with confidence, defending both judgments from the documents and their stated provenance.

## Lesson 3: The Kansas-Nebraska Act and Bleeding Kansas

**Lesson question:** What did the Kansas-Nebraska Act repeal, and what did popular sovereignty look like when settlers tried it?

**Reading scope:** The railroad behind the repeal: Stephen A. Douglas, chairman of the Senate's territories committee, needing Kansas and Nebraska organized so a transcontinental railroad could run west from Chicago, and the price the South named, the Missouri Compromise's line of 1820, thirty four years standing, repealed in so many words so the territories could decide slavery for themselves under Douglas's doctrine of popular sovereignty, the doctrine carried in this lesson as the act's own stated theory, that the people of a territory were perfectly capable of regulating their own domestic institutions. The act and the storm: passage in May 1854, the northern answer in mass meetings, sermons, and the Appeal of the Independent Democrats charging a plot to extend slavery, and the wreckage of the party system running through the rest of the unit, the Whig Party, already strained by the Compromise, dead within two years, the Democrats split by section, and an anti-Nebraska coalition gathering everyone the repeal had angered, which Lesson 4 names a party. Kansas as the doctrine's laboratory: the territory opened, New England emigrant aid societies sending free state settlers and Missourians crossing the border in organized thousands, the territorial election of March 1855 carried by Missouri voters who rode in, voted, and rode home, the border ruffians' name earned in that season, a proslavery legislature seated at Lecompton expelling its free state members, and the free state answer, a rival government at Topeka with its own constitution and governor, two governments claiming one territory while Washington recognized the fraudulent one. The violence in sequence: the sack of Lawrence in May 1856, a proslavery posse destroying the free state town's presses and hotel; the caning of Senator Charles Sumner on the Senate floor two days later by Congressman Preston Brooks of South Carolina, beaten nearly to death for a speech against Kansas and slavery, his empty seat kept by Massachusetts as an argument and Brooks resigned and reelected as the South's hero; and three nights after the sack, at Pottawatomie Creek, John Brown, an abolitionist of the Jackson unit's generation, taking five proslavery men from their cabins with four of his sons and two others and killing them with broadswords, the massacre stated with the same plainness as the sack and the caning, the lesson refusing to grade the violence by the side that dealt it. The territory's war and its settlement inside the lesson: guerrilla fighting through 1856 with the dead estimated near two hundred, a new governor restoring a armed truce, and the Lecompton Constitution of 1857, drafted by the proslavery convention and offered to voters in a referendum designed so that even voting it down kept slavery protected, President Buchanan endorsing it, Douglas breaking with his own president because the fraud betrayed popular sovereignty itself, Congress refusing, and Kansas entering the Union at last as a free state in January 1861, eleven days after South Carolina's secession had been answered by six more. The reading closes on the doctrine's balance sheet: popular sovereignty had promised to take slavery out of Congress and let neighbors settle it, and in Kansas it had produced two governments, a sacked town, a beaten senator, a massacre, and a small war, with the question no nearer settled than in 1820.

## Lesson 4: The Republican Party and Dred Scott

**Lesson question:** What did the Republican Party promise, and what did the Supreme Court try to settle in Dred Scott?

**Reading scope:** A party built from a repeal: the anti-Nebraska meetings of 1854 naming themselves Republican, in conscious echo of Jefferson's party, and gathering ex-Whigs, Free Soilers, anti-Nebraska Democrats, and temperance and nativist voters into a coalition whose single common plank could be stated in one line, no extension of slavery into the federal territories, a plank that left slavery untouched where state law protected it and promised to put it, in the phrase Lincoln would make famous, in the course of ultimate extinction. The free labor creed the plank rested on: the North's self image argued as economics, that free labor honored work, let the laborer rise, and made the territories the poor man's ladder, against a slave power said to degrade labor and monopolize the common lands, the creed stated in the era's own terms, including its exclusions, a party of free soil that often meant soil free of Black people as well as of slavery, Oregon's and Indiana's exclusion laws named as the creed's shadow. The first campaign: 1856, John C. Frémont, the Pathfinder, carrying eleven of the sixteen free states in the party's first national run while Buchanan won on Pennsylvania and the South, the returns teaching both sections the same arithmetic, that a purely northern party could now come within one state of the presidency and would not stay short forever. The case that tried to end the argument: Dred Scott, enslaved in Missouri, carried by his owner's army postings to Illinois and to Fort Snelling in what is now Minnesota, free soil both, suing for his family's freedom on the ground that residence there had made them free, the case eleven years in the courts while owners died and parties maneuvered. The decision of March 6, 1857, two days after Buchanan's inauguration, Chief Justice Roger B. Taney for a seven justice majority: that Black people, enslaved or free, were not and could not be citizens of the United States, having been regarded, in the opinion's most quoted sentence, as beings of an inferior order with no rights the white man was bound to respect; that Scott's residence on free soil had not freed him; and that the Missouri Compromise had been unconstitutional from the start, Congress having no power to bar slavery from the territories, so that the Republican platform itself, the promise to do by law what the Court now forbade, stood voided by the judiciary. The dissents and the answers: McLean and Curtis dissenting, Curtis's opinion dismantling the citizenship holding with the founding era's own record of free Black voters; Douglass answering in his letter to his former master Hugh Auld's world and in speeches that refused despair at the decision, the letter read in this lesson's activity; and Lincoln at Springfield in June 1858, accepting his Senate nomination with the House Divided speech, the bank's audited text, arguing that the decision, the repeal, and the administration's policy were parts of one tendency toward making slavery national and perpetual. The reading closes on the settlement that settled nothing: the Court had attempted to remove the slavery question from politics by constitutional decree, and within three years the decree's author would watch the government he served dissolve, while the party whose platform he had voided elected a president on it.

**Activity concept:** The Dred Scott file. **Vocabulary:** Republican Party, free labor, John C. Frémont, Dred Scott v. Sandford, Roger B. Taney, Abraham Lincoln, House Divided speech

## Lesson 5: The Debates, John Brown, the Election of 1860, and Secession

**Lesson question:** How did the argument of 1858 become the break of 1860 and 1861?

**Reading scope:** Seven meetings on the Illinois prairie: Lincoln challenging Douglas for the Senate in 1858, the debates at Ottawa, Freeport, Jonesboro, Charleston, Galesburg, Quincy, and Alton, crowds in the tens of thousands standing for hours in the open air, and the single question Lincoln pressed at Freeport until it split the Democracy, if the Court had held that neither Congress nor a territory could bar slavery, could a territory still keep it out, Douglas answering that it could, by refusing the local police laws slavery needed to live, the Freeport Doctrine, an answer that saved his Senate seat and cost him the South, whose leaders now demanded what his doctrine denied, a federal slave code for the territories. The debates' racial ground stated without softening: Lincoln at Charleston disavowing any purpose to bring about social and political equality of the races and assigning the superior position to the white race, while holding at Peoria and throughout that slavery was a moral, social, and political wrong whose spread must be stopped, the two positions carried together because both are in the record his century made. The raid: October 16, 1859, John Brown and twenty one men, five of them Black, seizing the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry to arm an enslaved rising that did not come, the town's first casualty a free Black railroad porter, Heyward Shepherd, shot by the raiders; the marines under Robert E. Lee storming the engine house; Brown tried for treason against Virginia and hanged on December 2, his courtroom speech and his last written note, that the crimes of this guilty land would never be purged away but with blood, making him in northern church bells a martyr and in southern minds the proof that abolition meant invasion, the militia companies forming across the South that winter standing in the reading as secession's first regiments. The election that four parties contested: the Democrats splitting at Charleston and Baltimore over the slave code demand, Douglas nominated by the northern wing and Breckinridge by the southern, Bell running on the Union alone, and Lincoln, nominated at Chicago on the platform of no extension, elected with 39.8 percent of the popular vote, a majority of the electoral college, and not one electoral vote, and in ten states not even a place on the ballot, from the South. Secession answering in its own words: South Carolina out on December 20, 1860, its Declaration of the Immediate Causes read here in the bank's audited text, six more states by February 1, the Crittenden Compromise's last terms failing in the Senate, the Confederate States organized at Montgomery with Jefferson Davis president, and Vice President Alexander Stephens at Savannah in March 1861 stating the new government's cornerstone in the bank's second audited text, that its foundations rested on the great truth of racial inequality and slavery as the Black man's natural condition, the reading carrying both statements whole because the unit's central question, what the war was about, must be answered first from what the seceding states said when they left. The last scene: Lincoln's First Inaugural of March 4, 1861, pledged against interfering with slavery in the states, holding the Union perpetual and federal property to be held, and closing on the mystic chords of memory, with Fort Sumter's provisions running out in Charleston harbor and two governments claiming one flag, the lesson ending where the next begins, on the fort.

**Vocabulary:** Lincoln-Douglas debates, Freeport Doctrine, Harpers Ferry, secession, Crittenden Compromise, Confederate States of America, Jefferson Davis

## Lesson 6: The War Begins: Sumter to Antietam

**Lesson question:** What did each side think would win the war, and what did the first seventeen months teach them?

**Reading scope:** The fort and the answer: April 12, 1861, the Confederate batteries opening on Fort Sumter, the garrison's thirty four hours and surrender on April 14, Lincoln's call for seventy five thousand volunteers the next day, and the Union's second secession, Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina leaving after the call, while the border states of Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri were held by occupation, political maneuver, and in Kentucky a neutrality both sides courted until it broke. The two theories of the war, stated with the arithmetic the war ran on: the Union's 22 million people against the Confederacy's 9 million, more than a third of them enslaved, the North's factories, railroads, and navy against the South's cotton and its coastline, and the strategies each chose, General Winfield Scott's Anaconda Plan, a naval blockade and a thrust down the Mississippi to strangle the Confederacy's trade and split it, against the Confederacy's defensive, hoping to outlast northern will and win European recognition on the strength of King Cotton, whose leverage collapsed as Britain turned to Indian and Egyptian cotton. The first field test: July 21, 1861, First Bull Run, picnic crowds riding out to watch and a Confederate victory that ended the hope of a ninety day war, followed by McClellan's building of the Army of the Potomac into the country's largest trained force and its commander's refusal to spend it. The year in the east and the west: McClellan's Peninsula campaign stalling before Richmond in the Seven Days battles of June and July 1862, Robert E. Lee in command and the war's character hardening, Second Bull Run in August, and meanwhile in the west Grant taking Forts Henry and Donelson, New Orleans falling to Farragut in April, and Shiloh, two days of slaughter in April 1862 that taught both sides the war's price would be counted in tens of thousands, with the Monitor and the Virginia fighting to a draw at Hampton Roads in March and ending the wooden navy's century. The liberties the war suspended, stated plainly: Lincoln suspending habeas corpus along the lines of military operations, the bank's audited proclamation of September 1862 carried in the lesson, Chief Justice Taney's Merryman ruling defied in 1861 and Maryland legislators arrested to hold the state, the lesson naming the tension and carrying it rather than resolving it. The day the blood settled it: September 17, 1862, Antietam, Lee's lost orders in McClellan's hands, the single bloodiest day in American history, some 22,700 casualties by the usual reckoning, a tactical draw and a strategic Union victory that ended the invasion and gave Lincoln the field he had waited for, with Gardner's camera reaching the battlefield days later and the photographs of the dead exhibited in New York that October, the first time Americans saw their war's work in a gallery, the record discussed in this lesson through the bank's described text rather than reproduced. The closing voice: Sullivan Ballou of Rhode Island, writing his wife Sarah on July 14, 1861, that he had no misgivings about the cause, and killed at Bull Run a week later, his letter carried in the lesson as the war's private register against its grand strategies. The reading ends where the war's purpose pivots, on September 22, 1862, with the Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation, which Lesson 7 takes up.

## Lesson 7: Emancipation and the War Transformed, 1863

**Lesson question:** How did emancipation change what the war was for, and for whom?

**Reading scope:** The road the lesson walks before the proclamation: the enslaved freeing themselves first, thousands walking to Fort Monroe where General Butler in May 1861 refused to return them, calling them contraband of war, the doctrine that let the Union army absorb their labor while the government insisted the war was only for the Union; the First Confiscation Act and the District of Columbia's compensated emancipation in April 1862, which freed the capital's slaves and paid loyal masters; Lincoln's measured waiting, the border states to be kept and a northern public to be carried, until Antietam let him speak. The measure itself, read in the bank's audited text with its limits stated on the page: the Emancipation Proclamation of January 1, 1863, declared free the enslaved in the areas in rebellion and said nothing of the loyal slave states, Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri, or of the occupied parishes of Louisiana and Tennessee, freeing where the Union could not yet enforce and standing aside where it could, the critics' charge stated with the supporters' answer, that it struck at the rebellion's labor system, opened the army's ranks, and ended any European sympathy for the South. Freedom as the enslaved executed it: the proclamation's words carried by the news itself, hundreds of thousands coming to the lines, the labor of the Confederacy withdrawing by the month, the estimates stated as estimates, and the war's accounting now showing emancipation as military fact however narrowly the measure was drawn. The soldiers: the Militia Act of 1862 authorizing Black enlistment, the United States Colored Troops, some 180,000 Black soldiers serving by war's end, roughly a tenth of the Union army, paid less than white soldiers until the equal pay fight of 1863 and 1864 and murdered rather than captured at Fort Pillow in April 1864; Douglass's "Men of Color, To Arms!" broadside of March 1863 in the bank's audited text; and the 54th Massachusetts at Fort Wagner in July 1863, the assault's cost and its demonstration. The year's other turning, told with the proclamation's: Chancellorsville in May and Jackson's death, Lee's second invasion, Gettysburg's three days in July, Vicksburg falling to Grant on July 4, and Lincoln's Address of November 19, 1863, in the bank's full text, the war rededicated to the proposition that all men are created equal and to the unfinished work the dead had left. The strain shown honestly: the New York draft riots of July 1863, four days of the city's poor protesting the draft and lynching Black New Yorkers, the war's other face stated plainly, with the same federal troops that fought at Gettysburg restoring order at cannon point. The reading closes on Robinson's Pictures of Slavery and Anti-Slavery of 1863, the bank's audited record presenting the book as what it is, a Philadelphia writer's defense of slavery as a divine institution, carried here as the institution arguing for itself in print in the middle of the war fought over it, and on the war's new arithmetic, that the Union now fought with the enslaved as its allies and the Confederacy fought with its labor force leaving the field, the question of whether freedom was the war's purpose or its instrument left for the documents. (Coordinator's correction, recorded in the manifest: this skeleton's first draft described the Robinson record as abolition's photographic evidence. The bank record presents the opposite, a pro-slavery defense, and the lesson follows the bank.)

## Lesson 8: Total War and Appomattox

**Lesson question:** How did the war end, and what did its ending settle?

**Reading scope:** The general and his arithmetic: Ulysses S. Grant in command of all Union armies from March 1864, his doctrine that the Confederacy's armies and not its cities were the objective, and the Overland campaign's ledger, the Wilderness, Spotsylvania, and Cold Harbor buying the ground with casualties the country had never paid before, the estimates stated as estimates, and Petersburg besieged from June 1864, the longest trench war in American history to that point. The sea of fire: Sherman's taking of Atlanta in September 1864, the cut loose from supply, the March to the Sea through Georgia's farms and railroads, the total war doctrine stated in his own orders and letters, the destruction measured as military logic and as terror, both carried, then the turn north through the Carolinas and Columbia burning, the lesson naming what the destruction cost the women and freed people in the armies' path rather than counting only the military gain. The election that decided the war's politics: 1864, the Democrats' platform calling the war a failure and demanding an armistice, McClellan against Lincoln, Atlanta's fall arriving in September in time to save the president's reelection, the soldiers' vote counted, and the meaning of the result stated, that the North had voted to finish the war it had voted to wage. The Union's other wartime work, in the lesson's middle as in the war: Congress, with the South gone, passing the Homestead Act and the Morrill Land-Grant Act of 1862 in the bank's audited texts, the West's farms and the states' colleges chartered while the armies fought, and the Women's Loyal National League gathering some 400,000 signatures for emancipation, the bank's audited address carried in the activity, the women's petition campaign driving the amendment's passage in the House on January 31, 1865, named here as a wartime event and no further, because its debates and settlement belong to the next unit. The end: Richmond evacuated and burning April 2 and 3, 1865, Lee's retreat cut at Appomattox Court House, the surrender on April 9, the terms generous by Lincoln's standing instruction, paroles, officers' sidearms kept, horses taken home for the spring planting, and Grant's order against rejoicing over a fallen foe. The verdict speech: the Second Inaugural of March 4, 1865, in the bank's full text, Lincoln naming slavery as the war's cause, American slavery as the offense, and the war as its woe, and closing that the country must bind up its wounds, the lesson carrying the address as the war's own verdict on the unit's essential question. The assassination and the measure: April 14, Booth at Ford's Theatre, Lincoln dead April 15, the war's last casualty, and Annie Davis of Maryland writing Lincoln in August 1864 to ask whether she was free, the bank's audited letter carried in the activity, because her letter measures the war's settlement at its edge, freedom declared by a measure that did not name her state, waiting on a constitution her state had only just begun to write. The reading closes on the two settlements the war made, the Union indivisible and slavery destroyed as a system, and on what it left, the freed people's land, safety, and vote, standing exactly where April 1865 left them, which is where the Reconstruction unit begins.

**Activity concept:** The ending file. Students work five records, the Second Inaugural Address in full (audited bank record), Annie Davis's letter to Abraham Lincoln of August 25, 1864 (audited bank record), the Women's Loyal National League address of May 1864 (audited bank record), the Homestead Act's first section (audited bank record), and Sherman's letter to the mayor of Atlanta of September 12, 1864 (named transcription, edition named, or a labeled teaching summary if no verbatim text can be verified), and for each one state what the war had settled and what it had left standing, then judge whether the Second Inaugural is right that the war was slavery's punishment or whether it was a political failure's last resort, and defend the judgment from the documents.

**Vocabulary:** Ulysses S. Grant, total war, William Tecumseh Sherman, March to the Sea, Thirteenth Amendment, Appomattox Court House, Second Inaugural Address
