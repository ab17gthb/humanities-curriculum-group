Writing Task: Who Gained, Who Lost?

The prompt. The war asked Americans at home for money, work, unity, and silence, and it did not ask all Americans in the same way. Write a paragraph that answers the lesson question: How did the war change American life at home, and who gained and who lost by the change? Use at least three of the evidence cards below. Your paragraph must name at least one specific group that gained and one specific group that lost, and it must support each with a fact from the cards.

The cards are summaries drawn from the lesson reading and activity. Write the history in your own sentences and quote facts, not the cards.

Card 1: The Managed Economy

The War Industries Board set priorities for raw materials and fixed prices on key goods. Liberty Bond drives raised roughly 17 billion dollars, about two thirds of the cost of the war, from buyers who were repaid with interest. Bond buying was voluntary in law and a public test of loyalty in practice.

Card 2: Persuasion and Prosecution

The Committee on Public Information fielded posters, pamphlets, films, and about 75,000 Four Minute Men to build support for the war. In the same years, prosecutors brought more than two thousand cases under the Espionage Act of 1917 and the Sedition Act of 1918. Charles Schenck was imprisoned for anti-draft leaflets, and Eugene V. Debs received ten years for a speech.

Card 3: Women and the Vote

About a million women entered war industries and related work for the first time. Suffrage leaders argued that this service proved women deserved the vote in a nation fighting for democracy. President Wilson endorsed the amendment in 1918 as a war measure, and the Nineteenth Amendment was ratified in 1920. Most women's war jobs ended when the men came home.

Card 4: The Great Migration

Emmett J. Scott, the federal investigator, reported that more than 400,000 Black southerners moved north in the three years after the war began in Europe. Migrants gained northern wages, schools, and the right to vote. Scott named the most potent cause as the treatment Black people received in the South, including peonage, disenfranchisement, and violence. Migrants also met crowded housing and, in 1919, race riots.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers both halves of the lesson question: what changed at home, and who gained and who lost by the change. A paragraph that lists changes without distributing gains and losses has answered half the prompt.
  • At least three cards used accurately. Card 1 must carry its figures: roughly 17 billion dollars and about two thirds of the cost of the war. Card 2 must keep persuasion and prosecution distinct. Card 4 must carry Scott's count of more than 400,000 migrants in three years.
  • At least one specific gaining group and one specific losing group, each with its fact: women and the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920; Black migrants and northern wages and voting rights; dissenters such as Schenck and Debs and prison; German Americans and suppressed institutions, where the student brings in the reading.
  • A real counterpoint answered, not dismissed. A gains paragraph must answer Card 2's prosecutions. A losses paragraph must answer Card 3's amendment or Card 4's wages and votes, and explain why the gain does not cancel the loss, or the reverse.

Common weak answers. Treating the home front as only sacrifice, with no gains recorded; treating bond buying as pure volunteering, against Card 1's loyalty test; crediting the war alone for the vote without the suffrage argument Card 3 records; describing the migration as caused only by northern wages, against Scott's finding in Card 4.

Historical note for the debrief. The Sedition Act was repealed in 1921 and Debs's sentence was commuted that year, while the Nineteenth Amendment and the Great Migration outlasted the war by generations. Students who weigh what lasted against what was reversed have found the strongest way to close the gains and losses account.
