The Home Front

Imperialism and World War I · Lesson 7 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Imperialism and World War I, Lesson 7 of 8. It follows Lesson 6, “Americans in the War,” which followed the army to France and the armistice of November 11, 1918. This lesson turns home: what the war demanded of the country the soldiers left, and what it changed there. Teach it seventh in the unit, before Lesson 8 takes up Wilson’s peace, the treaty fight, and the turn inward — the divisions this lesson records are the ground that fight stands on.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “The Home Front” and the guided notes, which follow the deck’s order exactly — managing production and money, selling the war, the law against dissent, fear at home, women, work, and the vote, and the Great Migration, closing with the strikes of 1919. The activity is “Liberty and Security: The Home Front on the Record” (five stations, worked under the question “Where Did the Government Cross the Line?”). The notebook page is “Who Gained, Who Lost,” the home front chart: what was asked, what power, what resulted. The quick check is “I Want You,” which sets Flagg’s poster against Schenck’s leaflets. The writing task is “Who Gained, Who Lost?” — one paragraph built from five evidence cards.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Lesson 6’s ending — the armistice and the scale of the army the country raised and shipped. Everything else is taught in order: the War Industries Board and Liberty Bonds, the Committee on Public Information and the Four Minute Men, the Espionage and Sedition Acts, the cases of Schenck and Debs, the campaign against German Americans and the lynching of Robert Prager, women’s war work and the Nineteenth Amendment, and the Great Migration in Emmett J. Scott’s count.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (continuity and change — opportunity widened and rights narrowed in the same war years), D2.His.16 (assess the purpose and intended audience of historical sources — a recruiting poster, a bond poster, and an anti-draft leaflet each read for what it was meant to do), D2.His.14 (analyze multiple and complex causes and effects — persuasion, law, and labor shortage weighed together in explaining who gained and who lost); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading’s order from production to migration), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task: one paragraph on who gained and who lost, built from five evidence cards).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to explain how the War Industries Board directed production and how Liberty Bonds paid for about two thirds of the war, how the Committee on Public Information persuaded rather than only educated, what the Espionage and Sedition Acts punished and what test Schenck v. United States stated, how suspicion fell on German Americans, how women’s war work became the argument that won the Nineteenth Amendment, and why hundreds of thousands of Black southerners moved north — ending able to name, for three groups, one gain and one loss from the war at home.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening, question, and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; managing production and money (slides 4–7) 7 minutes; selling the war (slides 8–10) 6 minutes; the law against dissent (slides 11–14) 9 minutes; fear at home (slides 15–16) 4 minutes; women, work, and the vote (slides 17–19) 6 minutes; the Great Migration (slides 20–22) 6 minutes; 1919, vocabulary, and the turn to work (slides 23–26) 7 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. The exit ticket has three parts — explain Liberty Bonds, state the clear and present danger test, and name one group that gained and one that lost, with evidence for each.

Key vocabulary

Liberty Bonds · War Industries Board · Committee on Public Information · Espionage Act · Sedition Act · Great Migration · Schenck v. United States

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame: Flagg and the home front

On the slide: the poster at right (Flagg’s Uncle Sam); the unit and lesson line “Imperialism and World War I · Lesson 7”; the title “The Home Front”; the standfirst — “A war fought overseas, and a country remade at home.”

  • Picture: James Montgomery Flagg’s Uncle Sam, from “I Want You for U.S. Army,” the most recognizable American recruiting image ever printed. Flagg drew Uncle Sam in 1916, the drawing first appeared on the cover of Leslie’s Weekly that July, before the United States entered the war, and the Army reused the drawing as a recruiting poster in 1917.
  • [Give students the dates plainly,] because the quick check tests them: the image predates the American war it came to stand for, and no student should treat 1917 as the year it was drawn.
  • The armies fought in Europe, but the war was also fought in American factories, theaters, courtrooms, and neighborhoods.
  • This lesson follows the home front: how the government raised money and goods, how it sold the war, how it punished dissent, and which Americans gained ground while others lost it.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the question — “How did the war change American life at home, and who gained and who lost by the change?”; below it, the unit question: “Why did the United States seek power overseas, and how did the First World War change the country that fought it?”

  • [Read the lesson question aloud] and keep both halves in view all period.
  • How did the war change American life at home? The federal government directed industry, borrowed billions from its own citizens, ran a national persuasion campaign, and prosecuted speech.
  • Who gained and who lost by the change? Women gained war jobs and then the vote. Black southerners gained a path north to wages and ballots.
  • German Americans lost language classes, music, and in one case a life. Dissenters lost their liberty for leaflets and speeches.
  • [Tell students the exit ticket] asks for one gaining group and one losing group with evidence for each, so they should keep a running tally as the sections move.
  • Under the unit question, this lesson answers how the war changed the country that fought it: the change ran in two directions at once, toward wider opportunity and toward narrower rights.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title “Money, persuasion, law, and movement”; six rows — A, Managing Production and Money (a civilian economy turned to war in months); B, Selling the War (a federal campaign to make a divided country act as one); C, The Law Against Dissent (two statutes, two thousand cases, one Supreme Court test); D, Fear at Home (suspicion aimed at German Americans and their neighbors); E, Women, Work, and the Vote (war work becomes an argument for the ballot); F, The Great Migration (hundreds of thousands move north for jobs and rights).

  • This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section.
  • Section A covers the managed economy: the War Industries Board directing production and Liberty Bonds paying for roughly two thirds of the war. Section B covers the Committee on Public Information and its persuasion campaign.
  • Section C covers the Espionage and Sedition Acts, the prosecutions under them, and the Schenck decision. Section D steps outside the courts to the fear aimed at German Americans.
  • Sections E and F cover the two great movements of people: women into war work and toward the vote, and Black southerners north in the Great Migration.
  • The reading closes with the strikes of 1919, and the deck closes there too.
  • [Point students to the order:] the activity stations, the notebook, and the exit ticket all follow this same order, so the map on this slide is also their study map for the unit test.

Slide 4 · Section A: Managing Production and Money (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (Christy’s bond poster); the section label “Section A”; the title “Managing Production and Money”; the lines — “A civilian economy, turned to war in months.”

  • Picture: Howard Chandler Christy’s “Fight or Buy Bonds,” printed for the Third Liberty Loan in 1917 and held by the Library of Congress. Read its structure before its art: a soldier and a flag on one side, and an either-or choice with no third position. Fight, or lend.
  • Students will meet this same poster at Station 4 of the activity, paired with a sentence from the Treasury, and will be asked to separate a financial appeal from a loyalty test.
  • [Set the section frame now.] In April 1917 the American army was small and American factories served peacetime customers.
  • Within months the federal government was setting production priorities, fixing prices, running the railroads, pressing families to save food, and borrowing billions from the public.
  • Section A asks how a government does that, and what it asks of ordinary people in the process.

Slide 5 · Directed: the War Industries Board

On the slide: five bullets — The board was created in 1917, and it was led from 1918 by Bernard Baruch. The board set priorities for raw materials. The board told factories what to produce. The board fixed prices on key goods. The railroads came under federal control, and families saved food for the armies.

  • [Teach the board as a system of direction, not a suggestion box,] because guided notes blanks one and two test exactly this.
  • The War Industries Board was created in 1917 and led from 1918 by the financier Bernard Baruch. It set priorities for raw materials, told factories what to produce, and fixed prices on key goods.
  • Around it stood companion measures: the railroads came under federal control, and a Food Administration pressed families to save food for the armies.
  • Production rose sharply, and students should name the reason accurately. A peacetime market, left alone, makes goods for whoever pays most. A war economy makes goods in the order the army needs them, and that ordering is a government act.
  • Ask: What could Baruch do to a factory owner who refused a priority order? (Let students reason about the power behind the paperwork before you confirm how much of it rested on pressure and patriotism.)

Slide 6 · Borrowed from the public: Liberty Bonds

On the slide: five bullets — Liberty Bonds were government bonds sold to the public, and they were repaid with interest. The Treasury ran five great loan drives, with rallies and quotas in nearly every town. Americans bought about 17 billion dollars in bonds. Bond sales covered about two thirds of the cost of the war. Buying a bond became a public test of loyalty.

  • [Define the instrument first, in the guided notes wording:] “Liberty Bonds: government bonds sold to the public to raise money for the war, repaid with interest.”
  • A buyer was a lender, not a donor, and the Treasury ran five great loan drives, with rallies and sales quotas reaching nearly every town.
  • Americans bought roughly 17 billion dollars in bonds, about two thirds of the cost of the war.
  • [Then teach the second half of the slide,] because the reading insists on it. Buying a bond became a public test of loyalty as well as a loan. Quotas were posted, names were known, and a neighbor who bought nothing had explaining to do.
  • That pressure is the bridge to the Christy poster from the divider: fight or buy leaves no respectable third choice.
  • Reading question one asks students to explain both the board and the bonds together, so keep the money and the production in one frame.

Slide 7 · The bond count: 17 billion dollars

On the slide: the big figure “$17 BILLION”; the label — “in Liberty Bonds bought by the American public”; the note — “Bond sales covered about two thirds of the cost of the war. The rest came from taxes and other borrowing.”

  • $17 billion — in Liberty Bonds bought by the American public
  • Bond sales covered about two thirds of the cost of the war
  • [Let the number sit before you explain it.] Seventeen billion dollars, in the money of 1917 and 1918, lent to the government by its own citizens in five loan drives.
  • Bond sales covered about two thirds of the cost of the war, a share students should copy with its dates and its source, the Treasury drives themselves.
  • Put the figure next to the board from the last section and the pairing becomes the section in one line: the board decided what the economy would make, and the bonds decided who would pay for it, which turned out to be the public itself, repaid later with interest.
  • [Note the voluntariness carefully for the activity verdict.] No law required a citizen to buy a bond. The pressure was social and organized, applied by rallies, quotas, and posters, and students will have to judge at Station 4 whether organized social pressure counts as the government asking or the government compelling.

Slide 8 · Section B: Selling the War (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right (Hopps’s ape poster); the section label “Section B”; the title “Selling the War”; the lines — “Posters, pamphlets, films, and 75,000 speakers.”

  • Picture: Harry R. Hopps’s “Destroy This Mad Brute,” printed in 1917: a drooling ape in a spiked helmet, a club labeled with German culture in his hand, a woman in his arm, and American ruins behind him.
  • [Carry the disclosure into the room:] this is an Army recruiting poster, not a Committee on Public Information product, and the lesson source line says so. The distinction is the section in miniature.
  • Many agencies sold this war, in many voices, and students who credit every poster to the committee have not read closely. What the committee did produce — posters, pamphlets, films, and speakers — fills the next two slides.
  • Teach this image as the emotional register of the campaign: an enemy drawn as an animal, a threat drawn as personal, and enlistment drawn as the only answer.
  • Station 1 of the activity asks students to test the committee claim of education against imagery in this register.

Slide 9 · The Committee on Public Information

On the slide: five bullets — President Wilson created the committee in April 1917, and the journalist George Creel led it. The committee printed millions of pamphlets, and it supplied news releases to newspapers. The committee made films, and its posters covered walls across the country. The committee called its work education. Its purpose was persuasion.

  • [Give the committee its full name and its definition in the guided notes wording:] “Committee on Public Information: the federal agency that promoted support for the war through posters, pamphlets, films, and speakers.”
  • President Wilson created it in April 1917 under the journalist George Creel.
  • Its output was industrial in scale: millions of pamphlets, a steady supply of news releases to newspapers, films, and posters covering walls across the country.
  • [Draw the reading distinction slowly,] because reading question two grades it. The committee presented its work as education, and much of what it published was factual. Its purpose, however, was persuasion: to make a divided country act and think as one.
  • A fact can be accurate and still be selected, timed, and framed to move a reader in one direction.
  • Students who can hold both halves — factual material and persuasive purpose — are ready for the stations. Students who pick one half only will misjudge either the poster or the prosecutions.

Slide 10 · Four minutes in the theater

On the slide: four bullets — About 75,000 volunteers served as speakers. They gave short patriotic talks while the film reels changed. One message was repeated in nearly every town. The message was to enlist, buy bonds, save food, and suspect spies.

  • The Four Minute Men are the committee at its most inventive, and students remember them when they forget the pamphlets.
  • About 75,000 volunteers gave short patriotic talks in movie theaters in the minutes while the reels were changed, a captive audience in the dark with nowhere else to look.
  • The name joined the Revolutionary minuteman to the length of the reel change. Their topics came from Washington bulletins: enlist, buy bonds, save food, report spies.
  • [Consider the reach with the class.] A poster can be walked past. A newspaper can be left unopened. A speaker in a theater, a respected local man in most cases, speaks to a whole town at leisure, in person, in a voice the audience knows.
  • That is persuasion with a local face, and it helps explain how national campaigns become neighborhood pressure, the same pressure that sold the bonds in Section A and watched the neighbors in Section D.
  • Keep the number, about 75,000, in the notes for the At a Glance table.

Slide 11 · Section C: The Law Against Dissent (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section C”; the title “The Law Against Dissent”; the line — “Two statutes, more than two thousand prosecutions, and a Supreme Court test for wartime speech.”

  • [Move the class from persuasion to compulsion,] and mark the change of instrument. In Section B the government asked, argued, and advertised. In this section it prosecuted.
  • Congress gave the government legal weapons against opposition in two steps: the Espionage Act of 1917 and the Sedition Act of 1918, broader and aimed directly at speech.
  • Prosecutors brought more than two thousand cases under the two laws.
  • Two of those cases carry this section. Charles Schenck mailed leaflets against the draft and carried his conviction to the Supreme Court, which answered with the clear and present danger test. Eugene Debs gave a speech in Ohio and ran for president from a prison cell.
  • [Tell students the activity’s design:] the activity places the statutes at Station 2 and Schenck at Station 3, side by side on purpose — first the law as written, then the law as the Court allowed it to be used.

Slide 12 · Two laws against opposition

On the slide: five bullets — The Espionage Act of 1917 punished spying, and it made obstructing recruiting a crime. The act also made insubordination in the armed forces a crime. The Sedition Act of 1918 punished disloyal or abusive language. It punished speech about the government, the Constitution, the flag, and the armed forces. Prosecutors brought more than two thousand cases under the two laws.

  • [Teach the two acts as a widening circle, using the guided notes definitions word for word.] “Espionage Act: the 1917 federal law that punished spying and acts intended to interfere with the military or obstruct recruiting.”
  • Its center was conduct close to the war machine: spies, sabotage, blocked recruiting, insubordination in the ranks.
  • “Sedition Act: the 1918 federal law that punished disloyal or abusive speech about the government, the Constitution, the flag, and the armed forces.” Its center was words.
  • A citizen could now be prosecuted for language itself, with no spy ring and no blocked recruiting station anywhere in the case.
  • Prosecutors brought more than two thousand cases under the two laws together.
  • Ask: Where would you draw the line between the two statutes? (Hold their answers for the Schenck slide, where the Supreme Court drew its own line and the country lived with it for fifty years.)

Slide 13 · Schenck and the clear and present danger test

On the slide: the poster at right (Flagg’s Uncle Sam); the title “Schenck and the clear and present danger test”; five bullets — Charles Schenck was an officer of the Socialist Party. He mailed leaflets that urged men to resist the draft. He was convicted under the Espionage Act. In 1919, a unanimous Supreme Court upheld the conviction. Justice Holmes held that speech could be punished when it created a clear and present danger.

  • Picture: Flagg’s Uncle Sam beside the text, the recruiting appeal Schenck was convicted of obstructing, drawn in 1916 and reused by the Army in 1917.
  • Charles Schenck, an officer of the Socialist Party, was convicted for mailing leaflets that urged men to resist the draft.
  • In Schenck v. United States in 1919, a unanimous Supreme Court upheld the conviction. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote that speech could be punished when it created a clear and present danger of an evil Congress had the power to prevent.
  • [Teach the test in the guided notes wording:] “Schenck v. United States: the 1919 Supreme Court case that upheld an Espionage Act conviction and stated the clear and present danger test for wartime speech.”
  • [Then press on the facts.] Schenck mailed paper. No recruit is shown to have refused because of his leaflets. Students must decide whether danger on paper met the test, which is exactly the judgment Station 3 demands.

Slide 14 · Debs: a speech, a prison cell

On the slide: four bullets — Eugene V. Debs was the leading American socialist. He gave a speech at Canton, Ohio, in 1918, and he praised draft resisters. He received a ten year sentence for the speech. He ran for president in 1920 from a federal prison cell.

  • Debs is the human scale of the two acts, and the reading gives him one paragraph on purpose.
  • Eugene V. Debs, the leading American socialist and a four-time candidate for president, spoke at Canton, Ohio, in 1918 and praised men who had resisted the draft. For that speech he received a ten year sentence.
  • In 1920 he ran for president from a federal prison cell and drew close to a million votes, ballots cast for a man the government held behind bars for words spoken at a picnic ground.
  • [Set Debs beside Schenck and the section argument completes itself.] Schenck shows the doctrine: the Court announced when speech may be punished. Debs shows the practice: the country’s best known opposition voice spent the election in prison.
  • Guided notes blanks thirteen and fourteen test the ten years and the prison cell. Exit ticket question two tests the Schenck test itself, in the student’s own words, so both cases must stay distinct in the notes.

Slide 15 · Section D: Fear at Home (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section D”; the title “Fear at Home”; the line — “Suspicion aimed at German Americans, enforced by neighbors as much as by law.”

  • [Mark the change of venue,] because this short section steps outside the courts. Sections B and C described the federal government: a committee, two statutes, a Supreme Court. Section D describes the public itself.
  • War feeling ran outside the law, in school boards that dropped German classes, orchestras that stopped playing German music, and grocers who renamed sauerkraut.
  • It also ran through organized watching: loyalty leagues and the American Protective League observed neighbors and reported suspected disloyalty to the Justice Department.
  • The reading closes the section with the case students must not forget: in April 1918 a mob in Illinois lynched Robert Prager, a German born immigrant who had applied for American citizenship.
  • [Frame the question the section leaves open.] The government did not order the mob. It had spent a year teaching the country to see disloyalty everywhere, and students will judge in the activity how far official persuasion answers for private violence.

Slide 16 · Patriotism, and persecution

On the slide: five bullets — Schools dropped German language classes. Orchestras stopped playing German music. Sauerkraut became liberty cabbage. The American Protective League watched neighbors. In April 1918, a mob lynched Robert Prager in Illinois.

  • [Work down the slide in order, from the petty to the lethal,] because the order is the argument.
  • Schools dropped German language classes. Orchestras stopped playing German music. Sauerkraut became liberty cabbage, a renaming students laugh at, until the next lines land.
  • Loyalty leagues and the American Protective League watched neighbors and reported suspected disloyalty to the Justice Department, turning suspicion into a civic duty performed on the people next door.
  • In April 1918 a mob in Illinois lynched Robert Prager, a German born immigrant who had applied for American citizenship. He had sought the status his killers claimed he lacked.
  • [Use the reading sentence as the slide verdict:] the line between patriotism and persecution was crossed often, usually against people with no power to answer back.
  • Guided notes blanks fifteen through seventeen test liberty cabbage, Prager, and the American Protective League by name, so read all three aloud as you close the slide.

Slide 17 · Section E: Women, Work, and the Vote (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section E”; the title “Women, Work, and the Vote”; the line — “War work opens factory gates, and the service becomes an argument for the ballot.”

  • [Turn from loss to gain, and say so.] The two sections before this one recorded what the war took from Americans at home: speech, safety, in one case a life. This section records what the war opened.
  • With millions of men in uniform and immigration from Europe cut off, the labor shortage reached into workplaces that had been closed to women for generations.
  • About a million women entered war industries and related work for the first time, in factories, on railroads, and in offices.
  • [Then the second movement of the section:] suffrage leaders converted that service into a constitutional argument, and the argument won. President Wilson endorsed the amendment in 1918 as a war measure, Congress passed it in 1919, and the states ratified the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920.
  • Students should track the chain — work, argument, amendment — because the writing task card on women and the vote grades exactly that chain and nothing looser.

Slide 18 · A million women into war work

On the slide: four bullets — Millions of men were in uniform, and immigration was cut off. Factories, railroads, and offices hired women. Jobs long closed to women opened. About a million women entered war industries for the first time.

  • [Start with the shortage,] because the reading does and because it keeps the section honest. The gates opened for two reasons at once: millions of men were in uniform, and immigration from Europe, the usual source of new industrial labor, was cut off by the war.
  • Factories, railroads, and offices hired women for jobs that had been closed to them.
  • About a million women entered war industries and related work for the first time.
  • [Students should hear both halves of that fact.] It was real work at real wages in places women had been told they did not belong, and it was also wartime expedient hiring that employers expected to reverse when the men came home, as in large part it did.
  • Guided notes blank eighteen tests the million figure. Hold the reversal for the closing slide, where the strikes and the returning soldiers end the wartime labor truce, and let this slide stand for the opening itself.

Slide 19 · Service becomes an argument: the Nineteenth Amendment

On the slide: four bullets — Suffrage leaders pointed to women’s war work. The nation fought for democracy abroad, and it denied women the vote at home. Wilson endorsed the amendment in 1918, as a war measure. Congress passed it in 1919, and the states ratified it in 1920.

  • [Teach this slide as an argument being won, step by step.]
  • Suffrage leaders pointed to women’s war work and asked how a nation fighting for democracy abroad could deny women the vote at home.
  • The war had handed them the strongest version of their claim: the government itself had asked women to sustain the war economy, and the service was public, counted, and praised.
  • President Wilson endorsed a federal amendment in 1918 as a war measure, his own framing, which students should quote accurately. Congress passed the Nineteenth Amendment in 1919, and the states ratified it in 1920.
  • Guided notes blanks nineteen and twenty test the 1918 endorsement and the 1920 ratification.
  • [Close by returning to the lesson question.] Here is the clearest gain of the home front: a constitutional right, won in part because the war made the old refusal impossible to defend in public. The next section follows the second great gain, and its harder landing in the North.

Slide 20 · Section F: The Great Migration (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section F”; the title “The Great Migration”; the line — “Hundreds of thousands of Black southerners move north for jobs and greater rights.”

  • [Use the guided notes definition to open the section, word for word:] “Great Migration: the movement of hundreds of thousands of Black southerners to northern cities for jobs and greater rights during and after the war years.”
  • The same labor shortage that opened factory gates to women in Section E opened them here. Northern factories, cut off from European immigrant labor, sent labor agents south, and Black newspapers such as the Chicago Defender carried word of jobs and higher wages north along the rail lines.
  • Beginning in 1916 the movement reshaped both regions: southern counties lost the workers their economy rested on, and Chicago, Detroit, and Cleveland gained new Black neighborhoods, churches, newspapers, and voters.
  • [Tell students the section’s evidence base:] the section runs on one investigator’s count and one set of named causes, both tested in the guided notes, and the reading holds the gain and the hardship together to the last line.

Slide 21 · Pushed out, pulled north

On the slide: five bullets — Northern factories sent labor agents south. The Chicago Defender carried word of jobs and wages north. Low wages, the crop lien system, and disenfranchisement pushed people out. The convict lease system, lynching, and violence also pushed people out. Wages, schools, and the right to vote pulled people north.

  • [Teach the migration as a decision made under pressure from both directions,] because guided notes Section F grades one push and one pull from every student.
  • The federal investigator Emmett J. Scott named the forces pushing people out: low wages and cheating in the crop lien system, disenfranchisement, the convict lease system, and violence, including lynching.
  • The pull was concrete: northern war industry jobs at higher wages, schools for children, and the right to vote, which most Black southerners had been stripped of at home. Migrants sought wages, schools, and voting rights, and relief from the system Scott cataloged.
  • [Then the landing, which the reading refuses to soften.] Migrants found real opportunity and also crowded housing, high rents, and white hostility that would turn violent in the riots of 1919.
  • A strong answer to reading question four holds both halves at once: pulled north by jobs, pushed out by oppression, and met in the North by both wages and violence.

Slide 22 · The migration count: 400,000 and Scott

On the slide: the big figure “400,000+”; the label — “Black southerners moved north in three years, by Scott’s count”; the note — “Emmett J. Scott, the federal investigator, published these figures in 1920. They are his reported count, not a census.”

  • 400,000+ — Black southerners moved north in three years, by Scott’s count
  • Scott published these figures in 1920; they are his reported count, not a census
  • [Attribute this number every time you say it,] because the attribution is part of the lesson. The federal investigator Emmett J. Scott reported that more than 400,000 Black people had moved north in the three years after the war began in Europe.
  • These are Scott’s figures, published in his 1920 study of wartime migration, and students should present them as his reported count, gathered from agents, railroads, and correspondents, not as a census enumeration.
  • The number still does its work. Four hundred thousand people in three years is a movement no one directed and no law ordered: families deciding, one household at a time, that the South Scott described — its crop liens, its disenfranchisement, its convict leases, its lynching — was no longer bearable beside northern wages.
  • Guided notes blanks twenty one and twenty two test Scott’s name and his 400,000 together. Keep them paired in your own speech all period, name and number, so students copy them paired.

Slide 23 · 1919: the home front comes apart

On the slide: five bullets — Wartime strike limits came off, and prices were still high. More than four million workers struck in 1919. After revolution in Russia, the strikes were read as revolution. Women kept the vote, and migrants kept moving. Dissenters kept the lesson that wartime rights could be withdrawn.

  • [Close the survey where the reading closes,] with the settlement of 1919.
  • Unions had accepted limits on strikes while the war lasted, and workers had watched prices rise faster than wages. When the controls came off, more than four million workers took part in strikes.
  • Employers and much of the public, frightened by revolution in Russia, read the strikes as revolution rather than bargaining, and most were defeated.
  • [Then give students the reading verdict in full,] because it is the lesson question answered in one breath. The home front ended the war divided: women had won the vote and Black families had begun a migration that would reshape the country, while dissenters had learned that wartime rights could be withdrawn by law and by mob alike.
  • Reading question five asks for three groups with one gain and one loss each. Any group named today qualifies, and students should now be able to build that answer without opening the reading again.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: money, production, and persuasion

On the slide: four terms with their definitions — Liberty Bonds, War Industries Board, Committee on Public Information, Espionage Act. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Say: “Liberty Bonds: government bonds sold to the public to raise money for the war, repaid with interest”
  • Say: “War Industries Board: the federal agency that coordinated war production and set priorities for industry during the war”
  • Say: “Committee on Public Information: the federal agency that promoted support for the war through posters, pamphlets, films, and speakers”
  • Say: “Espionage Act: the 1917 federal law that punished spying and acts intended to interfere with the military or obstruct recruiting”
  • Students confuse the board and the committee most often. The board directed things: materials, factories, prices. The committee directed opinion: posters, pamphlets, films, speakers.
  • One ran the economy of the war, the other ran its argument, and the Espionage Act stood behind both with prison for anyone who obstructed the first or answered the second too loudly.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: speech and movement

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — Sedition Act, Great Migration, Schenck v. United States. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: “Sedition Act: the 1918 federal law that punished disloyal or abusive speech about the government, the Constitution, the flag, and the armed forces”
  • Say: “Great Migration: the movement of hundreds of thousands of Black southerners to northern cities for jobs and greater rights during and after the war years”
  • Say: “Schenck v. United States: the 1919 Supreme Court case that upheld an Espionage Act conviction and stated the clear and present danger test for wartime speech”
  • Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly. Note the year and the target of the Sedition Act: 1918, and language itself.
  • The Great Migration definition carries both motives, jobs and greater rights, and students should keep both when they use the term.
  • [Fix the pairing aloud, because students pair this case with the wrong statute often:] Schenck was an Espionage Act conviction, reviewed in 1919, and the test it produced governed wartime speech cases for the next fifty years.
