The Home Front

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

Managing Production and Money

When the United States entered the war in April 1917, its army was small and its factories made goods for peacetime customers. The government had to turn a civilian economy into a war economy in months. The War Industries Board, created in 1917 and led from 1918 by the financier Bernard Baruch, set priorities for raw materials, told factories what to produce, and fixed prices on key goods. Railroads came under federal control, and a Food Administration pressed families to save food for the armies. Production rose sharply.

The war also had to be paid for. The Treasury sold Liberty Bonds, government bonds offered to the public in five great loan drives. Buyers lent the government money and were repaid with interest. Bond rallies and sales quotas reached nearly every town, and Americans bought roughly 17 billion dollars in bonds, about two thirds of the cost of the war. Buying a bond became a public test of loyalty as well as a loan.

Selling the War

President Wilson created the Committee on Public Information in April 1917, under the journalist George Creel, to build support for the war. The committee printed millions of pamphlets, supplied news releases to newspapers, made films, and organized about 75,000 volunteer speakers called Four Minute Men, who gave short patriotic talks in movie theaters while the reels were changed. Its posters covered walls across the country. The committee presented its work as education, and much of what it published was factual. Its purpose, however, was persuasion: to make a divided country act and think as one.

The Law Against Dissent

Congress also gave the government legal weapons against opposition. The Espionage Act of 1917 punished spying and made it a crime to obstruct recruiting or to cause insubordination in the armed forces, with fines up to ten thousand dollars and prison terms up to twenty years. The Sedition Act of 1918 went further and punished disloyal or abusive language about the government, the Constitution, the flag, and the armed forces. Prosecutors brought more than two thousand cases under the two laws.

Charles Schenck, an officer of the Socialist Party, was convicted for mailing leaflets that urged men to resist the draft. In Schenck v. United States in 1919, a unanimous Supreme Court upheld the conviction. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote that speech could be punished when it created a clear and present danger of an evil Congress had the power to prevent. Eugene V. Debs, the leading American socialist, received a ten year sentence for a speech in Canton, Ohio, in 1918 that praised draft resisters, and he ran for president in 1920 from a federal prison cell.

Fear at Home

War feeling also ran outside the courts. German Americans faced suspicion because of their origin. Schools dropped German language classes, orchestras stopped playing German music, and sauerkraut became liberty cabbage. In April 1918 a mob in Illinois lynched Robert Prager, a German born immigrant who had applied for American citizenship. Loyalty leagues and the American Protective League watched neighbors and reported suspected disloyalty to the Justice Department. The line between patriotism and persecution was crossed often, usually against people with no power to answer back.

Women, Work, and the Vote

The war opened paid work to women on a new scale. With millions of men in uniform and immigration from Europe cut off, factories, railroads, and offices hired women for jobs that had been closed to them. About a million women entered war industries and related work for the first time. The service was also an argument. Suffrage leaders pointed to women's war work and asked how a nation fighting for democracy abroad could deny women the vote at home. President Wilson endorsed a federal amendment in 1918 as a war measure. Congress passed the Nineteenth Amendment in 1919, and the states ratified it in 1920.

The Great Migration

The same labor shortage changed the map of Black America. Northern factories sent labor agents south, and Black newspapers such as the Chicago Defender carried word of jobs and higher wages north. Beginning in 1916, hundreds of thousands of Black southerners left for cities including Chicago, Detroit, and Cleveland. The federal investigator Emmett J. Scott reported that more than 400,000 Black people had moved north in the three years after the war began in Europe. Migrants sought wages, schools, and the right to vote, and relief from lynching and the crop lien system. They found real opportunity and also crowded housing, high rents, and white hostility that would turn violent in the riots of 1919.

What the War Left Behind

The war years stored up conflict in the workplace. Unions had accepted limits on strikes while the war lasted, and workers had watched prices rise faster than wages. When the controls came off in 1919, more than four million workers took part in strikes. Employers and much of the public, frightened by revolution in Russia, read the strikes as revolution rather than bargaining. The home front ended the war divided: women had won the vote and Black families had begun a migration that would reshape the country, while dissenters had learned that wartime rights could be withdrawn by law and by mob alike.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What did the War Industries Board do, and how did Liberty Bonds pay for the war? The board set priorities for raw materials, directed factories on what to produce, and fixed prices on key goods, coordinating a rapid conversion to war production. Liberty Bonds were loans from the public to the government, sold in four wartime drives and a fifth after the armistice. Sales of roughly 17 billion dollars covered about two thirds of the cost of the war, with buyers repaid with interest.

2. How did the Committee on Public Information work, and why does the reading call its purpose persuasion rather than only education? The committee printed pamphlets, fed news releases to newspapers, made films and posters, and fielded about 75,000 Four Minute Men who spoke in theaters. Much of its material was factual, which is why the committee called its work education. Its purpose was persuasion because its goal was not balanced information but unified support: enlistment, bond buying, food saving, and acceptance of wartime measures in a country divided by origin and opinion.

3. What did the Espionage Act and the Sedition Act punish, and what test did the Supreme Court state in Schenck v. United States? The Espionage Act (1917) punished spying, obstructing recruiting, and causing insubordination, with fines up to 10,000 dollars and up to twenty years in prison. The Sedition Act (1918) punished disloyal or abusive language about the government, the Constitution, the flag, and the armed forces. In Schenck (1919), a unanimous Court upheld Charles Schenck's conviction for anti-draft leaflets and stated the clear and present danger test: speech may be punished when it creates a clear and present danger of an evil Congress has the power to prevent.

4. Why did hundreds of thousands of Black southerners move north during the war years, according to the reading and to Emmett J. Scott? Northern war industries, cut off from European immigrant labor, needed workers and sent agents south, while Black newspapers advertised jobs and wages. Scott, the federal investigator, reported more than 400,000 Black migrants in the three years after the European war began and named the forces pushing people out: low wages and cheating in the crop lien system, disenfranchisement, the convict lease system, and violence. Migrants sought wages, schools, and voting rights. Strong answers hold both halves: pulled north by jobs, pushed south by oppression.

5. The lesson question asks who gained and who lost by the changes of the war years. Using three groups named in the reading, give one gain and one loss from the war at home. Accept any three groups with a documented gain and a documented loss. Examples: women gained war industry jobs and the Nineteenth Amendment (1920), while most war jobs were reclaimed by returning men after the armistice; Black migrants gained wages, schooling, and voting rights in the North, while facing crowded housing and the violence of 1919; German Americans lost language classes, cultural institutions, and in Robert Prager's case his life; dissenters such as Schenck and Debs lost liberty itself, with Debs imprisoned for a speech; workers gained union membership during the war and lost ground in the defeated strikes of 1919. The point students should reach: the same war expanded opportunity and contracted rights at the same time, and which one a person felt depended on who that person was.
