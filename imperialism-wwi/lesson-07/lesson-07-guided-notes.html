Guided Notes: The Home Front

Lesson question: How did the war change American life at home, and who gained and who lost by the change?

Unit question: Why did the United States seek power overseas, and how did the First World War change the country that fought it?

Key Terms

Liberty Bonds: government bonds sold to the public to raise money for the war, repaid with interest

War Industries Board: the federal agency that coordinated war production and set priorities for industry during the war

Committee on Public Information: the federal agency that promoted support for the war through posters, pamphlets, films, and speakers

Espionage Act: the 1917 federal law that punished spying and acts intended to interfere with the military or obstruct recruiting

Sedition Act: the 1918 federal law that punished disloyal or abusive speech about the government, the Constitution, the flag, and the armed forces

Great Migration: the movement of hundreds of thousands of Black southerners to northern cities for jobs and greater rights during and after the war years

Schenck v. United States: the 1919 Supreme Court case that upheld an Espionage Act conviction and stated the clear and present danger test for wartime speech

F answers: Push, any two: low wages and cheating under the crop lien system; disenfranchisement; the convict lease system; lynching and racial violence. Pull, any one: northern war industry jobs and higher wages; the chance to vote; better schools.

At a Glance, War Industries Board and Liberty Bonds: Said: voluntary cooperation and loans would equip the army without heavy new taxes. Outcome: production was directed by federal priorities and price fixing, and bond sales of roughly 17 billion dollars covered about two thirds of the cost of the war, under strong social pressure to buy.

At a Glance, Committee on Public Information: Said: the committee educated the public with facts about the war. Outcome: a national persuasion campaign of posters, pamphlets, films, and about 75,000 Four Minute Men, aimed at unified support rather than balanced information.

At a Glance, Espionage and Sedition Acts: Said: the laws protected the war effort against spies and obstruction. Outcome: more than two thousand prosecutions, including Schenck for leaflets and Debs for a speech, and a Supreme Court test that allowed wartime speech to be punished.

At a Glance, Great Migration: Said: federal investigators described a movement driven by conditions in the South and labor demand in the North. Outcome: more than 400,000 Black southerners moved north in three years, gaining wages and voting rights and meeting crowded housing and, in 1919, riot violence.

Exit ticket, question 3: accept any documented pair, for example women (war jobs and the vote in 1920) against German Americans (language and institutions suppressed, Prager lynched) or dissenters (Schenck and Debs imprisoned).
