# Image Sources: Lesson 7, The Home Front

Every image in this lesson is a real public domain poster, downloaded from Wikimedia Commons or the Library of Congress. No image was generated or altered beyond resizing for print. Print files live in the lesson assets folder; this record is kept in img/ to mirror the Gilded Age lesson structure.

## 1. hopps-mad-brute.jpg (Activity, Station 1)
- **Title:** Destroy This Mad Brute: Enlist, U.S. Army
- **Creator:** Harry R. Hopps, artist, for the United States Army
- **Date:** 1917
- **Repository:** Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division
- **Source URL:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Harry_R._Hopps,_Destroy_this_mad_brute_Enlist_-_U.S._Army,_03216u_edit.jpg
- **Public domain basis:** United States government recruiting poster published in 1917; copyright expired. Wikimedia Commons tags the file Public domain. The same record is carried in the TPSP bank as hopps-destroy-this-mad-brute-1917.
- **Teaching reason:** The clearest surviving example of official persuasion turning an enemy nation into an animal. Students read the helmet and club labels, the woman, and the ruins, then test the Committee on Public Information claim that its work was education against a poster that teaches nothing about the war itself.

## 2. christy-fight-or-buy-bonds.jpg (Activity, Station 4)
- **Title:** Fight or Buy Bonds, Third Liberty Loan
- **Creator:** Howard Chandler Christy (1873 to 1952), artist
- **Date:** 1917
- **Repository:** Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division (LCCN 2002708939)
- **Source URL:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fight_or_buy_bonds-Third_Liberty_Loan_LCCN2002708939.jpg (catalog: https://lccn.loc.gov/2002708939)
- **Public domain basis:** Poster published in the United States in 1917 for a federal loan drive; copyright expired. Library of Congress lists no known restrictions; Wikimedia Commons tags the file Public domain, confirmed through the Commons API metadata for this file (artist, 1917 date, Library of Congress credit, Public domain license).
- **Teaching reason:** The either or structure is the argument: fight or lend, with no third position. Paired with the McAdoo sentence quoted at the same station, it lets students separate a financial appeal from a loyalty test.

## 3. flagg-i-want-you.jpg (Quick Check)
- **Title:** I Want You for U.S. Army
- **Creator:** James Montgomery Flagg (1877 to 1960), artist
- **Date:** 1917 (image first published as the cover of Leslie's Weekly, July 6, 1916; adapted by the Army as a recruiting poster in 1917)
- **Repository:** Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division
- **Source URL:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:J._M._Flagg,_I_Want_You_for_U.S._Army_poster_(1917).jpg
- **Public domain basis:** United States Army recruiting poster published in 1917; copyright expired. Wikimedia Commons tags the file Public domain. The same record is carried in the TPSP bank as flagg-i-want-you-1917.
- **Teaching reason:** The most recognizable American recruiting image ever printed, and a study in direct address: one finger, one viewer, one instruction. The quick check sets it against Schenck's leaflets so students judge two printed appeals by direction and circumstance, not by medium.
- **Date disclosure:** The image predates its subject in one respect: Flagg drew Uncle Sam in 1916, before American entry into the war, and the Army reused the drawing in 1917. The caption and background note in the quick check state both dates, so no student is misled about when the image was made or when it was used.
