Neutrality Tested

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

Lesson question: Why did the United States enter the First World War in 1917 after three years of declared neutrality?

A War in Europe, a Neutral America

In 1914 Europe's great powers were bound into two rival alliance systems. Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy formed the Triple Alliance. France, Russia, and Great Britain formed the Triple Entente. The alliances turned a local quarrel into a general war. On June 28, 1914, a Serbian nationalist shot and killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary, at Sarajevo. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on July 28. Russia mobilized to defend Serbia. Germany declared war on Russia and France, marching through neutral Belgium. Britain declared war on Germany on August 4. President Woodrow Wilson proclaimed American neutrality that same day, and most Americans were glad to stay out.

Neutral Rights and Unequal Trade

Neutrality was a legal position, not a retreat from the world. A neutral nation could trade with all sides. Its ships could carry ordinary goods, and even contraband: goods, such as weapons and military supplies, that a belligerent claims the right to seize from neutral ships bound for its enemy. A belligerent could stop and search a neutral ship, but not lawfully sink it without warning or leave its crew to drown. Britain pressed against those rules with a blockade, stopping American ships and seizing cargoes. The United States protested without breaking with Britain. Meanwhile American trade tilted sharply. American farms and factories sold food, steel, and munitions to the Allies, and American banks lent them the money to pay. The blockade cut American trade with Germany to almost nothing. Neutrality was real in law; in practice, the American economy fed and armed one side.

The Lusitania and the Pledges

Germany answered the blockade with the submarine. A surface raider could stop and search a ship. A submarine, fragile on the surface, lost its chief advantage, surprise, if it warned first. In February 1915 Germany declared the waters around the British Isles a war zone and began unrestricted submarine warfare: a naval policy of sinking ships in a declared war zone without warning, including neutral and passenger ships. On May 7, 1915, the German submarine U-20 torpedoed the British passenger liner Lusitania off Ireland. The ship sank in eighteen minutes. Nearly 1,200 people died, among them 128 Americans. Germany replied that the liner had carried munitions and that travelers had been warned. Wilson refused to break relations. His notes demanded that Germany disavow the sinking, make reparation, and prevent attacks on passenger ships.

When a submarine sank the British liner Arabic in August 1915, Germany pledged not to sink passenger liners without warning. On March 24, 1916, a submarine torpedoed the French passenger steamer Sussex in the English Channel. The ship stayed afloat, but scores of passengers were killed or injured, including several Americans. Wilson warned that unless Germany abandoned such attacks, the United States would break diplomatic relations. On May 4, 1916, Germany answered with the Sussex Pledge: merchant vessels would not be sunk without warning and without saving human lives, unless a ship tried to escape or resist. German submarines generally observed the pledge for the rest of 1916.

Preparedness and the Election of 1916

The submarine crisis convinced many Americans that the country might be pulled into the war unprepared. The preparedness movement, backed by former president Theodore Roosevelt, demanded a larger army and navy. Opponents warned that a buildup would invite the war it claimed to prevent. Congress moved partway. The National Defense Act of June 1916 provided for a larger army, and a naval act funded a three-year building program. That November, Wilson won reelection by a narrow margin under the Democratic slogan "He kept us out of war." He had kept the peace and prepared for war at the same time.

The Chain Snaps: 1917

On January 9, 1917, Germany's leaders made the decision they had avoided for two years. They calculated that submarines turned loose without restriction could sink enough shipping to starve Britain into peace within months, before an American army could be raised and carried across the Atlantic. They knew it would probably bring the United States into the war and judged the gamble worth taking. Unrestricted submarine warfare resumed on February 1. Wilson broke diplomatic relations on February 3 and asked Congress to arm American merchant ships. Armed neutrality was his last alternative to war.

On March 1, American newspapers published the Zimmermann Telegram. British intelligence had intercepted and decoded the January message from German foreign minister Arthur Zimmermann to his representative in Mexico. If the United States entered the war, Mexico was to be offered an alliance, German financial support, and the recovery of Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. Coming on top of the submarine campaign, the telegram outraged opinion across the country. In the same weeks, revolution in Russia forced Czar Nicholas II to abdicate. The fall of Europe's most autocratic government let Wilson describe the Allies as democracies fighting empires. In mid-March, German submarines sank three American merchant ships, and Wilson's cabinet advised war without a dissenting vote.

The War Message

On April 2, 1917, Wilson asked a joint session of Congress to declare war. He built his case on the submarine campaign, which had swept every restriction aside, and concluded that "the right is more precious than peace." He also gave the war a purpose beyond the defense of shipping: "The world must be made safe for democracy." The Senate voted for war on April 4 and the House on April 6, and the United States entered the First World War. The chain from Sarajevo to the war message had taken thirty-two months.

Questions

1. How did the alliance system turn the shooting at Sarajevo into a general European war within five weeks?

2. In what sense was American neutrality real, and in what sense was it unequal in practice? Give evidence for both parts of your answer.

3. Trace the pledge sequence from the Lusitania to the Sussex Pledge. What did Germany promise in May 1916, and what had Wilson threatened in order to get the promise?

4. Why did Germany's leaders resume unrestricted submarine warfare in January 1917 when they knew it would probably bring the United States into the war?

5. Which single link in the chain do you judge decisive: the resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare, the Zimmermann Telegram, or the Russian Revolution? Defend your choice against the strongest case for one of the other two.

Teacher Answer Key

1. How did the alliance system turn a shooting into a general war? Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on July 28, 1914. Russia, tied to Serbia, mobilized. Germany, allied to Austria-Hungary, declared war on Russia and on France and attacked through neutral Belgium, which brought Britain, France's entente partner, into the war on August 4. Each declaration followed from alliance commitments, so a Balkan quarrel became a war of the great powers in about five weeks.

2. In what sense was neutrality real, and in what sense unequal? Real: the United States took no side, stayed out of the fighting for thirty-two months, traded in law with all belligerents, and protested British interference with its shipping as well as German attacks. Unequal: the British blockade cut American trade with Germany to almost nothing while American food, steel, cloth, and munitions flowed to Britain and France and American banks lent the Allies the purchase money, so the American economy supplied one side.

3. Trace the pledge sequence. After the Lusitania sinking (May 7, 1915; nearly 1,200 dead, including 128 Americans), Wilson demanded disavowal, reparation, and prevention of further attacks on passenger ships; Germany pledged after the Arabic sinking in August 1915 not to sink passenger liners without warning. After the Sussex was torpedoed on March 24, 1916, Wilson threatened to break diplomatic relations unless such attacks ended. The Sussex Pledge of May 4, 1916 promised that merchant vessels would not be sunk without warning and without saving human lives, unless a ship attempted escape or resistance.

4. Why did Germany resume unrestricted submarine warfare? German leaders calculated that unrestricted sinkings could cut Britain's food and supply lifeline and force Britain to make peace within months, before the United States could raise, train, and transport an army that could change the balance in France. They accepted American entry as a probable cost and judged that it would arrive too late to save Britain.

5. Which link was decisive? Accept any of the three defended against the strongest alternative. Submarine warfare: it was the stated ground of the war message, and Wilson broke relations over it in February, before the telegram was published. Zimmermann Telegram: it converted a dispute about shipping into a threat to American territory and united opinion outside the Northeast; against it, Mexico never acted and the offer was conditional on a war the submarine decision had already made likely. Russian Revolution: it removed the czar and made the democracy argument available; against it, it decided nothing by itself, and Wilson's stated grounds remained German conduct at sea.
