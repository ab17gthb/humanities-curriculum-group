Teacher Notes

Lesson 5 · Neutrality Tested

These are the speaker notes from the slide deck, in slide order, set for printing. The deck itself carries the main ideas in a few fragments per slide; the teaching is here. Print this and keep it beside you while you teach. Each entry is headed by its slide number and shows what is on that slide, so you can find your place without flipping back to the screen. Vocabulary definitions match the student guided notes word for word.

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: Neutrality Tested · Three years a declared neutral, then war in April 1917

The ship on screen is the British passenger liner Lusitania in service, photographed by the Bain News Service between 1907 and 1913 and held by the Library of Congress. Say the disclosure plainly before anything else: this photograph predates the sinking. The ship in the picture is intact and at work years before a German submarine torpedoed it on May 7, 1915. Hold the image for a moment. In August 1914 the United States declared itself neutral in a European war, and that declaration held in law for thirty-two months. Yet American farms, factories, banks, and ships were pulled toward one side, and German submarines kept meeting American travelers and American cargoes in the Atlantic. This lesson follows the chain link by link: Sarajevo, neutral rights, the Lusitania and the pledges, preparedness and the election of 1916, the German gamble of January 1917, and the war message. Students should leave able to name each link in order and to judge which single link was decisive.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: Why did the United States enter the First World War in 1917 after three years of declared neutrality? · Unit question: Why did the United States seek power overseas, and how did the First World War change the country tha

Today's question is the narrower one on the slide, and the unit question runs beneath it as the frame. The puzzle inside today's question is the delay. The causes students usually name first, the Lusitania above all, belong to 1915, yet the declaration of war came in April 1917. A cause that sits unchanged for two years cannot by itself explain the timing, so the lesson is built as a chain rather than a single event. Keep both halves of the wording in view all period: three years of declared neutrality, which were real, and entry in 1917, which was chosen only after Germany changed its own conduct at sea. The reading's fifth question, the notebook chart, and the writing task all return to this wording and ask students to pick the decisive link and defend the pick against the strongest alternative.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: One chain, six links · A · A War in Europe, a Neutral America · alliances, Sarajevo, and a neutral declaration · B · Neutral Rights and Unequal Trade · the blockade, contraband, and trade with one side · C · The Lusitani

This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section, A through F. The first two sections set the conditions: an alliance system that turned a Balkan shooting into a general war, and a neutrality that was genuine in law yet unequal in practice because the British blockade let American goods and American loans flow to the Allies alone. Section C is the long middle, two years of submarine attacks, American protests, and German pledges that held through 1916. Section D shows a country arming while it reelected a president on a peace slogan. Sections E and F are the break: Germany's January 1917 decision, the telegram, the Russian Revolution, the March sinkings, and the April war message. Tell students the notebook page, The Chain at a Glance, tracks exactly these links, so notes taken in this order arrive already organized for the writing task.

Slide 4 · Section A: A War in Europe, a Neutral America (divider)

On the slide: A War in Europe, a Neutral America · Two alliance systems, one shooting at Sarajevo, and a general war in five weeks.

Open with the structure, not the shooting. In 1914 Europe's great powers were bound into two rival alliance systems, and the alliances are what turned a local quarrel into a general war. Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy formed the Triple Alliance. France, Russia, and Great Britain formed the Triple Entente. Those six names and the two groupings are the first guided notes blanks, so name them slowly and put them on the board in two columns. Then set the American position beside the European one. The United States belonged to neither system, owed no ally a declaration of war, and had an ocean between itself and the fighting. That distance is why neutrality was available to Washington as a real policy rather than a hope. The next slide walks the five weeks from the shooting to the British declaration, step by step, because students must see the chain move before they can judge the longer chain that ends in 1917.

Slide 5 · Alliances, then the trigger

On the slide: Alliances, then the trigger · ▪ Triple Alliance: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy / ▪ Triple Entente: France, Russia, Great Britain / ▪ June 28, 1914: Archduke Franz Ferdinand shot at Sarajevo / ▪ Austria-Hungary decl

Walk the sequence as a chain of obligations, pausing at each declaration to ask who was bound to whom. On June 28, 1914, a Serbian nationalist shot and killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary, at Sarajevo. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on July 28. Russia mobilized to defend Serbia. Germany declared war on Russia and France and marched through neutral Belgium, and Britain declared war on Germany on August 4. President Woodrow Wilson proclaimed American neutrality that same day, and most Americans were glad to stay out. Both the archduke and the place are guided notes blanks, as are Belgium and Wilson, so spell Sarajevo aloud. Land the contrast that frames the whole lesson. Europe's governments had promised their way into the war in advance. The American government had promised nothing, which is why the American decision, when it finally came in 1917, had to be argued for rather than inherited.

Slide 6 · Five weeks: Sarajevo to a general war

On the slide: FIVE WEEKS · from the shots at Sarajevo to a general European war · June 28 to August 4, 1914: each declaration followed an alliance commitment, and the great powers were all in.

Dwell on the speed, because speed is the evidence that the system, not any single leader, drove the opening of the war. Thirty-seven days separate the shooting from the British declaration, and no government in that span found a working exit. Austria-Hungary moved first against Serbia. Russia's mobilization was itself treated as an act of war by Germany, whose war plan required defeating France quickly before turning east, and whose route into France ran through neutral Belgium. The invasion of Belgium, a small country whose neutrality the great powers had guaranteed by treaty, is what brought Britain in on August 4. Note the word neutral here and bank it for section B. In 1914 a neutral's rights were written into international practice, and Belgium shows both the strength and the limit of that protection: the violation gave Britain its ground for war, and it did not save Belgium. Wilson proclaimed American neutrality the day Britain declared war.

Slide 7 · Section B: Neutral Rights and Unequal Trade (divider)

On the slide: Neutral Rights and Unequal Trade · Neutral in law, and supplying one side in practice.

This section carries the lesson's most analytical idea, and the reading's second question tests it directly, so teach the two halves separately and then join them. The first half is legal. Neutrality was a legal position, not a retreat from the world, and it came with defined rights for American ships and defined limits on what belligerents could do to those ships. The second half is economic. While the law stayed neutral, the flow of goods and money did not, because sea power decided which belligerent Americans could actually reach. Hold both halves in view when students reach the exit ticket, which asks them to define two of this section's terms and give a fact for each. A student who can only recite the legal half has the definition of neutrality. A student who can add the blockade and the loans has the lesson's argument about how neutrality worked in practice between 1914 and 1917.

Slide 8 · What a neutral could do

On the slide: What a neutral could do · ▪ Trade with all sides, in law / ▪ Ships carrying ordinary goods, and even contraband / ▪ A belligerent could stop and search a neutral ship / ▪ Not sink it without warning, not leave its cr

Teach the rules before the violations, because every later crisis in this lesson is a dispute about which rule was broken. A neutral nation could trade with all sides. Its ships could carry ordinary goods, and even contraband, a term held for the vocabulary slide at the end of the lesson. A belligerent could stop and search a neutral ship and seize contraband cargo. What it could not lawfully do was sink the ship without warning or leave its crew to drown. Those two prohibitions are guided notes blanks, so state them twice. Britain pressed against the rules with a blockade, stopping American ships and seizing cargoes, and the United States protested without breaking with Britain. Ask students to hold the comparison for the next section: Britain took American property and answered protests with notes. Germany, in the next section but one, would take American lives. Wilson's different responses to the two belligerents grow out of that difference.

Slide 9 · Trade that tilted

On the slide: Trade that tilted · ▪ The British blockade: American ships stopped, cargoes seized / ▪ Food, steel, and munitions to the Allies / ▪ American banks lending the Allies the money to pay / ▪ Trade with Germany cut to alm

Now the economic half, in the reading's own order. American farms and factories sold food, steel, and munitions to the Allies, and American banks lent them the money to pay. Munitions and banks are guided notes blanks; say both plainly. The blockade cut American trade with Germany to almost nothing, not because American merchants refused German orders but because British sea power stood between American ports and German ones. A German buyer with cash could not collect his goods. An Allied buyer could, and could borrow the cash in New York when his own ran short. Then give students the reading's verdict sentence and have them copy its shape into their notes: neutrality was real in law; in practice, the American economy fed and armed one side. Germany understood this perfectly, and it explains, without excusing, the weapon Germany reached for in section C. A country being starved by a blockade it cannot break at the surface will look under the water.

Slide 10 · Section C: The Lusitania and the Pledges (divider)

On the slide: The Lusitania and the Pledges · A liner sunk in eighteen minutes, / then two years of promises

The photograph shows the Lusitania in service, taken by the Bain News Service between 1907 and 1913, and the disclosure belongs in your spoken teaching as well as in the printed caption: the photograph predates the sinking and shows the ship at work, not in distress. This section is the long center of the lesson, running from February 1915 to the end of 1916, and its shape surprises students. The famous sinking did not bring war. It brought notes, demands, and a sequence of German pledges that narrowed, incident by incident, until the Sussex Pledge of May 1916 held the submarines back for the rest of that year. Teach the section as a negotiation conducted through sinkings. Each pledge was bought with an American threat, and each one held only while Germany judged American goodwill worth more than unrestricted sinkings. When that judgment flipped in January 1917, the whole structure fell at once, which is why section E can move so fast.

Slide 11 · Germany's answer to the blockade

On the slide: Germany's answer to the blockade · ▪ A surface raider could stop and search / ▪ A submarine, fragile on the surface, lost surprise if it warned / ▪ February 1915: a declared war zone around the British Isles / ▪ Sink

Explain the tactical problem honestly, because students who understand it understand why the old rules broke. A surface raider could stop and search a ship and put its crew in boats before sinking it. A submarine, fragile on the surface, lost its chief advantage, surprise, if it warned first, and a surfaced submarine could be rammed or shelled by the very merchant ship it had stopped to search. In February 1915 Germany declared the waters around the British Isles a war zone and began the policy whose full definition waits for the vocabulary slide: sinking ships in that zone without warning, including neutral and passenger ships. War zone is the guided notes blank in this part of the section. Germany also argued that Britain had armed merchant ships and ordered them to ram, blurring the line the old rules assumed. Present that argument as Germany's, then return to the rule from section B. Passengers and crews still went into the water without warning, and Washington judged the practice, not the argument.

Slide 12 · 18 minutes: the Lusitania sinks

On the slide: 18 MINUTES · for the Lusitania to sink off Ireland on May 7, 1915 · Nearly 1,200 people died, among them 128 Americans. The United States protested, and did not go to war.

Slow the room down for this number. On May 7, 1915, the German submarine U-20 torpedoed the British passenger liner Lusitania off Ireland, and the ship sank in eighteen minutes, too fast for an orderly launch of her boats. Nearly 1,200 people died, among them 128 Americans. U-20, eighteen, and 128 are all guided notes blanks, so give each figure separately and watch the notebooks. Germany replied that the liner had carried munitions and that travelers had been warned in published notices, and both claims were true as far as they went; the reading's answer key expects students to know them. Wilson still refused to break relations. His notes demanded that Germany disavow the sinking, make reparation, and prevent attacks on passenger ships. Then land the timing point that the whole lesson turns on. The worst single incident of the neutrality years produced diplomacy, not war, and nearly two years of pledges followed it. Something else had to change before April 1917.

Slide 13 · Notes, threats, and pledges

On the slide: Notes, threats, and pledges · ▪ Arabic, August 1915: a pledge on passenger liners / ▪ Sussex torpedoed in the Channel, March 24, 1916 / ▪ Scores killed or injured, including several Americans / ▪ Wilson's threat: bre

Trace the sequence as cause and effect, because the reading's third question tests exactly this chain. When a submarine sank the British liner Arabic in August 1915, Germany pledged not to sink passenger liners without warning. On March 24, 1916, a submarine torpedoed the French passenger steamer Sussex in the English Channel. The ship stayed afloat, but scores of passengers were killed or injured, including several Americans. Passenger liners, Sussex, and diplomatic relations are the guided notes blanks here, and the pattern to name is escalation by incident: each attack was smaller than the Lusitania, and each American response was sharper. Wilson had spent a year sending notes. After the Sussex he attached a consequence, warning that unless Germany abandoned such attacks the United States would break diplomatic relations, the step just short of war. Berlin now had to price the submarine campaign against the near certainty of losing American neutrality entirely, and in May it paid. The next slide gives the price in full.

Slide 14 · The Sussex Pledge, May 4, 1916

On the slide: The Sussex Pledge, May 4, 1916 · ▪ Merchant vessels not sunk without warning / ▪ And not without saving human lives / ▪ Unless a ship tried to escape or resist / ▪ Generally observed for the rest of 1916 / ▪ Neutral

Give the pledge's terms in full, because the vocabulary definition at the end of the lesson is built from them and blank nine in the guided notes tests the second clause. On May 4, 1916, Germany promised that merchant vessels would not be sunk without warning and without saving human lives, unless a ship tried to escape or resist. Note the exception clause and teach it as written: a ship that ran or fought could still be sunk, which left German commanders a judgment call in the moment. German submarines generally observed the pledge for the rest of 1916. Pause on that fact, because it proves the pledge system worked on its own terms for eight months. The Atlantic in late 1916 was dangerous, argued over, and formally neutral. What ended the arrangement was not a fresh atrocity. It was a fresh calculation in Berlin, made in January 1917, about how fast Britain could be starved. Section D first shows what the United States did with the breathing room the pledge bought.

Slide 15 · Section D: Preparedness and the Election of 1916 (divider)

On the slide: Preparedness and the Election of 1916 · Arming in earnest, / while voting for peace

The photograph shows a preparedness parade in Washington, D.C., in 1916, held by the Library of Congress in the National Photo Company Collection. Crowds marching for a stronger army and navy were a new sight in American politics, and the parade is the right image for a section built on a contradiction. The submarine crisis convinced many Americans that the country might be pulled into the war unprepared, and a movement formed to demand a larger army and navy before the emergency arrived rather than after it. Congress moved partway in 1916, and that November the voters reelected Woodrow Wilson on a slogan that credited him with keeping the peace. Both things happened in the same year, with the same electorate. Teach the section as preparation and politics running together: the buildup made American entry in 1917 physically possible, and the election result told Wilson how narrow his mandate for war still was. He kept the peace and prepared for war at the same time, and students should be able to support both halves.

Slide 16 · The preparedness movement

On the slide: The preparedness movement · ▪ Backed by former president Theodore Roosevelt / ▪ A larger army and a larger navy, before any emergency / ▪ Opponents: a buildup invites the war it claims to prevent / ▪ National Defense

The second crop of the 1916 Washington parade shows the marchers closer, and it is worth saying to students that these are civilians campaigning, not soldiers drilling. The preparedness movement, backed by former president Theodore Roosevelt, demanded a larger army and navy. Roosevelt and the National Defense Act are guided notes blanks, so name both slowly. Opponents warned that a buildup would invite the war it claimed to prevent, an argument students will meet again in later units, so give it a fair hearing rather than a dismissive one. Congress moved partway. The National Defense Act of June 1916 provided for a larger army, and a naval act funded a three-year building program. Neither act made the United States ready in 1916, and teachers should say so. What they did was start the machinery: training camps, officer pipelines, shipyards under contract. When war came in April 1917, the country began from that start instead of from nothing, which is the strongest case for calling preparedness a link in the chain rather than background.

Slide 17 · He kept us out of war

On the slide: He kept us out of war · ▪ Wilson reelected in November 1916, by a narrow margin / ▪ The Democratic slogan: "He kept us out of war" / ▪ A campaign slogan, not a Wilson quotation / ▪ Peace kept, and war prepared for, i

Handle the slogan with the care the lesson's source note uses. He kept us out of war was the Democratic campaign slogan of 1916, not a Wilson quotation, and students should never put the words inside quotation marks under Wilson's name. The slogan is also blank eleven in the guided notes, so write it on the board exactly as it appears on this slide. Wilson won reelection by a narrow margin that November, and the closeness is part of the teaching. A president who had carried the country by a landslide might have read the result as a mandate for firmness. Wilson read his narrow win as a country that wanted peace kept and options open, which matches his conduct in the crisis that followed: break relations in February, ask to arm merchant ships, and go to Congress only after the March sinkings. Close the section by naming the tension students will judge in the writing task. Preparedness advocates could argue that 1916 readiness made 1917 entry possible. Peace voters could argue that nothing in the election authorized war. Both were describing the same year.

Slide 18 · Section E: The Chain Snaps: 1917 (divider)

On the slide: The Chain Snaps: 1917 · Ninety days from a decision in Berlin / to a declaration in Washington

The image is the head of the Zimmermann Telegram as received by the German ambassador to Mexico, dated January 19, 1917, and held by the United States National Archives. Students will read the telegram's text closely in the activity and again in the quick check, so here it stands as an artifact: a coded cable, intercepted and decoded by British intelligence, that crossed the Atlantic twice before it reached American newspapers. This section moves faster than any other in the lesson, and the speed is the point. Germany decided on January 9 to resume unrestricted sinkings. The sinkings resumed on February 1, relations broke on February 3, the telegram was published on March 1, revolution removed the czar in the same weeks, three American ships were sunk in mid-March, and a unanimous cabinet advised war. Six links in ninety days, after thirty months of stalemate. Ask students, before the next slide, which of those links Germany chose and which it merely caused.

Slide 19 · Germany's calculation

On the slide: Germany's calculation · ▪ Decided January 9, 1917, after two years of holding back / ▪ Sink enough shipping to starve Britain within months / ▪ Before an American army could be raised and carried over / ▪ American en

This is the slide the exit ticket's second question is built on, so teach the calculation as a calculation. On January 9, 1917, Germany's leaders made the decision they had avoided for two years. They calculated that submarines turned loose without restriction could sink enough shipping to starve Britain into peace within months, before an American army could be raised and carried across the Atlantic. Months and February 1 are guided notes blanks. They knew the resumption would probably bring the United States into the war and judged the gamble worth taking, because an American army that arrived after Britain had settled would arrive too late to change the result. Unrestricted submarine warfare resumed on February 1. Wilson broke diplomatic relations on February 3 and asked Congress to arm American merchant ships. Armed neutrality, American guns on American freighters without a declaration, was his last alternative to war. Name what Germany had thrown away: the Sussex Pledge had kept the United States out for eight months, and Berlin surrendered that benefit knowingly, in exchange for tonnage sunk per month.

Slide 20 · The telegram, published March 1

On the slide: The telegram, published March 1 · ▪ Intercepted and decoded by British intelligence / ▪ From Foreign Minister Arthur Zimmermann, January 1917 / ▪ To Mexico: an alliance, if the United States entered / ▪ German financ

The photograph shows the telegram document itself, the copy as received in Mexico, held by the National Archives. Walk the offer in the reading's order. British intelligence had intercepted and decoded the January message from German foreign minister Arthur Zimmermann to his representative in Mexico. If the United States entered the war, Mexico was to be offered an alliance, German financial support, and the recovery of Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. Those three states are guided notes blanks, and students should write all three. Then teach the limits as firmly as the offer. The proposal was conditional, triggered only by an American entry that the submarine decision had already made likely, and Mexico never acted on it. Its power in March 1917 was not military. A dispute about shipping rights, which many Americans west and south of the seaboard had followed from a distance, suddenly named American territory. Coming on top of the submarine campaign, the telegram outraged opinion across the country, including regions the Lusitania had never moved.

Slide 21 · The last links fall

On the slide: The last links fall · ▪ Revolution in Russia: Czar Nicholas II abdicates / ▪ The Allies describable as democracies against empires / ▪ Mid-March: three American merchant ships sunk / ▪ Wilson's cabinet advises war, w

Two developments in the same weeks removed Wilson's remaining hesitations, and they work in opposite directions, so separate them. In Russia, revolution forced Czar Nicholas II to abdicate; he and Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona share blank thirteen's line in the guided notes, which pairs the telegram and the revolution in one breath. The fall of Europe's most autocratic government let Wilson describe the Allies as democracies fighting empires, a description the czar's presence had always made awkward. That is an argument becoming available, not a cause of war, and the reading's answer key treats it exactly that way. The second development was material. In mid-March, German submarines sank three American merchant ships; three is blank fourteen. American crews were now being killed under a policy Berlin had resumed with open eyes. Wilson's cabinet advised war without a dissenting vote. By the last week of March the question in Washington was no longer whether, and Wilson spent it writing the address that section F takes up.

Slide 22 · Section F: The War Message (divider)

On the slide: The War Message · April 2, 1917: the case laid / before a joint session of Congress

The photograph shows President Woodrow Wilson addressing Congress, held by the Library of Congress, which dates it to 1917. Teach the disclosure that the printed captions in this lesson observe: the Library of Congress record does not name the specific address, so this deck does not claim the photograph shows the war message of April 2, 1917. It shows Wilson before Congress in the year of the war message, and that is all the record supports. The section itself is short because the event is single. After thirty-two months, Wilson went before a joint session and asked Congress to declare war, building his case on the submarine campaign and giving the war a purpose beyond the defense of shipping. Students have the full text of the message's key passages in the activity's fifth station. The two slides of this section divide the event into the argument Wilson made and the decision Congress took, because the writing task asks students to judge the chain, not merely to admire the speech.

Slide 23 · April 2, 1917

On the slide: April 2, 1917 · ▪ The case built on the submarine campaign / ▪ Every restriction swept aside / ▪ "The right is more precious than peace" / ▪ "The world must be made safe for democracy" / ▪ Senate, April 4; House, Ap

The second crop of the 1917 Wilson photograph accompanies the message itself, and the disclosure bears repeating in this slide's teaching: the Library of Congress dates the photograph to 1917 but does not name the address, so do not tell students this is the April 2 speech in progress. Now the event. On April 2, 1917, Wilson asked a joint session of Congress to declare war. He built his case on the submarine campaign, which had swept every restriction aside, and concluded that the right is more precious than peace, a clause quoted from within a longer sentence in the message, as the lesson's source note discloses. He also gave the war a purpose beyond the defense of shipping: the world must be made safe for democracy, which completes blank fifteen in the guided notes. The Senate voted for war on April 4 and the House on April 6, and the United States entered the First World War. The chain from Sarajevo to the war message had taken thirty-two months.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for neutrality and the sea war

On the slide: Words for neutrality and the sea war · neutrality · a nation's policy of taking no side in a war between other countries · Lusitania · a British passenger liner sunk by a German submarine off Ireland on May 7, 1915, kill

These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them. Neutrality is a nation's policy of taking no side in a war between other countries: the legal position sections A and B described, held by the United States from August 1914 to April 1917. The Lusitania is a British passenger liner sunk by a German submarine off Ireland on May 7, 1915, killing nearly 1,200 people, including 128 Americans; the definition carries the date and both death figures, so read them aloud. Unrestricted submarine warfare is a naval policy of sinking ships in a declared war zone without warning, including neutral and passenger ships, the policy of February 1915 and again of February 1917. Contraband is goods, such as weapons and military supplies, that a belligerent claims the right to seize from neutral ships bound for its enemy, the cargo category at the center of the British blockade dispute.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for the pledges and the turn

On the slide: Words for the pledges and the turn · Sussex Pledge · Germany's promise of May 4, 1916, after the Sussex attack, that its submarines would not sink merchant vessels without warning and without saving human lives · Zimmerm

Three more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly. The Sussex Pledge is Germany's promise of May 4, 1916, after the Sussex attack, that its submarines would not sink merchant vessels without warning and without saving human lives. Note for students that the definition names the promise and its two conditions, warning given and lives saved, and that Germany set the promise aside in January 1917. The Zimmermann Telegram is a secret January 1917 message from Germany's foreign minister proposing that Mexico ally with Germany against the United States and recover Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona; the three recovered territories are inside the definition itself, which is why the guided notes test them there. Preparedness is the movement and program in the United States before 1917 to strengthen the army and navy in case the country was drawn into the war. The definition's closing clause is the teaching point: the buildup was framed from the start as insurance against being drawn in, not as a plan to enter.

Slide 26 · Now your turn: Chain to War and exit ticket

On the slide: Chain to War · Return to the lesson question: why did the United States enter the war in 1917, after three years of declared neutrality? Work the chain, then judge its decisive link. · Activity · Chain to War: An Evidenc

Close by handing the lesson to its work, in the order students will meet it. The activity, Chain to War: An Evidence Lab, runs five stations in the chain's own order: the Lusitania in May 1915, the Sussex Pledge in May 1916, preparedness in 1915 and 1916, the Zimmermann Telegram from January to March 1917, and the war message in April 1917. At each station students record what the link changed, then the class builds one timeline and each student renders one verdict. The notebook page, The Chain to War, collects the same five links into the chart they will reuse on review day, so require it complete before the quick check. The quick check, The Last Links, is source based on the Zimmermann extract and tests close reading of the telegram's offer and its conditions. The writing task, The Decisive Link, asks for the judgment this lesson has been building toward: one link chosen, defended with evidence, and defended against the strongest case for one of the other two.
