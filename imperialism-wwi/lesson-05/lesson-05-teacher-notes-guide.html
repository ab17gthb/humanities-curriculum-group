Neutrality Tested

Imperialism and World War I · Lesson 5 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Imperialism and World War I, Lesson 5 of 8. It follows Lesson 4, “Open Doors and the Big Stick,” where the United States projected power overseas without formal colonies. This lesson turns to 1914 and asks why a country that declared neutrality in a European war, and held that declaration in law for thirty-two months, entered the war in April 1917. Teach it fifth in the unit, before Lesson 6, “Americans in the War,” takes up mobilization and the fighting in France.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading “Neutrality Tested” and the guided notes, which follow the deck’s order exactly — a war in Europe and a neutral America, neutral rights and unequal trade, the Lusitania and the pledges, preparedness and the election of 1916, the chain snapping in 1917, and the war message. The activity is “Chain to War: An Evidence Lab” (five stations, from the Lusitania to the war message). The notebook page is “The Chain to War,” one chart for the pledges, preparedness, the gamble, the telegram, and the message. The quick check is “The Last Links,” a close reading of the Zimmermann extract. The writing task is “The Decisive Link” — one link defended against the strongest alternative.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Lesson 4’s picture of the United States as an overseas power by 1914, because neutrality was the policy of a country already trading, lending, and projecting influence across two oceans. Everything else is taught in order: the two alliance systems and the July crisis of 1914, the legal rights of a neutral, the British blockade and the tilt of American trade, the submarine campaign and the Arabic and Sussex pledges, the preparedness movement and the election of 1916, Germany’s January 1917 gamble, the Zimmermann Telegram, the Russian Revolution, and Wilson’s war message.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (continuity and change — a neutrality held in law for thirty-two months, then abandoned link by link), D2.His.16 (assess the purpose and intended audience of historical sources — the Zimmermann Telegram and Wilson’s war message, each read for what it was meant to do), D2.His.14 (analyze multiple and complex causes and effects — a chain of causes judged for the decisive link rather than a single cause); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading’s chain from Sarajevo to the war message), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task: one decisive link defended against the strongest alternative).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to trace the chain from the shots at Sarajevo to the declaration of war in order — the alliance trap of 1914, neutral rights and the one-sided trade the blockade produced, the Lusitania and the pledges that held through 1916, preparedness and the election of 1916, Germany’s resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare, the Zimmermann Telegram, the Russian Revolution, and the March sinkings — state Wilson’s case in the war message of April 2, 1917, and judge which single link in the chain was decisive, defending the judgment against the strongest alternative.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening, question, and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; a war in Europe, a neutral America (slides 4–6) 6 minutes; neutral rights and unequal trade (slides 7–9) 7 minutes; the Lusitania and the pledges (slides 10–14) 11 minutes; preparedness and the election of 1916 (slides 15–17) 6 minutes; the chain snaps in 1917 (slides 18–21) 8 minutes; the war message (slides 22–23) 4 minutes; vocabulary and the turn to work (slides 24–26) 3 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. The exit ticket has three parts — define neutrality and contraband, explain why Germany abandoned the Sussex Pledge, and name the decisive link.

Key vocabulary

neutrality · Lusitania · unrestricted submarine warfare · contraband · Sussex Pledge · Zimmermann Telegram · preparedness

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line “Imperialism and World War I · Lesson 5”; the title “Neutrality Tested”; the standfirst — “Three years a declared neutral, then war in April 1917.”

  • Picture: The British passenger liner Lusitania in service, photographed by the Bain News Service between 1907 and 1913 and held by the Library of Congress. The photograph predates the sinking: the ship in the picture is intact and at work years before a German submarine torpedoed it on May 7, 1915.
  • [Say the disclosure plainly before anything else:] this photograph predates the sinking. Hold the image for a moment.
  • In August 1914 the United States declared itself neutral in a European war, and that declaration held in law for thirty-two months.
  • Yet American farms, factories, banks, and ships were pulled toward one side, and German submarines kept meeting American travelers and American cargoes in the Atlantic.
  • This lesson follows the chain link by link: Sarajevo, neutral rights, the Lusitania and the pledges, preparedness and the election of 1916, the German gamble of January 1917, and the war message.
  • Students should leave able to name each link in order and to judge which single link was decisive.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the question — “Why did the United States enter the First World War in 1917 after three years of declared neutrality?”; below it, the unit question: “Why did the United States seek power overseas, and how did the First World War change the country that fought it?”

  • Today’s question is the narrower one on the slide, and the unit question runs beneath it as the frame.
  • [Name the puzzle inside the question:] the delay. The causes students usually name first, the Lusitania above all, belong to 1915, yet the declaration of war came in April 1917.
  • A cause that sits unchanged for two years cannot by itself explain the timing, so the lesson is built as a chain rather than a single event.
  • Keep both halves of the wording in view all period: three years of declared neutrality, which were real, and entry in 1917, which was chosen only after Germany changed its own conduct at sea.
  • Ask: If the Lusitania was sunk in 1915, what still has to happen before a 1917 declaration makes sense? (Something must change in 1917 itself — Germany’s conduct at sea is where the lesson will find it.)
  • The reading’s fifth question, the notebook chart, and the writing task all return to this wording and ask students to pick the decisive link and defend the pick against the strongest alternative.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title “One chain, six links”; six rows — A, A War in Europe, a Neutral America (alliances, Sarajevo, and a neutral declaration); B, Neutral Rights and Unequal Trade (the blockade, contraband, and trade with one side); C, The Lusitania and the Pledges (a liner sunk, then promises made and kept); D, Preparedness and the Election of 1916 (a buildup, and a slogan about peace); E, The Chain Snaps: 1917 (the gamble, the telegram, and three ships sunk); F, The War Message (April 2, and a purpose beyond shipping).

  • This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section, A through F.
  • The first two sections set the conditions: an alliance system that turned a Balkan shooting into a general war, and a neutrality that was genuine in law yet unequal in practice because the British blockade let American goods and American loans flow to the Allies alone.
  • Section C is the long middle, two years of submarine attacks, American protests, and German pledges that held through 1916.
  • Section D shows a country arming while it reelected a president on a peace slogan.
  • Sections E and F are the break: Germany’s January 1917 decision, the telegram, the Russian Revolution, the March sinkings, and the April war message.
  • [Point students to their notebooks:] the notebook page, The Chain at a Glance, tracks exactly these links, so notes taken in this order arrive already organized for the writing task.

Slide 4 · Section A: A War in Europe, a Neutral America (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section A”; the title “A War in Europe, a Neutral America”; the line — “Two alliance systems, one shooting at Sarajevo, and a general war in five weeks.”

  • [Open with the structure, not the shooting.] In 1914 Europe’s great powers were bound into two rival alliance systems, and the alliances are what turned a local quarrel into a general war.
  • Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy formed the Triple Alliance. France, Russia, and Great Britain formed the Triple Entente.
  • Those six names and the two groupings are the first guided notes blanks, so name them slowly and put them on the board in two columns.
  • Then set the American position beside the European one. The United States belonged to neither system, owed no ally a declaration of war, and had an ocean between itself and the fighting.
  • That distance is why neutrality was available to Washington as a real policy rather than a hope.
  • The next slide walks the five weeks from the shooting to the British declaration, step by step, because students must see the chain move before they can judge the longer chain that ends in 1917.

Slide 5 · Alliances, then the trigger

On the slide: six bullets — The Triple Alliance joined Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy. The Triple Entente joined France, Russia, and Great Britain. On June 28, 1914, a Serbian nationalist shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on July 28, 1914. Russia mobilized, and Germany declared war on Russia and France. Germany marched through neutral Belgium, and Britain declared war on August 4, 1914.

  • [Walk the sequence as a chain of obligations,] pausing at each declaration to ask who was bound to whom.
  • On June 28, 1914, a Serbian nationalist shot and killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary, at Sarajevo.
  • Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on July 28. Russia mobilized to defend Serbia. Germany declared war on Russia and France and marched through neutral Belgium, and Britain declared war on Germany on August 4.
  • President Woodrow Wilson proclaimed American neutrality that same day, and most Americans were glad to stay out.
  • Both the archduke and the place are guided notes blanks, as are Belgium and Wilson, so spell Sarajevo aloud.
  • [Land the contrast that frames the whole lesson.] Europe’s governments had promised their way into the war in advance. The American government had promised nothing, which is why the American decision, when it finally came in 1917, had to be argued for rather than inherited.

Slide 6 · Five weeks: Sarajevo to a general war

On the slide: the big figure “FIVE WEEKS”; the label — “from the shots at Sarajevo to a general European war”; the note — “June 28 to August 4, 1914: each declaration followed an alliance commitment, and the great powers were all in.”

  • Five weeks — June 28 to August 4, 1914
  • [Dwell on the speed,] because speed is the evidence that the system, not any single leader, drove the opening of the war.
  • Thirty-seven days separate the shooting from the British declaration, and no government in that span found a working exit.
  • Austria-Hungary moved first against Serbia. Russia’s mobilization was itself treated as an act of war by Germany, whose war plan required defeating France quickly before turning east, and whose route into France ran through neutral Belgium.
  • The invasion of Belgium, a small country whose neutrality the great powers had guaranteed by treaty, is what brought Britain in on August 4.
  • [Bank the word neutral for Section B.] In 1914 a neutral’s rights were written into international practice, and Belgium shows both the strength and the limit of that protection: the violation gave Britain its ground for war, and it did not save Belgium.
  • Wilson proclaimed American neutrality the day Britain declared war.

Slide 7 · Section B: Neutral Rights and Unequal Trade (divider)

On the slide: the section label “Section B”; the title “Neutral Rights and Unequal Trade”; the line — “Neutral in law, and supplying one side in practice.”

  • This section carries the lesson’s most analytical idea, and the reading’s second question tests it directly, so teach the two halves separately and then join them.
  • The first half is legal. Neutrality was a legal position, not a retreat from the world, and it came with defined rights for American ships and defined limits on what belligerents could do to those ships.
  • The second half is economic. While the law stayed neutral, the flow of goods and money did not, because sea power decided which belligerent Americans could actually reach.
  • [Hold both halves in view for the exit ticket,] which asks students to define two of this section’s terms and give a fact for each.
  • A student who can only recite the legal half has the definition of neutrality. A student who can add the blockade and the loans has the lesson’s argument about how neutrality worked in practice between 1914 and 1917.

Slide 8 · What a neutral could do

On the slide: five bullets — A neutral nation could trade with all sides in law. Neutral ships could carry ordinary goods, and even contraband. A belligerent could stop and search a neutral ship. A belligerent could not sink a neutral ship without warning or leave its crew to drown. The United States protested Britain’s blockade but never broke relations with Britain.

  • [Teach the rules before the violations,] because every later crisis in this lesson is a dispute about which rule was broken.
  • A neutral nation could trade with all sides. Its ships could carry ordinary goods, and even contraband, a term held for the vocabulary slide at the end of the lesson.
  • A belligerent could stop and search a neutral ship and seize contraband cargo. What it could not lawfully do was sink the ship without warning or leave its crew to drown.
  • Those two prohibitions are guided notes blanks, so state them twice.
  • Britain pressed against the rules with a blockade, stopping American ships and seizing cargoes, and the United States protested without breaking with Britain.
  • Ask: What is the difference between what Britain took and what Germany would later take? (Britain took American property and answered protests with notes; Germany, in Section C, would take American lives — and Wilson’s different responses grow out of that difference.)

Slide 9 · Trade that tilted

On the slide: five bullets — The British blockade stopped American ships and seized their cargoes. American farms and factories sold food, steel, and munitions to the Allies. American banks lent the Allies the money to pay for those goods. The blockade cut American trade with Germany to almost nothing. Neutrality was real in law, but one-sided in practice.

  • [Take the economic half in the reading’s own order.] American farms and factories sold food, steel, and munitions to the Allies, and American banks lent them the money to pay.
  • Munitions and banks are guided notes blanks; say both plainly.
  • The blockade cut American trade with Germany to almost nothing, not because American merchants refused German orders but because British sea power stood between American ports and German ones.
  • A German buyer with cash could not collect his goods. An Allied buyer could, and could borrow the cash in New York when his own ran short.
  • [Give students the reading’s verdict sentence] and have them copy its shape into their notes: neutrality was real in law; in practice, the American economy fed and armed one side.
  • Germany understood this perfectly, and it explains, without excusing, the weapon Germany reached for in Section C. A country being starved by a blockade it cannot break at the surface will look under the water.

Slide 10 · Section C: The Lusitania and the Pledges (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label “Section C”; the title “The Lusitania and the Pledges”; the lines — “A liner sunk in eighteen minutes, then two years of promises.”

  • Picture: The Lusitania in service, taken by the Bain News Service between 1907 and 1913 and held by the Library of Congress. The photograph predates the sinking and shows the ship at work, not in distress.
  • [Repeat the disclosure in your spoken teaching.] This section is the long center of the lesson, running from February 1915 to the end of 1916, and its shape surprises students.
  • The famous sinking did not bring war. It brought notes, demands, and a sequence of German pledges that narrowed, incident by incident, until the Sussex Pledge of May 1916 held the submarines back for the rest of that year.
  • [Teach the section as a negotiation conducted through sinkings.] Each pledge was bought with an American threat, and each one held only while Germany judged American goodwill worth more than unrestricted sinkings.
  • When that judgment flipped in January 1917, the whole structure fell at once, which is why Section E can move so fast.

Slide 11 · Germany's answer to the blockade

On the slide: the photograph at right; four bullets — A surface raider could stop and search a ship before sinking it. A submarine was fragile on the surface and lost surprise if it warned first. In February 1915, Germany declared a war zone around the British Isles. Germany began sinking ships without warning, including neutral and passenger ships.

  • Picture: A detail crop of the Lusitania in service, photographed by the Bain News Service between 1907 and 1913 and held by the Library of Congress. The photograph predates the sinking of May 7, 1915; the ship shown is the liner at work, the most famous target of the policy this slide explains.
  • [Explain the tactical problem honestly,] because students who understand it understand why the old rules broke.
  • A surface raider could stop and search a ship and put its crew in boats before sinking it. A submarine, fragile on the surface, lost its chief advantage, surprise, if it warned first, and a surfaced submarine could be rammed or shelled by the very merchant ship it had stopped to search.
  • In February 1915 Germany declared the waters around the British Isles a war zone and began the policy whose full definition waits for the vocabulary slide: sinking ships in that zone without warning, including neutral and passenger ships.
  • War zone is the guided notes blank in this part of the section.
  • Germany also argued that Britain had armed merchant ships and ordered them to ram, blurring the line the old rules assumed. [Present that argument as Germany’s,] then return to the rule from Section B: passengers and crews still went into the water without warning, and Washington judged the practice, not the argument.

Slide 12 · 18 minutes: the Lusitania sinks

On the slide: the big figure “18 MINUTES”; the label — “for the Lusitania to sink off Ireland on May 7, 1915”; the note — “Nearly 1,200 people died, among them 128 Americans. The United States protested, and did not go to war.”

  • 18 minutes — the Lusitania sank off Ireland on May 7, 1915
  • Nearly 1,200 people died, among them 128 Americans
  • [Slow the room down for this number.] On May 7, 1915, the German submarine U-20 torpedoed the British passenger liner Lusitania off Ireland, and the ship sank in eighteen minutes, too fast for an orderly launch of her boats.
  • U-20, eighteen, and 128 are all guided notes blanks, so give each figure separately and watch the notebooks.
  • Germany replied that the liner had carried munitions and that travelers had been warned in published notices, and both claims were true as far as they went; the reading’s answer key expects students to know them.
  • Wilson still refused to break relations. His notes demanded that Germany disavow the sinking, make reparation, and prevent attacks on passenger ships.
  • [Land the timing point the whole lesson turns on.] The worst single incident of the neutrality years produced diplomacy, not war, and nearly two years of pledges followed it. Something else had to change before April 1917.

Slide 13 · Notes, threats, and pledges

On the slide: four bullets — In August 1915, the sinking of the Arabic brought a German pledge on passenger liners. A submarine torpedoed the Sussex in the Channel on March 24, 1916. The Sussex attack killed or injured scores of passengers, including several Americans. Wilson threatened to break diplomatic relations with Germany.

  • [Trace the sequence as cause and effect,] because the reading’s third question tests exactly this chain.
  • When a submarine sank the British liner Arabic in August 1915, Germany pledged not to sink passenger liners without warning.
  • On March 24, 1916, a submarine torpedoed the French passenger steamer Sussex in the English Channel. The ship stayed afloat, but scores of passengers were killed or injured, including several Americans.
  • Passenger liners, Sussex, and diplomatic relations are the guided notes blanks here, and the pattern to name is escalation by incident: each attack was smaller than the Lusitania, and each American response was sharper.
  • Wilson had spent a year sending notes. After the Sussex he attached a consequence, warning that unless Germany abandoned such attacks the United States would break diplomatic relations, the step just short of war.
  • Berlin now had to price the submarine campaign against the near certainty of losing American neutrality entirely, and in May it paid. The next slide gives the price in full.

Slide 14 · The Sussex Pledge, May 4, 1916

On the slide: five bullets — Germany promised that merchant vessels would not be sunk without warning. Germany also promised that merchant vessels would not be sunk without saving human lives. A ship that tried to escape or resist could still be sunk. German submarines generally observed the pledge for the rest of 1916. American neutrality now rested on a German promise.

  • [Give the pledge’s terms in full,] because the vocabulary definition at the end of the lesson is built from them and blank nine in the guided notes tests the second clause.
  • On May 4, 1916, Germany promised that merchant vessels would not be sunk without warning and without saving human lives, unless a ship tried to escape or resist.
  • [Note the exception clause and teach it as written:] a ship that ran or fought could still be sunk, which left German commanders a judgment call in the moment.
  • German submarines generally observed the pledge for the rest of 1916. [Pause on that fact,] because it proves the pledge system worked on its own terms for eight months.
  • The Atlantic in late 1916 was dangerous, argued over, and formally neutral. What ended the arrangement was not a fresh atrocity. It was a fresh calculation in Berlin, made in January 1917, about how fast Britain could be starved.
  • Section D first shows what the United States did with the breathing room the pledge bought.

Slide 15 · Section D: Preparedness and the Election of 1916 (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label “Section D”; the title “Preparedness and the Election of 1916”; the lines — “Arming in earnest, while voting for peace.”

  • Picture: A preparedness parade in Washington, D.C., in 1916, held by the Library of Congress in the National Photo Company Collection. Crowds marching for a stronger army and navy were a new sight in American politics.
  • The parade is the right image for a section built on a contradiction. The submarine crisis convinced many Americans that the country might be pulled into the war unprepared, and a movement formed to demand a larger army and navy before the emergency arrived rather than after it.
  • Congress moved partway in 1916, and that November the voters reelected Woodrow Wilson on a slogan that credited him with keeping the peace. Both things happened in the same year, with the same electorate.
  • [Teach the section as preparation and politics running together:] the buildup made American entry in 1917 physically possible, and the election result told Wilson how narrow his mandate for war still was.
  • He kept the peace and prepared for war at the same time, and students should be able to support both halves.

Slide 16 · The preparedness movement

On the slide: the photograph at right; five bullets — Former president Theodore Roosevelt backed the preparedness movement. Supporters demanded a larger army and a larger navy before any emergency arrived. Opponents warned that a buildup would invite the war it claimed to prevent. The National Defense Act of June 1916 provided for a larger army. A naval act funded a three-year building program.

  • Picture: A closer crop of the 1916 Washington preparedness parade, held by the Library of Congress. These are civilians campaigning, not soldiers drilling.
  • [Say that to students plainly:] these are civilians campaigning, not soldiers drilling.
  • The preparedness movement, backed by former president Theodore Roosevelt, demanded a larger army and navy. Roosevelt and the National Defense Act are guided notes blanks, so name both slowly.
  • Opponents warned that a buildup would invite the war it claimed to prevent, an argument students will meet again in later units, so give it a fair hearing rather than a dismissive one.
  • Congress moved partway. The National Defense Act of June 1916 provided for a larger army, and a naval act funded a three-year building program.
  • Neither act made the United States ready in 1916, and teachers should say so. What they did was start the machinery: training camps, officer pipelines, shipyards under contract.
  • When war came in April 1917, the country began from that start instead of from nothing, which is the strongest case for calling preparedness a link in the chain rather than background.

Slide 17 · He kept us out of war

On the slide: four bullets — Wilson won reelection in November 1916 by a narrow margin. The Democratic slogan was “He kept us out of war.” The slogan was a campaign line, not a Wilson quotation. Americans kept the peace and prepared for war in the same year.

  • [Handle the slogan with care.] “He kept us out of war” was the Democratic campaign slogan of 1916, not a Wilson quotation, and students should never put the words inside quotation marks under Wilson’s name.
  • The slogan is also blank eleven in the guided notes, so write it on the board exactly as it appears on this slide.
  • Wilson won reelection by a narrow margin that November, and the closeness is part of the teaching. A president who had carried the country by a landslide might have read the result as a mandate for firmness.
  • Wilson read his narrow win as a country that wanted peace kept and options open, which matches his conduct in the crisis that followed: break relations in February, ask to arm merchant ships, and go to Congress only after the March sinkings.
  • [Close the section by naming the tension students will judge in the writing task.] Preparedness advocates could argue that 1916 readiness made 1917 entry possible. Peace voters could argue that nothing in the election authorized war. Both were describing the same year.

Slide 18 · Section E: The Chain Snaps: 1917 (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label “Section E”; the title “The Chain Snaps: 1917”; the lines — “Ninety days from a decision in Berlin to a declaration in Washington.”

  • Picture: The head of the Zimmermann Telegram as received by the German ambassador to Mexico, dated January 19, 1917, and held by the United States National Archives.
  • Students will read the telegram’s text closely in the activity and again in the quick check, so here it stands as an artifact: a coded cable, intercepted and decoded by British intelligence, that crossed the Atlantic twice before it reached American newspapers.
  • This section moves faster than any other in the lesson, and the speed is the point. Germany decided on January 9 to resume unrestricted sinkings. The sinkings resumed on February 1, relations broke on February 3, the telegram was published on March 1, revolution removed the czar in the same weeks, three American ships were sunk in mid-March, and a unanimous cabinet advised war.
  • Ninety days — six links, after thirty months of stalemate
  • Ask: Before the next slide — which of those links did Germany choose, and which did it merely cause? (The resumption was chosen in Berlin; the telegram’s publication, the revolution, and the cabinet’s advice followed from choices and events Germany did not control.)

Slide 19 · Germany's calculation

On the slide: six bullets — Germany made the decision on January 9, 1917, after two years of holding back. German leaders calculated that submarines could sink enough shipping to starve Britain within months. They expected Britain to settle before an American army could be raised and carried across the Atlantic. They judged American entry probable, and worth the risk. Unrestricted sinkings resumed on February 1, and Wilson broke relations on February 3. Wilson’s last alternative to war was armed neutrality.

  • [Teach the calculation as a calculation,] because the exit ticket’s second question is built on this slide.
  • On January 9, 1917, Germany’s leaders made the decision they had avoided for two years. They calculated that submarines turned loose without restriction could sink enough shipping to starve Britain into peace within months, before an American army could be raised and carried across the Atlantic.
  • Months and February 1 are guided notes blanks.
  • They knew the resumption would probably bring the United States into the war and judged the gamble worth taking, because an American army that arrived after Britain had settled would arrive too late to change the result.
  • Unrestricted submarine warfare resumed on February 1. Wilson broke diplomatic relations on February 3 and asked Congress to arm American merchant ships.
  • Armed neutrality — American guns on American freighters without a declaration — was his last alternative to war.
  • [Name what Germany had thrown away:] the Sussex Pledge had kept the United States out for eight months, and Berlin surrendered that benefit knowingly, in exchange for tonnage sunk per month.

Slide 20 · The telegram, published March 1

On the slide: the photograph at right; five bullets — British intelligence intercepted and decoded the telegram. Foreign Minister Arthur Zimmermann sent the message in January 1917. Germany offered Mexico an alliance if the United States entered the war. Germany also promised Mexico financial support. Mexico would recover Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona.

  • Picture: The Zimmermann Telegram document itself, the copy as received in Mexico, held by the United States National Archives.
  • [Walk the offer in the reading’s order.] British intelligence had intercepted and decoded the January message from German foreign minister Arthur Zimmermann to his representative in Mexico.
  • If the United States entered the war, Mexico was to be offered an alliance, German financial support, and the recovery of Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona.
  • Those three states are guided notes blanks, and students should write all three.
  • [Teach the limits as firmly as the offer.] The proposal was conditional, triggered only by an American entry that the submarine decision had already made likely, and Mexico never acted on it. Its power in March 1917 was not military.
  • A dispute about shipping rights, which many Americans west and south of the seaboard had followed from a distance, suddenly named American territory. Coming on top of the submarine campaign, the telegram outraged opinion across the country, including regions the Lusitania had never moved.

Slide 21 · The last links fall

On the slide: four bullets — Revolution in Russia forced Czar Nicholas II to abdicate. With the czar gone, the Allies could be described as democracies against empires. In mid-March, German submarines sank three American merchant ships. Wilson’s cabinet advised war without a dissenting vote.

  • [Separate the two developments,] because they work in opposite directions.
  • In Russia, revolution forced Czar Nicholas II to abdicate; he and Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona share blank thirteen’s line in the guided notes, which pairs the telegram and the revolution in one breath.
  • The fall of Europe’s most autocratic government let Wilson describe the Allies as democracies fighting empires, a description the czar’s presence had always made awkward. That is an argument becoming available, not a cause of war, and the reading’s answer key treats it exactly that way.
  • The second development was material. In mid-March, German submarines sank three American merchant ships; three is blank fourteen. American crews were now being killed under a policy Berlin had resumed with open eyes.
  • Wilson’s cabinet advised war without a dissenting vote. By the last week of March the question in Washington was no longer whether, and Wilson spent it writing the address that Section F takes up.

Slide 22 · Section F: The War Message (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label “Section F”; the title “The War Message”; the lines — “April 2, 1917: the case laid before a joint session of Congress.”

  • Picture: President Woodrow Wilson addressing Congress, held by the Library of Congress, which dates the photograph to 1917. The catalog record does not name the specific address, so this deck does not claim the photograph shows the war message of April 2, 1917.
  • [Teach that disclosure as the captions observe it.] The photograph shows Wilson before Congress in the year of the war message, and that is all the record supports.
  • The section itself is short because the event is single. After thirty-two months, Wilson went before a joint session and asked Congress to declare war, building his case on the submarine campaign and giving the war a purpose beyond the defense of shipping.
  • Students have the full text of the message’s key passages in the activity’s fifth station.
  • The two slides of this section divide the event into the argument Wilson made and the decision Congress took, because the writing task asks students to judge the chain, not merely to admire the speech.

Slide 23 · April 2, 1917

On the slide: the photograph at right; five bullets — Wilson built his case on the submarine campaign. He said the campaign had swept every restriction aside. “The right is more precious than peace.” “The world must be made safe for democracy.” The Senate voted for war on April 4, and the House followed on April 6.

  • Picture: A second crop of the 1917 photograph of Wilson addressing Congress, held by the Library of Congress. The catalog dates the photograph to 1917 but does not name the address, so do not tell students this is the April 2 speech in progress.
  • [Repeat that disclosure in this slide’s teaching.] Now the event.
  • On April 2, 1917, Wilson asked a joint session of Congress to declare war. He built his case on the submarine campaign, which had swept every restriction aside, and concluded that the right is more precious than peace, a clause quoted from within a longer sentence in the message, as the lesson’s source note discloses.
  • He also gave the war a purpose beyond the defense of shipping: the world must be made safe for democracy, which completes blank fifteen in the guided notes.
  • The Senate voted for war on April 4 and the House on April 6, and the United States entered the First World War.
  • The chain from Sarajevo to the war message had taken thirty-two months.

  • Say: “Lusitania: a British passenger liner sunk by a German submarine off Ireland on May 7, 1915, killing nearly 1,200 people, including 128 Americans”
  • Say: “unrestricted submarine warfare: a naval policy of sinking ships in a declared war zone without warning, including neutral and passenger ships”

  • Neutrality is the legal position Sections A and B described, held by the United States from August 1914 to April 1917.
  • The Lusitania definition carries the date and both death figures, so read them aloud.
  • Unrestricted submarine warfare is the policy of February 1915 and again of February 1917. Contraband is the cargo category at the center of the British blockade dispute.

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for the pledges and the turn

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — Sussex Pledge, Zimmermann Telegram, preparedness. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: “Sussex Pledge: Germany's promise of May 4, 1916, after the Sussex attack, that its submarines would not sink merchant vessels without warning and without saving human lives”
  • Say: “Zimmermann Telegram: a secret January 1917 message from Germany's foreign minister proposing that Mexico ally with Germany against the United States and recover Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona”
  • Say: “preparedness: the movement and program in the United States before 1917 to strengthen the army and navy in case the country was drawn into the war”
  • The Sussex Pledge definition names the promise and its two conditions, warning given and lives saved, and Germany set the promise aside in January 1917.
  • The Zimmermann Telegram definition carries the three recovered territories inside it, which is why the guided notes test them there.
  • The preparedness definition’s closing clause is the teaching point: the buildup was framed from the start as insurance against being drawn in, not as a plan to enter.
