Writing Task: The Decisive Link

The prompt. Why did the United States enter the First World War in 1917 after three years of declared neutrality? Write a paragraph or short essay that names the one link in the chain you judge decisive, supports the judgment with at least two of the evidence cards below, and answers the strongest point that can be made for a different link.

The cards are summaries drawn from the lesson reading and the activity stations. Write the history in your own sentences, and do not copy the cards word for word.

Card 1: The Submarine Decision

On January 9, 1917, Germany's leaders decided to resume unrestricted submarine warfare on February 1, calculating that sinkings without warning could starve Britain into peace within months, before an American army could reach France. They decided knowing it would probably bring the United States into the war. Wilson broke relations on February 3, and German submarines sank three American merchant ships in mid-March.

Card 2: The Pledges That Held

After the Lusitania sinking in May 1915, Wilson's notes demanded disavowal, reparation, and prevention, and Germany pledged after the Arabic sinking not to sink passenger liners without warning. After the Sussex attack in March 1916, the Sussex Pledge of May 4, 1916 promised that merchant vessels would not be sunk without warning and without saving human lives. It held for the rest of 1916, proof that diplomacy had twice pulled the two countries back.

Card 3: The Telegram

British intelligence decoded a January 1917 message from German foreign minister Arthur Zimmermann. If the United States entered the war, Mexico was to be offered an alliance, generous financial support, and the recovery of Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. Newspapers published it on March 1, 1917; Zimmermann admitted it was genuine, and Mexico never acted on the offer.

Card 4: The Tilted Neutrality

From 1914 the British blockade cut American trade with Germany to almost nothing, while American farms and factories sold food, steel, and munitions to the Allies and American banks lent them the money to pay. By 1917 an Allied defeat would have left those loans unpaid. The United States had taken no side in law, but its economy was committed to one side's survival.

Card 5: The Democracy Case

In March 1917 revolution forced Czar Nicholas II of Russia to abdicate, removing the Allied side's most autocratic government. On April 2, Wilson asked Congress for war on the ground of the submarine campaign and gave the war a larger purpose: "The world must be made safe for democracy." Congress declared war on April 6.

Write Your Answer

Write for a reader who knows the events but has not decided which one caused the outcome. Aim for about 300 words. Keep every event in its own year: evidence from 1915 and 1916 can explain the state of opinion Wilson faced, but it cannot be the trigger for a decision taken in 1917.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that names one decisive link and gives the deciding reason, not a list of causes with no verdict. Any of the five cards can carry the claim if the reasoning is sound.
  • At least two cards used accurately. Card 1 for the German calculation and the February and March sinkings; Card 2 for the record that diplomacy had worked twice, which any trigger argument must explain away; Card 3 for the telegram and its territorial offer; Card 4 for the economic commitment built up under neutrality; Card 5 for the Russian Revolution and the framing of the war message.
  • The strongest point for a different link stated fairly and answered. A submarine argument must answer Card 2: sinkings in 1915 and 1916 were settled by notes, so the answer has to identify what had changed, namely the abandonment of all restriction and the March sinkings of American ships. A telegram argument must answer the chronology in Card 1: Wilson broke relations on February 3, before publication. A tilted-neutrality argument must answer why a commitment present since 1915 produced war only in 1917.
  • Chronology discipline: an essay that makes the Lusitania sinking itself the direct cause of a declaration two years later, without the pledge sequence in between, loses the evidence point.

Model outline for the debrief. Submarine side: claim; Card 1 (a deliberate German decision, taken with American entry priced in); Card 5 (Wilson's own stated ground was the submarine campaign); answer to Card 3 (the telegram inflamed opinion, but it proposed action conditional on a war the submarine decision had already made likely, and Mexico never moved). Telegram side: claim; Card 3 (territory named, authenticity admitted, opinion united outside the Northeast); Card 2 (the pledge system had contained every earlier crisis, so outrage alone had never been enough before opinion shifted); answer to Card 1 (breaking relations is not war, and six weeks of armed neutrality followed February 1 without a war message). Credit either verdict argued this way.
