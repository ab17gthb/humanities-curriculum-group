An Empire in the Philippines

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A Treaty Signed in Paris

On December 10, 1898, Spain and the United States signed the Treaty of Paris. Spain gave up Cuba, Puerto Rico, and Guam and ceded the Philippine Islands to the United States for 20 million dollars. No Filipino signed the treaty or was heard at Paris. The Philippine envoy Felipe Agoncillo traveled to Paris and Washington for independence, and officials refused to receive him. The treaty settled the islands between two empires while their people built a government of their own.

The Debate in the Senate

The treaty needed two thirds of the Senate, and through January 1899 the outcome stayed in doubt. Expansionists argued that the Philippines offered markets, a coaling station on the route to China, and a duty to govern people they claimed could not govern themselves. They warned that Germany or Japan would seize the islands if America let them go. Opponents answered that ruling millions without their consent broke the founding principle of the United States. The Senate ratified the treaty on February 6, 1899, by 57 votes to 27, one vote more than required.

The League Against Empire

The American Anti-Imperialist League, organized in Boston in 1898, coordinated the opposition. Its leaders ranged from former president Grover Cleveland and diplomat Carl Schurz to industrialist Andrew Carnegie, who offered 20 million dollars for Philippine independence, labor leader Samuel Gompers, and, from 1901, Mark Twain. Members disagreed on why annexation was wrong. Some argued from the Declaration of Independence that governments rest on consent of the governed. Others feared Filipino labor competition, or objected to governing people they deemed racially unfit for citizenship. The League's platform of October 1899 called subjugation of any people criminal aggression.

Aguinaldo and the Philippine Republic

Filipinos had been fighting Spain since 1896. Their leader, Emilio Aguinaldo, returned from exile in May 1898 on an American naval vessel and resumed the revolt with American encouragement, although the United States never promised independence in writing. On June 12, 1898, Aguinaldo proclaimed Philippine independence at Kawit, and Filipino forces took control of most of Luzon outside Manila. A congress at Malolos wrote a constitution, and on January 23, 1899, the Philippine Republic was inaugurated with Aguinaldo as president. American officials treated the republic as an insurrection against authority Filipinos had never accepted.

Benevolent Assimilation

President William McKinley announced his policy in a proclamation of December 21, 1898: control of the islands had been ceded to the United States, and military government would extend over the archipelago. The United States came, it promised, not as invaders or conquerors, but as friends. McKinley named the mission benevolent assimilation, the mild sway of justice and right in place of arbitrary rule, and warned that resistance would meet the strong arm of authority. General Elwell Otis, commanding in Manila, sent Aguinaldo a copy with the claims of sovereignty removed, but the unedited text reached him anyway. On January 5, 1899, Aguinaldo protested: he had never agreed to American sovereignty, and he had returned to fight for independence.

War

On February 4, 1899, two days before the Senate vote, an American sentry shot at Filipino soldiers near Manila, and open war began. American forces won the early set battles and took the capital, Malolos. In November 1899 Aguinaldo disbanded his regular army and ordered guerrilla warfare: small bands ambushed patrols and lived off the towns. The United States sent about 126,000 soldiers to the islands during the war. Troops captured Aguinaldo in March 1901; he swore allegiance to the United States and called for peace. Resistance continued until President Theodore Roosevelt declared the war over on July 4, 1902.

Methods and Costs

Guerrilla war blurred the line between soldier and civilian, and methods hardened. Troops burned villages and crops. In Batangas, much of the population was forced into guarded reconcentration camps, where disease spread quickly. Interrogators used the water cure, forcing water into a prisoner until he talked. After Filipino fighters killed 48 Americans at Balangiga, Samar, in September 1901, General Jacob Smith ordered his men to kill and burn and make Samar a howling wilderness. He was court-martialed and retired. The costs fell heaviest on Filipinos. The Office of the Historian gives more than 4,200 American dead, more than 20,000 Filipino combatants dead, and an estimate of as many as 200,000 Filipino civilian deaths from violence, famine, and disease. That figure is an estimate, not a count; some estimates run higher.

The Insular Cases

The Supreme Court then decided what the new possessions were in law. In the Insular Cases, beginning with Downes v. Bidwell in 1901, the Court held that Puerto Rico and the Philippines were not foreign countries, but neither were they fully part of the United States. Justice Edward White, concurring in Downes, described Puerto Rico as foreign to the United States in a domestic sense. The rulings created the unincorporated territory: belonging to the United States, not on the road to statehood, and under the Constitution only in part.

Civil Government

Military rule gave way by stages. On July 4, 1901, William Howard Taft became civil governor, heading a commission building schools, courts, and a civil service, with English as the language of instruction. The Philippine Organic Act of 1902 confirmed civil government and promised an assembly, which met in 1907. Filipinos held local offices and sat in the assembly, while an American governor general kept final authority and no date for independence was set. The islands remained a colony, governed in the name of preparing their people for a self-government they had proclaimed in 1899.

Teacher Answer Key

1. The Treaty of Paris was signed by Spain and the United States. What did it decide about the Philippines, what did the United States pay, and who was not heard at Paris? It ceded the Philippine Islands from Spain to the United States for 20 million dollars. No Filipino signed the treaty or was heard at Paris. The Philippine envoy Felipe Agoncillo traveled to Paris and Washington to argue for independence, and officials refused to receive him.

2. The Senate ratified the treaty by 57 votes to 27. Why does the reading call that margin close, and what two arguments did the expansionists make that the opponents had to answer? Ratification required two thirds of the Senate. With 84 votes cast, 56 were required, so 57 votes cleared the requirement by a single vote. Expansionist arguments: the islands offered markets and a coaling station on the route to China, and the Filipinos, in their claim, could not yet govern themselves, so American rule was a duty. Opponents answered that ruling millions without their consent broke the founding principle of the United States, and that Germany or Japan would take the islands was a warning, not a justification.

3. Name two members of the Anti-Imperialist League coalition who came from very different parts of American life, and give two different reasons members opposed annexation. Any two of: former president Grover Cleveland, diplomat Carl Schurz, industrialist Andrew Carnegie, labor leader Samuel Gompers, and Mark Twain (who joined in 1901). Reasons: principled opposition from the Declaration of Independence, that governments rest on consent of the governed; fear of competition from Filipino workers; and objection to governing people considered racially unfit for citizenship. The coalition agreed on the vote, not on the reasons.

4. McKinley's proclamation promised that the United States came as friends and named the mission benevolent assimilation. What else did the same proclamation order, and how did Aguinaldo learn its real terms? It ordered American military government extended over the whole archipelago and warned that resistance would meet the strong arm of authority. General Otis sent Aguinaldo a copy with the claims of sovereignty removed, but the unedited text reached Aguinaldo by another route, and on January 5, 1899, he protested that he had never agreed to American sovereignty.

5. The reading gives Filipino deaths their own accounting. State the figures it gives for American dead, Filipino combatant dead, and Filipino civilian dead, and explain why the civilian figure must be stated as an estimate rather than a count. More than 4,200 American dead; more than 20,000 Filipino combatants dead; as many as 200,000 Filipino civilian deaths from violence, famine, and disease, per the Office of the Historian. The civilian figure is an estimate because no authority counted civilian deaths during the war. The deaths came from fighting, burned villages and destroyed crops, reconcentration camps, and epidemics across hundreds of islands over three years, and historians reconstruct the total from population records rather than from a register of the dead. Some estimates run higher.
