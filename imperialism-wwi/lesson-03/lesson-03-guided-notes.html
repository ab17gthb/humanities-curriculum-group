Guided Notes: An Empire in the Philippines

Lesson question: Did taking the Philippines extend American ideals or contradict them?

Unit question: Why did the United States seek power overseas, and how did the First World War change the country that fought it?

Key Terms

Philippine-American War: the war fought from 1899 to 1902 between the United States and the Philippine Republic over American annexation of the Philippines

anti-imperialism: opposition to acquiring and ruling overseas colonies, especially on the ground that governing a people without its consent violates republican principles

Emilio Aguinaldo: the Filipino revolutionary leader who proclaimed Philippine independence in 1898 and served as president of the Philippine Republic

guerrilla warfare: fighting by small, mobile bands that ambush, raid, and disperse instead of meeting an enemy army in open battle

Benevolent Assimilation: the name President McKinley gave his Philippine policy in his proclamation of December 21, 1898, claiming that American rule would substitute just government for arbitrary rule

Insular Cases: the Supreme Court decisions beginning in 1901 that held the new island territories were not fully part of the United States and that the Constitution did not apply there in full

civil government: the rule of a territory by civilian officials acting under law, in place of direct military rule

At a glance, annex: strongest claim, the United States had a duty to govern people its supporters claimed could not govern themselves, and the islands gave markets and a station on the route to China; behind it, Beveridge's March of the Flag argument and McKinley's proclamation.

At a glance, reject: strongest claim, governing a people without its consent violates the Declaration of Independence; behind it, the League platform's phrase criminal aggression and the consent of the governed.

At a glance, independence: strongest claim, the Filipinos had already formed a republic with a constitution, an army, and a capital, so the question was not whether they could govern but whether the United States would let them; behind it, the Malolos constitution and republic of January 23, 1899, and Aguinaldo's protest of January 5, 1899.

Exit ticket 1: promise, friendship, protection of homes, property, and rights, and just government in place of arbitrary rule; threat, military government over the whole archipelago and the strong arm of authority against resistance. Exit ticket 2: ratification required two thirds of the votes cast. Of 84 votes, 56 were required, so the 57th vote was the whole margin; one senator switching sides defeats the treaty. Exit ticket 3: extended, accept the proclamation's promise of rights, schools, courts, and eventual elected assembly under civil government. Contradicted, accept the treaty signed without Filipinos, rule without consent, guerrilla war methods such as reconcentration and the water cure, and an estimated 200,000 Filipino civilian deaths.
