An Empire in the Philippines

Imperialism and World War I · Lesson 3 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Imperialism and World War I, Lesson 3 of 8. It follows Lesson 2, "The Spanish-American War," which ended with the Treaty of Paris signed on December 10, 1898, and ratified by one vote above two thirds. This lesson takes up the largest thing that treaty carried — the Philippines — and asks what the purchase set in motion: an organized opposition at home, a republic the United States refused to recognize, a three-year war, and the court decisions and civil government that housed the colony afterward. Teach it third in the unit, before Lesson 4, "Open Doors and the Big Stick," widens the lens to China and Latin America.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading "An Empire in the Philippines" and the guided notes, which follow the deck's order exactly — a treaty and a vote, the League against empire, the Philippine Republic, benevolent assimilation and war, and courts and civil government, closing with the Argument at a Glance table. The activity is the "Annexation Debate Simulation: The Senate Question, 1899" (five roles, five documents, then the vote). The notebook page collects the argument chart: three positions, their claims, and the evidence behind each. The quick check is "School Begins," reading the 1899 Puck cartoon, caricatures included, as evidence. The writing task is "Extend or Contradict?" — the lesson question argued from the documents, with the civilian death estimate stated as an estimate wherever it is used.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Lesson 2's settlement — the Treaty of Paris terms place by place, cession as a term, and the fact that Filipino leaders had declared a republic and were never consulted — plus Lesson 1's motives for the turn overseas. Everything else is taught in order: the Senate debate and the 57-to-27 vote, the Anti-Imperialist League and its divided coalition, Aguinaldo and the Malolos Republic, the Benevolent Assimilation proclamation and how its edited copy reached Aguinaldo, the guerrilla war and its methods and costs, the Insular Cases, and Taft's civil government.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (continuity and change — a republic founded on consent of the governed acquiring a colony), D2.His.16 (assess the purpose and intended audience of historical sources — the Benevolent Assimilation proclamation, including the copy edited for its Filipino reader, and the School Begins cartoon), D2.Civ.12 (analyze how people use and challenge laws and institutions — treaty ratification, the League's platform, and the Insular Cases); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's accounts of the treaty, the debate, the republic, the war, and the settlement in law), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task: arguing the lesson question from the documents).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to state what the Treaty of Paris decided about the Philippines, what the United States paid, and who was not heard at Paris; explain why a 57-to-27 Senate vote was in fact decided by a single vote; name the Anti-Imperialist League's unlikely coalition and separate the three different reasons its members opposed annexation; describe the Philippine Republic that existed before ratification; hold the Benevolent Assimilation proclamation's promise and its threat in the same reading; follow the war from set battles to guerrilla warfare and state its methods and its costs, with the civilian figure given as an estimate; and explain what the Insular Cases decided and what civil government under Taft did and did not change.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening, question, and map (slides 1–3) 6 minutes; a treaty and a vote (slides 4–7) 8 minutes; the League against empire (slides 8–10) 7 minutes; the Philippine Republic (slides 11–13) 7 minutes; benevolent assimilation and war (slides 14–19) 12 minutes; courts and civil government (slides 20–23) 6 minutes; vocabulary and the turn to work (slides 24–26) 4 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. The exit ticket has three parts — what McKinley promised and what he threatened in the same proclamation, why 57 to 27 was a one-vote decision, and one piece of evidence for each side of the lesson question.

Key vocabulary

Philippine-American War · anti-imperialism · Emilio Aguinaldo · guerrilla warfare · Benevolent Assimilation · Insular Cases · civil government

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line "Imperialism and World War I · Lesson 3"; the title "An Empire in the Philippines"; the standfirst — "A treaty signed in Paris, a republic proclaimed at Malolos, and a war over consent."

  • Picture: Filipino prisoners of war at Pasig, photographed in 1899 and published by Underwood and Underwood as a stereograph; the Library of Congress holds the copy this crop comes from. It shows captured Filipino soldiers under guard in the opening year of the war, seated in rows while American soldiers stand over them.
  • Say what the photograph is and is not. It does not show a battle, and students should not read it as one.
  • [Use it to set the puzzle of the hour.] In December 1898 the United States bought the Philippine Islands from Spain for 20 million dollars.
  • By the time this photograph was taken, American soldiers were fighting the very people who had proclaimed their own republic the year before.
  • How a purchase in Paris became a war in Luzon, and what that war cost, is the lesson.

Slide 2 · The lesson question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type — "Did taking the Philippines extend American ideals or contradict them?"; the unit question in italic beneath it — "Why did the United States seek power overseas, and how did the First World War change the country that fought it?"

  • Put the lesson question at the center of the hour and keep it visible, because the exit ticket and the writing task both answer it directly.
  • It is a genuine two-sided question, and the lesson is built to give each side its strongest evidence.
  • The extension case rests on the promises: just government in place of arbitrary rule, schools, courts, an elected assembly.
  • The contradiction case rests on the record: a treaty signed without Filipinos, a republic treated as an insurrection, rule without consent, and a war whose civilian deaths are estimated at as many as 200,000.
  • The unit question underneath asks why the United States sought power overseas at all, and this lesson gives the fullest answer in the unit: markets, strategy, claimed duty, and a debate the country nearly voted the other way.
  • [Tell students their job today is not to pick a side early.] It is to collect the evidence for both.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "An empire decided, debated, and fought over"; the five rows — A, A Treaty and a Vote (Paris, December 1898; the Senate decides by one vote); B, The League Against Empire (Boston organizes the case against annexation); C, The Philippine Republic (independence at Kawit; a constitution at Malolos); D, Benevolent Assimilation and War (a promise of friendship, then three years of war); E, Courts and Civil Government (the Insular Cases, Taft, and an assembly in 1907).

  • This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section.
  • We start in Paris, where Spain and the United States settled the Philippines between themselves, and in Washington, where the Senate approved that settlement by the narrowest margin the Constitution allows.
  • Then the organized opposition: the American Anti-Imperialist League and the unlikely coalition behind it.
  • Section C turns the camera around and looks at the same months from the Filipino side: a revolt against Spain, a proclaimed independence, a written constitution, a republic.
  • Section D puts the American promise and the American war side by side, including the methods used and the deaths that followed.
  • Section E closes with the law and the government the United States built on the islands it kept.
  • By the end, students hold every piece of evidence the writing task asks them to weigh.

Slide 4 · Section A: A Treaty and a Vote (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section A"; the title "A Treaty and a Vote"; the lines — "The treaty was signed in Paris on December 10, 1898." and "The Senate approved it by a margin of one vote."

  • Picture: The same Pasig prisoners image from the title slide, cropped for this slot: Filipino prisoners of war in 1899, from the Underwood and Underwood stereograph held by the Library of Congress.
  • It belongs at the head of this section for a reason students should hear stated plainly. The treaty and the vote came first, and the prisoners came after.
  • Every document in this section was produced by men who never saw the Philippines: diplomats in Paris trading islands they did not live on, senators in Washington arguing about people most had never met.
  • The Filipino envoy who traveled to both capitals to plead for independence was refused a hearing in each.
  • [Keep that sequence in front of the class through the next three slides:] first the signatures, then the single vote, then the war the signatures and the vote set in motion.

Slide 5 · A treaty signed in Paris

On the slide: five bullets — Spain and the United States signed the treaty on December 10, 1898. Spain gave up Cuba, Puerto Rico, and Guam. Spain ceded the Philippines for 20 million dollars. No Filipino signed the treaty, and no Filipino was heard at Paris. Officials refused envoy Felipe Agoncillo a hearing in Paris and Washington.

  • [Work the guided notes blanks in order,] because the first three answers all sit in this one paragraph of the reading.
  • The Treaty of Paris, signed December 10, 1898, ended the Spanish-American War. Spain gave up Cuba, Puerto Rico, and Guam, and ceded the Philippine Islands to the United States for 20 million dollars.
  • Then slow down on the absence at the center of the document. No Filipino signed the treaty or was heard at Paris.
  • The Philippine envoy Felipe Agoncillo traveled to Paris and then to Washington to argue for independence, and officials in both capitals refused to receive him.
  • The reading closes the section with the contrast students should copy into their thinking: the treaty settled the islands between two empires, while their people were building a government of their own.
  • Section C shows that government. First, the treaty had to survive the Senate.

Slide 6 · The debate in the Senate

On the slide: five bullets — Ratification needed two thirds of the Senate. Expansionists promised markets and a coaling station on the route to China. Expansionists claimed a duty to govern people they said could not govern themselves. Expansionists warned that Germany or Japan would seize the islands. Opponents answered that rule without consent breaks the founding principle.

  • Through January 1899 the outcome stayed in doubt, and students should understand both cases at full strength before they see the vote.
  • The expansionist case had three parts. The Philippines offered markets and a coaling station on the route to China, a practical argument about trade and steamships.
  • Supporters claimed a duty to govern people they argued could not govern themselves, a claim Section C tests against the Malolos constitution.
  • And they warned that Germany or Japan would seize the islands if America let them go, which was a real strategic fear in 1899, not an invention.
  • The opposition case was constitutional. Ruling millions of people without their consent broke the founding principle of the United States, the principle of the Declaration of Independence itself.
  • Neither side was arguing about whether the islands had value. They were arguing about what the republic was allowed to do with its power.

Slide 7 · 57 to 27: the ratification vote

On the slide: the big figure "57 TO 27"; the label — "Senate vote to ratify the Treaty of Paris, February 6, 1899"; the note — "One vote more than the two thirds required. One senator switching sides defeats the treaty."

  • 57 to 27 — one vote more than the two thirds required
  • [Dwell on the arithmetic,] because the exit ticket tests it and students reliably get it wrong on first reading.
  • Ratification required two thirds of the Senate. Eighty-four votes were cast on February 6, 1899, and two thirds of 84 is 56. The treaty received 57 votes, one vote more than required.
  • Ask: The count looks one-sided, 57 to 27 — what happens if one supporter switches sides? The count falls to 56 to 28, the two-thirds line is missed, and the treaty fails.
  • Everything after this slide in the lesson — the war, the court cases, the civil government — rests on that margin.
  • Two days before the vote, on February 4, fighting had already begun outside Manila, and senators voted knowing American and Filipino soldiers were shooting at each other.
  • The closeness of the vote is the first evidence students should log for the writing task: annexation was not a national consensus. It was a contested choice, made by the narrowest constitutional margin.

Slide 8 · Section B: The League Against Empire (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section B"; the title "The League Against Empire"; the lines — "The opposition organized in Boston in 1898." and "A Puck cartoon taught empire as a schoolroom."

  • Picture: School Begins, drawn by Louis Dalrymple and published in Puck magazine on January 25, 1899, while the Senate debate was still running; the Library of Congress holds the print. Uncle Sam stands as a schoolmaster before four children labeled Philippines, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and Cuba, while other students, including a Black child, a Native American child, and Chinese and Mexican figures, watch from the room.
  • [Name the problem in the image directly, the way the quick check does:] the cartoon draws its Filipino, Hawaiian, Puerto Rican, and Cuban figures as racial caricatures, the standard style of American political cartooning in 1899.
  • That is not a reason to look away from it. It is evidence about how annexation was sold and pictured at home, and students will analyze this same cartoon in the quick check.
  • This section studies the Americans who fought the treaty anyway.

Slide 9 · A coalition across American life

On the slide: the photograph at right; five bullets — The American Anti-Imperialist League organized in Boston in 1898. Former president Grover Cleveland and diplomat Carl Schurz led it. Industrialist Andrew Carnegie offered 20 million dollars for Philippine independence. Labor leader Samuel Gompers stood in the same coalition. Mark Twain joined its leadership from 1901.

  • Picture: School Begins again, the Dalrymple cartoon from Puck, January 25, 1899. Here its work is to show the public argument the League was answering: empire pictured at home as a schoolroom, with the new possessions seated as children.
  • The American Anti-Imperialist League, organized in Boston in 1898, coordinated the opposition to annexation, and its membership list is the fastest way to show students how wide that opposition ran.
  • Its leaders ranged from former president Grover Cleveland and the diplomat Carl Schurz to the industrialist Andrew Carnegie, who offered 20 million dollars of his own money for Philippine independence — the exact sum the United States had just paid Spain.
  • Labor leader Samuel Gompers stood in the same coalition, and Mark Twain joined its leadership from 1901.
  • [Pause on the strangeness of that lineup.] A former president, a steel magnate, a union chief, and the most famous writer in the country agreed on almost nothing else in public life. They agreed on this vote, and the League made anti-imperialism a national argument.
  • The next slide separates what they agreed on from why, because the coalition held together on the conclusion while its members reasoned from opposite premises.

Slide 10 · One vote, three different reasons

On the slide: four bullets — Some argued from the Declaration of Independence: governments rest on the consent of the governed. Others feared competition from Filipino workers. Still others objected to governing people they deemed racially unfit for citizenship. The October 1899 platform called subjugation "criminal aggression."

  • [Teach the disagreement inside the League honestly,] because students respect the lesson more when it refuses to tidy the past.
  • Some members argued from the Declaration of Independence: governments rest on the consent of the governed, so ruling the Philippines without Filipino consent was wrong in principle, whatever it paid.
  • Others feared competition from Filipino labor, an economic argument about wages at home.
  • Still others objected to governing people they deemed racially unfit for citizenship, which means part of the anti-imperialist coalition opposed empire from racial prejudice, not from respect for Filipino rights.
  • The League's platform of October 1899 called the subjugation of any people criminal aggression, the phrase the guided notes test.
  • Flag the date for students: the platform came eight months after the February Senate vote, so it states the League's settled position during the war, not the argument that nearly beat the treaty.

Slide 11 · Section C: The Philippine Republic (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section C"; the title "The Philippine Republic"; the lines — "Independence was proclaimed at Kawit on June 12, 1898." and "A republic was inaugurated at Malolos on January 23, 1899."

  • Picture: Emilio Aguinaldo at about 1898. Students deserve the disclosure the printed documents carry: the surviving scan, published in 1899 in the book War in the Philippines and held through Wikimedia Commons, is small, 209 by 299 pixels, so the portrait prints and projects soft by design. No sharper period portrait was available to use honestly.
  • Aguinaldo was twenty-nine years old in the year of this portrait, a revolutionary who had already led one revolt against Spain, accepted exile, and returned to fight again.
  • Everything in this section follows from taking him and his government seriously as political actors rather than as background to an American debate.
  • While Washington argued over the treaty, Filipinos proclaimed independence, wrote a constitution, seated a congress, and inaugurated a republic.
  • The question Section C answers is simple: what exactly did the United States refuse to recognize?

Slide 12 · Aguinaldo and the revolt

On the slide: the photograph at right; five bullets — Filipinos had been fighting Spain since 1896. Aguinaldo returned from exile in May 1898 on an American naval vessel. The United States encouraged the revolt but never promised independence in writing. Aguinaldo proclaimed independence at Kawit on June 12, 1898. Filipino forces held most of Luzon outside Manila.

  • Picture: The Aguinaldo portrait again, at about 1898. The man in the photograph was leading a revolt against Spain two years before the United States entered the war, and he returned to it with American help.
  • [Give the chronology carefully,] because the moral weight of the section sits inside it.
  • Filipinos had been fighting Spain since 1896, two years before the United States entered the picture.
  • Their leader, Emilio Aguinaldo — in the guided notes definition, the Filipino revolutionary leader who proclaimed Philippine independence in 1898 and served as president of the Philippine Republic — returned from exile in May 1898 on an American naval vessel and resumed the revolt with American encouragement.
  • Hold students to the reading's exact qualification: the United States never promised independence in writing.
  • On June 12, 1898, Aguinaldo proclaimed Philippine independence at Kawit, and Filipino forces took control of most of Luzon outside Manila.
  • By the time Spain signed the islands away in Paris that December, the Spain being paid for them no longer controlled most of the largest island. The United States had bought a claim. The Filipinos held the ground.

Slide 13 · A government of their own

On the slide: five bullets — A congress at Malolos wrote a constitution. The Philippine Republic was inaugurated on January 23, 1899. Aguinaldo served as its president. American officials called the republic an insurrection. That word claimed an authority Filipinos had never accepted.

  • Two weeks before the Senate ratified the treaty, the Philippine Republic already existed in working form.
  • A congress at Malolos wrote a constitution, and on January 23, 1899, the republic was inaugurated with Aguinaldo as president.
  • That sequence answers the expansionist claim from Section A directly. Senators had argued that Filipinos could not govern themselves; at Malolos, Filipinos were governing themselves, with a constitution, a congress, a capital, and an army in the field.
  • American officials treated the republic as an insurrection against American authority. [Have students sit with that word.]
  • An insurrection is a revolt against a government a people has accepted. Filipinos had never accepted American sovereignty; Spain had sold a title it could not deliver, and the republic's leaders said so in writing.
  • Whether a government can be an insurrection against rulers it never consented to is the constitutional heart of the lesson question, and Aguinaldo's protest in the next section makes the argument from his own position.

Slide 14 · Section D: Benevolent Assimilation and War (divider)

On the slide: the section label "Section D"; the title "Benevolent Assimilation and War"; the line — "A proclamation promising friendship, a warning of force, and a war that ran to 1902."

  • This section holds the lesson's central tension in a single document and a single war.
  • On December 21, 1898, President McKinley issued the proclamation that named American policy Benevolent Assimilation. It promised friendship and just government. It also ordered military rule over the whole archipelago and warned that resistance would meet the strong arm of authority.
  • Six weeks later the shooting started, two days before the Senate vote.
  • The war that followed lasted, by the official American declaration, until July 4, 1902, and it changed character within its first year, from set battles between armies to a guerrilla struggle across the islands.
  • Methods hardened as the war lengthened, and the costs fell heaviest, by a wide margin, on Filipino civilians.
  • [Teach the promise and the war together, in the same breath,] because the writing task asks students to judge the distance between them.

Slide 15 · Benevolent Assimilation

On the slide: five bullets — McKinley issued the proclamation on December 21, 1898. The United States came, it promised, not as invaders or conquerors, but as friends. It promised the mild sway of justice and right in place of arbitrary rule. It ordered military government extended over the whole archipelago. It warned that resistance would meet the strong arm of authority.

  • [Read the proclamation the way the exit ticket will: one document, two voices.]
  • The first voice is the promise. Control of the islands had been ceded to the United States, McKinley announced, and the United States came not as invaders or conquerors, but as friends.
  • He named the mission Benevolent Assimilation — in the guided notes definition, the name President McKinley gave his Philippine policy in his proclamation of December 21, 1898, claiming that American rule would substitute just government for arbitrary rule.
  • The second voice is the order inside the same proclamation. Military government would extend over the whole archipelago, and resistance would meet the strong arm of authority.
  • Students should hear that both voices are authentic and neither cancels the other. The promise described the intention officials stated. The order described what would happen to any Filipino who acted on the independence proclaimed at Kawit.
  • The next slide shows how the man the proclamation was aimed at actually received it.

Slide 16 · How Aguinaldo learned the terms

On the slide: five bullets — General Otis sent Aguinaldo a copy with the sovereignty claims removed. The unedited text reached Aguinaldo anyway. Aguinaldo protested on January 5, 1899. He had never agreed to American sovereignty. A sentry fired on February 4, 1899, and open war began.

  • This episode is small, documented, and worth teaching slowly.
  • General Elwell Otis, commanding in Manila, sent Aguinaldo a copy of the proclamation with the claims of sovereignty removed, apparently hoping the softened text would keep the peace while Washington decided.
  • The unedited text reached Aguinaldo anyway. On January 5, 1899, he protested in writing: he had never agreed to American sovereignty, and he had returned from exile to fight for independence, the cause he had been fighting since 1896.
  • [Students should weigh what the editing itself reveals.] The commanding general judged that the proclamation's real terms, read plainly, would provoke the resistance it claimed to welcome as friendship.
  • On February 4, 1899, two days before the Senate vote, an American sentry shot at Filipino soldiers near Manila, and open war began.
  • Neither capital voted for that first shot. Both governments had spent two months making it likely.

Slide 17 · War, then guerrilla war

On the slide: five bullets — American forces won the early set battles and took Malolos, the capital. The United States sent about 126,000 soldiers during the war. Aguinaldo disbanded his regular army in November 1899. His forces turned to guerrilla warfare: small bands that ambush, raid, and disperse. Aguinaldo was captured in March 1901, and fighting ran to July 4, 1902.

  • The war had two phases, and the second explains the methods on the next slide.
  • American forces won the early set battles and took the capital, Malolos. In November 1899 Aguinaldo disbanded his regular army and ordered guerrilla warfare — in the guided notes definition, fighting by small, mobile bands that ambush, raid, and disperse instead of meeting an enemy army in open battle.
  • The Philippine-American War, again in the definition students copy, is the war fought from 1899 to 1902 between the United States and the Philippine Republic over American annexation of the Philippines.
  • About 126,000 American soldiers sent during the war
  • Troops captured Aguinaldo in March 1901; he swore allegiance to the United States and called for peace.
  • Resistance continued anyway, until President Theodore Roosevelt declared the war over on July 4, 1902. Name the date's symbolism without leaning on it: independence declared ended, on Independence Day, by the occupying power.

Slide 18 · Methods and costs

On the slide: the photograph at right; five bullets — American troops burned villages and crops. Reconcentration camps in Batangas filled, and disease spread quickly. Interrogators used the water cure on prisoners. Filipino fighters killed 48 Americans at Balangiga in September 1901. General Jacob Smith ordered Samar made a howling wilderness and was court-martialed.

  • Picture: Filipino prisoners of war at Pasig, 1899, the Underwood and Underwood stereograph. The men in rows are the human material of this slide: a war against an army that had dissolved into the population turned prisoners, camps, and villages into its battlegrounds.
  • [Do not rush this slide, and do not soften its contents.]
  • Guerrilla war blurred the line between soldier and civilian, and American methods hardened in response. Troops burned villages and crops.
  • In Batangas, much of the population was forced into guarded reconcentration camps, where disease spread quickly among crowded people short of food and clean water.
  • Interrogators used the water cure, forcing water into a prisoner until he talked.
  • After Filipino fighters killed 48 Americans at Balangiga, on Samar, in September 1901, General Jacob Smith ordered his men to kill and burn and make Samar a howling wilderness.
  • Smith was court-martialed and retired, which students should note in both directions: the order was given by a general, and the army did punish it, after it was given.
  • The next slide puts the human total on the screen. Insist, there and everywhere, that the civilian figure is an estimate, because no one counted.

Slide 19 · 200,000 estimated Filipino civilian deaths

On the slide: the big figure "200,000"; the label — "estimated Filipino civilian deaths"; the note — "An estimate, not a count. Some estimates run higher."

  • 200,000 estimated Filipino civilian deaths — an estimate, not a count
  • [Give this number its full weight and its full qualification, in that order.]
  • The Office of the Historian gives more than 4,200 American dead and more than 20,000 Filipino combatants dead.
  • More than 4,200 American dead · More than 20,000 Filipino combatants dead
  • For civilians, it gives an estimate of as many as 200,000 Filipino civilian deaths from violence, famine, and disease. That figure is an estimate, not a count, and the reading says so in plain words; some estimates run higher.
  • No authority registered civilian deaths during the war. The total is reconstructed from population records across hundreds of islands over three years, counting people who died in burned villages, in reconcentration camps, and in the epidemics and hunger that followed destroyed crops.
  • Ask: Students sometimes discount a number because it is estimated — why is the estimate the honest form of this fact? Because no government office was counting Filipino villagers; the uncertainty is part of the record, not a weakness in it.
  • The writing task expects students to state the figure as an estimate wherever they use it.

Slide 20 · Section E: Courts and Civil Government (divider)

On the slide: the section label "Section E"; the title "Courts and Civil Government"; the line — "What the new possessions were in law, and who governed them in practice."

  • The war answered who held the islands by force. This section answers the two questions force could not settle.
  • The first was legal: what were Puerto Rico and the Philippines inside the American constitutional order — places the United States owned but had not made states and did not intend, in the near term, to let go?
  • The Supreme Court took that question in the Insular Cases beginning in 1901, while the war was still being fought.
  • The second question was practical: who would govern day to day, soldiers or civilians, and with what Filipino participation?
  • The answer came in stages: a civil governor in 1901, an organic act in 1902, an elected assembly in 1907, and final authority kept in American hands throughout, with no date set for independence.
  • [Hold both answers against the lesson question as you teach.] Each is evidence, for opposite sides of it.

Slide 21 · The Insular Cases

On the slide: five bullets — The cases began with Downes v. Bidwell in 1901. The territories were not foreign countries, but they were not fully part of the United States. Justice White called such territory foreign to the United States in a domestic sense. The rulings created a new category: the unincorporated territory. The territory belonged to the United States, and the Constitution applied only in part.

  • Define the term from the guided notes first: the Insular Cases are the Supreme Court decisions beginning in 1901 that held the new island territories were not fully part of the United States and that the Constitution did not apply there in full.
  • In the first of them, Downes v. Bidwell in 1901, the Court held that Puerto Rico and the Philippines were not foreign countries, but neither were they fully part of the United States.
  • Justice Edward White, concurring, described such a territory as foreign to the United States in a domestic sense, a phrase students find strange until they see the category it built.
  • The rulings created the unincorporated territory: belonging to the United States, not on the road to statehood, and under the Constitution only in part.
  • Put the consequence plainly. The same flag now flew over two kinds of ground: ground where the Constitution followed in full, and ground where it followed only as far as Congress and the Court allowed.

Slide 22 · Civil government

On the slide: five bullets — William Howard Taft became civil governor on July 4, 1901. His commission built schools, courts, and a civil service, with English as the language of instruction. The Philippine Organic Act of 1902 confirmed civil government. An elected Philippine assembly met in 1907. Final authority stayed American, and no date for independence was set.

  • Close the content with the definition in use: civil government is the rule of a territory by civilian officials acting under law, in place of direct military rule.
  • Military rule gave way by stages. On July 4, 1901, William Howard Taft became civil governor, heading a commission building schools, courts, and a civil service, with English as the language of instruction.
  • The Philippine Organic Act of 1902 confirmed civil government and promised an assembly, which met in 1907. Filipinos held local offices and sat in that assembly.
  • [Then give the limit its full sentence,] because it is the fact the writing task turns on: an American governor general kept final authority, and no date for independence was set.
  • The islands remained a colony, governed in the name of preparing their people for a self-government they had proclaimed in 1899.
  • Students now have the whole arc, from the Paris signatures to the 1907 assembly, and the argument is theirs to judge.

Slide 23 · The argument at a glance

On the slide: four bullets — Annex: the islands offered markets, a station on the route to China, and a claimed duty to govern. Reject: governing without consent violates the Declaration of Independence. Independence: the Filipinos had already built a republic, with a constitution and a capital. Underneath all three positions sits one question: whose consent counts?

  • [Consolidate the three positions before the vocabulary slides,] because the guided notes table, the notebook page, and the simulation all organize the lesson this way.
  • The annexation case, at its strongest: the islands offered markets and a station on the route to China, and their rulers claimed a duty to govern people they judged unready, pointing to schools, courts, and the promised assembly as the work beginning.
  • The rejection case, at its strongest: governing a people without its consent violates the Declaration of Independence, and the League named subjugation criminal aggression.
  • The independence case, at its strongest: the Filipinos had already formed a republic with a constitution, an army, and a capital, so the question was never whether they could govern, but whether the United States would let them.
  • Have students fill the Argument at a Glance table now, claim and backing document for each row, while all three cases are in front of them.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for the war and the opposition

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — Philippine-American War, anti-imperialism, Emilio Aguinaldo. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Say: "Philippine-American War: the war fought from 1899 to 1902 between the United States and the Philippine Republic over American annexation of the Philippines"
  • Say: "anti-imperialism: opposition to acquiring and ruling overseas colonies, especially on the ground that governing a people without its consent violates republican principles"

Slide 25 · Vocabulary: words for the fighting and the rule that followed

On the slide: four terms with their definitions — guerrilla warfare, Benevolent Assimilation, Insular Cases, civil government. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: "guerrilla warfare: fighting by small, mobile bands that ambush, raid, and disperse instead of meeting an enemy army in open battle"
  • Say: "Benevolent Assimilation: the name President McKinley gave his Philippine policy in his proclamation of December 21, 1898, claiming that American rule would substitute just government for arbitrary rule"
  • Say: "Insular Cases: the Supreme Court decisions beginning in 1901 that held the new island territories were not fully part of the United States and that the Constitution did not apply there in full"
  • Say: "civil government: the rule of a territory by civilian officials acting under law, in place of direct military rule"
  • Four more terms, again matching the guided notes exactly.
  • Guerrilla warfare is the fighting Aguinaldo ordered in November 1899, and the war's hardest methods followed from it.
  • Civil government is the system Taft headed from July 4, 1901.
  • Together the four terms trace the lesson's second half: the fighting, the promise that named it, the law that housed it, and the government that outlasted it.
