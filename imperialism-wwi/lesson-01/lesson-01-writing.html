Writing Task: Why the Turn Overseas

The prompt. Answer the lesson question in a developed essay of about 300 words: Why did the United States look overseas for territory, markets, and influence in the 1890s? A strong answer does more than list motives. It explains how the motives worked together, and it answers the critics rather than ignoring them. Use at least three pieces of evidence from the bank, present the strongest objection from bank item 6 fairly, and explain why the expansionists prevailed or why the objection should have stopped them.

The bank is drawn from the lesson reading and stations. Write in your own sentences. Quotation sources and editions are named in the bank and in the teacher guide.

Evidence Bank

1. In 1890 the Superintendent of the Census reported that a frontier line could hardly be said to exist. Turner, in 1893, argued that free land and a moving frontier had explained American development, and predicted that "the American energy will continually demand a wider field for its exercise."

2. Mahan's The Influence of Sea Power Upon History (1890) joined production, shipping, and colonies and stations into one strategy of sea power. The Naval Act of 1890 authorized the first three American battleships. Steam warships carried limited coal, so fleets needed coaling stations along their routes, as at Pearl Harbor (rights obtained in 1887) and Pago Pago (rights obtained in 1878).

3. Farms and factories produced a surplus the home market could not buy, and the depression that began in 1893 closed mills and dropped prices. Beveridge stated the case in 1898: Americans were "raising more than we can consume" and "making more than we can use," and therefore had to find "new markets for our produce, new occupation for our capital, new work for our labor."

4. Josiah Strong's Our Country (1885) taught that the Anglo-Saxon race represented civil liberty and a pure spiritual Christianity and was being trained by God to spread its institutions over the earth. Missionaries had worked in Hawaii since 1820, and writers of the period ranked peoples and assigned stronger nations a duty to govern weaker ones.

5. In Hawaii, reciprocity (1875) admitted sugar duty free, businessmen overthrew Queen Liliuokalani in January 1893 with American backing, and President Cleveland withdrew the annexation treaty after concluding the overthrow had been wrongful. In Cuba, rebellion began in 1895 and Spain's reconcentration policy killed civilians in very large numbers.

6. The critics answered from the start. Schurz argued in 1893 that Americans could own plantations, build railroads, and work mines in the tropics without annexing anything, and warned that annexation would damage the people's character and institutions. Others objected that governing peoples without their consent contradicted the Declaration of Independence.

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • A claim that answers the lesson question directly and treats the turn overseas as the product of several motives working together, not as a single cause and not as an accident.
  • At least three pieces of evidence from the bank, used accurately. The Census report and Turner support the wider field claim. Mahan, the Naval Act of 1890, and the Pearl Harbor and Pago Pago rights support the sea power claim. The surplus, the depression of 1893, and Beveridge support the markets claim. Strong and the missionary presence support the mission claim. Hawaii and Cuba show the motives converging on real places.
  • A fair statement of the objection from bank item 6, in the critics' own terms: trade and investment without annexation, damage to the people's character and institutions, or rule without consent. A sentence that names the critics without stating their argument has not met this requirement.
  • An answer to the objection, or a reasoned concession of it. Strong answers explain why expansionists found the objection unpersuasive, or show it working, as in Cleveland's refusal in Hawaii, and then being set aside.
  • Explanation that connects the evidence to the claim. A paragraph that lists three motives without saying how they reinforced each other has assembled evidence without making the argument, and should earn partial credit.

Quotation sources for teacher reference. Turner: "The Significance of the Frontier in American History" (1893), Annual Report of the American Historical Association for 1893. Beveridge: "March of the Flag," Indianapolis, September 16, 1898, quoted from the TPSP bank text (1898 Indianapolis pamphlet, via Teaching American History); the phrases appear in the bank as separate short quotations from two passages of the speech. Strong is summarized in bank item 4 and Schurz in bank item 6; the verbatim passages for both are in the activity, from Our Country, revised edition (American Home Missionary Society, 1891), and "Manifest Destiny," Harper's New Monthly Magazine, October 1893, reprinted in Speeches, Correspondence and Political Papers of Carl Schurz, vol. 5 (1913), Wikisource transcription.

Model opening for teacher reference. The United States looked overseas in the 1890s because several arguments that had been building for a decade suddenly pointed in the same direction. Turner gave the turn a past: if free land and a moving frontier had made the American character, then the Census report of 1890 announced a national problem, and his prediction that American energy would demand a wider field told readers where the energy would go. Mahan gave the turn a machine: production and shipping required stations and a battle fleet, and Congress authorized the first three battleships in the year his book appeared. A full model continues in this pattern, adding the surplus evidence and Strong, then stating Schurz's objection fairly before answering it or conceding it.

Common weak answers. Motive lists name the causes without explaining how they reinforced each other or why they persuaded people in the 1890s rather than earlier. Single cause answers cannot explain Hawaii, where sugar, a harbor, and missionary descendants converged. Answers that omit the critics have written the expansionists' pamphlet rather than the history, because the debate is part of the evidence.
