Guided Notes: The Turn Overseas

Lesson question: Why did the United States look overseas for territory, markets, and influence in the 1890s?

Unit question: Why did the United States seek power overseas, and how did the First World War change the country that fought it?

Key Terms

imperialism: a policy of extending a nation's power by taking territory or control over other peoples and lands

navalism: the policy of making a powerful navy the foundation of national strength and overseas influence

coaling station: a port where steamships stop to load coal, water, and supplies on a long voyage

annexation: the formal act by which a country adds territory to itself

jingoism: boastful and aggressive patriotism that urges war or expansion against other nations

frontier thesis: Frederick Jackson Turner's argument that free land and a moving frontier explained American development and shaped the national character

protectorate: a territory controlled and protected by a stronger nation

Hawaii as evidence. A supporter: sugar entered the American market duty free, Pearl Harbor gave the navy its Pacific station, and a friendly government in Honolulu secured both. An opponent: an independent queen was overthrown with American backing and American troops, and the president himself concluded the overthrow had been wrongful. Accept either pairing when the fact cited is accurate.

The Motives at a Glance, completed. The wider field: the frontier made the American character, and its closing called for a new field overseas. Sea power: production, shipping, and stations joined by a battle fleet; a steel navy and coaling stations on world routes. Markets: a surplus the home market could not buy; foreign markets, above all in China. Mission: Anglo-Saxons represented liberty and Christianity and were trained to spread their institutions; governing and uplifting peoples judged weaker. The critics: trade did not require annexation; commerce without colonies, no rule without consent, no large armaments.

Exit ticket 1. The Census reported that a frontier line could hardly be said to exist, which let Turner and others argue that the movement that had formed the country had ended and that American energy would look for a wider field.

Exit ticket 2. Any two, correctly paired. Sea power, argued by Mahan. Markets for surplus production, argued by Beveridge and by farm and business groups. Mission, argued by Strong and the missionary societies. The wider field after the frontier, drawn from Turner.

Exit ticket 3. Accept any answer that combines at least two motives with accurate facts and names one objection, such as Schurz on armaments and institutions, labor on wages, or the consent argument from the Declaration of Independence.
