The Turn Overseas

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A Continent Filled

For most of the nineteenth century, American expansion meant moving west. Settlers, soldiers, and railroads carried the authority of the United States to the Pacific, taking land from Native nations and from Mexico as they went. By 1890 the Superintendent of the Census reported that settlement had so broken into the unsettled area that a frontier line could hardly be said to exist any longer.

In 1893 the historian Frederick Jackson Turner turned that report into an argument, claiming that free land and a continually moving frontier explained American development, and that frontier life had produced the energy, individualism, and democratic habits of the American character. His closing sentence warned that the first period of that history was over. Some listeners drew a conclusion Turner stated only in general terms. A people trained by movement would demand a wider field, and the wider field lay overseas.

Sea Power and the New Navy

Captain Alfred Thayer Mahan gave that conclusion a strategy. In The Influence of Sea Power Upon History (1890), Mahan argued that greatness rested on sea power, which joined three things together. A nation needed production at home, shipping to carry its products to distant markets, and colonies and naval stations that supported the shipping. A country that produced more than it consumed needed the sea, commanded by a battle fleet.

Steel warships made stations necessary, because a steam fleet burned coal and carried only a limited supply. Mahan therefore urged a modern navy and stations along the routes its ships would travel. Congress was already listening. The Naval Act of 1890 authorized the first three American battleships, and a steel fleet began to replace the wooden navy of an earlier era.

More Than the Home Market Could Buy

The economic argument began with surplus. Farms and factories now produced more wheat, cotton, steel, and machinery than American buyers could purchase. In the depression that began in 1893, mills closed and prices fell. Farmers, manufacturers, and merchants argued that the country had to sell abroad or choke on what it made.

Asia drew the most attention. Americans spoke of China as a market of hundreds of millions of customers, an estimate of its population that made even a small share of its trade worth fortunes to American mills. Reaching it required coaling stations on the route and warships to protect the trade. Senator Albert Beveridge stated the case in 1898. Americans, he said, were raising more than they could consume and making more than they could use, and therefore had to find new markets for their produce.

A Mission Beyond the Borders

Other Americans described expansion as a duty rather than a bargain. Protestant missionaries had sailed to Hawaii in 1820 and later to China and Japan, and missionary societies sent money and workers across the Pacific. The minister Josiah Strong gave that argument its widest audience. His book Our Country, published in 1885, taught that the Anglo-Saxon race represented two great ideas, civil liberty and a pure spiritual Christianity, and that God was training that race to spread its institutions over the earth.

Strong expressed ideas about race that many white Americans shared. Writers ranked the peoples of the world, placed English speaking Protestants at the top, and concluded that stronger nations had a duty to govern peoples they judged weaker. Such ideas let expansion present itself as service. The missionary argument and the market argument pointed in the same direction, and each made the other easier to accept.

Hawaii: The Drive Becomes a Fact

Hawaii showed where the arguments could lead. American missionaries had arrived in 1820; their descendants became sugar planters and advisers. A reciprocity treaty in 1875 let Hawaiian sugar enter the United States duty free. When the treaty was renewed in 1887, the United States gained the exclusive right to use Pearl Harbor as a naval station. The islands were becoming an American sugar field and coaling stop while remaining independent.

Queen Liliuokalani came to the throne in 1891 and moved to restore the authority of the monarchy. In January 1893 a committee of American and European businessmen, backed by the American minister and by troops landed from an American warship, overthrew her government. The provisional government that replaced it asked Washington for annexation. President Grover Cleveland investigated, concluded that the overthrow had been wrongful, and withdrew the annexation treaty. The planters declared a republic and waited.

Cuba: The Crisis That Turned Talk Into War

Cuba supplied the emergency. The island lay ninety miles from Florida, held sugar plantations in which Americans had invested heavily, and had rebelled against Spanish rule before. In 1895 Cubans rose again. Spain answered with a policy of reconcentration, forcing rural Cubans into guarded towns where disease and hunger killed civilians in very large numbers. American newspapers printed shocking accounts of the policy, and many readers sympathized with a rebellion against a European empire. Cuba turned a decade of talk toward war, and war in 1898 would put territory in American hands.

The Argument From the Start

None of this went unopposed. Carl Schurz warned in 1893 that Americans could trade with the tropics without annexing anything. Taking tropical peoples into the Union, he argued, would burden the republic with large armaments and damage its institutions. Labor leaders feared that colonial goods and labor would undercut American wages. Other opponents objected that governing people without their consent contradicted the Declaration of Independence, while some opposed annexation because they did not want island peoples admitted to citizenship. Expansion was an argument Americans made to each other, and in the 1890s the argument was still open.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What did the Census of 1890 report about the frontier, and what did Turner argue the frontier had done? The Superintendent of the Census reported in 1890 that settlement had so broken into the unsettled area that a frontier line could hardly be said to exist. Turner argued in his 1893 paper, "The Significance of the Frontier in American History," that the existence of free land and its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward, explained American development, and that frontier life had produced the energy, individualism, and democratic habits he treated as the national character. His closing sentence, quoted verbatim in the activity, is from the paper as published in the Annual Report of the American Historical Association for 1893 (Washington, 1894).

2. What three things did Mahan join together in sea power, and why did a steam navy make overseas stations necessary? Production, shipping, and colonies and naval stations that facilitated and protected shipping. A steam fleet burned coal and carried only a limited supply, so warships on distant service had to stop to refuel, take on water and supplies, and make repairs. Without stations along a route, a fleet could not stay on that route.

3. How did the surplus argument work? Farms and factories produced more than American buyers could purchase, which expansionists treated as the cause of falling prices and closed mills in depressions such as the one that began in 1893. The remedy was foreign markets, above all in Asia, secured by coaling stations, a protective navy, and government pressure to keep trade doors open. Beveridge's 1898 statement, from "March of the Flag" (Indianapolis, September 16, 1898; text as carried in the TPSP bank, from the 1898 Indianapolis pamphlet via Teaching American History), is quoted in the reading in indirect form and verbatim in the quick check: Americans were raising more than they could consume and making more than they could use, so they had to find new markets for their produce, new occupation for their capital, and new work for their labor.

4. How did the missionary argument and the market argument make each other easier to accept? Strong's teaching presented expansion as a duty and a service, which gave the search for markets a moral purpose beyond profit. In Hawaii the two arguments had the same carriers: missionaries arrived in 1820, and their descendants became the sugar planters who profited from the 1875 reciprocity treaty, gained Pearl Harbor rights in 1887, and led the overthrow of Liliuokalani in 1893. Accept answers that connect Strong's claim of mission to any one of these facts and explain the connection.

5. Why did the United States look overseas in the 1890s? Full credit answers combine at least three motives and name a serious objection. The motives are the closed frontier and the wider field, Mahan's sea power strategy and the steel navy's need for coaling stations, the search for markets for surplus production, and the missionary and racial claim of a duty to spread American institutions. Hawaii shows the motives converging, and Cuba supplied the crisis. The strongest objections are Schurz's argument that trade did not require annexation and that colonies would burden the republic and damage its institutions, and the consent argument from the Declaration of Independence. Answers that list motives without the critics have told only the expansionists' half of the lesson. [Blueprint 1]
