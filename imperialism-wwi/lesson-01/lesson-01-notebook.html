Teacher Answer Key

Motive or voiceThe claimThe evidenceWhat it called for
The wider field (Turner)Free land and a moving frontier explained American development and formed the national character. With the frontier gone, American energy would demand a wider field.The Census of 1890 reported that a frontier line could hardly be said to exist. Turner delivered the frontier thesis in Chicago in 1893.Turner proposed nothing himself. His readers called for a new field overseas, where expansion could continue now that free land at home was finished.
Sea power (Mahan)National greatness rests on sea power, which joins production, shipping, and colonies and stations that protect shipping.Mahan's The Influence of Sea Power Upon History appeared in 1890. The Naval Act of 1890 authorized the first three battleships. Steam warships burned coal and carried a limited supply.A modern battle fleet, protection for commerce, and coaling stations along the routes American ships traveled, at Pearl Harbor, Pago Pago, and beyond.
Markets for surplusFarms and factories produced more than American buyers could purchase, so the surplus had to be sold abroad or production would choke.The depression that began in 1893 closed mills and dropped prices. Beveridge in 1898: Americans were raising more than they could consume and making more than they could use. China was spoken of as a market of hundreds of millions.Foreign markets, above all in Asia, reached by coaling stations and protected by warships, with the government pressing to keep trade doors open.
Mission (Strong)The Anglo-Saxon race represented civil liberty and a pure spiritual Christianity, and God was training it to spread its institutions over the earth.Josiah Strong's Our Country (1885) reached a wide church readership. Missionaries had worked in Hawaii since 1820 and across the Pacific. Writers of the period ranked peoples and assigned the strongest a duty to govern the weaker.Expansion presented as service: governing and uplifting peoples judged weaker, through missions, schools, and, where supporters thought it necessary, American rule.
Hawaii and CubaThe motives converged on real places. Hawaii offered sugar, a harbor, and a station on the route to Asia. Cuba offered a nearby crisis that made intervention feel necessary.Reciprocity in 1875, Pearl Harbor rights in 1887, the overthrow of Liliuokalani in 1893, and Cleveland's refusal to annex. In Cuba, rebellion in 1895 and Spain's reconcentration policy killed civilians in very large numbers.Annexation in Hawaii, pressed by planters and naval advocates and resisted by Cleveland. In Cuba, an end to Spanish rule, by intervention if necessary, which became war in 1898.
The critics (Schurz)Trade and investment did not require annexation, and annexing tropical peoples would burden the republic and damage its institutions.Schurz, "Manifest Destiny" (1893): Americans could own plantations, build railroads, and work mines in the tropics without taking the countries into the Union. Labor feared colonial competition, and others cited government by consent.Commerce without colonies. No rule over peoples without their consent, no large armaments, and no abandonment of the republic's conservative traditions of policy.

The argument in one sentence. Strong answers combine at least two motives and keep the critics in the sentence. The United States looked overseas because the frontier that had absorbed its expansion was reported closed, its farms and factories produced a surplus its home market could not buy, its new steel navy needed coaling stations on the routes to Asia, and its missionary and racial ideas dressed the whole program as a duty, while critics answered that trade needed no colonies and that rule without consent betrayed the republic.
