Americans in the War

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

Raising an Army by Draft

In April 1917 the regular army was small, and volunteers alone could not fill the ranks fast enough. Congress answered with the Selective Service Act of May 1917, which set up conscription, compulsory enrollment for military service, run by local civilian boards. On June 5, 1917, nearly 10 million men registered on a single day. About 24 million men registered in all. About 2.8 million were drafted, and millions more volunteered. Local boards examined each man, classified him, and decided who would go first, with single men without dependents generally called before married men. Draftees then reported to hastily built training camps, where they learned to march and shoot, often with wooden rifles at first because real weapons were scarce.

Crossing the Atlantic in Convoy

An army raised in the United States still had to cross an ocean patrolled by German submarines, called U-boats. The Allied answer was the convoy system, in which troopships and supply ships sailed together in groups under the protection of destroyers and other escort vessels. A U-boat captain who found one convoy faced many guns at once, and one torpedo could sink only one ship in the group. Convoys zigzagged to spoil a submarine aim and ran without lights at night. The system worked. Shipping losses fell sharply after convoys were adopted in 1917. The first small American force had landed in June 1917. By November 1918, about 2 million American soldiers were in France.

Pershing and an Independent Army

The American force in France was the American Expeditionary Forces, commanded by General John J. Pershing. British and French commanders, whose armies had been bled for three years, wanted American soldiers fed into their own units as replacements. Pershing refused. He insisted that Americans would fight as an independent army, under American officers, in their own sector, once trained and present in strength. The argument cost time and caused friction, but it gave the United States its own army and a stronger voice in any peace that followed. While it went on, American divisions trained behind the lines and served quiet stretches in French trenches beside veteran units.

Trench Warfare and New Weapons

What the Doughboys, the nickname for American infantry soldiers, met in France was trench warfare on a scale no American army had seen. Two lines of trenches faced each other across no man's land, open ground cut by shell craters and tangled with wire. Soldiers lived in dugouts below ground and endured mud, rats, and lice. New and improved weapons made attack costly. Machine guns swept open ground, heavy artillery tore the trenches apart, and poison gas forced men to fight in masks. Tanks and aircraft were appearing, but in 1918 they had not yet broken the deadlock by themselves. An attack usually began with an artillery barrage, after which infantry crossed open ground against wire, machine guns, and shell fire.

St. Mihiel and the Meuse-Argonne

In September 1918 the American army fought its first great independent battle at St. Mihiel, a bulge in the German line south of Verdun. About 550,000 American soldiers took part, supported by French troops, tanks, and aircraft. The attack, begun on September 12, pinched out the bulge in about four days against a German force already pulling back. Pershing then moved his army to a harder task. From September 26 until the end of the war, about 1.2 million American soldiers fought in the Meuse-Argonne offensive, the largest battle American forces had fought to that date. The ground was forested and hilly, the roads were clogged, and German defenses were deep. Progress was slow and costly. An estimated 26,000 American soldiers died in the offensive, more than in any other American battle of the war.

Black Soldiers in a Segregated Army

About 370,000 Black Americans served in a segregated army whose leaders doubted them. Most were kept out of combat divisions and assigned to labor units that unloaded ships, built roads, dug trenches, and buried the dead. The work was essential and often dangerous, and it was given to Black soldiers because white officers assumed they were fit for labor rather than combat. The exceptions proved what the policy denied. The 369th Infantry, a Black regiment from New York, fought under French command with French weapons and spent about 191 days in the front line, longer than any other American regiment. France decorated the whole regiment.

Influenza Moves With the Armies

Disease traveled the same routes as the soldiers. An influenza pandemic, a worldwide outbreak of flu, appeared in American camps in the spring of 1918, including Camp Funston in Kansas, and crossed the Atlantic in crowded troopships. A far deadlier wave struck in the autumn, at the height of the fighting. Influenza and the pneumonia that often followed it killed an estimated 675,000 people in the United States, and estimates of deaths worldwide run to at least 50 million. In the American forces, disease killed more soldiers than battle did. Of about 116,500 American deaths in the war, roughly 53,000 came in battle and about 63,000 from disease and other nonbattle causes, most of them from influenza and pneumonia.

The Armistice

By November 1918 Germany was losing its allies, its army was falling back, and its leaders asked for terms. On November 11, 1918, the two sides signed an armistice, an agreement to stop fighting, which took effect at 11 in the morning. Fighting stopped, but no peace treaty had yet been written, and the questions the war had raised, about empires, borders, and a league to keep the peace, were still unanswered.

Teacher Answer Key

1. How did conscription under the Selective Service Act work, and why did Congress choose it instead of relying on volunteers alone? The Selective Service Act of May 1917 required men to register, about 24 million in all, and local civilian boards examined and classified them and decided the order of call, generally single men without dependents first. About 2.8 million men were drafted. Congress chose conscription because the regular army was small and volunteers could not fill the ranks fast enough for a war of this size.

2. Explain how the convoy system protected troopships from U-boats. Troopships and supply ships sailed in groups escorted by destroyers and other warships, so a submarine faced many guns and one torpedo could sink only one ship. Any two features: set routes, zigzag courses to spoil a submarine aim, running without lights at night, naval escort. Losses fell sharply after convoys were adopted in 1917.

3. What did Pershing insist on, and what did the Allies want instead? Pershing insisted the American Expeditionary Forces fight as an independent army under American officers in their own sector once trained and present in strength. British and French commanders wanted Americans fed into their units as replacements a few thousand at a time. It mattered because an independent army gave the United States its own force on the ground and a stronger voice in the peace, at the cost of time and friction with the Allies.

4. Compare St. Mihiel and the Meuse-Argonne. St. Mihiel, September 12 to 16, 1918: about 550,000 American soldiers, supported by French troops, tanks, and aircraft, pinched out the salient in about four days against a German force already pulling back. The Meuse-Argonne, September 26 to November 11: about 1.2 million American soldiers, the largest American battle to that date, fought through forested, hilly ground with deep German defenses and clogged roads, at an estimated cost of 26,000 American dead. St. Mihiel was the first great independent operation and a quick success; the Argonne was larger, much slower, and far costlier.

5. Choose one fact and explain what it shows that a battle account would miss. Disease: of about 116,500 American deaths, roughly 53,000 came in battle and about 63,000 from disease and other nonbattle causes, most from influenza and pneumonia, so mobilization itself, crowded camps, ships, and hospitals, was a cause of death on a scale larger than combat, and the flu pandemic belongs inside the war story, not beside it. Segregation: about 370,000 Black Americans served, but most were assigned to labor units, unloading ships, building roads, and burying the dead, because white officers assumed them fit for labor rather than combat; the 369th Infantry under French command, about 191 days in line and decorated by France, shows both the injustice of the policy and the record Black soldiers built against it.

Figures note for teachers. Registration, draft, and strength figures are the standard rounded figures reported by the Selective Service System and the Army. Death figures follow the standard Army accounting of about 53,000 battle deaths and about 63,000 nonbattle deaths. The Meuse-Argonne death figure and the influenza death figures are estimates and are labeled as estimates in the student text. Worldwide influenza deaths are especially uncertain; estimates run from about 50 million upward.
