Writing Task: How Did the United States Mobilize, and What Did Its Soldiers Meet?

The prompt: How did the United States mobilize, and what did its soldiers meet in France? Write a paragraph that answers both halves of the lesson question. Your paragraph must cover the system that raised and moved the army and the war that army found at the front. Use the scaffold below to build the paragraph before you write the final version.

Step 1: State Your Claim

Write your claim in one sentence. It should name the mobilization system and the kind of war at the front in a single line.

Now write the complication in one sentence: one way the system hurt the very soldiers it was built to move and supply.

Step 2: Harvest the Evidence

For each row, write one specific fact you can use in your paragraph. You need facts from at least two rows, and at least one fact must be a figure.

Step 3: Write the Paragraph

Build your paragraph in this order: claim, first piece of evidence and what it shows, second piece of evidence and what it shows, the complication, closing sentence that answers both halves of the question. Sentence starters you may use: "The draft worked by..." / "At the front, a soldier faced..." / "The same system also..."

Teacher Answer Key and Rubric

Sample paragraph. The United States mobilized by building a system, conscription at home, convoys across the Atlantic, and an independent army in France, and its soldiers met a war of trenches, machine guns, and disease. The Selective Service Act registered about 24 million men and drafted about 2.8 million of them through local civilian boards, and the convoy system then carried about 2 million soldiers to France by November 1918 under naval escort, with shipping losses falling sharply once convoys were adopted. At the front, Pershing kept the American Expeditionary Forces independent, and the Doughboys attacked at St. Mihiel with about 550,000 men and in the Meuse-Argonne with about 1.2 million, crossing no man's land against wire, machine guns, artillery, and gas. The same system also spread influenza through the crowded camps and troopships it depended on, and disease killed about 63,000 American soldiers, more than the about 53,000 killed in battle. The armistice of November 11, 1918, ended a war the mobilization system had made possible and had also made deadlier than the fighting alone.

Scoring rubric. Full credit should be awarded if the student:

  • Answers both halves of the lesson question, mobilization and the war at the front, rather than only one.
  • Uses at least two specific facts from the lesson, drawn from at least two rows of the evidence chart, with at least one figure stated accurately (for example about 24 million registered, about 2.8 million drafted, about 2 million in France, about 550,000 at St. Mihiel, or about 1.2 million in the Meuse-Argonne).
  • Includes the complication: the crowded camps, trains, and ships of the mobilization system spread influenza, so disease deaths (about 63,000) exceeded battle deaths (about 53,000), or the segregated assignment of most Black soldiers to labor while the 369th Infantry fought under French command.
  • Handles at least one accuracy point correctly: an armistice is an agreement to stop fighting, not a peace treaty; Pershing insisted on an independent army rather than replacements for Allied units; or convoys grouped ships under escort rather than sending them alone.

Common weak responses. A paragraph that describes the trenches without the draft, convoys, or camps answers only half the question. A paragraph that gives the influenza figures without connecting them to the crowding the system created treats the pandemic as an accident beside the war. A paragraph that calls the armistice a peace treaty, or Pershing's army a part of the British or French armies, has the central terms wrong and should be sent back for those corrections.
