Guided Notes: Wilson's Peace and the Turn Inward

Lesson question: Why did the United States refuse the League of Nations it had proposed, and what did the country turn toward in 1920?

Unit question: Why did the United States seek power overseas, and how did the First World War change the country that fought it?

Key Terms

Fourteen Points: President Woodrow Wilson's program for a just peace, presented to Congress on January 8, 1918, in fourteen numbered principles that ended with a general association of nations

League of Nations: the general association of nations created by the Treaty of Versailles in 1919, whose members promised to preserve peace and protect one another against external aggression

Treaty of Versailles: the treaty signed on June 28, 1919, between Germany and the Allied and Associated Powers, which created the League of Nations and imposed territorial losses, disarmament, and reparations on Germany

reparations: payments imposed on a defeated nation for the loss and damage its aggression caused during a war

Lodge reservations: the fourteen conditions Senator Henry Cabot Lodge and the Senate Foreign Relations Committee attached to ratification of the Treaty of Versailles, chiefly reserving to Congress the decision on any United States obligation under Article 10

Red Scare: the fear in 1919 and 1920 that Communists and other radicals were plotting revolution in the United States, and the government campaign against suspected radicals that the fear produced

normalcy: Warren G. Harding's word in the 1920 campaign for a return to ordinary life after war, with less government activism at home and less involvement abroad

Senate fight at a glance. Wilson and his supporters: ratify the treaty as written, with the League Covenant intact. On Article 10: keep the collective guarantee as the heart of the League. Lodge reservationists: ratify only with the fourteen reservations. On Article 10: no United States obligation unless Congress provides for it in each case. Irreconcilables: reject the treaty and the League in any form. On Article 10: accept no version of the obligation.

Exit ticket 1. The treaty with the Lodge reservations failed because Wilson ordered Democrats to vote no; the treaty without reservations failed because reservationists and irreconcilables voted no. Each version was blocked by a different combination.

Exit ticket 2. Lodge: Reservation 2 protected the constitutional power of Congress to declare war. Wilson: Reservation 2 destroyed the Article 10 guarantee that made the League a collective defense against aggression.

Exit ticket 3. Any two: the strike wave of 1919, the Red Summer race violence in Chicago and at Elaine, the bombings of 1919, and the Palmer raids. Each made the war era feel like disorder at home and strengthened the appeal of a quiet return to ordinary life.
