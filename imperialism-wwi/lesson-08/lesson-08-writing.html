Writing Task: The League Refused

The prompt. Why did the United States refuse the League of Nations it had proposed, and what did the country turn toward in 1920? Write a short essay that answers both parts. Rank the causes of the refusal, use at least three of the evidence cards below, and answer the strongest argument against your ranking.

Cards 1 to 4 are summaries drawn from the lesson reading. Card 5 quotes a named source. Write the history in your own sentences and quote facts, not the cards.

Card 1: The Proposal and the Bargain

Wilson's Fourteen Points (January 8, 1918) ended with a general association of nations. At Paris in 1919 the League Covenant became part of the treaty, while freedom of the seas was dropped and former German and Ottoman colonies passed to the victors as mandates.

Card 2: Article 10 and Reservation 2

Article 10 of the Covenant bound League members to preserve one another's territory and independence against external aggression. Lodge's Reservation 2 declared that the United States assumed no such obligation, and no obligation to use its armed forces under the treaty, unless Congress provided for it in each particular case. Lodge defended it as protecting Congress's power to declare war; Wilson called it the destruction of the treaty's heart.

Card 3: The Votes

Wilson toured the country in September 1919, collapsed at Pueblo, and suffered a stroke on October 2. On November 19, 1919, the Senate rejected the treaty with reservations, 39 to 55, and without reservations, 38 to 53. On March 19, 1920, the reserved treaty won 49 votes to 35, a majority but short of the two thirds ratification requires. The irreconcilables voted against every version.

Card 4: The Year 1919

More than 3,600 strikes involved some four million workers in 1919. The Red Summer brought race violence to Chicago and Elaine, Arkansas. Bombs mailed to officials fed a Red Scare, and Attorney General Palmer's raids arrested several thousand suspected radicals in January 1920 and deported 249 aliens in December 1919.

Card 5: Normalcy, in Harding's Words

Warren G. Harding, speech at the Home Market Club, Boston, May 14, 1920: "America's present need is not heroics, but healing; not nostrums, but normalcy; not revolution, but restoration; not agitation, but adjustment; not surgery, but serenity; not the dramatic, but the dispassionate; not experiment, but equipoise; not submergence in internationality, but sustainment in triumphant nationality."

Teacher Guide and Rubric

Full credit requires:

  • An answer to both parts of the prompt: a ranked explanation of the refusal, and an account of the turn to Harding and normalcy in 1920. An essay that treats only the Senate fight does not meet the prompt.
  • A ranking that can be tested against the votes. Acceptable chief causes include the Article 10 dispute itself, Wilson's refusal to compromise compounded by his stroke, Lodge's management of the reservations, and the two thirds rule working with three blocs that would not combine. A list of causes with no ranking does not meet the prompt.
  • At least three cards used accurately. Anchor facts: Fourteen Points, January 8, 1918; treaty signed June 28, 1919; stroke, October 2, 1919; Senate votes of November 19, 1919 (39 to 55 and 38 to 53) and March 19, 1920 (49 to 35); Harding's normalcy speech, May 14, 1920, and his victory with about 60 percent of the popular vote.
  • A real counterargument answered, not dismissed. Against a Wilson centered ranking, the strongest counter is that a majority of the Senate voted for the reserved treaty in March 1920 and only the two thirds rule and Lodge's terms stood in the way. Against a Lodge centered ranking, the strongest counter is Card 3: Wilson ordered his own party to kill the version most likely to pass.
  • Credit answers that connect 1919 to 1920 through Card 4: the strikes, race violence, and Red Scare made the war era feel like disorder, and normalcy was the voters' answer to that feeling as much as a verdict on the League.

Sample claim. The United States refused its own League chiefly because Article 10 asked the Senate to promise in advance what the Constitution left to Congress, and neither Wilson nor Lodge would yield on that point; in 1920 a country worn down by the strikes, violence, and raids of 1919 chose Harding's normalcy, ordinary life at home and no League abroad, over Wilson's unfinished crusade.
