Wilson's Peace and the Turn Inward

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

A Plan for Peace

President Woodrow Wilson presented his peace program to Congress on January 8, 1918. The Fourteen Points opened with general principles: open agreements reached in public, freedom of the seas, lower trade barriers, reduced armaments, and a fair adjustment of colonial claims that weighed the interests of the peoples concerned. Further points applied those principles to territory, calling for German forces to leave Russia, Belgium, and France and for an independent Poland. The fourteenth point proposed a general association of nations to guarantee the independence and territory of all states, great and small. Wilson intended a peace without victory, enforced by the association acting together.

The Bargaining at Paris

Wilson carried the program to Paris in person in 1919, negotiating with David Lloyd George of Britain, Georges Clemenceau of France, and Vittorio Orlando of Italy. The British and French leaders, whose countries had borne the fighting, wanted Germany weakened and made to pay. Wilson won his fourteenth point. The Covenant of the League of Nations became the first part of the treaty, and its Article 10 bound members to preserve one another's territory and independence against external aggression. On other points he compromised or lost. Freedom of the seas was dropped, colonial claims were settled by giving former German and Ottoman territories to the victors as mandates to administer, and Germany lost territory, its colonies, and most of its armed forces.

Versailles: Guilt and Reparations

The treaty was signed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles on June 28, 1919. Article 231 stated that Germany accepted responsibility for causing all the loss and damage of the war, which the Allies charged to German aggression. Germans called this the war guilt clause. It supplied the legal basis for reparations. Article 232 required Germany to compensate the Allies for damage to civilians and their property, while admitting that German resources could not cover the whole loss. A Reparation Commission would fix the total. Ratification required a two thirds vote of the Senate. Wilson had asked voters in 1918 to return a Democratic Congress; they elected Republican majorities instead, and he had taken no leading Republican senator to Paris.

The Fight in the Senate

Senator Henry Cabot Lodge of Massachusetts, the Republican chairman of the Foreign Relations Committee, led the opposition. A small group of irreconcilables, led by Senator William E. Borah of Idaho, opposed joining any league in any form. Lodge wrote the reservationists' conditions into fourteen reservations. The central one, Reservation 2, declared that the United States assumed no obligation under Article 10 to preserve another country's territory or independence, or to use its armed forces, unless Congress so provided in each particular case. Lodge argued that this protected the power of Congress to declare war. Wilson answered that Reservation 2 cut the heart out of the treaty, since Article 10 was the League's promise to act together against aggression, and he ordered Democrats to vote no on that version.

The Tour, the Stroke, and the Votes

In September 1919 Wilson traveled some 8,000 miles by train, speaking for the treaty as written. Exhausted, he collapsed after a speech at Pueblo, Colorado, and the tour ended. On October 2, 1919, he suffered a severe stroke that left him partially paralyzed. From his sickroom he still refused the Lodge reservations. On November 19, 1919, the Senate voted twice. The treaty with the Lodge reservations failed, 39 to 55, as Wilson's Democrats and the irreconcilables together voted no. The treaty without reservations failed, 38 to 53. A last vote on March 19, 1920, gave the treaty with reservations 49 votes to 35, a majority but short of two thirds. The United States never ratified the treaty, never joined the League, and made a separate peace with Germany in 1921.

The Troubled Year 1919

While the treaty fight ran, the country was turning inward in anger. The year 1919 brought more than 3,600 strikes involving some four million workers, including a general strike in Seattle and national strikes in steel and coal. Race violence scarred the Red Summer of 1919. In Chicago a week of rioting that began on July 27 left 38 people dead, 23 of them Black. At Elaine, Arkansas, white posses and troops killed Black sharecroppers who had organized a union. Fear of revolution fed these conflicts. The Russian Revolution of 1917 had created a Communist government, American radicals formed Communist parties in 1919, and bombs mailed to officials that spring, one exploding outside the home of Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer, convinced many Americans that revolution was being plotted at home.

The Red Scare and the Election of 1920

Palmer answered with raids. On November 7, 1919, federal agents raided radical offices in a dozen cities. On January 2, 1920, agents arrested several thousand suspected radicals in more than thirty cities. That December the government had deported 249 aliens, including the anarchist Emma Goldman, on the transport Buford. The raids swept up many with no part in any plot, and support faded when Palmer's predicted May Day revolution did not come. In November 1920 voters completed the turn. Republican Warren G. Harding, who had promised in May that the country needed "not heroics, but healing; not nostrums, but normalcy," defeated Democrat James M. Cox with about 60 percent of the popular vote and 404 electoral votes to 127. Normalcy meant ordinary life again: less reform at home and no League abroad.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What did the Fourteen Points propose, and what was the fourteenth point? Wilson's January 8, 1918 program proposed open agreements, freedom of the seas, lower trade barriers, reduced armaments, fair colonial adjustments, and territorial settlements including an independent Poland. The fourteenth point proposed a general association of nations to guarantee the political independence and territorial integrity of all states, great and small. That association became the League of Nations. [Blueprint 12]

2. What did Articles 231 and 232 of the Treaty of Versailles require of Germany? Article 231 made Germany accept responsibility for causing all the loss and damage of the war, charged to German aggression. Article 232 required Germany to compensate the Allies for damage to civilians and their property, while recognizing that German resources could not cover complete reparation. A Reparation Commission would set the amount. [Blueprint 12]

3. How did Lodge's Reservation 2 change the obligation in Article 10, and why did Wilson refuse it? Article 10 bound League members to respect and preserve one another's territory and independence against external aggression. Reservation 2 declared that the United States assumed no such obligation, and no obligation to use its armed forces under the treaty, unless Congress provided for it in each particular case. Lodge defended this as protecting Congress's power to declare war. Wilson held that it destroyed the collective guarantee at the center of the League and ordered Democrats to vote no. [Blueprint 13]

4. Explain how the treaty could fail even though a majority voted for it with reservations in March 1920. Ratification requires two thirds of the Senate. The blocs never combined behind one version. Wilson's Democrats killed the treaty with Lodge reservations on November 19, 1919 (39 to 55), irreconcilables and Wilson loyalists killed it without reservations (38 to 53), and on March 19, 1920, the reserved version won 49 to 35, a majority but short of the two thirds required. Wilson's stroke and refusal to compromise, and his weakened position after the 1918 elections, stand behind each vote. [Blueprint 13]

5. Connect 1919 to 1920. The strikes, the Red Summer violence, and the bomb scares made 1919 feel like a country coming apart, and the Palmer raids answered disorder with further violations of rights. Voters in 1920 chose Harding's promise of healing and normalcy, about 60 percent of the popular vote and 404 electoral votes, over Cox and the League. The result closed the war era and opened the Twenties. [Blueprints 14, 15]
