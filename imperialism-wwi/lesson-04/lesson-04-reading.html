Open Doors and the Big Stick

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

Notes Instead of a Colony in China

By 1899 the great powers had divided coastal China into spheres of influence. A sphere of influence is a region in which one outside power holds dominant trading and investment privileges over the others. Germany held privileges in Shandong, Russia held ports and railways in Manchuria, and Britain led in the Yangtze valley. The United States held no sphere, and Secretary of State John Hay feared the spheres would harden into colonies and shut American trade out. On September 6, 1899, Hay sent notes to the powers asking each to promise equal treatment for the trade of all nations inside its sphere. The policy became known as the Open Door: the 1899 and 1900 United States policy calling for equal trading rights for all nations in China and for China to remain one country. The powers answered with conditions. Hay treated the answers as acceptance in March 1900.

The Boxer Rebellion

Many Chinese blamed foreigners, and a Qing government that failed to resist them. The Boxers, members of a society whose name translates as the Society of Righteous and Harmonious Fists, attacked missionaries, Chinese Christians, railroads, and telegraph lines. The Boxer Rebellion is the 1900 uprising in China by the Boxers, a society opposed to foreign influence, put down by an eight-nation force that included United States troops. In June 1900 the Boxers and Qing troops besieged the legations in Beijing for about eight weeks. An alliance of eight nations sent an expedition that fought its way to Beijing in August and lifted the siege. Foreign deaths numbered in the hundreds. Chinese Christians died in the tens of thousands, and Boxers and Chinese soldiers died in large numbers. During the siege Hay sent a second Open Door note, on July 3, 1900, asking the powers to preserve China as one country. The Boxer Protocol of 1901 forced China to pay an indemnity of about 333 million dollars over 39 years.

A Quieter Bargain with Japan

With Japan, the rising power in Asia, the United States bargained instead. In October 1906 the San Francisco school board ordered Japanese and Korean students into a segregated school, and Japan protested the order as an insult to a treaty partner. President Theodore Roosevelt persuaded the board to withdraw the order, and in return Japan agreed in 1907 and 1908 to stop issuing passports to laborers bound for the mainland United States. This was the Gentlemen's Agreement: the 1907 and 1908 understanding in which Japan limited the emigration of its laborers to the United States and the United States ended the segregation of Japanese students in San Francisco schools. The bargain did not end discrimination: California barred Japanese immigrants from owning farmland in 1913.

Panama: A Revolution and a Canal

A canal across Central America would move the United States Navy between the oceans in weeks, and the best route crossed Panama, then a province of Colombia. When Colombia's senate rejected the canal treaty in 1903, Panamanian leaders declared independence on November 3. United States warships kept Colombian troops from reaching the isthmus, the United States recognized Panama within days, and the new republic signed a treaty giving the United States a canal zone ten miles wide for 10 million dollars and an annual payment. The Panama Canal is the ship canal across the Isthmus of Panama, built by the United States between 1904 and 1914, linking the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. Construction killed about 5,600 workers, by the official count, most of them West Indian laborers. The canal opened on August 15, 1914. Colombia received 25 million dollars in 1921, in what many Colombians read as payment for a lost province.

The Roosevelt Corollary in the Caribbean

Roosevelt argued that the canal made Caribbean order an American duty. Britain, Germany, and Italy had blockaded Venezuela in 1902 and 1903 to collect debts, and Roosevelt feared European warships returning elsewhere, to the Dominican Republic, on the route to Panama. In his Annual Message of December 6, 1904, he announced the Roosevelt Corollary: Theodore Roosevelt's 1904 addition to the Monroe Doctrine claiming a United States right to intervene in Latin American countries guilty of chronic wrongdoing or unable to pay their debts. A country that kept order and paid its obligations, he said, need fear no interference. One that did not could face a United States international police power, so that Europe had no reason to intervene. The first test came in the Dominican Republic in 1905, where the United States took over the customhouses, collected the import taxes, and paid foreign creditors a fixed share. Later presidents cited it to justify occupations of Cuba, Nicaragua, Haiti, and the Dominican Republic.

Dollars in Place of Bullets

President William Howard Taft tried to work through banks instead of landing troops. Their policy was dollar diplomacy: President Taft's policy of using American loans and investment, backed by government pressure, to extend United States influence abroad. Taft said his diplomacy substituted dollars for bullets. In Nicaragua, the United States backed the overthrow of President Jose Santos Zelaya in 1909, then arranged American bank loans and an American collector of customs. When Nicaraguans revolted in 1912, about 2,500 Marines and sailors landed to protect the government the loans supported, and a Marine guard stayed for years. Each instrument in this lesson projected power without a formal colony, and each sent its bill to someone else: to China in indemnity payments, to Colombia in a lost province, to canal workers in lives, and to Caribbean peoples in customs revenue and governments they did not choose.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What problem did Hay's Open Door notes of September 6, 1899, try to solve, and what exactly did he ask the powers to promise? The powers had divided coastal China into spheres of influence, and the United States, which held no sphere, feared the spheres would harden into colonies and shut American trade out. Hay asked each power to promise that, inside its sphere, it would not interfere with treaty ports or vested interests, that the Chinese treaty tariff would apply to the goods of all nations with the duties collected by the Chinese government, and that it would charge no higher harbor dues or railroad rates to other nations than to its own. Full text: Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1899, pp. 129 to 130.

2. Trace the Boxer Rebellion from its causes to the Boxer Protocol. Include who the Boxers attacked, how the siege in Beijing ended, and what the Protocol cost China. Many Chinese blamed foreigners and a Qing government that failed to resist them. The Boxers attacked missionaries, Chinese Christians, and the railroads and telegraph lines carrying foreign influence. In June 1900 Boxers and Qing troops besieged the Beijing legations for about eight weeks. An eight-nation expedition, including United States troops, fought to Beijing in August and lifted the siege. The Boxer Protocol of 1901 forced China to pay an indemnity of about 333 million dollars over 39 years. Foreign deaths numbered in the hundreds; Chinese Christians died in the tens of thousands, and Boxers and Qing soldiers died in large numbers during the fighting and occupation.

3. How did the United States get the right to build the Panama Canal, and what did the canal cost Panama, Colombia, and the workers who built it? Colombia's senate rejected the canal treaty in 1903. Panamanian leaders declared independence on November 3, 1903. United States warships kept Colombian troops from reaching the isthmus, the United States recognized Panama within days, and Panama signed a treaty giving the United States a ten-mile-wide canal zone, 10 million dollars, and an annual payment. Costs: Colombia lost the province of Panama and received 25 million dollars in 1921 under a treaty many Colombians read as compensation; Panama surrendered control of the zone across its middle; and about 5,600 workers, most of them West Indian, died during the American building years by the official count.

4. State the Roosevelt Corollary in your own words. Then explain how the Dominican customs takeover of 1905 followed from it. The Corollary, announced in the Annual Message of December 6, 1904 (text: American Presidency Project, Fourth Annual Message), holds that if a Western Hemisphere country keeps order and pays its obligations it need fear no interference, but that chronic wrongdoing or inability to govern may force the United States, under the Monroe Doctrine, to exercise an international police power. In the Dominican Republic in 1905 the United States took over the customhouses, collected the import taxes, and paid foreign creditors a fixed share. Collecting the debts itself removed the reason a European power might give for intervening, which was the purpose the Corollary stated.

5. Compare the Open Door and dollar diplomacy. Both projected power without ruling a formal colony. How did each one work, and what happened in Nicaragua when dollar diplomacy was challenged? The Open Door worked by diplomatic notes: the United States asked the powers for promises of equal trade treatment in China and a preserved Chinese state, taking no territory to govern. Dollar diplomacy worked by finance backed by government pressure: American bank loans, an American customs collector, and United States support for a friendly government, as in Nicaragua after 1909. When Nicaraguans revolted in 1912, about 2,500 Marines and sailors landed to protect the government the loans supported, and a Marine guard stayed for years. Strong answers note the limit this exposes: the financial instrument depended on the military one standing behind it.
