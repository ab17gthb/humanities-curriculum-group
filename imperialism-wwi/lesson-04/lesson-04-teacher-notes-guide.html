Open Doors and the Big Stick

Imperialism and World War I · Lesson 4 · Teacher Notes Guide — slide-by-slide

When to teach this

Placement: Imperialism and World War I, Lesson 4 of 8. It follows Lesson 3, "An Empire in the Philippines," where the United States ruled territory directly. This lesson widens the lens to the places it mostly did not rule — China, Japan, Panama, and the Caribbean — and asks what it used instead of colonies there: diplomatic notes, a bargain, a protected revolution, a claimed police power, and bank loans. Teach it fourth in the unit, before Lesson 5, "Neutrality Tested," turns to 1914 and the European war.

What it pairs with

Pairs with: the reading "Open Doors and the Big Stick" and the guided notes, which follow the deck's order exactly — notes instead of a colony in China, the Boxer Rebellion, a bargain with Japan, Panama and the canal, the Roosevelt Corollary, and dollar diplomacy in Nicaragua. The activity is "Instruments of Power Stations" (five stations, five instruments). The notebook page is the instrument chart: two oceans, compared. The quick check is "The Big Stick in the Caribbean Sea," reading the Rogers cartoon from 1904. The writing task is "Power Without Colonies?" — judge one instrument and its cost.

Prior knowledge students need

Prior knowledge: Lessons 2 and 3 — the Treaty of Paris settlement and direct rule in the Philippines and Puerto Rico — because this lesson is defined against them, plus Lesson 1's motives for the turn overseas. Everything else is taught in order: spheres of influence, Hay's Open Door notes of 1899 and 1900, the Boxer Rebellion and the Boxer Protocol, the San Francisco school order and the Gentlemen's Agreement, the Panama revolution and canal, the Roosevelt Corollary and the Dominican customs takeover, and dollar diplomacy in Nicaragua.

Standards

Standards: C3 Framework D2.His.13 (continuity and change — power projected without formal colonies, instrument by instrument), D2.His.16 (assess the purpose and intended audience of historical sources — Hay's Open Door note, Roosevelt's Corollary passage, Taft's own words, and the period cartoons, each captioned for exactly what it is), D2.Civ.12 (analyze how people use and challenge laws and institutions — treaty, doctrine, and customs collection as instruments of policy); Common Core literacy RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.2 (the reading's accounts of China, Japan, Panama, the Caribbean claim, and Nicaragua), WHST.9-10.1 (the writing task: judging one instrument and its cost from the documents).

What students will learn

Content statement: Students will be able to name five instruments the United States used to project power without ruling formal colonies — the diplomatic note, armed force behind it, a negotiated bargain, control of a route, a claimed police power, and loans backed by force — and give one fact for each; explain what Hay asked for in 1899 and why the collector detail mattered; trace the Boxer Rebellion from its targets to the indemnity China paid; explain how the United States got the right to build the Panama Canal and what the canal cost Panama, Colombia, and its workers; state the Roosevelt Corollary and how the Dominican customs takeover followed from it; and explain what happened in Nicaragua in 1912 when dollar diplomacy was challenged.

Timing and materials

Timing: One class period, about 50 minutes — opening, question, and map (slides 1–3) 5 minutes; notes instead of a colony in China (slides 4–6) 8 minutes; the Boxer Rebellion (slides 7–9) 7 minutes; a bargain with Japan (slides 10–12) 7 minutes; Panama, a revolution and a canal (slides 13–15) 9 minutes; the Roosevelt Corollary (slides 16–18) 6 minutes; dollar diplomacy in Nicaragua (slides 19–21) 5 minutes; vocabulary and the turn to work (slides 22–25) 3 minutes. Materials: the slide deck, the reading, and the guided notes. The exit ticket has three parts — name the instruments used in China and Nicaragua, apply the Corollary to a debt default, and name one instrument's cost.

Key vocabulary

Open Door · sphere of influence · Boxer Rebellion · Roosevelt Corollary · dollar diplomacy · Panama Canal · Gentlemen's Agreement

Slide-by-slide notes

Slide 1 · Opening frame

On the slide: title image (full-bleed, navy band); the unit and lesson line "Imperialism and World War I · Lesson 4"; the title "Open Doors and the Big Stick"; the standfirst — "Notes, warships, a bargain, and bank loans across two oceans."

  • Picture: Putting His Foot Down, a chromolithograph cartoon by J. S. Pughe from Puck in 1899, held by the Library of Congress. Uncle Sam stands on a map of China holding a paper marked "Trade Treaty China" while rulers of the European powers cut at the map with shears.
  • [Open with the puzzle the cartoon poses.] The United States wanted the trade and the influence the other powers were carving out for themselves, yet in China it took no colony and held no sphere of its own.
  • This lesson follows the instruments it used instead, across two oceans: diplomatic notes in China, a quiet bargain with Japan, a revolution backed by warships in Panama, a claimed police power in the Caribbean, and bank loans under President Taft.
  • Each instrument projected power without a formal colony.
  • Each one also sent a cost to the people on the receiving end, and students should track both sides of that ledger from the first slide to the exit ticket.

Slide 2 · The essential question

On the slide: the lesson question in large type — "How did the United States project power in Asia and Latin America without ruling formal colonies there?"; the unit question in italic beneath it — "Why did the United States seek power overseas, and how did the First World War change the country that fought it?"

  • [Read the lesson question slowly,] because its wording does analytical work.
  • "Project power" is the verb: the United States found ways to shape what other governments and peoples could do.
  • "Without ruling formal colonies" is the limit that defines this lesson, and it separates this lesson from the last two. In the Philippines and Puerto Rico the United States ruled territory directly. In China, Japan, Panama, and the Caribbean it mostly did not, and the question is what it used instead.
  • Students should finish the hour able to name five instruments — the note, armed force, control of a route, a claim to police power, and loans backed by force — and to give one fact for each.
  • The unit question runs underneath, and this lesson is its clearest case of seeking power overseas short of annexation.
  • Keep the lesson question visible, return to its exact wording at the end, and note that the third exit ticket question asks students to name the cost of one instrument to the people who received it.

Slide 3 · The lesson in one slide

On the slide: the title "Five instruments, two oceans"; the six rows — A, Notes instead of a colony in China (Hay, the spheres, and equal trade); B, The Boxer Rebellion (an uprising, a siege, and an eight-nation force); C, A bargain with Japan (a school order withdrawn, passports limited); D, Panama: a revolution and a canal (independence, a zone ten miles wide, a canal by 1914); E, The Roosevelt Corollary (order and debts, policed by the United States); F, Dollar diplomacy in Nicaragua (loans, a customs collector, and Marines in 1912).

  • This is the map of the lesson, and it follows the guided notes section by section.
  • Sections A and B are set in China: first the diplomatic notes Secretary of State John Hay sent in 1899, then the Boxer Rebellion of 1900, which showed the armed force standing behind the paper.
  • Section C stays in Asia but changes method: a negotiated bargain with Japan.
  • Sections D, E, and F cross the Pacific to Latin America: the Panama Canal and the revolution that cleared its path, the Roosevelt Corollary that claimed a police power over the Caribbean, and dollar diplomacy in Nicaragua, where loans replaced troops until troops landed anyway.
  • [Tell students to keep the two oceans separate in their notes] and to attach one named place and one date to every instrument.
  • The activity asks them to classify all five stations by instrument, so the names used on this slide are the names they will need there.

Slide 4 · Section A: Notes Instead of a Colony in China (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section A"; the title "Notes Instead of a Colony in China"; the line — "Paper promises stood in place of a province."

  • Picture: A portrait crop of the same 1899 Puck cartoon that opened the lesson, J. S. Pughe's Putting His Foot Down, held by the Library of Congress. Uncle Sam fills the frame, one hand on his sword, holding the trade treaty, while two kneeling rulers trim the edge of the map of China with shears.
  • [Use the crop to fix the American position before the facts begin.] Every other power in the cartoon is cutting off a piece. The United States is standing on the map with a piece of paper. That paper is the subject of this section.
  • By 1899 the great powers had divided coastal China into spheres of influence, and the United States held none.
  • Secretary of State John Hay had to protect American trade in a country his country did not rule and could not partition. His answer was a set of notes, and Section A takes the division of China first, then the notes themselves, promise by promise.

Slide 5 · A coast divided into spheres

On the slide: the photograph at right; five bullets — Germany held privileges in Shandong. Russia held ports and railways in Manchuria. Britain held the lead in the Yangtze valley. The United States held no sphere of its own. Hay feared the spheres would harden into colonies and shut American trade out.

  • Picture: The Pughe cartoon again — Uncle Sam standing on the map of China with a trade treaty while European rulers cut at the map. Here its work is to show the division this slide lists: a coast already carved into spheres, with no American sphere among them.
  • [Start with the key term in its full guided notes form.] A sphere of influence is a region in which one outside power holds dominant trading and investment privileges over the others.
  • By 1899 the great powers had divided coastal China into such spheres. Germany held privileges in Shandong, Russia held ports and railways in Manchuria, and Britain led in the Yangtze valley.
  • The United States held no sphere, and that absence drove everything Secretary of State John Hay did next.
  • His fear was practical, not abstract. If each sphere hardened into a formal colony, its holder could favor its own merchants, charge foreign goods more, and in time shut American trade out of the largest market in Asia.
  • [Point out what a sphere was not.] It was not yet annexation, and China still had its own government. The whole lesson turns on that middle ground — power exercised inside another country's borders without ruling the country outright — and China in 1899 is the first example.

Slide 6 · Hay's Open Door notes, 1899

On the slide: five bullets — On September 6, 1899, Hay sent notes to the powers. He asked for equal treatment for the trade of all nations. Chinese officials would still collect the duties. The powers answered with conditions. In March 1900, Hay treated the answers as acceptance.

  • [Give students the policy in its full guided notes form first.] The Open Door is the 1899 and 1900 United States policy calling for equal trading rights for all nations in China and for China to remain one country.
  • On September 6, 1899, Hay sent notes to the powers asking each to promise equal treatment for the trade of all nations inside its sphere.
  • The activity reads the note itself, and its three promises are worth naming here: no interference with treaty ports or vested interests, the Chinese treaty tariff applied to the goods of all nations with the duties collected by the Chinese government, and no higher harbor dues or railroad rates charged to foreigners than to the sphere holder's own people.
  • The collector detail carries the policy. Revenue stayed Chinese, so the note claimed access for everyone's trade while leaving China's government formally in charge of its own ports.
  • The powers answered with conditions, each making its promise depend on the others, and in March 1900 Hay simply treated the answers as acceptance and announced the policy as settled.

Slide 7 · Section B: The Boxer Rebellion (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section B"; the title "The Boxer Rebellion"; the line — "An uprising against foreign influence led to a siege in Beijing."

  • Picture: By Kasai Torajiro, a Japanese artist — a chromolithograph print from 1900, not a photograph. It shows allied troops attacking a gate in Beijing during the Boxer Rebellion, with Japanese and American flags visible among the attackers, and it is held by the Library of Congress.
  • [Tell students plainly that it is a print, made to be sold and admired,] because the distinction between a print and a photograph is part of reading any image from this lesson honestly.
  • The scene shows the moment the paper policy of Section A met armed force.
  • Many Chinese blamed foreigners for their country's weakness, and a Qing government that failed to resist them.
  • In 1900 that anger turned into an uprising, foreign troops marched on the capital, and the Open Door survived because an eight-nation army, including Americans, fought its way to Beijing. Section B traces the rebellion from its targets to its price.

Slide 8 · Attack, siege, and relief

On the slide: the photograph at right; five bullets — The Boxers were the Society of Righteous and Harmonious Fists. They targeted missionaries, Chinese Christians, railroads, and telegraph lines. In June 1900, the Beijing legations were besieged for about eight weeks. Eight nations sent the force that lifted the siege in August. During the siege, Hay's second note called for keeping China one country.

  • Picture: The Kasai print again — allied troops attacking a gate in Beijing in 1900, a chromolithograph, not a photograph. Here its work is to show the relief this slide describes: an eight-nation force fighting its way to the capital.
  • [Give the term in full first.] The Boxer Rebellion is the 1900 uprising in China by the Boxers, a society opposed to foreign influence, put down by an eight-nation force that included United States troops.
  • The Boxers, members of a society whose name translates as the Society of Righteous and Harmonious Fists, attacked missionaries, Chinese Christians, railroads, and telegraph lines — the people and the hardware carrying foreign influence into the countryside.
  • In June 1900 the Boxers and Qing government troops besieged the foreign legations in Beijing for about eight weeks. An alliance of eight nations sent an expedition that fought its way to Beijing in August and lifted the siege.
  • During the siege, on July 3, 1900, Hay sent his second Open Door note, asking the powers to preserve China as one country.
  • [Hold the two facts together for students:] the same government that asked for a whole and open China in July had soldiers marching on its capital in August.

Slide 9 · 333 million dollars

On the slide: the big figure "333 MILLION"; the label — "dollars: the indemnity the Boxer Protocol charged China"; the note — "Payable over 39 years. Foreign deaths numbered in the hundreds; Chinese Christians died in the tens of thousands."

  • 333 million dollars, payable over 39 years
  • [Dwell on the bill,] because it is the cost side of the Open Door made countable.
  • The Boxer Protocol of 1901 forced China to pay an indemnity of about 333 million dollars over 39 years, a debt hung on the Chinese government by the same powers whose trade the Open Door kept open.
  • Put the human figures beside the financial one, as the reading does. Foreign deaths during the rebellion numbered in the hundreds.
  • Chinese Christians died in the tens of thousands
  • Boxers and Chinese soldiers died in large numbers during the fighting and the occupation that followed.
  • Students should see the asymmetry without being pushed to a slogan. The preserved China of Hay's second note was also a China billed for its own invasion, and its people paid in lives first and in silver for two generations after.
  • When the exit ticket asks for the cost of one instrument to the people on the receiving end, this slide is the richest source of an answer in the whole lesson.

Slide 10 · Section C: A Bargain with Japan (divider)

On the slide: the section label "Section C"; the title "A Bargain with Japan"; the line — "No notes to six powers this time: a protest, a withdrawn order, and an agreement."

  • This section has no divider photograph, and the reason is the state of the record rather than an oversight. The lesson's verified images cover China, Panama, and the Caribbean. The bargain with Japan was conducted in protests, meetings, and withheld passports, and no verified period image of it sits in the lesson set.
  • [The contrast with China is the place to start.] In China the United States dealt with European powers over the head of a weakening Qing government.
  • Japan was a rising power in Asia, a treaty partner that expected to be treated as an equal and said so when it was not. So the method changed.
  • There were no notes demanding promises from six governments. There was a local school board order in San Francisco, a formal Japanese protest, a president who intervened in a city decision, and a reciprocal understanding in 1907 and 1908.
  • Section C takes the order first, then the bargain it produced, then the discrimination the bargain left standing.

Slide 11 · A school order and a protest, 1906

On the slide: four bullets — In October 1906, San Francisco ordered Japanese and Korean students segregated. Japan protested that the order insulted a treaty partner. Roosevelt stepped in and persuaded the board. The board withdrew the order.

  • [Slow down on how small the trigger was and how large the response became.]
  • In October 1906 the San Francisco school board ordered Japanese and Korean students into a segregated school.
  • Japan protested the order as an insult to a treaty partner, which is the phrase students should keep. Tokyo was not asking for a favor. It was claiming the respect owed to one government by another under treaty, and it expected Washington to answer for a California city's decision.
  • President Theodore Roosevelt persuaded the board to withdraw the order.
  • Ask: Why would a president spend his authority on one school board? The reading's framing supplies the answer: with Japan, the rising power in Asia, the United States bargained instead of dictating. A humiliated Japan was a strategic problem in the Pacific, where American trade, the Philippines, and the new canal route all depended on calm relations.
  • The withdrawal was the American half of a trade, and the next slide gives the Japanese half.

Slide 12 · The Gentlemen's Agreement, 1907 and 1908

On the slide: four bullets — Japan stopped issuing passports to laborers bound for the mainland. The United States ended the San Francisco segregation. The agreement was no law and no treaty: it was an understanding between governments. In 1913, California barred Japanese immigrants from owning farmland.

  • [Give the term in its full guided notes form.] The Gentlemen's Agreement is the 1907 and 1908 understanding in which Japan limited the emigration of its laborers to the United States and the United States ended the segregation of Japanese students in San Francisco schools.
  • Note the form of the instrument, because this lesson is a catalogue of forms. This was not a statute and not a ratified treaty. It was an exchange of commitments between two governments, each giving the other a face-saving way to step back: Tokyo limited the departure of its own laborers, and Washington delivered the withdrawal of the school order.
  • Then give the limit the reading insists on. The bargain did not end discrimination. California barred Japanese immigrants from owning farmland in 1913, six years after the agreement.
  • Students should leave this section able to say both things at once: the United States treated Japan as a power to bargain with, and Japanese people in America still faced laws aimed at them by name.

Slide 13 · Section D: Panama: A Revolution and a Canal (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section D"; the title "Panama: A Revolution and a Canal"; the line — "A province broke away, and a canal zone was born."

  • Picture: The Gatun Locks of the Panama Canal looking toward the Atlantic entrance, photographed for the Byron Company and held by the Library of Congress. Date it carefully for students: it is about 1913, during construction, a year before the canal opened, which is why barges and work crews fill the lock chamber instead of ocean shipping.
  • A canal across Central America would move the United States Navy between the oceans in weeks instead of a voyage around South America, and the best route crossed Panama, then a province of Colombia.
  • This section has two halves that students must not merge. The first is acquisition: a rejected treaty, a revolution, American warships, and a canal zone granted within days. The second is construction: a decade of digging, thousands of dead workers, and an opening in August 1914.
  • [One more caution belongs here.] The route map in the lesson documents is a diagram drawn for the activity, not a historical source, and its caption says so.

Slide 14 · Revolution on the isthmus, 1903

On the slide: six bullets — The best canal route crossed Panama, a province of Colombia. Colombia's senate rejected the canal treaty. On November 3, 1903, Panama declared independence. United States warships kept Colombian troops from the isthmus. The United States recognized Panama within days and signed a treaty for a zone ten miles wide. The terms were 10 million dollars, plus an annual payment.

  • [Walk the sequence in order,] because the guided notes test it step by step and the order is the argument.
  • A canal across Central America would move the United States Navy between the oceans in weeks, and the best route crossed Panama, then a province of Colombia.
  • When Colombia's senate rejected the canal treaty in 1903, Panamanian leaders declared independence on November 3. United States warships kept Colombian troops from reaching the isthmus, so the revolution succeeded without Colombia being able to contest it on the ground.
  • The United States recognized Panama within days, and the new republic signed a treaty giving the United States a canal zone ten miles wide for 10 million dollars and an annual payment.
  • Ask: What was the instrument, precisely? It was not annexation of Panama, and Panama remained a country. It was a revolution protected by American sea power, followed immediately by a treaty with the government that sea power had made possible.
  • Control of a route was obtained without ruling the nation the route crossed.

Slide 15 · Building the canal, 1904 to 1914

On the slide: the photograph at right; four bullets — The canal took a decade of digging across the isthmus. About 5,600 workers died, most of them West Indian laborers. On August 15, 1914, the SS Ancon made the first official transit. In 1921, Colombia received 25 million dollars.

  • Picture: The SS Ancon in the canal channel on August 15, 1914, the day of the first official transit, from the records of the Panama Canal at the National Archives.
  • [Give the term in full here.] The Panama Canal is the ship canal across the Isthmus of Panama, built by the United States between 1904 and 1914, linking the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans.
  • The route students traced in the activity diagram runs from Colon through the Gatun Locks and Gatun Lake, across the Culebra Cut, and down to Panama City, about 50 miles inside the ten-mile-wide zone. Remember that the diagram is a teaching drawing, not a source.
  • The costs belong on this slide in plain numbers.
  • About 5,600 workers died, most of them West Indian laborers
  • Colombia received 25 million dollars in 1921, in what many Colombians read as payment for a lost province.
  • The canal was a triumph of engineering and a transfer of other people's land, labor, and lives, and the lesson keeps both sentences.

Slide 16 · Section E: The Roosevelt Corollary (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section E"; the title "The Roosevelt Corollary"; the line — "The big stick was written into doctrine."

  • Picture: The Big Stick in the Caribbean Sea, a cartoon by William Allen Rogers from 1904, held by the Library of Congress. Roosevelt wades barefoot through the Caribbean carrying a large club and towing a line of warships behind him.
  • [Read the ship labels with students,] because they state the policy in three words: The Receiver, The Sheriff, and Debt Collector. The shores are labeled Santo Domingo, Cuba, Mexico, and Panama.
  • The cartoon appeared the same year Roosevelt announced the Corollary, and it understood him.
  • His argument began with a real event. Britain, Germany, and Italy had blockaded Venezuela in 1902 and 1903 to collect debts, landing European warships in the hemisphere the Monroe Doctrine claimed to guard.
  • Roosevelt's answer was to claim the collecting for the United States. Section E gives the doctrine in his own terms, then its first test in the Dominican Republic a year later.

Slide 17 · The Corollary, December 6, 1904

On the slide: the photograph at right; four bullets — The Corollary was an addition to the Monroe Doctrine of 1823. A country that kept order and paid its obligations had no interference to fear. Chronic wrongdoing could bring a United States international police power. The stated aim was to leave Europe no reason to intervene.

  • Picture: The Rogers Big Stick cartoon again, from 1904. Here its work is to show the claim this slide states: Roosevelt in the Caribbean with a club, towing warships labeled as receiver, sheriff, and debt collector.
  • [Give the term in its full guided notes form first.] The Roosevelt Corollary is Theodore Roosevelt's 1904 addition to the Monroe Doctrine claiming a United States right to intervene in Latin American countries guilty of chronic wrongdoing or unable to pay their debts.
  • Roosevelt announced it in his Annual Message of December 6, 1904, and the activity reads his passage whole.
  • His test had two sides. A country that kept order and paid its obligations, he said, need fear no interference. One that did not could face a United States international police power, so that Europe had no reason to intervene.
  • [Students should hear the move the Corollary makes.] The Monroe Doctrine of 1823 told European powers to stay out of the Americas. The Corollary turned that warning outward into a warrant inward: to keep Europeans from intervening, the United States claimed the right to intervene first, and to decide for itself which governments counted as orderly.
  • Later presidents cited it to justify occupations of Cuba, Nicaragua, Haiti, and the Dominican Republic.

Slide 18 · First test: the Dominican Republic, 1905

On the slide: four bullets — American officials took over the customhouses. They collected the import taxes themselves. Foreign creditors received a fixed share of the revenue. The canal route gained a guarded approach.

  • The first test came quickly, and it shows what the police power looked like in practice.
  • Roosevelt argued that the canal made Caribbean order an American duty, and he feared European warships returning to the Dominican Republic, on the route to Panama, as they had gone to Venezuela.
  • In 1905 the United States took over the Dominican customhouses, collected the import taxes, and paid foreign creditors a fixed share of the revenue.
  • [Pause on the choice of target.] Customs duties were the income a Caribbean government lived on, so collecting them was governing in everything but name, while the country kept its flag, its president, and its formal independence.
  • That is the lesson question in miniature: power projected, colony avoided.
  • It also removed the excuse for European intervention, which was the purpose Roosevelt stated.
  • When students reach Section F, ask them to remember this arrangement, because dollar diplomacy in Nicaragua used the same customs house, reached by loans instead of a doctrine.

Slide 19 · Section F: Dollar Diplomacy in Nicaragua (divider)

On the slide: the divider photograph at right; the section label "Section F"; the title "Dollar Diplomacy in Nicaragua"; the line — "Taft promised dollars in place of bullets, with force held in reserve."

  • Picture: The cover of Puck for February 24, 1909, by Samuel D. Ehrhart, held by the Library of Congress. Outgoing President Theodore Roosevelt, dressed as a cowboy, hands a baby labeled "My Policies" to incoming President William Howard Taft, who wears a nursemaid's apron and bonnet, while Roosevelt's secretary William Loeb, dressed as a bellboy, carries the big stick out behind them.
  • [Students must be told exactly what this cartoon is and is not.] It documents the 1909 transfer of Roosevelt's policies to Taft. It does not depict dollar diplomacy itself, and no period cartoon specifically about dollar diplomacy could be verified for this lesson, so the policy is carried by Taft's own words and the Nicaragua account.
  • Taft's claim was that his diplomacy substituted dollars for bullets. Section F states the policy, then follows it into Nicaragua, where the claim met its test in 1912.

Slide 20 · Dollars in place of bullets

On the slide: four bullets — Taft's instrument was American loans and investment. The loans were backed by government pressure, not by annexation. Customs revenue secured the loans. Nicaragua became the test case.

  • [Give the term in its full guided notes form.] Dollar diplomacy is President Taft's policy of using American loans and investment, backed by government pressure, to extend United States influence abroad.
  • Taft said his diplomacy substituted dollars for bullets, a phrase from his Fourth Annual Message of December 3, 1912, which the activity quotes.
  • [Explain the mechanism in plain steps,] because students often hear the slogan and miss the machinery. The government encouraged American bankers to lend to Caribbean governments Washington wanted to steady.
  • The loans were secured by the borrower's customs revenue, the same revenue the United States had collected directly in the Dominican Republic.
  • A government that owed American banks, and whose customs were watched by an American collector, had strong reasons to keep Washington satisfied, all without annexation, a governor, or a flag change.
  • The instrument was finance, and its enforcement clause was written in customs receipts. Whether it could work without soldiers behind it is the question Nicaragua answered.

Slide 21 · Nicaragua, 1909 to 1912

On the slide: five bullets — In 1909, Washington backed the overthrow of President Zelaya. Then came American bank loans and an American customs collector. In 1912, a revolt rose against the government the loans supported. About 2,500 Marines and sailors landed. A Marine guard stayed for years.

  • [List the steps in the guided notes order,] because Section F asks students to reproduce them.
  • In Nicaragua, the United States backed the overthrow of President Jose Santos Zelaya in 1909. It then arranged American bank loans to the new government and set an American collector over Nicaraguan customs, the revenue that secured the loans.
  • When Nicaraguans revolted in 1912 against the government those arrangements supported, about 2,500 Marines and sailors landed to protect it, and a Marine guard stayed for years afterward.
  • [Draw the conclusion the reading draws, in its own careful terms.] Each instrument in this lesson projected power without a formal colony, and each sent its bill to someone else: to China in indemnity payments, to Colombia in a lost province, to canal workers in lives, and to Caribbean peoples in customs revenue and governments they did not choose.
  • Nicaragua adds a sharper point. The dollars had not replaced the bullets. They had only decided where the bullets would be aimed, and in 1912 they were fired in defense of a loan.

Slide 22 · Vocabulary: words for China

On the slide: three terms with their definitions — Open Door, sphere of influence, Boxer Rebellion. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly, because the unit test draws on them.

  • Say: "Open Door: the 1899 and 1900 United States policy calling for equal trading rights for all nations in China and for China to remain one country"
  • Say: "sphere of influence: a region in which one outside power holds dominant trading and investment privileges over the others"
  • Say: "Boxer Rebellion: the 1900 uprising in China by the Boxers, a society opposed to foreign influence, put down by an eight-nation force that included United States troops"
  • These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.
  • Both halves of the Open Door definition belong in it — access and preservation — because Hay's second note in July 1900 added the second half while the legations were under siege.
  • Stress the word "privileges" in sphere of influence. A sphere was influence inside another country's territory, short of owning it.
  • The Boxer Rebellion definition names the suppressors as carefully as the rebels, and students should notice that the force, not any single nation, ended the uprising.

Slide 23 · Vocabulary: words for the Caribbean claim

On the slide: two terms with their definitions — Roosevelt Corollary, dollar diplomacy. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: "Roosevelt Corollary: Theodore Roosevelt's 1904 addition to the Monroe Doctrine claiming a United States right to intervene in Latin American countries guilty of chronic wrongdoing or unable to pay their debts"
  • Say: "dollar diplomacy: President Taft's policy of using American loans and investment, backed by government pressure, to extend United States influence abroad"
  • Two more definitions, in guided notes order, and together they name the two Caribbean instruments.
  • Both triggers in the Corollary definition deserve attention — wrongdoing and unpaid debts — because the second one turned a financial problem into grounds for intervention and produced the Dominican customs takeover within a year.
  • The phrase "backed by government pressure" is the honest core of dollar diplomacy. Without it, loans are just banking. With it, a loan becomes an instrument of state, and Nicaragua in 1912 showed how short the distance was between the pressure and the Marines who finally supplied it.

Slide 24 · Vocabulary: words for the route and the bargain

On the slide: two terms with their definitions — Panama Canal, Gentlemen's Agreement. These definitions match the guided notes word for word, and students should copy them exactly.

  • Say: "Panama Canal: the ship canal across the Isthmus of Panama, built by the United States between 1904 and 1914, linking the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans"
  • Say: "Gentlemen's Agreement: the 1907 and 1908 understanding in which Japan limited the emigration of its laborers to the United States and the United States ended the segregation of Japanese students in San Francisco schools"

  • The dates in the Panama Canal definition are the building years, and students should attach the human figures to them: about 5,600 workers dead, most of them West Indian laborers, and a zone ten miles wide held under a treaty signed days after independence.
