Part 3: Source-Based Questions (15 points)

Source A. Albert J. Beveridge, "March of the Flag," Indianapolis, September 16, 1898

"Fellow citizens, it is a noble land that God has given us; a land that can feed and clothe the world; a land whose coastlines would enclose half the countries of Europe; a land set like a sentinel between the two imperial oceans of the globe, a greater England with a nobler destiny. ... Shall the American people continue their resistless march toward the commercial supremacy of the world? Shall free institutions broaden their blessed reign as the children of liberty wax in strength, until the empire of our principles is established over the hearts of all mankind? ... shall we occupy new markets for what our farmers raise, new markets for what our factories make, new markets for what our merchants sell ..."

Text as printed in Albert J. Beveridge, "March of the Flag," 1898 (Teaching American History; 1898 Indianapolis pamphlet). Cuts are marked with ellipses. The passage on new markets is cut before a clause on what American ships would carry.

A1. (2 points) According to Source A, what two gifts does Beveridge say God has given the United States, in the land itself and in its position between two oceans?

A2. (3 points) Explain the two arguments Beveridge joins in Source A, commercial supremacy through new markets and a mission to spread free institutions. Then state the answer the Anti-Imperialist League made to the mission argument.

Source B. The Zimmermann Telegram, January 1917

"We intend to begin on the first of February unrestricted submarine warfare. We shall endeavor in spite of this to keep the United States of America neutral. In the event of this not succeeding, we make Mexico a proposal of alliance on the following basis: make war together, make peace together, generous financial support and an understanding on our part that Mexico is to reconquer the lost territory in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. ... Please call the President's attention to the fact that the ruthless employment of our submarines now offers the prospect of compelling England in a few months to make peace. Signed, ZIMMERMANN"

Text in the English translation transmitted by Ambassador Walter Hines Page, February 24, 1917 (National Archives). Cuts are marked with ellipses; the omitted passage leaves the detailed settlement to Mexico and adds a suggestion that Mexico invite Japan to join the alliance and mediate with Germany.

B1. (2 points) According to Source B, what does Germany intend to begin on the first of February, and what three things does it offer Mexico in the proposed alliance?

B2. (3 points) The telegram was published in American newspapers on March 1, 1917. Explain why its publication pushed the United States toward war, using the territorial offer and the submarine statement in Source B.

Source C. Treaty of Versailles, Articles 231 and 232, June 28, 1919

Article 231. "The Allied and Associated Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies."

Article 232. "The Allied and Associated Governments recognise that the resources of Germany are not adequate, after taking into account permanent diminutions of such resources which will result from other provisions of the present Treaty, to make complete reparation for all such loss and damage. The Allied and Associated Governments, however, require, and Germany undertakes, that she will make compensation for all damage done to the civilian population of the Allied and Associated Powers and to their property ..."

Text as printed in The Treaty of Versailles: Articles 231-232, June 28, 1919 (Avalon Project, Yale Law School). The Article 232 passage is cut at the end, marked with an ellipsis; the omitted text continues the list of compensable damage.

C1. (2 points) According to Article 231 in Source C, what does Germany accept, and what cause of the loss and damage does the article name?

C2. (3 points) Article 232 admits one limit and then imposes one requirement. State both, and explain how Article 231 served as the legal basis for the reparations Germany was required to pay.

Part 3.

A1. The land (1 point): a noble land that can feed and clothe the world, with coastlines that would enclose half the countries of Europe. The position (1 point): the land is set like a sentinel between the two imperial oceans of the globe, a greater England with a nobler destiny. [1]

A2. Three points. Markets (1 point): Beveridge asks whether Americans will continue their resistless march toward the commercial supremacy of the world and occupy new markets for what their farmers raise, their factories make, and their merchants sell, the surplus argument of Lesson 1. Mission (1 point): free institutions are to broaden their blessed reign until the empire of American principles is established over the hearts of all mankind, so expansion is presented as a duty, not only a trade. The League answer (1 point): governing a people without its consent violates republican principles, so the mission claim fails on the nation's own founding ground when it is used to rule Filipinos or other peoples who have not consented. [1]

B1. The intention (1 point): Germany intends to begin unrestricted submarine warfare on the first of February, while endeavoring to keep the United States neutral. The offer (1 point, all three for the point): make war together and make peace together, generous financial support, and an understanding that Mexico is to reconquer the lost territory in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. [7]

B2. Three points. Hostile intent on American soil (1 point): a secret German proposal offered a neighboring country the recovery of three American states, which made the war in Europe a direct threat to the United States rather than a distant quarrel. Confirmation of the submarine issue (1 point): the same message announced the ruthless employment of submarines to compel England to make peace within a few months, confirming that the February 1 resumption was a settled policy and not a misunderstanding that diplomacy could revise. Timing and opinion (1 point): published on March 1, weeks after the resumption and days before German submarines sank three American merchant ships in mid-March, the telegram united opinion behind the cabinet's advice and Wilson's April 2 war message in a way the submarine sinkings alone had not. [7]

C1. The acceptance (1 point): Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage suffered by the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals. The cause (1 point): the war was imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies. [12]

C2. Three points. The limit (1 point): the Allies recognise that the resources of Germany are not adequate, after the permanent diminutions the treaty itself causes, to make complete reparation for all such loss and damage. The requirement (1 point): Germany nevertheless undertakes to make compensation for all damage done to the civilian population of the Allied and Associated Powers and to their property. The legal basis (1 point): Article 231 first establishes responsibility, affirming that Germany accepts responsibility for causing all the loss and damage, so Article 232's compensation requirement and the later fixing of a sum by the Reparation Commission rest on an admitted liability rather than on a sum imposed without a stated ground; this is the ground on which Germans read the article as a war guilt clause, a reading the teacher note for the source records. [12]
