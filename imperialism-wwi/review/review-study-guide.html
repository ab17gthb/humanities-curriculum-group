Part 2: Review Questions

Lesson 1: The Turn Overseas

1. Name three motives Americans gave for expansion in the 1890s, and state the person or group from the lesson who argued for each one.

2. Trace Hawaii from the reciprocity treaty of 1875 to the overthrow of Queen Liliuokalani in January 1893. Include the Pearl Harbor right gained in 1887 and President Cleveland's conclusion about the overthrow.

3. How did events in Cuba turn a decade of expansion talk toward war in 1898? Cover the rebellion that began in 1895, Spain's reconcentration policy, and the distance of Cuba from Florida.

Lesson 2: The Spanish-American War

4. What happened to the USS Maine on February 15, 1898, and what work did yellow journalism do in the weeks that followed?

5. State the promise of the Teller Amendment, then explain how the fighting of the war, from Dewey at Manila Bay to the Rough Riders around Santiago, reached beyond that promise.

6. State the terms of the Treaty of Paris, and explain how the Platt Amendment qualified the independence Cuba received. Note also what killed far more American soldiers than Spanish fire.

Lesson 3: An Empire in the Philippines

7. State the two sides of the Senate annexation debate over the Philippines. Include the principle the Anti-Imperialist League invoked against governing the islands.

8. Who was Emilio Aguinaldo, what did President McKinley's Benevolent Assimilation proclamation of December 21, 1898 claim, and how did the Philippine-American War of 1899 to 1902 test that claim? Include the method called guerrilla warfare.

9. What did the Insular Cases hold, beginning in 1901, and what did civil government replace in the islands?

Lesson 4: Open Doors and the Big Stick

10. What did Secretary Hay's Open Door notes of 1899 and 1900 ask of the other powers in China, what is a sphere of influence, and how did the Boxer Rebellion of 1900 end?

11. How did the United States gain the Panama Canal Zone, and what did the canal, built between 1904 and 1914, join?

12. Contrast the Roosevelt Corollary, dollar diplomacy, and the Gentlemen's Agreement as three different instruments of American power. State the president and the region attached to each.

Lesson 5: Neutrality Tested

13. Why was American neutrality hard to hold between 1914 and 1916? Cover contraband, the British blockade, and the direction of American trade and loans.

14. Trace the submarine crisis from the sinking of the Lusitania on May 7, 1915 to the Sussex Pledge of May 4, 1916. State the dead of the Lusitania and the exact promise Germany made in the pledge.

15. Lay out the chain of 1917 in order: the resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare on February 1, the Zimmermann Telegram, the Russian Revolution, and Wilson's war message and the declaration of war. Then state which link you judge decisive and why.

Lesson 6: Americans in the War

16. Explain how the United States raised and moved its army: conscription, the American Expeditionary Forces under General Pershing, and the convoy system.

17. What did American soldiers meet in France? Cover trench warfare and no man's land, St. Mihiel, and the Meuse-Argonne, and give the date and meaning of the armistice.

18. State two facts that complicate a simple story of American service in the war: the segregated service of Black soldiers, many assigned to labor battalions, and the influenza pandemic. Label the pandemic figures as estimates.

Lesson 7: The Home Front

19. Describe the managed home front. State what the War Industries Board, Liberty Bonds, and the Committee on Public Information each did.

20. Contrast the Espionage Act of 1917 with the Sedition Act of 1918, state the holding and test of Schenck v. United States in 1919, and name one other prosecution from the lesson.

21. Who gained ground during the war years, and on what evidence? Cover women's war work and the suffrage argument, the Great Migration, and one cost that stored up labor unrest.

Lesson 8: Wilson's Peace and the Turn Inward

22. What did the Fourteen Points propose, and what did the Treaty of Versailles actually impose on Germany? Cover reparations and the responsibility language of Article 231.

23. Why did the Senate reject the treaty? Cover the Lodge reservations and Article 10, Wilson's refusal to accept them, his speaking tour and stroke, and the votes that followed.

24. Describe the troubled year 1919 and the turn that followed: the strikes, race violence, the Red Scare and the Palmer raids, and Harding's normalcy victory in the election of 1920.

Part 3: The Arguments of the Era

Every lesson in this unit is one side of an argument the country was having with itself. The test asks you to argue these positions, not just name them. Read each pair below and be ready to state both sides with evidence.

Mission against consent. Expansionists such as Senator Albert Beveridge argued that a noble land and a mighty people had a mission to carry free institutions across the world and to occupy new markets for American farms and factories. The Anti-Imperialist League answered that governing a people without its consent violates republican principles, and that a republic which rules colonies abroad will damage its own institutions at home.

Liberation against conquest. The United States declared war on Spain in 1898 under the Teller Amendment, promising not to annex Cuba and to leave its government to the Cuban people. The Treaty of Paris then transferred Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States, and the Platt Amendment kept a right of intervention over an independent Cuba. Whether the war freed Cuba, took an empire, or did both is the question the evidence has to answer.

Formal colonies against informal power. In China the United States asked only for an Open Door, equal trading rights for all nations in a China that remained one country. In Latin America it added the Roosevelt Corollary, a claimed right of intervention, and dollar diplomacy, influence carried by loans and investment backed by government pressure. Critics on the receiving end answered that a note, a loan, and a warship could control a country's life without the honesty of annexation.

Neutral rights against the submarine. The United States held that a neutral nation could trade with all sides and that its ships and passengers could not be sunk without warning. Germany answered that the British blockade starved its people while American factories and banks supplied its enemies, and that the submarine could survive only by striking unseen. The Sussex Pledge held the argument off for most of 1916; the resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare on February 1, 1917 settled it in favor of war.

Liberty against security at home. The government managed production through the War Industries Board, raised money through Liberty Bonds, and promoted the war through the Committee on Public Information, then punished dissent under the Espionage Act of 1917 and the Sedition Act of 1918. Charles Schenck and Eugene V. Debs answered that the First Amendment protected their speech. The Supreme Court in Schenck v. United States upheld the conviction under a clear and present danger test, and the boundary it drew governed wartime speech for a generation.

Internationalism against the turn inward. Wilson proposed the Fourteen Points and a League of Nations whose members promised to preserve peace and protect one another against external aggression. Senator Lodge and the reservationists answered that Article 10 would surrender to an international body the decision Congress alone should make. Wilson refused the reservations, the Senate rejected the treaty, and in 1920 the country elected Harding on a promise of normalcy, a return to ordinary life with less involvement abroad.

Teacher Answer Key

Part 1: Key Terms (model definitions, as given in the guided notes)

Lesson 1. imperialism: a policy of extending a nation's power by taking territory or control over other peoples and lands. navalism: the policy of making a powerful navy the foundation of national strength and overseas influence. coaling station: a port where steamships stop to load coal, water, and supplies on a long voyage. annexation: the formal act by which a country adds territory to itself. jingoism: boastful and aggressive patriotism that urges war or expansion against other nations. frontier thesis: Frederick Jackson Turner's argument that free land and a moving frontier explained American development and shaped the national character. protectorate: a territory controlled and protected by a stronger nation.

Lesson 2. yellow journalism: sensational newspaper reporting that stretched or invented facts to sell papers and stir public feeling. USS Maine: the American battleship that exploded and sank in Havana harbor on February 15, 1898, killing 266 officers and men and becoming the rallying cause of the war. Teller Amendment: the 1898 congressional provision promising that the United States would not annex Cuba and would leave its government to the Cuban people. Rough Riders: the First United States Volunteer Cavalry, the regiment led by Theodore Roosevelt that won fame in the fighting around Santiago, Cuba. Treaty of Paris: the December 1898 treaty that ended the war and transferred Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines from Spain to the United States. cession: the formal transfer of territory from one country to another by treaty. Platt Amendment: the 1901 congressional provision, written into Cuba's constitution, that gave the United States the right to intervene in Cuba and to hold naval stations there.

Lesson 3. Philippine-American War: the war fought from 1899 to 1902 between the United States and the Philippine Republic over American annexation of the Philippines. anti-imperialism: opposition to acquiring and ruling overseas colonies, especially on the ground that governing a people without its consent violates republican principles. Emilio Aguinaldo: the Filipino revolutionary leader who proclaimed Philippine independence in 1898 and served as president of the Philippine Republic. guerrilla warfare: fighting by small, mobile bands that ambush, raid, and disperse instead of meeting an enemy army in open battle. Benevolent Assimilation: the name President McKinley gave his Philippine policy in his proclamation of December 21, 1898, claiming that American rule would substitute just government for arbitrary rule. Insular Cases: the Supreme Court decisions beginning in 1901 that held the new island territories were not fully part of the United States and that the Constitution did not apply there in full. civil government: the rule of a territory by civilian officials acting under law, in place of direct military rule.

Lesson 4. Open Door: the 1899 and 1900 United States policy calling for equal trading rights for all nations in China and for China to remain one country. sphere of influence: a region in which one outside power holds dominant trading and investment privileges over the others. Boxer Rebellion: the 1900 uprising in China by the Boxers, a society opposed to foreign influence, put down by an eight-nation force that included United States troops. Roosevelt Corollary: Theodore Roosevelt's 1904 addition to the Monroe Doctrine claiming a United States right to intervene in Latin American countries guilty of chronic wrongdoing or unable to pay their debts. dollar diplomacy: President Taft's policy of using American loans and investment, backed by government pressure, to extend United States influence abroad. Panama Canal: the ship canal across the Isthmus of Panama, built by the United States between 1904 and 1914, linking the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. Gentlemen's Agreement: the 1907 and 1908 understanding in which Japan limited the emigration of its laborers to the United States and the United States ended the segregation of Japanese students in San Francisco schools.

Lesson 5. neutrality: a nation's policy of taking no side in a war between other countries. Lusitania: a British passenger liner sunk by a German submarine off Ireland on May 7, 1915, killing nearly 1,200 people, including 128 Americans. unrestricted submarine warfare: a naval policy of sinking ships in a declared war zone without warning, including neutral and passenger ships. Sussex Pledge: Germany's promise of May 4, 1916, after the Sussex attack, that its submarines would not sink merchant vessels without warning and without saving human lives. Zimmermann Telegram: a secret January 1917 message from Germany's foreign minister proposing that Mexico ally with Germany against the United States and recover Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. contraband: goods, such as weapons and military supplies, that a belligerent claims the right to seize from neutral ships bound for its enemy. preparedness: the movement and program in the United States before 1917 to strengthen the army and navy in case the country was drawn into the war.

Lesson 6. conscription: compulsory enrollment for military service, also called the draft. American Expeditionary Forces: the United States forces sent to fight in Europe under General John J. Pershing. convoy system: the grouping of troopships and supply ships to sail together under naval escort against submarines. trench warfare: fighting from lines of dug trenches facing each other across open ground called no man's land. armistice: an agreement to stop fighting, signed on November 11, 1918, that ended the fighting in the First World War. Doughboys: a nickname for American infantry soldiers in the First World War. influenza pandemic: the worldwide outbreak of influenza in 1918 and 1919 that killed millions of people, including an estimated 675,000 in the United States.

Lesson 7. Liberty Bonds: government bonds sold to the public to raise money for the war, repaid with interest. War Industries Board: the federal agency that coordinated war production and set priorities for industry during the war. Committee on Public Information: the federal agency that promoted support for the war through posters, pamphlets, films, and speakers. Espionage Act: the 1917 federal law that punished spying and acts intended to interfere with the military or obstruct recruiting. Sedition Act: the 1918 federal law that punished disloyal or abusive speech about the government, the Constitution, the flag, and the armed forces. Great Migration: the movement of hundreds of thousands of Black southerners to northern cities for jobs and greater rights during and after the war years. Schenck v. United States: the 1919 Supreme Court case that upheld an Espionage Act conviction and stated the clear and present danger test for wartime speech.

Lesson 8. Fourteen Points: President Woodrow Wilson's program for a just peace, presented to Congress on January 8, 1918, in fourteen numbered principles that ended with a general association of nations. League of Nations: the general association of nations created by the Treaty of Versailles in 1919, whose members promised to preserve peace and protect one another against external aggression. Treaty of Versailles: the treaty signed on June 28, 1919, between Germany and the Allied and Associated Powers, which created the League of Nations and imposed territorial losses, disarmament, and reparations on Germany. reparations: payments imposed on a defeated nation for the loss and damage its aggression caused during a war. Lodge reservations: the fourteen conditions Senator Henry Cabot Lodge and the Senate Foreign Relations Committee attached to ratification of the Treaty of Versailles, chiefly reserving to Congress the decision on any United States obligation under Article 10. Red Scare: the fear in 1919 and 1920 that Communists and other radicals were plotting revolution in the United States, and the government campaign against suspected radicals that the fear produced. normalcy: Warren G. Harding's word in the 1920 campaign for a return to ordinary life after war, with less government activism at home and less involvement abroad.

Part 2: Review Questions

1. Three motives, correctly paired. Any three. Sea power, argued by Captain Alfred Thayer Mahan in 1890: greatness rested on production, shipping, and colonies and naval stations joined by a battle fleet. Markets, argued by Senator Albert Beveridge in 1898: farms and factories produced a surplus the home market could not buy, so Americans had to find new markets for what they raised and made. Mission, argued by the minister Josiah Strong in Our Country (1885): the Anglo-Saxon race represented civil liberty and a pure spiritual Christianity and was being trained to spread its institutions. The wider field, drawn from Frederick Jackson Turner: free land and a moving frontier had explained American development, and the census of 1890 reported that a frontier line could hardly be said to exist any longer.

2. Hawaii, 1875 to 1893. A reciprocity treaty in 1875 let Hawaiian sugar enter the United States duty free. When the treaty was renewed in 1887, the United States gained the exclusive right to use Pearl Harbor as a naval station. Queen Liliuokalani came to the throne in 1891. In January 1893 a committee of businessmen, backed by the American minister and by troops landed from an American warship, overthrew her government. President Grover Cleveland concluded that the overthrow had been wrongful and withdrew the annexation treaty.

3. Cuba and the turn to war. Cuba lay ninety miles from Florida. In 1895 Cubans rose against Spanish rule. Spain answered with reconcentration, forcing rural Cubans into guarded towns where disease and hunger killed civilians in very large numbers. A nearby colony in rebellion, a harsh Spanish policy that American newspapers reported daily, and the explosion of the Maine in February 1898 turned a decade of expansion talk into war.

4. The Maine and the yellow press. The battleship Maine exploded and sank in Havana harbor on February 15, 1898, killing 266 officers and men. Yellow journalism, sensational reporting that stretched or invented facts to sell papers and stir public feeling, blamed Spain at once and kept the demand for war before the public while the official inquiry proceeded. The slogan drawn from the disaster made the ship the rallying cause of the war.

5. The Teller promise and the fighting. The Teller Amendment of 1898 promised that the United States would not annex Cuba and would leave its government to the Cuban people. The fighting reached far beyond Cuba: Commodore George Dewey destroyed the Spanish Pacific squadron at Manila Bay in the Philippines on May 1, 1898, and American forces then took Puerto Rico as well. In Cuba the First United States Volunteer Cavalry, the Rough Riders led by Theodore Roosevelt, won fame in the fighting around Santiago. A war declared for Cuban freedom ended with the United States holding an empire across two oceans.

6. The Treaty of Paris and the Platt Amendment. The Treaty of Paris of December 1898 ended the war and transferred Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines from Spain to the United States, a formal transfer of territory called a cession. Cuba became independent in name, but the Platt Amendment of 1901, written into Cuba's constitution, gave the United States the right to intervene in Cuba and to hold naval stations there, which made Cuba a protectorate, a territory controlled and protected by a stronger nation. Disease, not Spanish fire, killed far more American soldiers; the death toll from disease was far larger than the toll of battle.

7. The annexation debate. Expansionists argued that the Philippines gave the United States a base and a market at the gates of Asia, and that American rule would bring just government. The Anti-Imperialist League and its allies answered that governing a people without its consent violates republican principles, the ground stated in the lesson's definition of anti-imperialism, and that a republic could not hold colonies without becoming an empire. The Senate ratified the treaty in February 1899 by the narrowest of margins, days after fighting had already begun in Manila.

8. Aguinaldo, Benevolent Assimilation, and the war. Emilio Aguinaldo was the Filipino revolutionary leader who proclaimed Philippine independence in 1898 and served as president of the Philippine Republic. McKinley's proclamation of December 21, 1898 claimed that American rule would substitute just government for arbitrary rule, and he named the policy Benevolent Assimilation. The Philippine-American War of 1899 to 1902 tested the claim: after defeat in open battle, Filipino forces turned to guerrilla warfare, fighting by small, mobile bands that ambush, raid, and disperse instead of meeting an enemy army in open battle, and the American campaign against them brought heavy Filipino military and civilian deaths, the civilian figure an estimate in the lesson.

9. The Insular Cases and civil government. The Insular Cases, the Supreme Court decisions beginning in 1901, held that the new island territories were not fully part of the United States and that the Constitution did not apply there in full. Civil government, the rule of a territory by civilian officials acting under law, replaced direct military rule in the Philippines, without settling the question the cases had answered in the government's favor: by what right a republic held subjects who were not citizens on a path to statehood.

10. The Open Door and the Boxers. Hay's notes called for equal trading rights for all nations in China and for China to remain one country, against a map of spheres of influence, regions in which one outside power held dominant trading and investment privileges over the others. The Boxer Rebellion of 1900, an uprising by the Boxers, a society opposed to foreign influence, was put down by an eight-nation force that included United States troops, after which the powers kept China open to all of their trade rather than dividing it outright.

11. Panama and the canal. The United States backed Panama's revolution against Colombia in 1903 and at once recognized the new republic, which granted a canal zone. The Panama Canal, the ship canal across the Isthmus of Panama built by the United States between 1904 and 1914, linked the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans, so the two-ocean navy Mahan had called for could move between them without the long voyage around South America.

12. Three instruments of power. The Roosevelt Corollary (Theodore Roosevelt, Latin America, 1904) claimed a United States right to intervene in Latin American countries guilty of chronic wrongdoing or unable to pay their debts, an addition to the Monroe Doctrine. Dollar diplomacy (President Taft, Latin America and East Asia) used American loans and investment, backed by government pressure, to extend United States influence abroad. The Gentlemen's Agreement (Theodore Roosevelt, Japan, 1907 and 1908) was an understanding in which Japan limited the emigration of its laborers to the United States and the United States ended the segregation of Japanese students in San Francisco schools. One instrument threatened troops, one moved money, and one traded concessions; none required ruling a formal colony.

13. Neutrality under strain. A neutral nation could trade with all sides, but a belligerent could seize contraband, goods such as weapons and military supplies bound for its enemy. Britain enforced a blockade, stopping American ships and seizing cargoes, and cut American trade with Germany to almost nothing, while American farms and factories sold food, steel, and munitions to the Allies and American banks lent them the money to pay. Trade that was lawful for a neutral ran in one direction in practice, and Germany answered the blockade with the submarine, which could not observe the old rules of warning and rescue.

14. Lusitania to the Sussex Pledge. The Lusitania, a British passenger liner, was sunk by a German submarine off Ireland on May 7, 1915, killing nearly 1,200 people, including 128 Americans; the ship went down in eighteen minutes. After the Arabic sinking Germany pledged not to sink passenger liners without warning. After the Sussex was torpedoed in March 1916, Wilson threatened to break diplomatic relations unless such attacks ended. The Sussex Pledge of May 4, 1916 promised that Germany's submarines would not sink merchant vessels without warning and without saving human lives.

15. The chain of 1917. In order: Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare on February 1, 1917, calculating that sinkings could starve Britain into peace within months, before an American army could be raised and carried across the Atlantic; Wilson broke diplomatic relations two days later. The Zimmermann Telegram, published March 1, proposed that Mexico ally with Germany and recover Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. Revolution in Russia forced Czar Nicholas II to abdicate in March. German submarines sank three American merchant ships in mid-March, and Wilson's cabinet advised war without a dissenting vote. Wilson addressed Congress on April 2, 1917, and Congress declared war on April 6. Credit any link defended as decisive with two facts; the submarine answers rest on Wilson's own stated grounds, and the telegram answers on the territorial offer and its timing.

16. Raising and moving the army. Conscription, compulsory enrollment for military service, also called the draft, raised the mass army that volunteering could not. The men became the American Expeditionary Forces, the United States forces sent to fight in Europe under General John J. Pershing, who insisted they fight as an independent American army. The convoy system, the grouping of troopships and supply ships to sail together under naval escort against submarines, carried some two million men across the Atlantic with very small losses to U-boats.

17. What the soldiers met. Trench warfare, fighting from lines of dug trenches facing each other across open ground called no man's land, behind wire and under machine guns, artillery, and gas. The American army's first major independent operation cleared the St. Mihiel salient in September 1918, and the Meuse-Argonne offensive that followed was the largest battle in American history to that date, with about 26,000 American deaths, a figure the lesson labels an estimate of the toll commonly given. The armistice, an agreement to stop fighting signed on November 11, 1918, ended the fighting in the First World War.

18. Segregation and influenza. Black soldiers served in a segregated army; most were assigned to labor battalions that unloaded ships, built roads, and buried the dead, while combat units such as the 369th Infantry fought, in that regiment's case under French command, a service the lesson's February 1919 photograph records after the armistice. The influenza pandemic, the worldwide outbreak of 1918 and 1919, moved with the armies and through the training camps; it killed millions worldwide, at least 50 million by the estimate the lesson uses, including an estimated 675,000 people in the United States, more than American battle deaths. Both figures are estimates and are labeled as such in the lesson.

19. The managed home front. The War Industries Board, the federal agency that coordinated war production and set priorities for industry during the war, decided which factories received scarce materials and what they made. Liberty Bonds, government bonds sold to the public to raise money for the war, repaid with interest, drafted the savings of millions of households into the war budget. The Committee on Public Information, the federal agency that promoted support for the war through posters, pamphlets, films, and speakers, supplied the official persuasion that accompanied the draft of men and money.

20. Espionage, Sedition, and Schenck. The Espionage Act of 1917 punished spying and acts intended to interfere with the military or obstruct recruiting. The Sedition Act of 1918 went further and punished disloyal or abusive speech about the government, the Constitution, the flag, and the armed forces. In Schenck v. United States (1919) the Supreme Court upheld an Espionage Act conviction and stated the clear and present danger test for wartime speech. Accept Eugene V. Debs, jailed for an anti-draft speech, as the other prosecution, or another case accurately drawn from the lesson.

21. Gains and costs at home. Women took war jobs in factories, offices, and the services, and their visible war work strengthened the suffrage argument that won the Nineteenth Amendment. The Great Migration, the movement of hundreds of thousands of Black southerners to northern cities for jobs and greater rights during and after the war years, remade the population of the industrial North. The cost side: wartime controls held wages and prices in a managed economy that came apart in 1919, and the labor unrest the war years stored up broke out in the strikes of that year. Anti-German hysteria at home is also creditable evidence of a loss borne by German Americans.

22. The Fourteen Points and Versailles. The Fourteen Points, presented to Congress on January 8, 1918, were Wilson's program for a just peace in fourteen numbered principles that ended with a general association of nations. At Paris much of the program gave way. The Treaty of Versailles, signed on June 28, 1919, created the League of Nations and imposed territorial losses, disarmament, and reparations on Germany, payments imposed on a defeated nation for the loss and damage its aggression caused. Article 231 stated that Germany accepted responsibility for all the loss and damage of a war imposed by the aggression of Germany and her allies, the legal basis on which the payments rested and the clause Germans read as a verdict of sole war guilt.

23. The League fight. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge and the Senate Foreign Relations Committee attached fourteen Lodge reservations to ratification, chiefly reserving to Congress the decision on any United States obligation under Article 10, the article that bound members to protect one another against external aggression. Wilson ordered Democrats to vote against the treaty with the reservations rather than accept them. His national speaking tour to force the Senate's hand ended with his collapse and a severe stroke in October 1919. The Senate rejected the treaty with reservations in November 1919 and again in March 1920, and rejected it without reservations as well, so the United States never joined the League of Nations it had proposed.

24. 1919 and the turn inward. The year 1919 brought a wave of strikes, including a general strike in Seattle and national strikes in steel and coal, and race violence in Chicago and at Elaine, Arkansas, much of it aimed at the Black veterans and migrants of the war years. The Red Scare, the fear in 1919 and 1920 that Communists and other radicals were plotting revolution, produced the government campaign of the Palmer raids, mass arrests and deportations of suspected radicals under Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer. In the election of 1920 Warren G. Harding won on normalcy, his word for a return to ordinary life after war, with less government activism at home and less involvement abroad, and the country turned toward the Twenties.
