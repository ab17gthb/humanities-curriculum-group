The Spanish-American War

Directions: Read the full text, then answer the questions at the end.

Cuba in Rebellion

Cuba had been a Spanish colony for four centuries when Cubans rose in rebellion in 1895 to win independence. Spain sent General Valeriano Weyler to crush the revolt. Weyler ordered Cuban civilians in rebellious districts to move into fortified towns, a policy called reconcentration. In the crowded towns, disease and hunger killed large numbers of Cubans. Contemporary estimates put the deaths in the tens of thousands, and some ran far higher. Americans held sugar investments in Cuba, ninety miles from Florida. President William McKinley pressed Spain to end reconcentration, and Spain recalled Weyler, but the rebellion continued. In January 1898 the battleship Maine arrived in Havana harbor on a friendly visit.

The Maine and the Yellow Press

The USS Maine was the American battleship that exploded and sank in Havana harbor on February 15, 1898, killing 266 officers and men and becoming the rallying cause of the war. Two New York newspapers, William Randolph Hearst's Journal and Joseph Pulitzer's World, were fighting each other for readers, and both filled their pages with dramatic accounts of the disaster. Their style of reporting had a name: yellow journalism, sensational newspaper reporting that stretched or invented facts to sell papers and stir public feeling. Headlines in the yellow press treated Spanish guilt as settled fact, although no evidence connected Spain to the explosion. The slogan "Remember the Maine" spread across the country. A naval court of inquiry concluded that a submarine mine outside the ship had set off part of her ammunition. The same report stated that it had obtained no evidence fixing responsibility on any person. Later investigations disagreed, and the cause is still debated. In the spring of 1898, however, the public argument moved faster than the evidence.

A Promise Attached to the War

In April 1898 Congress adopted resolutions declaring Cuba free and independent, demanding that Spain withdraw its forces, and authorizing the president to use the army and navy to carry the resolutions out. A fourth resolution, offered by Senator Henry M. Teller of Colorado, was the Teller Amendment: the 1898 congressional provision promising that the United States would not annex Cuba and would leave its government to the Cuban people. It answered in advance the charge that the war meant annexation. McKinley signed the resolutions on April 20, and on April 25 Congress declared war, dating the start of the conflict to April 21.

War on Two Oceans

The first battle of the war took place not in Cuba but in the Philippines, Spain's colony in the Pacific. On May 1, 1898, Commodore George Dewey led an American squadron into Manila Bay and destroyed the Spanish Pacific fleet in a single morning. No American sailor was killed by Spanish fire. Manila surrendered to American forces in August. The Teller Amendment limited American claims on Cuba. It said nothing about the Philippines.

The land campaign in Cuba aimed at Santiago, where a Spanish fleet had taken shelter. American troops landed east of the city in June. On July 1 they attacked Spanish positions at El Caney and on the San Juan Heights. On Kettle Hill, one of those heights, the Rough Riders charged alongside regular army regiments, including the Black troopers of the 9th and 10th Cavalry. The Rough Riders were the First United States Volunteer Cavalry, the regiment led by Theodore Roosevelt that won fame in the fighting around Santiago, Cuba. Roosevelt had resigned a navy post to serve. On July 3 the Spanish fleet tried to escape and American warships destroyed it. Santiago surrendered on July 17. American forces occupied Puerto Rico, and Spain signed an armistice on August 12.

The Treaty of Paris

The Treaty of Paris, signed on December 10, 1898, set the terms of peace. Spain gave up its claim to Cuba, which was to be independent under a temporary American occupation. Spain also made a cession, the formal transfer of territory from one country to another by treaty, three times over. Puerto Rico and Guam passed to the United States outright, and Spain ceded the Philippine Islands in return for a payment of twenty million dollars. Filipino leaders had declared their own republic. They were not parties to the treaty and had not consented to the transfer. The Senate ratified the treaty on February 6, 1899, one vote above the two-thirds majority required. Cuba became formally independent in 1902, but before American troops withdrew, Congress passed the Platt Amendment in 1901 and required Cuba to write it into its constitution. The Platt Amendment was the 1901 congressional provision, written into Cuba's constitution, that gave the United States the right to intervene in Cuba and to hold naval stations there. Under it, the United States leased Guantanamo Bay as a naval station in 1903. Cuba was independent in form and a protectorate in practice.

A Short War and Its Real Cost

The fighting lasted less than four months, but the cost figures tell a different story. Fewer than 400 American servicemen were killed in battle or died of wounds. Disease killed far more. Typhoid spread through training camps in the United States, and malaria and yellow fever struck troops in Cuba. In all, about 2,400 American servicemen died during the war, the great majority from illness rather than Spanish fire. The war left the United States holding Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines, ruling millions of people overseas, and supervising an independent Cuba. Americans argued from the moment the treaty was signed about whether the war had freed Cuba, taken an empire, or done both.

Teacher Answer Key

1. What happened to the Maine, and what did the inquiry conclude? The battleship USS Maine exploded and sank in Havana harbor on February 15, 1898, killing 266 officers and men. The naval court of inquiry concluded that the explosion of a submarine mine had caused the partial explosion of two or more of the ship's forward magazines. The court also reported that it had obtained no evidence fixing responsibility for the destruction on any person or persons. (Wording follows President McKinley's Special Message to Congress transmitting the report, March 28, 1898, as reproduced by the American Presidency Project and in Foreign Relations of the United States, 1898.)

2. What did the Teller Amendment promise, and why did it matter? The Teller Amendment was the fourth resolution in the congressional war resolutions of April 1898. It stated that the United States disclaimed any intention to exercise sovereignty, jurisdiction, or control over Cuba except to pacify it, and that it would leave the government and control of the island to its people. It mattered because it answered in advance the charge that the war was a war of annexation, and because later measures, above all the Platt Amendment, were judged against it. (Text: joint resolution of April 20, 1898, Fourth Resolution.)

3. Two results of the fighting, one in the Philippines and one in Cuba. Philippines: on May 1, 1898, Commodore George Dewey's squadron destroyed the Spanish Pacific fleet in Manila Bay in a single morning, with no American sailor killed by Spanish fire; Manila surrendered in August. Cuba: American forces took the San Juan Heights on July 1, where the Rough Riders, the First United States Volunteer Cavalry led by Theodore Roosevelt, charged on Kettle Hill alongside regulars including the 9th and 10th Cavalry; the Spanish fleet at Santiago was destroyed on July 3 and the city surrendered on July 17.

4. Terms of the Treaty of Paris for the four places. Cuba: Spain relinquished its claim; Cuba was not ceded to the United States and became independent after a temporary American occupation. Puerto Rico: ceded to the United States. Guam: ceded to the United States. The Philippines: ceded to the United States in return for a payment of twenty million dollars. (Treaty of Paris, December 10, 1898, Articles I to III, Avalon Project, Yale Law School.)

5. Free Cuba, take an empire, or both? Accept any position defended with two accurate facts, one concerning the Platt Amendment. Liberation evidence: Spain's rule and the reconcentration deaths; the Teller Amendment's promise; Cuba's formal independence in 1902. Empire evidence: the cessions of Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines; the twenty million dollar payment for the Philippines without Filipino consent; the Platt Amendment written into Cuba's constitution, giving the United States a right to intervene and to hold naval stations such as Guantanamo Bay (leased 1903), which made independent Cuba a protectorate. Both at once is the position the full record supports most directly: the war ended Spanish rule in Cuba and transferred an island empire to the United States in the same settlement.
