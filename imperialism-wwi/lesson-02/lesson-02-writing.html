Writing Task: Free Cuba, Take an Empire, or Both?

Directions: Answer the lesson question in one well-built paragraph of eight to twelve sentences. Use the scaffold below to plan, then write your paragraph in the space provided. Your paragraph will be graded on the rubric in the teacher key.

Prompt. Was the war with Spain fought to free Cuba, to take an empire, or both?

Step 1: Gather Evidence on Both Sides

Fill in the chart from the lesson reading and the headline and evidence lab. You need at least two facts in each column before you choose a claim. A both-at-once claim uses facts from both columns. A one-sided claim uses one column and must answer the other.

Step 2: Build the Paragraph

Use this structure. The sentence starters are optional; the moves are not.

  • Claim. State your answer in the first sentence. Starter: The war did both at once: it freed Cuba from Spain and, in the same settlement, gave the United States...
  • Evidence 1. Give a specific fact, with the document or event named. Starter: Before the fighting began, Congress attached the Teller Amendment, which promised...
  • Explanation 1. Say what that fact proves about your claim.
  • Evidence 2. Give a second specific fact, from the other column if your claim is both at once. Starter: The Treaty of Paris then ceded..., which is a cession, meaning...
  • Explanation 2. Say what that fact proves about your claim.
  • Counterargument and answer. Name the strongest argument against your claim and answer it. Starter: A critic of my claim would point to the Platt Amendment, but that document...
  • Conclusion. Restate the claim in one sentence that a reader could argue with.

Words you must use accurately: cession, Treaty of Paris, and one of Teller Amendment, Platt Amendment, yellow journalism, or Rough Riders, each with its meaning shown by how you use it.

cession: the formal transfer of territory from one country to another by treaty

Teacher Key and Rubric

A Strong Model Paragraph (for teacher reference)

The war did both at once: it freed Cuba from Spain and, in the same settlement, gave the United States an empire. Before the fighting began, Congress attached the Teller Amendment to the war resolutions, promising that the United States would not annex Cuba and would leave its government to the Cuban people, and Cuba did become an independent republic in 1902. The Treaty of Paris tells the other half of the story. By cession, the formal transfer of territory from one country to another by treaty, Spain handed the United States Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines, the last in return for twenty million dollars and without the consent of the Filipino leaders who had declared their own republic. A defender of a liberation-only claim would answer that the Teller promise was kept, since Cuba was never annexed. The promise was kept in its letter and narrowed in practice, because the Platt Amendment, written into Cuba's constitution before American troops withdrew, gave the United States a right to intervene in Cuba and to hold naval stations there, as it did at Guantanamo Bay. Spain's rule over Cuba ended, and an American island empire began; the war accomplished the first by means that produced the second.

Rubric

Full credit (10 points) should be awarded as follows:

  • Claim (2 points). The first sentence answers the prompt directly with one of the three positions, stated so a reader could disagree with it.
  • Evidence (3 points). At least two specific facts from the lesson, with documents or events named accurately (for example, the Teller Amendment promise of April 1898; the cessions in Articles II and III of the Treaty of Paris; the twenty million dollar payment for the Philippines; the Platt Amendment's intervention right and naval stations; Guantanamo Bay leased in 1903).
  • Explanation (2 points). Each fact is connected to the claim in the student's own words, not left as a list.
  • Counterargument (2 points). The strongest opposing argument is stated fairly (the Teller promise and Cuban independence for liberation only, or the scale of the cessions for empire only) and answered with evidence.
  • Vocabulary (1 point). Cession and Treaty of Paris are used accurately, plus one further term from the required list used in a way that shows its meaning.

Common deductions. A paragraph that praises or condemns the war without a named document or fact earns no evidence points. Treating the Maine explosion as proven Spanish sabotage is a content error; the 1898 inquiry blamed a mine and fixed responsibility on no one. A both-at-once claim with evidence from only one column earns at most half of the evidence points.
